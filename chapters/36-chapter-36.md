## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** Six days after the attack on Pearl Harbor, on December 13, 1941, Churchill boarded the battleship HMS *Duke of York* and crossed the Atlantic for the United States. Protected by escorts and weathering heavy weather, the ship reached the Norfolk Navy Yard in Virginia on December 22, 1941; the president greeted the prime minister shortly afterward at a Washington airfield.[^ch36-whha-churchill]
- **The conference.** Churchill came for a summit with Roosevelt and the senior American military staff that ran from December 22, 1941, to January 14, 1942 and was code-named ARCADIA. He told the Americans that its purpose was to “review the whole war plan in light of reality and new facts, as well as the problems of production and distribution.” The conference set the strategic direction of the Anglo-American war effort and established the American Joint Chiefs of Staff as a counterpart to the British Chiefs of Staff Committee, creating the Combined Chiefs of Staff.[^ch36-arcadia]
- **What was decided and what was not.** The two governments reaffirmed “Germany First,” the earlier agreement that Germany was the primary enemy in a two-front war, over Churchill’s worry that the Japanese attacks would divert American effort to the Pacific. Brigadier General Dwight D. Eisenhower, then in the War Department’s War Plans Division, wrote that the conference had to recommend a scheme for waging “the war against Germany and Japan, enemies which were widely separated geographically” and concluded flatly that “we had to attack to win.”[^ch36-arcadia]
- **The public moment.** Churchill addressed a joint session of Congress on December 26, 1941, telling the members, “I cannot help reflecting that if my father had been American and my mother British, instead of the other way round, I might have got here on my own,” and warning that “many disappointments and unpleasant surprises await us.”[^ch36-churchill-congress]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the dates, the crossing, the conference, the decisions, and the texts. It cannot show what every American took from the alliance, and the outline has not yet located a scholarly account of the domestic reception of Churchill’s visit.[^ch36-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In December 1941 no one knew that the war would end in 1945, that the Soviet Union would bear the largest share of German casualties, that the atomic bomb would be used, or that the Grand Alliance would fracture into the Cold War. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch36-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A journey opens a problem rather than settling it.** The scene’s claim is not that Arcadia decided the war but that it revealed the coalition’s central difficulty: two allies with different geography, resources, and postwar aims had to agree on a common strategy while each retained its own. Churchill’s shipboard crossing dramatizes the distance the alliance had to bridge.[^ch36-whha-churchill][^ch36-arcadia]
- **The stakes.** The conference’s creation of the Combined Chiefs of Staff marks the moment when coalition warfare became an institution rather than a sentiment, which is why it makes a useful opening. The chapter follows how that institution bargained through the second front, the Mediterranean, and the Pacific.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did coalition warfare remake American power and belonging?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from a Virginia anchorage to the Combined Chiefs, to the shipyards and assembly lines, to the incarceration camps, to Auschwitz and Hiroshima, and to the founding of the United Nations in San Francisco.[^ch36-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The dates, the crossing, the conference, and the quotations are documented. The scene may not stage the crossing weather, cabin conversations, or the private thinking of Churchill or Roosevelt beyond what records state.[^ch36-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the second front, the atomic bomb, or the Cold War.[^ch36-check-scene]
- **No lone-hero framing.** The scene must not reduce the coalition to the friendship of two leaders. The Joint Chiefs, the British delegation, and the Combined Chiefs did the institutional work.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-check-silences]

## 1. Coalition and European strategy

### 1.1 The claim: coalition warfare as bargaining

- **The claim.** The Grand Alliance won the war in Europe through constant bargaining among allies with different priorities, and the American preference for a direct cross-channel invasion of France competed throughout 1941–43 with the British preference for Mediterranean operations.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present coalition strategy as a negotiated sequence, not a plan handed down by Washington, because the second-front delay was the alliance’s central political conflict.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-stoler]
- **What the record can show.** It can show conferences, decisions, and deployments. It cannot show that any single strategy was obviously correct, and the chapter should treat the alternatives fairly.[^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 Germany first and the Combined Chiefs

- **The claim.** At Arcadia the two powers reaffirmed the “Germany First” principle and built the Combined Chiefs of Staff to coordinate strategy, giving the coalition a permanent institution.[^ch36-arcadia]
- **The institution.** The conference created the American Joint Chiefs of Staff as a counterpart to the British Chiefs of Staff Committee; the British delegation at Arcadia included Admiral of the Fleet Sir Dudley Pound, Air Chief Marshal Sir Charles Portal, and Field Marshal Sir John Dill, who remained in Washington as the British liaison after the conference.[^ch36-arcadia]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the institution and its members. It cannot show that “Germany First” was ever fully honored, and the chapter should note the persistent Pacific claims on American resources.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 1.3 The second-front debate and the Mediterranean

- **The claim.** The Allies deferred the cross-channel invasion until 1944 and fought first in North Africa, Sicily, and Italy, a sequence that pleased the British and frustrated the Soviet Union, which bore the heaviest German assault.[^ch36-war-conferences][^ch36-casablanca]
- **The sequence.** The Casablanca Conference of January 14–24, 1943 resolved to concentrate against Germany, to invade Sicily and the Italian mainland, to intensify strategic bombing, and to keep pressure on Japan in the Pacific; the Tehran Conference later in 1943 secured confirmation of the cross-channel invasion and a Soviet promise to enter the war against Japan.[^ch36-casablanca][^ch36-war-conferences]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the decisions and their sequence. It cannot show that the delay lengthened the war, and the chapter should present that as an open question.[^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 Unconditional surrender and coalition aims

- **The claim.** At Casablanca Roosevelt announced the policy of “unconditional surrender,” which bound the coalition to total victory and later became a subject of historical dispute over whether it prolonged German resistance.[^ch36-casablanca]
- **The policy.** Roosevelt stated on the conference’s final day that he and Churchill had decided that the only way to ensure postwar peace was unconditional surrender, while adding that the policy did not mean the destruction of the German people.[^ch36-casablanca]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the announcement and its stated meaning. It cannot show that the policy stiffened German resistance, and the chapter should attribute that claim to the historians who make it.[^ch36-casablanca][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 Map 27 and the European campaigns

- **The claim.** The European war was won through the campaigns the coalition planned together: the North Africa and Italian operations, the strategic bombing campaign, the Normandy invasion of June 6, 1944, and the advance into Germany.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences]
- **Figure and map.** The registered visual `ch36-map-01` (Map 27, “American deployments, 1941–1945”) belongs to §§1–2. It should distinguish the European and Mediterranean theaters from the Pacific, show the major deployment and supply routes, and identify the observation date of each element. Its caption and source line must state that the evidence is official Army and naval histories and National Archives campaign maps, and the production record is `repo-map27`. The map must distinguish coalition theaters and supply routes rather than implying that all American forces moved under one command.[^ch36-check-map27][^ch36-arcadia]
- **What the record can show.** It can show deployments and campaigns. It cannot show operational causation from a map alone, and the chapter should keep strategy in the prose.[^ch36-check-map27]

## 2. Pacific campaigns

### 2.1 The claim: a second war with its own logic

- **The claim.** The Pacific war followed its own geography and command structure, and its conduct was marked by a racialized hatred on both sides that the European war did not match.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-dower]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the Pacific campaigns together with the racial ideology that shaped them, because the two cannot be separated without distorting either.[^ch36-dower][^ch36-check-debate-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show campaigns, casualties, and propaganda. It cannot show what every combatant believed, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch36-check-silences]

### 2.2 Midway and the turn of the Pacific war

- **The claim.** The Battle of Midway of June 4–7, 1942 was a five-day naval engagement in the central Pacific that changed the course of the Pacific war.[^ch36-midway]
- **The context.** The battle followed the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor and a string of Japanese advances; American code-breaking and Admiral Chester W. Nimitz’s decisions shaped the encounter, in which Japan lost four fleet carriers in a single day.[^ch36-midway]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the engagement and its immediate losses. It cannot show that Midway alone decided the war, and the chapter should present it as a turning point rather than the end of the contest.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 The island campaigns and command

- **The claim.** From 1943 American forces advanced across the central and southwestern Pacific under two commands, Nimitz’s in the central Pacific and General Douglas MacArthur’s in the southwest, converging toward Japan.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-iwo-okinawa]
- **The campaigns.** The advance through the Gilberts, Marshalls, and Marianas was followed in 1945 by the invasions of Iwo Jima on February 19 and Okinawa on April 1, each a costly assault against entrenched Japanese defenders.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the campaigns and their costs. It cannot show that the two-command arrangement was efficient, and the chapter should present the command rivalry as a coordination problem.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 2.4 Iwo Jima, Okinawa, and the calculus of invasion

- **The claim.** The bloodshed on Iwo Jima and Okinawa shaped American estimates of what an invasion of the Japanese home islands would cost, and those estimates entered the debate over the atomic bomb.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-walker]
- **The figures.** Iwo Jima cost nearly 6,900 American dead, and Okinawa cost more than 12,000 American dead with total American casualties exceeding 50,000, alongside far larger Japanese losses. The chapter should give each figure with its date, definition, and source.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-check-numbers]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the casualties. It cannot show that the projected invasion casualties dictated the atomic decision, and the chapter should treat that as an open question.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-check-atomic]

### 2.5 Race, atrocity, and the conduct of the Pacific war

- **The claim.** Both sides in the Pacific war dehumanized the other, and the racial character of the fighting shaped atrocity, surrender, and memory; John W. Dower argues that the Pacific war should be understood as a race war.[^ch36-dower]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should set the racialization of the enemy against the home-front racism that produced the incarceration of Japanese Americans (§4), linking battlefront and home front.[^ch36-dower][^ch36-eo9066]
- **What the record can show.** It can show propaganda and policy. It cannot show that racial hatred alone explains specific atrocities, and the chapter should distinguish ideology, orders, and circumstances.[^ch36-check-debate-works]

## 3. The arsenal and home fronts

### 3.1 The claim: a negotiated mobilization

- **The claim.** American victory rested on an industrial mobilization that converted the world’s largest economy to war production, and that mobilization was negotiated among government, corporations, and organized labor rather than simply commanded.[^ch36-war-production]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the war economy as a set of bargains over production, wages, and profits, because those bargains shaped who benefited from the boom.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-check-homefront]
- **What the record can show.** It can show output and policy. It cannot show that mobilization ended the Depression by itself, and the chapter should connect it to the prewar New Deal (Ch. 34).[^ch36-check-homefront][^ch36-check-ch35]

### 3.2 Out-producing the enemy

- **The claim.** Roosevelt set extraordinary production goals after Pearl Harbor and created the War Production Board in 1942 to coordinate them, telling the country that “powerful enemies must be out-fought and out-produced.”[^ch36-war-production]
- **The output.** In the three years after Midway, the United States built 17 aircraft carriers to Japan’s six; over the war American industry provided nearly two-thirds of all Allied military equipment, producing about 297,000 aircraft, 193,000 artillery pieces, 86,000 tanks, and two million army trucks, and American industrial production doubled in four years.[^ch36-war-production]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the totals. It cannot show that any single agency caused the expansion, and the chapter should attribute the numbers to their sources and note their rounding.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Women workers and the limits of “Rosie”

- **The claim.** More than six million women entered the workforce during the war, taking jobs in factories, shipyards, and heavy industry, but the “Rosie the Riveter” image obscured the discrimination, the unequal pay, and the postwar pressure to leave.[^ch36-rosie]
- **The evidence.** Roosevelt’s Executive Order 8802 banning discrimination in defense jobs facilitated new roles, and companies were encouraged to hire women who had not normally worked, including mothers. The “Rosie the Riveter” name came from a 1942 song, and the “We Can Do It!” poster was a War Production Board image.[^ch36-rosie]
- **What the record can show.** It can show employment and policy. It cannot show that the war durably changed women’s work, and the chapter should present the wartime opening as temporary.[^ch36-rosie][^ch36-check-homefront]

### 3.4 Mexican labor, migration, and the Bracero Program

- **The claim.** Facing a farm-labor shortage, the United States and Mexico concluded the Mexican Farm Labor Agreement of August 4, 1942, creating the Bracero Program, which eventually brought more than four million Mexican workers to American agriculture and railroads.[^ch36-bracero]
- **The terms and the limits.** The two governments agreed on protocols meant to protect braceros from discrimination and poor wages, but workers still faced surcharges for room and board, deducted pay, and exposure to dangerous chemicals. The program ran until 1964.[^ch36-bracero]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the agreement and the totals. It cannot show that the protections were enforced, and the chapter should present the gap between the protocols and the experience.[^ch36-bracero][^ch36-check-refuge]

### 3.5 Migration, rationing, and the home-front bargain

- **The claim.** The war moved millions of Americans toward defense plants and military bases, and rationing, war bonds, and higher taxes made the civilian population a partner in the war economy.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-rosie]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should show the home front as both opportunity and strain, connecting the boom to the housing shortages and racial conflicts that erupted in 1943.[^ch36-detroit][^ch36-double-v]
- **What the record can show.** It can show movement and policy. It cannot show that the boom was evenly shared, and the chapter should name the regional and racial inequalities it left.[^ch36-check-homefront][^ch36-check-silences]

### 3.6 The Office of War Information and the “good war” image

- **The claim.** The government and the culture industries produced an image of the war as a unified democratic crusade, an image that later earned the name “the good war” and that obscured the conflict’s exclusions.[^ch36-terkel][^ch36-dower]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should use the “good war” label as a remembered description to analyze, not as a neutral verdict, and should set it against the incarceration, the race riots, and the refugee crisis.[^ch36-terkel][^ch36-cwric]
- **What the record can show.** It can show propaganda and memory. It cannot show that most Americans believed the image, and the chapter should mark the gap between official culture and popular experience.[^ch36-check-silences]

## 4. Citizenship, incarceration, and refuge

### 4.1 The claim: belonging tested

- **The claim.** The war tested who belonged to the nation, and it did so through sacrifice and exclusion at once: Japanese Americans were incarcerated and their sons were drafted, Black Americans fought a “Double V” campaign at home and abroad, and Jewish refugees were largely kept out even as the Holocaust became known.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-double-v][^ch36-wyman]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should hold service and exclusion together, because the same state that refused and imprisoned some citizens also demanded their labor and their lives.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-double-v]
- **What the record can show.** It can show policy, service, and resistance. It cannot show that exclusion and sacrifice were experienced the same way by everyone, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch36-check-silences]

### 4.2 Executive Order 9066 and the incarceration

- **The claim.** On February 19, 1942 Roosevelt issued Executive Order 9066, authorizing military commanders to exclude civilians from designated areas; without naming any ethnic group, the order led to the forced removal and incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, nearly 70,000 of them American citizens.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-manzanar]
- **The process and the sites.** Under the order, Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt’s Western Defense Command imposed curfews and then forced evacuation on forty-eight hours’ notice; families were moved first to temporary assembly centers and then to ten relocation centers in seven states, including Manzanar in California’s Owens Valley, where more than 10,000 people were crowded into barbed-wired, guarded barracks by September 1942.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-manzanar]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the order, the numbers, and the conditions. It cannot show that the incarceration was a military necessity; the government’s own later commission rejected that justification.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-cwric]
- **The terms.** The book’s prose should prefer “incarceration” and the euphemism “relocation centers” only as the government’s period terms; “internment,” strictly, describes the detention of enemy aliens, which does not describe the citizen majority. Explain the distinction at first use.[^ch36-check-incarceration-terms][^ch36-cwric]

### 4.3 The In Focus: the 1943 “Application for Leave Clearance”

**In Focus.** The In Focus reads the form the government called the “Application for Leave Clearance,” distributed in 1943 by the War Relocation Authority and the War Department to adults in the incarceration camps. Two questions carried the loyalty test. Question 27 asked, “Are you willing to serve in the armed forces of the United States on combat duty, wherever ordered?” Question 28 asked whether the respondent would “swear unqualified allegiance to the United States of America and faithfully defend the United States from any and all attacks by foreign and domestic forces, and forswear any form of allegiance or disobedience to the Japanese Emperor, or any other foreign government, power, or organization.” The form was, in part, a recruiting and release instrument: the War Department wanted to register draft-age men, and the War Relocation Authority wanted a list of adults eligible to leave. Its effects fell differently on different respondents. Many Issei — the immigrant generation, barred by law from naturalized citizenship — feared that answering “yes” to Question 28 would leave them stateless. Many Nisei — the citizen generation — feared that answering “yes” implied they had previously been loyal to the emperor. A significant minority refused, qualified, or answered “no” to both questions; roughly 12,000 of the 78,000 adults who received the form were eventually segregated at Tule Lake. The box should distinguish these groups and their legal situations, and should not confuse them with the separate draft resisters, most of whom answered “yes” to the loyalty questions and refused induction.[^ch36-loyalty-questions][^ch36-no-no][^ch36-cwric][^ch36-manzanar]

- **The registered status.** No image is assigned to this chapter, so the section is planned as a textual close reading of the form. Reproducing a facsimile of the questionnaire would require a new register entry, with its own caption, credit, rights, and alt text, before it could be added.[^ch36-check-focus][^ch36-check-image]
- **The exact text.** The wording of Questions 27 and 28 is quoted from the University of Denver’s *Behind Barbed Wire* project and must be collated against a surviving copy of the form itself.[^ch36-loyalty-questions]
- **The numbers and their source.** The figure of about 12,000 segregated out of about 78,000 respondents is drawn from Densho’s “No-no boys” entry, which cites *The Evacuated People: A Quantitative Description* (1946); the chapter should give the underlying counts and their source rather than the rounded figure alone.[^ch36-no-no]
- **What must not be done.** The box must not treat the “no-no” respondents as uniformly disloyal, must not use the questionnaire to justify the incarceration it administered, and must not let the form stand in for the whole experience of camp life, which §4.2 and §4.5 present in its own terms.[^ch36-check-focus][^ch36-check-silences]

### 4.4 Nisei service, Korematsu, and the 442nd

- **The claim.** Japanese Americans served in large numbers even as their families remained imprisoned, and the Supreme Court upheld their exclusion, creating a lasting constitutional wound.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-korematsu]
- **The service.** The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, activated in early 1943 and made up mostly of Hawaiian Nisei with a mainland contingent, fought in Italy and France alongside the 100th Infantry Battalion and became the most decorated unit for its size and length of service in American military history; more than 25,000 Japanese Americans served in the war overall.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-manzanar]
- **The case.** In *Korematsu v. United States* (1944) the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion of a citizen of Japanese descent as constitutional at the time it was imposed, while acknowledging that all legal restrictions curtailing the rights of a single racial group are “immediately suspect.” Justice Jackson’s dissent warned that the Court had validated “the principle of racial discrimination in criminal procedure and of transplanting American citizens.”[^ch36-korematsu]
- **What the record can show.** It can show service and the opinions. It cannot show that service erased exclusion, and the chapter should present the two together.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-korematsu]

### 4.5 Black Americans, the Double V, and the riots of 1943

- **The claim.** Black Americans pressed a “Double V” campaign for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home, and the wartime migration to industrial cities produced racial violence, most terribly in Detroit in June 1943.[^ch36-double-v][^ch36-detroit]
- **The campaign.** Black communities and institutions threw themselves into the war effort while demanding first-class citizenship, and the Pittsburgh *Courier’s* “Double V” campaign tied military service to the demand for equality. Black colleges and universities trained thousands for defense work.[^ch36-double-v]
- **The violence.** In Detroit, a fight at Belle Isle on June 20, 1943 ignited two days of rioting that killed 34 people, 25 of them Black, and that grew from overcrowded housing and unequal access to jobs and services.[^ch36-detroit]
- **What the record can show.** It can show organizing and violence. It cannot show that the war produced a single Black politics, and the chapter should present the wartime demands as varied.[^ch36-double-v][^ch36-check-silences]

### 4.6 Refugees, rescue, and American limits

- **The claim.** American refugee policy during the war was restrictive, and the government moved slowly and partially even as evidence of the Holocaust accumulated; the War Refugee Board, created only in January 1944, rescued many but could not undo the earlier refusal.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-wrb-nwwii]
- **The record.** Nazi persecution of Jews was reported from the start of the war, yet the United States had no refugee law or asylum policy, the passengers of the *St. Louis* found no refuge, and Roosevelt created the War Refugee Board only in January 1944; by then, more than five million European Jews had already been murdered.[^ch36-wrb-nwwii][^ch36-wrb-ushmm]
- **What the record can show.** It can show policy and its timing. It cannot show that a more generous policy would have saved a specific number, and the chapter should present the counterfactual as an argument, not a count.[^ch36-check-refuge][^ch36-check-debate-works]

## 5. Holocaust, atomic weapons, surrender, and the United Nations

### 5.1 The claim: rescue, annihilation, and the founding of a new order

- **The claim.** The war’s final year combined fitful rescue of Europe’s Jews, the invention and use of atomic weapons, the unconditional surrender of Germany and Japan, and the creation of the United Nations, and those developments must be told together because they belonged to the same moment.[^ch36-wrb-ushmm][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-un-charter]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should resist separating the rescue story from the annihilation, and the atomic story from the founding of the United Nations, because the actors faced them as one problem: what the victors owed a shattered world.[^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-un-charter]
- **What the record can show.** It can show policy and its texts. It cannot show the moral weighing in any actor’s mind, and the chapter should present the decisions as contested then and now.[^ch36-check-atomic][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The War Refugee Board and the limits of rescue

- **The claim.** The War Refugee Board, established by executive order on January 22, 1944, funneled money and diplomatic pressure into rescue and relief, saving many thousands in the war’s final months, but it began late and within a hostile immigration regime.[^ch36-wrb-nwwii][^ch36-wrb-ushmm]
- **The work.** The Board sent about $12 million overseas for relief, pressured neutral and Latin American governments to protect refugees, appointed representatives in Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, Portugal, and Britain, and helped some 8,000 refugees reach Palestine through negotiations in Turkey, though one ship, the *Mefkure*, was torpedoed at sea.[^ch36-wrb-ushmm]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the Board’s operations and their scale. It cannot show that its creation redeemed earlier inaction, and the chapter should hold the two together.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-check-refuge]

### 5.3 The bombing debate and the politics of rescue

- **The claim.** Whether the Allies should have bombed the rail lines to Auschwitz became a central historical dispute over American rescue policy, one that David S. Wyman made into the sharpest charge of abandonment.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-wrb-ushmm]
- **The debate.** The chapter should state the strongest version of each side: that military and political leaders chose not to divert resources to rescue, and that the feasibility and effect of bombing the killing sites were uncertain and contested at the time. Attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-check-debate-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show what the Allies knew and when. It cannot show what a bombing raid would have accomplished, and the chapter should present that as counterfactual.[^ch36-check-refuge]

### 5.4 The Manhattan Project and Trinity

- **The claim.** Under the top-secret Manhattan Project, the United States built the world’s first atomic weapons at Oak Ridge, Hanford, and Los Alamos, and tested the plutonium bomb at the Trinity site on July 16, 1945.[^ch36-manhattan][^ch36-trinity]
- **The project and the test.** The project began after Albert Einstein’s 1939 letter warned Roosevelt of the possibility of an atomic weapon; it pursued uranium and plutonium paths in parallel and acquired its sites through eminent domain, displacing Tribes, farming communities, and homesteaders, some of whom had thirty days to leave. Robert Oppenheimer directed the Los Alamos laboratory and named the test “Trinity”; the device was detonated on the Jornada del Muerto in southern New Mexico.[^ch36-manhattan][^ch36-trinity]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the project, the test, and the sites. It cannot show that the bomb was necessary to end the war, which is the debate of §6.[^ch36-trinity][^ch36-check-atomic]

### 5.5 Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and the surrender

- **The claim.** The United States bombed Hiroshima on August 6, 1945 and Nagasaki on August 9, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan, Japan announced its surrender, and the formal instrument was signed aboard the USS *Missouri* on September 2, 1945.[^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-surrender]
- **The statement.** Truman announced that an American airplane had dropped one bomb on Hiroshima with the power of more than 20,000 tons of TNT and said, “It is an atomic bomb. It is a harnessing of the basic power of the universe.” The Japanese instrument of surrender proclaimed “the unconditional surrender to the Allied Powers” of all Japanese armed forces, and General Douglas MacArthur signed for the Allies, with the time recorded as four minutes past nine.[^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-surrender]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the bombings, the statements, and the surrender. It cannot show that either bomb alone caused the surrender, and the chapter should present the Soviet declaration of war on August 8 and the timing of the surrender together.[^ch36-hasegawa][^ch36-check-atomic]

### 5.6 The United Nations Charter at San Francisco

- **The claim.** Even before the war ended, the Allies built a new international organization; representatives of fifty nations met in San Francisco, signed the United Nations Charter on June 26, 1945, and brought it into force on October 24, 1945.[^ch36-un-charter]
- **The institution.** The charter created a General Assembly of all members and a Security Council whose five wartime victors — China, France, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and the United States — held permanent seats and individual veto power, an arrangement that reflected the war’s power and would shape the coming confrontation.[^ch36-un-charter][^ch36-check-ch37]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the text and the structure. It cannot show that the organization would keep the peace, and the chapter should present the charter as a promise whose record lay ahead.[^ch36-un-charter]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The Grand Alliance fought and won the war through negotiation.** The Combined Chiefs of Staff, the Germany-first principle, and the succession of conferences were real institutions and decisions, whatever their cost.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences]
- **Industrial mobilization was decisive.** American production of aircraft, ships, tanks, and vehicles far exceeded that of the Axis and provided most Allied equipment.[^ch36-war-production]
- **The incarceration was not a military necessity.** The government’s own commission concluded that the broad causes were race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership, and that a grave injustice was done.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-eo9066]
- **The Pacific war was racialized on both sides.** The evidence of propaganda, policy, and atrocity supports treating the Pacific war as a race war.[^ch36-dower]
- **The atomic bombs were used, and the surrender followed.** The dates, the strikes, and the instrument of surrender are documented.[^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-surrender]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the atomic bomb necessary to end the war?** One position holds that the bombings avoided a costly invasion of Japan and saved lives; another holds that Japan was already near surrender and that the bomb was aimed partly at Soviet power. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch36-stinson][^ch36-alperovitz][^ch36-walker]
- **Did the atomic bombs or the Soviet declaration of war cause the surrender?** Tsuyoshi Hasegawa argues that the Soviet entry played the greater role; the orthodox account emphasizes the bombs. The chapter should weigh both against the Japanese records.[^ch36-hasegawa][^ch36-walker]
- **Did the second-front delay lengthen the war?** The British defend the Mediterranean strategy; the Americans and the Soviets believed a cross-channel invasion should have come sooner. The chapter should present the strategic arguments, not only the outcomes.[^ch36-stoler][^ch36-war-conferences]
- **Could the United States have rescued more Jews?** Wyman’s “abandonment” thesis and its critics differ on what was feasible and what officials decided. The chapter should present the strongest version of each.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-check-debate-works]
- **How large was the war’s human cost?** Global mortality estimates vary widely and rest on different methods; the chapter should give ranges, name the methods, and refuse to present one number as settled.[^ch36-mortality][^ch36-check-mortality]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the United States helped win a coalition war of enormous destructive power and, in the same years, imprisoned its own citizens, restricted refugees, and used atomic weapons against cities. Neither a victory story nor a betrayal story explains the whole.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-truman-hiroshima]
- **Contingency.** The second front’s timing, the incarceration, the rescue policy, and the atomic decision were choices taken by people who could have chosen otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-alperovitz]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold structural pressures — geography, industrial capacity, alliance politics, racial ideology — together with the contingent choices made within them.[^ch36-stoler][^ch36-dower]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that coalition warfare expanded American power abroad and sharpened the question of who belonged at home, and that the war’s endings — rescue, annihilation, the bomb, and the United Nations — left a world the victors only partly controlled.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-un-charter]

## Legacies

The war left the United States a permanent national security state. The agencies, alliances, and deployment patterns built between 1941 and 1945 fed directly into the Cold War institutions that Chapter 37 describes, and the Pentagon, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, and the network of overseas bases outlasted the coalition that created them.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-check-ch37]

The war also left an accounting. The incarceration of Japanese Americans produced a generations-long struggle for redress that ended in a formal apology and a $20,000 payment to each surviving internee under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, and the memory of the camps became a touchstone for later arguments about security and civil liberty.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-cwric]

The atomic bombings began an argument that has not ended. The decision to use the bombs, their role in the surrender, and their place in the Cold War arms race remain contested, and the chapter should note that the debate is live rather than closed.[^ch36-alperovitz][^ch36-hasegawa]

The “good war” image proved durable and selective. It celebrated the mobilization and the victory while leaving the incarceration, the race riots, and the refugee crisis at the margins, which is why the chapter treats memory as part of the history rather than a footnote to it.[^ch36-terkel][^ch36-cwric]

- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, generals, and organized institutions and under-represents the incarcerated, the displaced, the conscripted poor, and the dead of occupied and colonized places. ## Summary

- At the Arcadia Conference of December 1941 the United States and Britain reaffirmed “Germany First” and created the Combined Chiefs of Staff, giving the Grand Alliance a permanent planning institution.[^ch36-arcadia]
- The Battle of Midway of June 4–7, 1942 changed the course of the Pacific war, and the campaigns of 1945 on Iwo Jima and Okinawa produced casualty figures that shaped the debate over invading Japan.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-iwo-okinawa]
- American industry produced nearly two-thirds of all Allied military equipment, including about 297,000 aircraft, 86,000 tanks, and two million army trucks, and more than six million women entered the workforce.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-rosie]
- The Mexican Farm Labor Agreement of August 4, 1942 created the Bracero Program, which eventually brought more than four million Mexican workers to American agriculture and railroads.[^ch36-bracero]
- Executive Order 9066 of February 19, 1942 led to the incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, nearly 70,000 of them citizens, and the 1943 leave-clearance questionnaire segregated some 12,000 respondents at Tule Lake.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-no-no]
- Japanese Americans served in large numbers, and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team became the most decorated unit for its size and length of service, even as the Supreme Court upheld their exclusion in *Korematsu*.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-korematsu]
- Black Americans pressed a “Double V” campaign for victory abroad and equality at home, and the Detroit riot of June 1943 killed 34 people, 25 of them Black.[^ch36-double-v][^ch36-detroit]
- The War Refugee Board, created on January 22, 1944, funded relief and negotiated rescues, but it began after more than five million European Jews had already been murdered.[^ch36-wrb-nwwii][^ch36-wrb-ushmm]
- The Manhattan Project tested the first atomic bomb at Trinity on July 16, 1945; the United States bombed Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9; Japan signed the instrument of surrender aboard the USS *Missouri* on September 2, 1945.[^ch36-trinity][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-surrender]
- Fifty nations signed the United Nations Charter in San Francisco on June 26, 1945, and it entered into force on October 24, 1945.[^ch36-un-charter]

> *A closing note.* Between December 1941 and September 1945 the United States helped win the largest war in history and, in the same years, imprisoned its own citizens, turned away refugees, and used atomic weapons against cities. Coalition warfare expanded American power abroad and sharpened the question of who belonged at home. The chapter’s question — how did coalition warfare remake American power and belonging? — is answered by that double record of victory and exclusion, and by endings that left a world the victors only partly controlled.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-un-charter]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in February 1946, when the American diplomat George F. Kennan sent the “Long Telegram” from Moscow describing the Soviet Union as a relentless and expansionist adversary. Chapter 37 takes up the Cold War and asks how a wartime alliance became a permanent security confrontation. The Grand Alliance that Chapter 36 describes was already fracturing as it ended.[^ch36-check-bridge][^ch36-check-ch37]
