# Chapter 47. The Obama Era (2008–2016)

> “It’s the answer spoken by young and old, rich and poor, Democrat and Republican, black, white, Latino, Asian, Native American, gay, straight, disabled and not disabled — Americans who sent a message to the world that we have never been a collection of red states and blue states; we are, and always will be, the United States of America.”[^ch47-obama-grant-park-2008]
>
> — Barack Obama, election-night remarks, Grant Park, Chicago, November 4, 2008.[^ch47-obama-grant-park-2008]

> “The single most important thing we want to achieve is for President Obama to be a one-term president.”[^ch47-mcconnell-2010]
>
> — Mitch McConnell, interview with *National Journal*, October 23, 2010.[^ch47-mcconnell-2010]

> “Whatever Obama’s successes and failures as president, his election will remain a symbolic watershed in our country’s troubled racial history. The all-but-total absence of other blacks in the highest offices today underscores both Obama’s singular achievement and how far we remain from a post-racial politics.”[^ch47-foner-2011]
>
> — Eric Foner, “Today’s Color Line,” *The Nation*, January 24, 2011.[^ch47-foner-2011]

Three voices, three measurements of the same election. Obama described a country that had never truly been divided into red and blue. McConnell described the same result as the opening of a campaign to remove him. Foner described a symbolic watershed that had not ended the racial hierarchy the election seemed to transcend. They disagree about whether the Obama years were a moment of unity, a period of organized opposition, or a country still living inside its oldest divisions.[^ch47-check-epigraphs]

On the night of November 4, 2008, Barack Obama addressed supporters in Grant Park, in his home city of Chicago. The returns made him the president-elect; in January he would become the forty-fourth president of the United States. He won 365 electoral votes to John McCain’s 173, and 69,498,516 popular votes to McCain’s 59,948,323. Obama took 52.93 percent of the popular vote; McCain took 45.65 percent. Turnout reached 131,313,820 votes, or 58.23 percent of an estimated voting-age population of 225,499,000.[^ch47-fec-2008]

He began with the doubt the campaign had been built to answer. “If there is anyone out there who still doubts that America is a place where all things are possible … tonight is your answer.” He described “lines that stretched around schools and churches in numbers this nation has never seen.” He told the story of Ann Nixon Cooper, 106 years old, born “just a generation past slavery.” And he warned his supporters that the work had only begun: “The road ahead will be long. Our climb will be steep.”[^ch47-obama-grant-park-2008]

The size of the crowd in Grant Park was disputed and method-dependent, and no count is used here. What the record establishes is the date, the returns, and the text of the address. It cannot establish what each person in the park felt, how any one of them had decided, or whether the coalition would hold. On that night no one knew how large the recovery program would be, that the fight over health care would consume a year, that the Tea Party would sweep the 2010 midterms, or that the country would elect a different kind of president in 2016. The scene holds only the moment when a new majority celebrated a decisive victory and spoke of one people. The question it left open is the question of this chapter: why did political renewal and recovery coexist with intensified polarization, and by what distinct steps?[^ch47-check-scene][^ch47-check-scope]

This chapter covers the years from the election of 2008 through the end of the Obama administration on January 20, 2017.[^ch47-check-dates-appD] It asks how a government elected on a promise of recovery and a different kind of politics produced both durable changes and a more divided country. Chapter 46 owns September 11, the war on terror, and the financial collapse of 2008; Chapter 48 owns the 2015–16 campaign and the first Trump presidency. This chapter takes up the election and the coalition, the recovery program and the reform of finance, the health-care law, the wars and agreements of the period, the rights decisions and social movements, and the debate over what the era meant.[^ch47-check-scope][^ch47-check-ch46][^ch47-check-ch48]

The chapter draws on official election returns, presidential addresses and the *Public Papers*, statutes and roll calls, budget and labor data, Census and HHS reports, court opinions, Justice Department investigations, and the secondary literature. It treats “the Obama era,” “the recovery,” “Obamacare,” “the Tea Party,” “Occupy,” “the surge,” “the Arab Spring,” “the pivot,” “targeted killing,” and “post-racial” as contested names. The record over-represents presidents, officials, and journalists and under-represents the uninsured, unauthorized immigrants, the deportees, the drone-strike victims, the imprisoned, and the poor. Where those voices do not survive, the chapter names the silence rather than inventing a witness. Every statistic carries its source, units, denominator, and base year, because “the recovery,” “the middle class,” and “polarization” name clusters of measures that do not agree.[^ch47-check-names][^ch47-check-silences][^ch47-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the 2008 election, the coalition, the 2010 and 2014 midterms, and the electoral map. §2 examines the Recovery Act, the auto rescue, Dodd-Frank, and the Tea Party and Occupy movements. §3 examines health reform, the Affordable Care Act’s design and litigation, and its effect on coverage, with a close reading of Palin’s “death panel” post. §4 examines Afghanistan, the drone program, the Arab Spring, Libya and Benghazi, Syria, Iran, Paris, and the rebalance toward Asia. §5 examines marriage and voting rights, DACA, Ferguson and Black Lives Matter, the failure of gun legislation, and the money and media environment. §6 asks what the era settled and what remains open. The chapter begins in Grant Park, where a new coalition celebrated a decisive victory and spoke of one people.[^ch47-check-scope]

## 1. The election and coalition (2008–2014)

### 1.1 The claim: a decisive victory that did not settle the country

**The claim.** The 2008 election gave Obama a clear popular and electoral majority and assembled a coalition of young, minority, urban, and suburban voters. Within two years that coalition lost the House, and the president’s party suffered one of its largest midterm defeats in decades. The victory and the backlash belong to one political story. They are not a triumph followed by an unrelated reversal.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-pew-2008-coalition][^ch47-fec-2010]

The section runs from the returns and the composition of the electorate through the procedural constraints of the Senate and the 2010 midterm repudiation. It ends with Map 38 and the question of what the changing electoral geography can and cannot show.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-check-map38]

### 1.2 The returns and the coalition

The Federal Election Commission’s compilation records the size of the victory. Obama won 365 electoral votes to McCain’s 173. He won 69,498,516 popular votes, or 52.93 percent, to McCain’s 59,948,323, or 45.65 percent. The total of 131,313,820 votes was equal to 58.23 percent of the Census Bureau’s estimated voting-age population of 225,499,000. That turnout rate is a ratio to an estimated population, not a count of eligible or registered voters.[^ch47-fec-2008]

The coalition was broader than the popular-vote margin alone suggests. Pew Research Center’s analysis of the National Election Pool exit polls found that Obama won 66 percent of voters under 30, 66 percent of Hispanic voters, and 95 percent of Black voters, while taking 43 percent of the White vote. The electorate that year was 39 percent Democratic and 32 percent Republican, a shift from the evenly divided electorate of 2004. Those shares are estimates. Exit polls carry sampling error, their demographic categories changed across cycles, and they describe self-reported voters rather than a census of the country. The chapter uses them as the best contemporaneous portrait of the coalition, not as a substitute for the returns.[^ch47-pew-2008-coalition]

The coalition had a history. Obama’s campaign organized in caucus states and in communities earlier nominees had treated as peripheral, and it turned out young, Black, and Hispanic voters in numbers that changed several states. It also won enough White working-class and suburban voters to hold states that had voted Republican in 2004. The result was a majority assembled from different and partly conflicting parts. Whether those parts could be held together was the governing question.[^ch47-pew-2008-coalition][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Governing against a filibuster

Democrats entered 2009 with unified control of the presidency and both chambers. The House had 257 Democrats and 178 Republicans. The Senate had 57 Democrats, 41 Republicans, and two independents who caucused with the Democrats. Arlen Specter of Pennsylvania switched parties in April 2009, and Al Franken of Minnesota was seated in July after a prolonged recount. The caucus grew, but the Senate’s cloture rule still required 60 votes to end debate on most legislation. A single defection or absence could stop a bill.[^ch47-house-party-divisions][^ch47-senate-filibuster]

The constraint was procedural and political at once. The House could pass legislation with a simple majority. The Senate needed a supermajority for almost everything, and the Democratic caucus included progressives who wanted a larger program and moderates who feared the deficit and the political cost. The result was negotiation, delay, and selective use of budget reconciliation. What the rules prevented must be distinguished from what the leadership chose not to attempt, because “gridlock” describes an outcome, not a cause.[^ch47-senate-filibuster][^ch47-check-sources]

The emergency gave the new administration an argument for speed, not a consensus. The House and Senate had different constituencies and electoral calendars. The majority could pass a stimulus and a health law, but not without cost, and the cost was paid in 2010.[^ch47-senate-filibuster][^ch47-fec-2010]

### 1.4 The 2010 backlash

On November 2, 2010, Republicans gained 63 House seats. The new House had 242 Republicans and 193 Democrats, a swing larger than any since 1948. Republicans gained six Senate seats, leaving Democrats with 51, Republicans with 47, and two independents caucusing with the Democrats. The president called the result a “shellacking.” “I’m not recommending for every future President that they take a shellacking like they — like I did last night,” he said at his news conference the next day. He took responsibility for the pace of the recovery and for the public’s sense of overreach.[^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-house-party-divisions][^ch47-obama-shellacking-2010]

The defeat ended the legislative window for the administration’s domestic program. It did not end the program. The Affordable Care Act remained law, the financial-reform statute remained law, and the administration continued to govern through regulation and executive action. But the House majority now belonged to the opposition, and the Senate’s 60-vote threshold became a weapon as well as a constraint. In 2014 Republicans won control of the Senate, with 54 seats to the Democrats’ 44 and two independents. The House moved to 247 Republicans and 188 Democrats. The second Obama term would be a term of divided government.[^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-fec-2014][^ch47-house-party-divisions]

The swing was not the work of a single movement. It combined the slow recovery, the health-care fight, the Tea Party, and the ordinary tendency of the president’s party to lose seats in a midterm. Each factor had its own limits; none explains the whole result.[^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 Map 38: the changing electoral geography

Map 38 plots the changing electoral geography from 2000 through 2016. It uses official election returns matched to census geography, states its years and geographic units, and normalizes the vote as a share or a percentage-point change. Its purpose is to show that the coalition that elected Obama in 2008 was geographically distinct and did not simply persist. It is not a map of motives. A shift in a county’s margin is a measurement of votes, not evidence that any group changed its mind.[^ch47-check-map38]

The map’s periods are separate. The panel through 2016 belongs to this chapter; the 2020 and 2024 results belong to Chapters 48 and 49 and are not part of the 2000–2016 narrative. The map cannot carry a causal story by itself. It can show where the Democratic vote improved or declined; it cannot show why.[^ch47-check-map38][^ch47-pew-2008-coalition]

## 2. Stimulus, autos, Dodd-Frank, Occupy, and the Tea Party (2009–2011)

### 2.1 The claim: a recovery program that was real, contested, and incomplete

**The claim.** The administration and the 111th Congress responded to the financial emergency with a large fiscal stimulus, a rescue of the automobile industry, and a rewrite of financial regulation. The measures limited the damage. They did not produce a recovery that was as fast, as broad, or as equally shared as their authors promised. The Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street organized opposite complaints about who the government had saved.[^ch47-arra-2009][^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012]

The section runs from the Recovery Act through the automobile rescue and Dodd-Frank to the two movements and the measured recovery. The unemployment series is in the foreground, because the distance between the aggregate economy and household experience is the section’s central problem.[^ch47-crs-arra-2009][^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009]

### 2.2 The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 became Public Law 111-5 on February 17, 2009. Its stated purposes were to make supplemental appropriations “for job preservation and creation, infrastructure investment, energy efficiency and science, assistance to the unemployed, and State and local fiscal stabilization.” The language described an emergency program, not a permanent expansion of government.[^ch47-arra-2009]

The Congressional Research Service describes a conference agreement of roughly $787 billion. About $308 billion took the form of discretionary outlay increases, $267 billion of mandatory outlay increases, and $211 billion of revenue reductions. The House version had been nearly $820 billion and the Senate version roughly $838 billion. The final number was smaller than either, and its composition was a compromise among tax cuts, aid to states, extended unemployment benefits, and spending on infrastructure, energy, and health information technology.[^ch47-crs-arra-2009]

The effect of the act is a debate about a counterfactual. Alan S. Blinder and Mark Zandi estimated that without the government’s response, real gross domestic product in 2011 would have been about $1.8 trillion lower and the unemployment rate about 6.5 percentage points higher. Those are model-based estimates, not observations. The Congressional Budget Office and other modelers produced their own ranges, and the ranges depended on assumptions about how households and firms would have behaved without the act. Critics argued that the stimulus was too small, too slow, and too tilted toward tax cuts and state aid. Supporters argued that it prevented a second Depression. The range and the method, not a single multiplier, are the relevant measure.[^ch47-blinder-zandi-2010][^ch47-cbo-arra-impact][^ch47-krugman-2012][^ch47-check-numbers]

The “jobs saved or created” counts that appeared in the act’s reporting were estimates, not counts of identifiable workers. They were derived from models and from recipients’ reports, and they could not be checked against a world in which the act had not passed. That does not make them false. It makes them conditional. The political argument over the stimulus repeatedly treated a model as a body count.[^ch47-cbo-arra-impact][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 2.3 The rescue of the automobile industry

In 2009 the federal government, through the Treasury and a presidential task force, provided funds to General Motors and Chrysler as they restructured in bankruptcy. The Treasury later reported that it had committed about $79.7 billion to the automakers and their financing arms and recovered about $70.4 billion, a net loss of roughly $9.3 billion. It exited Chrysler in 2011 and reported recovering about 90 percent of its investment in that company.[^ch47-treasury-auto]

Supporters credit the intervention with saving hundreds of thousands of jobs and preserving a domestic industrial base. Critics call it an improper use of public money that favored union workers and some creditors over others. The amounts are documented. The employment claims are estimates, and the accounting depends on how “recovered,” “loss,” and the boundaries of the programs are defined. The definitions matter, and the accounting is contested.[^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-check-numbers]

The government supplied capital, set conditions, and withdrew. The precedent was neither nationalization nor a pure market response; it became a template for later arguments about industrial policy.[^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 2.4 The Dodd-Frank Act

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act became Public Law 111-203 on July 21, 2010. It stated its purposes as promoting financial stability, ending “too big to fail,” protecting taxpayers by ending bailouts, and protecting consumers from abusive practices. It created the Financial Stability Oversight Council, a consumer financial protection bureau, and a resolution regime for large failing firms. It imposed new capital, derivatives, and mortgage rules. Much of the substantive detail was left to rulemaking.[^ch47-dodd-frank-2010]

Supporters argue that the act reduced the risk of another systemic collapse by making the largest firms hold more capital, submit to stress tests, and prepare for resolution. Critics argue that it imposed heavy costs on smaller institutions and codified the advantages of the largest banks. The act did not break up those firms. It subjected them to a new supervisory architecture and tried to make their failure less catastrophic.[^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 2.5 The Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street

The Tea Party took its name and much of its energy from broadcasts and protests in 2009. On February 19, 2009, Rick Santelli, a CNBC commentator on the floor of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, attacked the administration’s mortgage-relief plan and called for a “Chicago Tea Party.” On April 15, tax-day rallies were held in cities across the country. The movement that followed was decentralized, local, and suspicious of Washington. Theda Skocpol and Vanessa Williamson argue that it was largely a reaction by older, middle-class conservatives to the recession, the stimulus, and the health law, and that it reshaped Republican primaries and pulled the party rightward.[^ch47-tea-party-2009][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012]

Occupy Wall Street began on September 17, 2011, when protesters occupied Zuccotti Park in lower Manhattan. The movement spread the language of “the 99 percent” and “the 1 percent.” It deliberately refused a single leader and a single platform, relying on assemblies and consensus. Police cleared the park that November. Its demands were never reduced to a legislative program, but its vocabulary entered the politics of inequality and remained there.[^ch47-occupy-2011]

The two movements rejected the government’s handling of the crisis from opposite directions. The Tea Party objected to bailouts, deficits, and the health law; Occupy objected to bank power, inequality, and the influence of the wealthy. They were not the same “populist moment.” Their objects of anger and their relation to the parties were different, and the difference should not be flattened.[^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]

Crowd sizes at the 2009 rallies and the 2011 occupations are contested estimates. Any figure requires its source; otherwise the events can be described without a precise count. The political importance of the movements does not depend on a crowd estimate.[^ch47-check-numbers][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012]

### 2.6 The measure: a recovery that lagged

The unemployment rate peaked at 10.2 percent in October 2009, the highest since April 1983. It remained near or above 8 percent for years. The economy began to grow again in late 2009, but the labor market recovered slowly and unevenly. The series requires its definitions and seasonal adjustment.[^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009]

Real median household income did not regain its 2007 level during the Obama years. The 2015 median was $56,516, the first annual increase since 2007, and it remained 1.6 percent below the 2007 figure in real terms. The two series measure different things, and together they show that an aggregate recovery and a household recovery are not the same.[^ch47-census-income-2016]

The distribution of wealth was starker. In the Federal Reserve’s 2016 Survey of Consumer Finances, the median White family held $171,000 in net worth, the median Black family $17,600, and the median Hispanic family $20,700. The White–Black gap in median net worth widened from $132,800 in 2013 to $153,500 in 2016. Those figures describe families and self-identified racial groups; they do not explain the causes of the gap. They do show that the recovery’s gains were not shared equally and that the racial wealth gap was not closing.[^ch47-fed-wealth-2017]

## 3. Health reform (2009–2015)

### 3.1 The claim: the largest coverage expansion in decades, carried by one party and fought over for years

**The claim.** The Affordable Care Act expanded health-insurance coverage through subsidies, an individual requirement to hold insurance, insurance-market rules, and a Medicaid expansion. It reduced the number of uninsured Americans substantially. It also remained a partisan fight that moved through Congress, the courts, and the states. The law changed who could get coverage. It did not settle the argument over what government owed the sick and the poor.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]

The section runs from the legislative fight through the design of the law, the In Focus close reading of the “death panel” claim, the litigation, and the coverage results.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-nfib-2012][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]

### 3.2 Making the law

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act became Public Law 111-148 on March 23, 2010. A companion reconciliation act, Public Law 111-152, made further changes on March 30, 2010. The Senate had passed the bill 60–39 on December 24, 2009. The House passed it 219–212 on March 21, 2010, with every Republican and 34 Democrats voting against it. The law passed without a single Republican vote after a year of negotiation, a special Senate election in Massachusetts in January 2010, and the use of budget reconciliation.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-aca-votes]

The design was a set of interlocking parts. The individual requirement was meant to bring healthy people into the pool so that the rules against discrimination based on preexisting conditions would not drive premiums up. The subsidies made coverage affordable above the Medicaid threshold; the exchanges gave individuals and small businesses a regulated market; the Medicaid expansion covered the poorest adults; and the essential-benefit standard defined what a plan had to cover. Each part depended on the others, and each became a separate political fight.[^ch47-aca-2010]

The law was a compromise. The public option that many progressives wanted was dropped, and the individual requirement, which had originated in conservative think tanks, became the price of universal coverage. The result relied on private insurers and regulated competition rather than a single government program. That design helped it pass and left it vulnerable to charges that it had preserved the insurers it was meant to discipline.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 3.3 The In Focus: the “death panel” post

**In Focus.** On August 7, 2009, Sarah Palin, the former Alaska governor and 2008 Republican vice-presidential nominee, posted a note on Facebook. “The America I know and love,” she wrote, “is not one in which my parents or my baby with Down Syndrome will have to stand in front of Obama’s ‘death panel’ so his bureaucrats can decide, based on a subjective judgment of their ‘level of productivity in society,’ whether they are worthy of health care.”[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009]

Section 1233 of H.R. 3200, “Advance Care Planning Consultation,” would have had Medicare pay for voluntary end-of-life counseling. It did not require patients to receive counseling, and it did not create a panel. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the provision would cost a net $2.7 billion over 10 years. FactCheck.org and PolitiFact found that the phrase “death panel” did not appear in the bill and that the provision did not authorize anything like the claim. Palin published a longer follow-up on August 12 arguing that the counseling would be coercive in a cost-cutting bill. The end-of-life provision was dropped from the final law.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-check-focus]

The post is evidence of how a claim about a statutory provision moved through social media. It is not evidence that death panels existed. It shows how anxiety about cost, rationing, and bureaucratic power attached to a modest provision and became a national fact. Palin’s exact words, the provision she cited, and the bill’s text belong side by side.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-check-focus]

### 3.4 The courts

In *National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius*, decided June 28, 2012, the Supreme Court upheld the individual requirement as a valid exercise of Congress’s taxing power. The vote on the mandate was 5–4. A majority also held that the Medicaid expansion exceeded Congress’s spending power as written and was therefore optional for the states. The decision kept the law alive and changed its reach. It let states refuse the expansion, and the states that did so left many of their poorest residents without coverage.[^ch47-nfib-2012]

In *King v. Burwell*, decided June 25, 2015, the Court held that tax credits were available on federally run exchanges as well as state-run ones. The vote was 6–3. The decision preserved subsidies in the states that had declined to establish their own exchanges, where the coverage expansion otherwise depended on the ruling.[^ch47-king-burwell-2015]

The litigation did not overturn the law. It changed the geography of coverage. The Medicaid expansion became a state choice, and the choice followed politics. The rulings changed the map of who was insured, not only the constitutional argument.[^ch47-nfib-2012][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]

### 3.5 The coverage result

Department of Health and Human Services analysis found that the number of uninsured nonelderly Americans fell from about 48 million in 2010 to about 28 million in 2016, before rising again. The uninsured rate reached a historic low in 2016. The estimate comes from the National Health Interview Survey and depends on the survey, the year, and the definition of coverage. The series and its source are the relevant evidence.[^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]

Coverage is not the same as access, affordability, or health. A person with insurance may still face deductibles, narrow networks, and high drug costs; a person without insurance may receive charity or emergency care. The law reduced the number of uninsured. It did not make health care free, and it did not eliminate the gaps in the system.[^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021][^ch47-check-numbers]

## 4. Afghanistan, drones, the Arab Spring, Syria, Iran, Paris, and Asia policy (2009–2016)

### 4.1 The claim: a foreign policy of limited wars, strikes, and agreements

**The claim.** The administration narrowed the war on terror’s stated aims while expanding its instruments. It sent more troops to Afghanistan, expanded the drone and targeted-killing program, and reached a set of diplomatic agreements from the Iran nuclear deal to the Paris climate accord. It promised a “rebalance” toward Asia. The record combines genuine achievements, unresolved wars, and costs that fell on people far from Washington.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015][^ch47-crs-asia-rebalance-2012]

The section runs from Afghanistan and the 2009 troop increase through the drone program, the Arab Spring and Libya, the Benghazi attack, the Syrian chemical-weapons framework, the Iran and Paris agreements, and the Asia rebalance.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-awlaki-2011][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015]

### 4.2 Afghanistan and the 2009 troop increase

In his December 1, 2009, address at West Point, Obama said he had “determined that it is in our vital national interest to send an additional 30,000 U.S. troops to Afghanistan.” He announced that “after 18 months, our troops will begin to come home,” and he set the goal to “disrupt, dismantle, and defeat al Qaeda in Afghanistan and Pakistan.” He described Afghanistan as a country that had “moved backwards,” cited corruption, the drug trade, and a resurgent Taliban, and tied the effort to Pakistan.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009]

The speech was the president’s case, not a neutral account. It presented the troop increase, which became known as the surge, as a limited commitment with a deadline and argued that the alternative was a permanent occupation or a precipitous withdrawal. The surge did not defeat the Taliban. Violence fell in some areas and rose in others; the Afghan government remained weak; the drawdown began in July 2011, and the combat mission ended in 2014. The war continued beyond the Obama administration. Whether a different strategy could have succeeded is not settled.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Drones and targeted killing

The administration expanded the use of armed drones and “targeted killing” outside declared battlefields. On September 30, 2011, a drone strike in Yemen killed Anwar al-Awlaki, an American-born cleric who had become a leader of al Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula. The same strike killed Samir Khan, another American, and two others. Al-Awlaki was the first United States citizen knowingly targeted and killed by a drone strike.[^ch47-awlaki-2011]

Supporters argued that the strikes were lawful self-defense against an enemy that could not be reached by other means, that they were precise, and that they disrupted plots. Critics argued that they bypassed due process and the courts, killed bystanders, and set a precedent for killing citizens without trial. Whether a particular strike was lawful is not settled by the fact that it killed a person the government had named as a target.[^ch47-awlaki-2011][^ch47-check-debate-works]

The record of a drone strike includes the target and the official rationale. It often does not include the names, ages, or number of the dead. That asymmetry is part of the program’s history. Independent estimates of civilian casualties use different methods and reach different totals. The asymmetry belongs in the record, and the estimates must be used with their methods; the absence of a count is not evidence that no one else died.[^ch47-awlaki-2011][^ch47-check-silences][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 4.4 The Arab Spring, Libya, and Benghazi

In 2011 mass protests toppled longstanding governments across the Middle East and North Africa. In Tunisia, President Zine al-Abidine Ben Ali fled on January 14. In Egypt, President Hosni Mubarak resigned on February 11. The uprisings were not one event. They had different demands, militaries, outcomes, and American involvements, and “the Arab Spring” is not the name of a single movement.[^ch47-arab-spring-2011]

In Libya, the United States joined a NATO-led intervention under United Nations Security Council Resolution 1973, adopted on March 17, 2011. The resolution authorized member states to take “all necessary measures” to protect civilians. NATO’s Operation Unified Protector enforced an arms embargo, maintained a no-fly zone, and protected civilian areas from March 31 to October 31, 2011. The campaign helped remove Muammar Qaddafi, who was killed on October 20. No NATO troops fought on the ground. Supporters credit the intervention with preventing a massacre; it also left a fractured country with rival militias and weak institutions. Both parts belong in the record.[^ch47-unsc-1973][^ch47-nato-libya-2011]

On September 11–12, 2012, attackers killed four Americans at the United States facilities in Benghazi, Libya: Ambassador J. Christopher Stevens, Sean Smith, Glen Doherty, and Tyrone Woods. An independent Accountability Review Board examined the attack and issued 29 recommendations on December 19, 2012. It found “systemic failures and leadership and management deficiencies” that left the mission’s security inadequate. The attack became the subject of prolonged congressional inquiry.[^ch47-clinton-benghazi-2012][^ch47-benghazi-arb][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 Syria and the chemical-weapons framework

After a chemical-weapons attack in Syria in August 2013, the United States and Russia announced a framework on September 14, 2013, for the elimination of Syria’s chemical weapons under the Chemical Weapons Convention. Syria joined the convention, and the Organization for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons began verifying the destruction of the declared stockpile. The framework avoided a military strike that the administration had seemed to threaten after its “red line” warning.[^ch47-syria-framework-2013]

The episode tested the administration’s credibility. It had warned that the use of chemical weapons would cross a line, and then it accepted a diplomatic solution instead of a strike. Supporters argued that the framework removed much of Syria’s chemical arsenal without a war. Critics argued that the threat had been exposed as hollow and that the regime retained chemical weapons. The framework did not end the civil war or the use of chemical weapons.[^ch47-syria-framework-2013][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.6 The Iran nuclear agreement

On July 14, 2015, the P5+1, the European Union, and Iran reached the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action. The agreement was intended to ensure that Iran’s nuclear program would be exclusively peaceful. Adoption Day was October 18, 2015. Implementation Day was January 16, 2016, when the International Atomic Energy Agency verified Iran’s implementation and nuclear-related sanctions were lifted.[^ch47-jcpoa-2015]

Supporters argued that the agreement verifiably limited Iran’s program without a war. Opponents argued that it granted relief while leaving Iran a path to a weapon after the agreement’s limits expired. Both positions belong in the record, along with the verification reports. The agreement did not address Iran’s regional role, and its later status, including the American withdrawal in 2018, belongs to Chapter 48.[^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works][^ch47-check-ch48]

### 4.7 Paris and the climate framework

The Paris Agreement was adopted by 195 Parties at the United Nations Climate Change Conference in Paris on December 12, 2015. It entered into force on November 4, 2016. Its goal is to hold the global average temperature increase “to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels” and to pursue efforts “to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels.”[^ch47-paris-2015]

The agreement rested on voluntary national pledges rather than binding emissions targets. The United States participated through executive action rather than a ratified treaty. Those features were weaknesses and conditions of agreement at once: they allowed a broad coalition to sign, and they left the accord dependent on the politics of its members. The mechanism and the limits are the relevant history; the agreement did not solve the problem it addressed.[^ch47-paris-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.8 The rebalance toward Asia

In the fall of 2011 and early 2012, the administration announced that it would intensify its role in the Asia-Pacific region across diplomacy, economics, security, human rights, and the environment. The policy was called the “rebalance” or the “pivot.” It was a statement of priority, not a guarantee of resources. The announced measures must be distinguished from claims about their effects.[^ch47-crs-asia-rebalance-2012]

“Pivot” and “rebalance” are contested labels. Supporters argued that the United States had been overinvested in the Middle East and needed to prepare for the century’s center of gravity. Critics argued that the policy was mostly rhetoric and did not survive the crises that followed. The announced measures and the resources attached to them are the relevant record, and it is thin in places.[^ch47-crs-asia-rebalance-2012][^ch47-check-names]

## 5. Marriage, immigration, race, protest, and media (2010–2016)

### 5.1 The claim: rights expanded while the country polarized

**The claim.** In the administration’s second term, the Supreme Court extended marriage equality nationwide and struck down part of the Voting Rights Act. The president created a deferred-action program for unauthorized immigrants brought to the United States as children. The killing of Black Americans by police and vigilantes produced the Black Lives Matter movement. Congress failed to pass gun legislation after a school massacre, and money and social media reshaped the public sphere. Rights expanded while the country polarized. The two developments were not opposites; they were produced by the same conflicts.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-shelby-2013][^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

The section runs from marriage and voting rights through immigration, Ferguson and Black Lives Matter, the gun debate, and the media and money environment. It ends on the distribution of the recovery and the background to 2016.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015][^ch47-citizens-united-2010]

### 5.2 Marriage equality

In *Obergefell v. Hodges*, decided June 26, 2015, the Supreme Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires a state to license a marriage between two people of the same sex and to recognize a marriage between two people of the same sex when their marriage was lawfully licensed and performed out of state. “They ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law,” Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote for the majority. “The Constitution grants them that right.” The vote was 5–4.[^ch47-obergefell-2015]

The decision followed the Court’s 2013 ruling in *United States v. Windsor*, a shift in public opinion, and years of state-level organizing. It did not come from the Court alone. The plaintiffs, the lawyers, the voters, and the movement that had pressed the issue made the case possible. The dissent argued that the Court had short-circuited a democratic debate that was still moving.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 Immigration and DACA

On June 15, 2012, Secretary of Homeland Security Janet Napolitano issued a memorandum setting out how the department would exercise prosecutorial discretion toward certain young people brought to the United States as children. The program became known as Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals. The memorandum listed criteria: arrival before age 16, continuous residence for at least five years, being in school or having graduated or served honorably in the military, and not having been convicted of specified offenses.[^ch47-daca-2012]

DACA granted deferred action and work authorization. It did not grant legal status or citizenship. It was a use of executive discretion, not a statute, and its legal footing was contested. The program protected young people from deportation and allowed them to work. It did not give them a path to citizenship, and it did not substitute for the comprehensive immigration reform that Congress did not pass. The program’s protections were not the comprehensive reform it was meant to anticipate.[^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 5.4 Ferguson, Black Lives Matter, and racialized reaction

On August 9, 2014, Michael Brown, an 18-year-old Black man, was shot and killed by Ferguson, Missouri, police officer Darren Wilson. The killing set off protests in Ferguson and around the country. A Department of Justice criminal investigation concluded in March 2015 that the evidence did not support federal criminal charges against Wilson. A separate civil-rights investigation found “a pattern or practice of unlawful conduct within the Ferguson Police Department” that violated the First, Fourth, and Fourteenth Amendments. The department’s practices, the report found, were shaped by the city’s “focus on revenue rather than by public safety needs.”[^ch47-doj-brown-2015][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

The two findings are not contradictory. One asked whether a federal criminal case could be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The other asked whether the police department had a pattern of unconstitutional conduct.[^ch47-doj-brown-2015][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

Three Black organizers, Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi, created the #BlackLivesMatter project in 2013 in response to the acquittal of George Zimmerman in the killing of Trayvon Martin. The movement grew after Ferguson and spread nationally. Its account of policing, mass incarceration, and structural racism became a language for a generation of activists; its critics argued that it overstated the role of race and understated the role of crime and policing. The movement’s own account and the strongest criticism belong together.[^ch47-blm-about][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

Michael Tesler argues that the Obama years saw White political attitudes become more polarized by racial considerations, not less. His book, *Post-Racial or Most-Racial?*, uses survey evidence to show that racial attitudes became more closely tied to evaluations of politicians, policies, and parties. Other accounts stress economic anxiety, party identity, and media. The strongest version of the racialization thesis is that racial attitudes became a more powerful predictor of political choice, not that every voter was racist. Survey trends can show that attitudes and preferences moved together; they cannot prove what any individual was thinking.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-taylor-2016][^ch47-check-debate-works][^ch47-check-silences]

Foner’s 2011 warning belongs here. The election of a Black president was a watershed, not the end of the racial hierarchy. The gap between the symbolism of 2008 and the policing of 2014 is one of the chapter’s central measures of the era.[^ch47-foner-2011][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

### 5.5 Sandy Hook and the limits of legislation

On December 14, 2012, a gunman killed 20 children and six adults at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut. The state’s investigation documented the attack and the shooter’s preparation. The killings produced a national demand for gun legislation, and President Obama made a personal appeal for action.[^ch47-sandy-hook-2012]

On April 17, 2013, the Senate voted on the Manchin–Toomey amendment to expand background checks. It received 54 votes in favor and 46 against. It needed 60 to overcome the Senate’s threshold, and it failed. The vote was the clearest test of whether a mass shooting could change gun policy. It could not. A handful of votes would have changed the outcome; it was not inevitable.[^ch47-senate-gun-vote-2013][^ch47-sandy-hook-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 5.6 Money, media, and the public sphere

In *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, decided January 21, 2010, the Supreme Court held that limiting independent political expenditures by corporations, unions, and other collective entities violates the First Amendment. The decision did not address direct contributions to candidates. It opened a channel for independent spending. How much that channel mattered became one of the era’s arguments about money in politics, and the amounts depend on how independent expenditures are counted.[^ch47-citizens-united-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]

Social media had helped organize the Obama campaign and the movements of the period. It also accelerated rumor and partisan information. The “death panel” post is one instance. The same platforms that allowed a movement to assemble without a headquarters allowed a falsehood to circulate without a fact-check.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-citizens-united-2010]

### 5.7 The measure: recovery, inequality, and the 2016 background

The recovery was unevenly shared. Unemployment remained high for years; real median household income did not regain its 2007 level; the uninsured fell but remained tens of millions; and the racial wealth gap widened. Those series do not tell one story. They tell several stories about who recovered and who did not. Their definitions differ, and the differences should stand.[^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021][^ch47-fed-wealth-2017]

The slow recovery, stagnant wages, and regional divergence are part of the policy background that Chapter 48 takes up as the 2016 campaign. This chapter names them and does not narrate the campaign. The connection is not that the economy “caused” the 2016 result. It is that the recovery set the conditions in which the campaign was fought, and that different voters experienced those conditions differently.[^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-check-ch48]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The election, the statutes, the decisions, and the agreements are documented. Obama won 365 electoral votes and 52.93 percent of the popular vote. Republicans gained 63 House seats in 2010 and won control of the Senate in 2014. The Recovery Act became law on February 17, 2009; the Affordable Care Act on March 23, 2010; and Dodd-Frank on July 21, 2010.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-fec-2014][^ch47-house-party-divisions][^ch47-arra-2009][^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010]

The courts upheld the individual mandate and the exchange subsidies. They required states to license same-sex marriages and struck down the Voting Rights Act coverage formula. The troop increase, the Iran and Paris agreements, DACA, the Ferguson findings, and the rise of Black Lives Matter have documented dates. The uninsured fell from about 48 million in 2010 to about 28 million in 2016, and real median household income did not regain its 2007 level.[^ch47-nfib-2012][^ch47-king-burwell-2015][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-shelby-2013][^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015][^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015][^ch47-blm-about][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021][^ch47-census-income-2016]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the recovery program proportionate and effective? One account holds that the stimulus, the auto rescue, and the financial reform prevented a second Depression. Another holds that the response was too small, too slow, and too kind to banks. The counterfactual cannot be observed, and the disagreement is partly about which model and which distributional question matter most.[^ch47-blinder-zandi-2010][^ch47-krugman-2012][^ch47-census-income-2016]

How much was structural constraint and how much was choice? The Senate’s supermajority rule, the opposition, and the divided party limited what was possible. The administration also chose its priorities and compromises. The distinction changes who is responsible for the result.[^ch47-senate-filibuster][^ch47-check-debate-works]

Was the era defined by racial reaction? Tesler argues that racial attitudes became more central to White politics; others stress economic anxiety, party identity, and media. The strongest version of the thesis is not that every voter was motivated by race. It is that racial attitudes became a more powerful predictor of political choice.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]

Did movements from below or decisions from above drive the rights gains? *Obergefell* was a court decision and the product of a movement. DACA was an executive action and a response to years of organizing by young immigrants. Both belong in the account.[^ch47-taylor-2016][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-daca-2012]

Was “post-racial” a promise, a hope, or a myth? The election of 2008 did not end racial inequality. It did show that a Black candidate could assemble a national majority. Both facts belong in the answer.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-pew-2008-coalition][^ch47-foner-2011]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The Obama years produced real and durable changes. A coverage expansion, a financial-reform statute, rights decisions, and international agreements changed the country and the world. Their reach and cost were contested. Recovery and rights gains coexisted with organized opposition, racialized reaction, and an unevenly shared economy. The chapter’s question is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-census-income-2016]

The contingencies were specific. The stimulus’s size, the health law’s design, the 2010 losses, the failure of the 2013 gun bill, and the Iran and Paris agreements were choices. A larger stimulus, a public option, a different sequencing of health reform, a Senate that did not filibuster, an Iran deal that was not reached: each is a place where the outcome could have been different. The financial emergency, the Senate’s rules, the economy’s distribution, and the media environment set the conditions; the choices made within them decided the form. The coexistence of renewal and polarization is the era’s defining problem.[^ch47-crs-arra-2009][^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]

## Legacies

The recovery program and the health law left institutions and expectations that outlasted the administration that built them. The Affordable Care Act’s coverage expansion, the consumer-protection and resolution machinery of Dodd-Frank, and the precedent of a large countercyclical fiscal response became durable features of American government. Later administrations would defend, revise, or attack them. The question of how much government should do in an emergency remained central to American politics.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-arra-2009]

The rights decisions and the movements that pressed them changed the country and its conflicts. Marriage equality, the debate over the Voting Rights Act after *Shelby County*, the deferred-action program, and the rise of Black Lives Matter fixed terms that later politics took up. The conflicts they opened did not end when the administration did.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-shelby-2013][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015][^ch47-daca-2012]

The era’s polarization became the ground on which the next election was fought. The uneven recovery, the organized opposition, the racialized reaction, and the changed media environment set terms that Chapter 48 takes up as it examines the 2016 campaign and the first Trump presidency. The Obama years did not cause everything that followed. They created the conditions in which the next political turn was possible.[^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-ch48]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 2008 and 2017 the United States elected its first Black president and assembled a new coalition, passed a major recovery program and a major health-coverage expansion, rescued its automobile industry and rewrote financial regulation, fought an unfinished war and a covert strike campaign, reached agreements on Iran’s nuclear program and the global climate, extended marriage equality, and saw a movement rise against police violence. All of that happened while the recovery was unevenly shared and the political system grew more polarized. The chapter’s question — why did political renewal and recovery coexist with intensified polarization, and by what distinct steps? — is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-census-income-2016]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on June 16, 2015, when Donald J. Trump descended an escalator at Trump Tower in New York to announce his candidacy for president. Chapter 48 takes up the campaign, the 2016 election, and the first Trump presidency. This chapter ends with the unfinished recovery, the rights gains, and the polarization of the Obama years; the next turns to the politics that grew from them.[^ch47-check-ch48][^ch47-census-income-2016]
