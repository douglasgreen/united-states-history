## Epigraphs

> “To be able to borrow upon good terms, it is essential that the credit of a nation should be well established.”[^ch13-hamilton-report]

— Alexander Hamilton, *Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit*, January 9, 1790.[^ch13-hamilton-report]

> “Let me now take a more comprehensive view, and warn you in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally.”[^ch13-avalon-farewell]

— George Washington, Farewell Address, September 19, 1796.[^ch13-avalon-farewell]

> “We are all republicans. We are all federalists.”[^ch13-loc-peaceful]

— Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1801.[^ch13-loc-peaceful]

> “Except for the era of the Civil War, the last several years of the eighteenth century were the most politically contentious in United States history.”[^ch13-cfr-wood]

— Gordon S. Wood, *Empire of Liberty: A History of the Early Republic, 1789–1815* (2009), as quoted by Gideon Rose.[^ch13-cfr-wood]

- **Why these four.** A treasury secretary insisted that a nation’s ability to borrow rested on its credit. A president leaving office warned in the most solemn terms against the spirit of party. His successor declared that the parties were brethren of the same principle. A modern historian answers that, apart from the Civil War, these were the most contentious years in the nation’s history. The four disagree about whether the decade’s conflicts were a fiscal necessity, a danger to the republic, a temporary misunderstanding, or its deepest early crisis.[^ch13-check-epigraphs]
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Hamilton’s sentence is quoted from the Founders Online transcription of the report; collate it against the printed *Papers of Alexander Hamilton*. Washington’s sentence is quoted from the Avalon Project’s text of the Farewell Address; collate it against a scholarly edition and confirm the September 19, 1796, publication date in the *American Daily Advertiser*. Jefferson’s sentences are quoted from the Library of Congress exhibition’s excerpt of the First Inaugural; collate them against the manuscript or the *National Intelligencer* printing. Wood’s sentence is quoted as Gideon Rose reproduces it from *Empire of Liberty*; locate it in the printed book before setting it in type.[^ch13-check-epigraphs][^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-loc-peaceful][^ch13-cfr-wood]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the fiscal program caused the party system, that Washington opposed all organized politics, that Jefferson’s conciliation ended the conflict, or that the decade was uniquely violent. They stage the chapter’s questions: how did a written frame become a working and partisan government, and whose power did it secure?

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The journey and the setting.** On April 16, 1789, two days after official notification of his election, George Washington left Mount Vernon for New York, traveling through Alexandria, Baltimore, Wilmington, Philadelphia, Trenton, Princeton, New Brunswick, and Bridgetown, and crossing to New York City by barge on April 23. Congress met in New York’s former City Hall, rechristened Federal Hall and under renovation by Pierre L’Enfant.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]
- **The day.** Congress’s joint committee set the ceremony for Thursday, April 30, 1789, and moved the oath to the outer gallery adjoining the Senate Chamber “to the end that the Oath of Office may be administered to the President in the most public manner, and that the greatest number of people of the United States, and without distinction, may witness the solemnity.” At half past noon Washington rode alone in the state coach from Franklin Square to Federal Hall; city troops, members of Congress, and department heads preceded him, and foreign ministers and citizens followed.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]
- **The oath.** After being received by Congress, Washington stepped onto the balcony, followed by the senators and representatives. Chancellor Robert Livingston of New York administered the constitutional oath: “I do solemnly swear that I will faithfully execute the office of President of the United States, and will, to the best of my ability, preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution of the United States.” Washington repeated it, then kissed the Bible; Livingston called out, “Long live George Washington, President of the United States,” and a salvo of 13 cannons fired.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]
- **The Bible.** The book was borrowed at the last moment from St. John’s Lodge No. 1, Ancient York Masons, after it was discovered that none had been provided; it was a King James Version printed in London in 1767, opened at random to Genesis 49:13. It was held on a crimson cushion by Samuel Otis, secretary of the Senate.[^ch13-nps-inaugural-bible][^ch13-wiki-inaugural-bible]
- **The disputed addition.** The most familiar account holds that Washington added “So help me God” and kissed the Bible. The only contemporaneous account of the oath, from the French consul the Comte de Moustier, reports the constitutional oath with no mention of the phrase; the earliest known source attributing the addition to Washington is Washington Irving, who was six years old at the time, and it first appears about 60 years after the event. The scene must leave the addition uncertain.[^ch13-wiki-inaugural-bible][^ch13-check-oath]
- **Afterward.** Except for the oath, the law required no further ceremony, but Washington read his inaugural address in the Senate Chamber, then proceeded with Congress to St. Paul’s Church for divine service; fireworks ended the day. The address ran about ten minutes and was well received; in it Washington declined any salary beyond actual expenses.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-mountvernon-inaugural]
- **The weather.** The Joint Congressional Committee on Inaugural Ceremonies records the weather as “clear and cool.” That is the only weather detail the scene should use.[^ch13-inaugural-senate]

### The scene’s argument

- **A frame becomes a government.** The scene should open with the city filling and the balcony being prepared, and let the reader watch a written plan become an office held by a person. The point is not that the ceremony was sacred; it is that the government the Constitution described had no precedents and had to be invented in public.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]
- **The crowd and the question of who is included.** The committee wanted the oath administered “without distinction,” so the greatest number could witness it. That phrase sits beside the exclusions the new government would defend: the enslaved, Native nations, and women took no part in choosing it.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-check-names]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: How did constitutional machinery become a functioning and partisan government?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that Washington’s personal prestige made the government work. The machinery was contested from its first session: a fiscal program, a frontier war, an Atlantic revolution, and an organized opposition each tested what the government could do and whom it would serve.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-state-haiti]
- **The widening.** The scene sits on the balcony in April 1789. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader through the First Congress and the decade of conflict to the transfer of power in 1801.[^ch13-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- No settled account of the oath additions. “So help me God” and the kiss are reported, but the phrase is not in the contemporaneous account; the scene may describe the kiss and state the dispute, but must not assert the addition as fact.[^ch13-wiki-inaugural-bible][^ch13-check-oath]
- No invented crowd dialogue, weather, or interior thought. The constitutional oath, the kiss, Livingston’s exclamation, the cannon salute, and the “clear and cool” weather are documented; the crowd’s feelings are not.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-inaugural-senate][^ch13-check-scene]
- No claim that Washington chose the Bible passage deliberately or that the ceremony was planned in every detail; the Bible arrived at the last moment and was opened at random.[^ch13-nps-inaugural-bible][^ch13-check-scene]
- No claim that the crowd represented the people of the United States as a whole; the scene should name those the new government excluded.[^ch13-check-names]

## 1. Government begins

### 1.1 The First Congress and the machinery of government

- **The claim.** The First Congress did not merely legislate; it built the departments and courts through which the government would act, inventing the machinery the Constitution described only in outline.
- **The setting.** The First Congress met at Federal Hall in New York, the temporary capital, with a quorum in the House on April 1, 1789, and in the Senate on April 6; it counted the electoral votes and certified Washington’s election before his inauguration.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]
- **The departments.** Congress created the Departments of State, Treasury, and War, and the office of Attorney General, and the president assembled what became the cabinet. The new government also carried forward “heads of executive departments of the government under the Confederation,” whose functions it absorbed.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-check-judiciary]
- **The courts.** The Judiciary Act of 1789 established the federal court system, including the Supreme Court and the district and circuit courts, and left the state courts in place. The act is one of the chapter’s clearest examples of the First Congress turning constitutional language into institutions.[^ch13-check-judiciary]
- **The Bill of Rights.** The same Congress took up Madison’s amendments and sent twelve to the states; ten were ratified by December 15, 1791. Chapter 12 owns that story, so this chapter cross-refers to it rather than repeating it.[^ch13-check-ch12]
- **The consequence.** By the end of its first session, the government had departments, courts, revenue, and a promised bill of rights, but no settled answer to how powerful it would be. The fiscal program in §2 would test that question first.[^ch13-check-judiciary][^ch13-hamilton-report]

### 1.2 Washington’s cabinet and the invention of the presidency

- **The claim.** The presidency had no precedents, and Washington’s choices—about advice, ceremony, and retirement—shaped the office as much as the Constitution did.
- **The office.** Washington took the oath prescribed by Article II and then delivered an address that the law did not require, setting a precedent for the inaugural address. In it he declined any salary beyond actual expenditures, and he asked Congress to consider amendments under Article V.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-mountvernon-inaugural]
- **The cabinet.** Washington gathered the heads of the departments as advisers, and the cabinet became the arena where Hamilton and Jefferson fought over policy. The cabinet was not described in the Constitution; it was an invention of practice.[^ch13-state-genet]
- **The circle.** Washington’s household and reception practices, and the way the first president balanced republican simplicity against the dignity of the office, became models for later administrations. The chapter should present these as contested choices, not as inevitable forms.[^ch13-check-judiciary]
- **The consequence.** Because the office’s powers were ambiguous, the disputes of the 1790s were partly disputes about the presidency itself—who could advise it, how it could act, and how it should pass power on. That question returns in §6.[^ch13-state-genet]

### 1.3 Revenue, the census, and the reach of the federal government

- **The claim.** The federal government’s first business was money, and the revenue acts and the first census made its reach concrete.
- **The revenue.** Congress passed a tariff act in 1789 imposing duties on imports and tonnage, the primary source of federal revenue, which would fund the government and service the debt. Hamilton’s later reports built on this foundation.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-gwu-funding]
- **The census.** The first census, taken in 1790 under the Constitution’s three-fifths rule, counted the population for representation and taxation and produced the apportionment of House seats. It made the enslaved population visible to the federal government as a fraction for power while denying it any voice.[^ch13-check-slavery][^ch13-check-ch12]
- **The reach.** Customs collection, the revenue cutters, and the census created a federal administration that touched ports, households, and the frontier. The chapter should show the state as a working apparatus, not only a set of ideas.[^ch13-gwu-funding]
- **The consequence.** Revenue made the fiscal program possible and made the excise crisis of §3 inevitable: a government that taxed whiskey would have to enforce the tax. That link is the chapter’s spine.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-gwu-funding]

## 2. Hamilton’s system

### 2.1 The Report on Public Credit and funding at par

- **The claim.** Hamilton’s first great report proposed to fund the national debt at face value and to assume the states’ debts, converting the war’s IOUs into a permanent national obligation.
- **The problem.** The Confederation had amassed a large war debt but lacked the power to tax; the Constitution gave Congress the power to levy duties and taxes, and the first tariff act supplied revenue. Hamilton argued that sound public credit was a condition of borrowing, prosperity, and national survival.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-wiki-public-credit]
- **The report.** Delivered January 9, 1790, the roughly 40,000-word report called for “redemption”—full payment at face value to the current holders of government securities—and for federal assumption of state debts. It counted about $40 million in domestic debt, $12 million in foreign debt, and about $25 million in state debts, a combined total near $77 million.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-hamilton-edu-assumption]
- **The argument.** Hamilton held that any compromise on the sanctity of contracts would destroy confidence, and that concentrating capital would encourage investment and economic growth. He rejected “repudiation” and Madison’s “discrimination,” which would have paid original holders and current purchasers separately.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-gwu-funding]
- **The consequence.** Funding at par rewarded speculators who had bought depreciated certificates and angered those who had sold them cheaply. That grievance became the first organized opposition to the government and the first theme of party conflict.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-gwu-funding]

### 2.2 Assumption and the Compromise of 1790

- **The claim.** Assumption stalled on sectional interest and was passed only through the bargain that moved the permanent capital to the Potomac.
- **The deadlock.** Southern states, especially Virginia, had largely paid down their war debts and resisted assuming the debts of others; Madison led the opposition, demanding a settlement of accounts first. By June 1790 the question had brought Congress to a standstill.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit]
- **The bargain.** Jefferson, newly returned as secretary of state, brokered a private agreement in which Madison withdrew his opposition to assumption and Hamilton supported locating the permanent capital on the Potomac. The Residence Bill passed the House on July 9, 1790, and the Assumption Bill on July 26.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790]
- **What it did.** Assumption consolidated the states’ debts into a national debt and tied creditors to the new government’s success. It also gave the federal government a direct stake in the states’ fiscal affairs, which opponents called “consolidation.”[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790]
- **The consequence.** The compromise linked the capital’s location to the fiscal program and made the sectional character of the new politics visible. It also deepened the division between Hamilton’s allies and the Virginia opposition.[^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790][^ch13-state-jay]

### 2.3 The bank and the constitutional argument

- **The claim.** The bill for a national bank forced the first great constitutional argument over implied powers, and Washington’s decision to sign it set a lasting precedent.
- **The bank.** Hamilton proposed a Bank of the United States, modeled partly on the Bank of England, with branches, a uniform currency, and a place to deposit and borrow federal money. Congress passed the bill; the House vote was 39 to 20, and Washington signed it on February 25, 1791.[^ch13-house-first-bank]
- **Jefferson’s objection.** Jefferson argued that the incorporation of a bank was not among the enumerated powers, that the “necessary and proper” clause allowed only means without which an enumerated power would be nugatory, and that to take a single step beyond the boundaries drawn around Congress’s powers would be to claim “a boundless field of power, no longer susceptible of any definition.”[^ch13-jefferson-bank-opinion]
- **Hamilton’s answer.** Hamilton argued that every power vested in a government is sovereign and includes the right to employ all means requisite and fairly applicable to its ends, unless precluded by the Constitution. Washington accepted this reading and signed the bill.[^ch13-hamilton-bank-opinion][^ch13-house-first-bank]
- **The consequence.** The bank controversy gave the opposition a constitutional vocabulary and gave the government a working financial institution. It is the clearest early example of the “implied powers” doctrine that would shape American governance.[^ch13-hamilton-bank-opinion][^ch13-jefferson-bank-opinion]

### 2.4 Manufactures, the excise, and the emerging opposition

- **The claim.** Hamilton’s program for manufactures and his excise taxes completed a design for a commercial, fiscal-military state, and the opposition to that design became a party.
- **Manufactures.** In December 1791 Hamilton submitted his *Report on the Subject of Manufactures*, urging protective tariffs, bounties, and internal improvements to break Britain’s manufacturing hold. Jefferson and his allies feared that manufacturing would create dependent wage workers and undermine the agrarian independence they saw as the basis of republican citizenship.[^ch13-gilder-manufactures]
- **The excise.** Hamilton proposed excise taxes, including the tax on distilled spirits that Congress enacted in 1791, to service the assumed debt. The whiskey excise fell hardest on western farmers who distilled their grain for market.[^ch13-wiki-whiskey][^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey]
- **The figure.** Figure 2 plots funded debt against customs revenue from 1790 to 1801, in nominal dollars, distinguishing the debt stock from annual receipts. It should show that customs duties supplied most federal revenue while the funded debt remained large; as context, one estimate has the debt falling from about 30 percent of gross domestic product in 1790 to about 6 percent by 1811. The last fiscal observation may be 1801, not an unexplained later year.[^ch13-check-figure][^ch13-treasury-debt][^ch13-hsus-revenue][^ch13-cato-debt]
- **The opposition.** By 1791 an organized opposition had formed around the memory of the Revolution, suspicion of “consolidation,” and the conviction that the fiscal program enriched a monied interest at the expense of farmers and veterans. That opposition, not a preexisting party, is the subject of §4.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-check-sources]

## 3. The Northwest war and Whiskey Rebellion

### 3.1 The Ohio Country and the war of the confederacy

- **The claim.** The new government inherited a war in the Ohio Country, where a confederation of Native nations fought to hold a boundary the Treaty of Paris had ignored.
- **The treaty problem.** The 1783 Treaty of Paris ended the war with Britain but allowed the British to remain in the Northwest posts until the United States resolved a land issue with Native nations who had been British allies. The United States treated the treaty as transferring sovereignty; the nations did not.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]
- **The confederacy.** The Chippewa, Ottawa, Potawatomi, Shawnee, Delaware, Miami, and Wyandot formed a federation to halt American incursions, and they rejected unilateral land cessions. Their position rested on the Ohio River as a boundary, not on the purchase of particular tracts.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-myaamia-greenville]
- **The first campaign.** In 1790 a federal expedition under General Josiah Harmar marched into the Miami villages and was roughly handled; the confederacy under Mihšihkinaahkwa (Little Turtle) and Blue Jacket demonstrated that it could fight the new army to a standstill.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-myaamia-stclair]
- **The consequence.** The war made the federal government’s military weakness visible and gave the confederacy leverage. The next campaign would be larger and would fail worse.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]

### 3.2 St. Clair’s defeat and Wayne’s Legion

- **The claim.** In November 1791 the confederacy destroyed the largest federal army sent west, the worst defeat the United States Army would suffer at Native hands.
- **The expedition.** General Arthur St. Clair, governor of the Northwest Territory, moved from Fort Washington near Cincinnati with about 1,400 men to build Fort Jefferson and then advance against the confederacy.[^ch13-myaamia-stclair]
- **The battle.** On the morning of November 4, 1791, a coalition under Little Turtle of the Miami, Blue Jacket of the Shawnee, and Buckongahelas of the Delaware attacked near the Wabash River and drove the Americans from the field. St. Clair’s force suffered 647 killed and 271 wounded, with camp followers among the dead; the accounts of the total differ and must be given as ranges.[^ch13-myaamia-stclair][^ch13-check-numbers]
- **The response.** Washington placed General Anthony Wayne in command of the Legion of the United States and rebuilt the army through disciplined training and a chain of forts. Wayne advanced slowly, avoiding the ambush that had destroyed St. Clair.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]
- **The consequence.** St. Clair’s defeat forced the government to take the western war seriously and made military capacity a federal project. It also gave the confederacy its greatest victory and its greatest dilemma.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-check-sources]

### 3.3 Fallen Timbers and the Treaty of Greenville

- **The claim.** Wayne’s victory at Fallen Timbers in 1794 broke the confederacy’s military position and produced the Treaty of Greenville, which ceded most of Ohio and opened the Northwest to American settlement.
- **The battle.** On August 20, 1794, Wayne led the Legion from Fort Roche de Bout; after a five-mile march the Americans met about 1,100 warriors in dense, tornado-felled timber. Wayne’s infantry broke the Native line and drove it from the field; the warriors retreated toward the British Fort Miamis, whose commander refused them entry.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]
- **The treaty.** The Treaty of Greenville, signed August 3, 1795, ended hostilities and established a boundary beginning at the mouth of the Cuyahoga River and running west and south. In exchange for peace, goods, and annuities, the nations ceded their claims to the lands east and south of the line, including strategic posts and river crossings. Tecumseh refused to sign.[^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]
- **The Myaamia reading.** The Myaamia describe the treaty as an independent agreement made after the military defeat, when their villages faced another winter without shelter or food; the peace was real but the boundary was soon violated by settlement. The chapter should present the treaty from both sides.[^ch13-myaamia-greenville]
- **The consequence.** Greenville transferred most of Ohio and parts of Indiana, Illinois, and Michigan to the United States and set the pattern of treaty-and-encroachment that would continue across the continent. It is the chapter’s clearest link between fiscal-military consolidation and dispossession.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-check-slavery]

### 3.4 In Focus: the Treaty of Greenville

- **In Focus: the Treaty of Greenville.** **In Focus.** The treaty opens by naming the parties: “the United States of America, and the tribes of Indians called the Wyandots, Delawares, Shawanees, Ottawas, Chippewas, Pattawatimas, Miamis, Eel Rivers, Weas, Kickapoos, Piankeshaws, and Kaskaskias.” Article 1 declares that “all hostilities shall cease; peace is hereby established, and shall be perpetual.” Article 2 restores prisoners on both sides. Article 3 draws the boundary and, “in consideration of the peace now established; of the goods formerly received from the United States; of those now to be delivered; and of the yearly delivery of goods now stipulated to be made hereafter,” the nations “cede and relinquish forever, all their claims to the lands lying eastwardly and southwardly of the general boundary line now described.”[^ch13-avalon-greenville] Read closely, the document is a peace treaty and a land cession at once. It treats the nations as contracting parties with the power to cede and to receive annuities, even as the United States understood the cession as extinguishing Native title to most of Ohio. The treaty also allowed American free passage “through their country” along the chain of posts, converting military posts into a permanent infrastructure of movement. The Myaamia remember the same document as the end of a war they could no longer sustain and the beginning of a peace the United States did not keep. The box should let both readings stand and note that the treaty text itself says nothing about how the boundary would be enforced.[^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-myaamia-greenville][^ch13-check-focus]

### 3.5 The Whiskey Rebellion and federal authority

- **The claim.** When western distillers resisted the excise, Washington called out the militia and marched west, and the government proved it could enforce federal law—without firing a shot.
- **The resistance.** The whiskey excise of 1791 provoked violence and intimidation in western Pennsylvania; in July 1794 a federal marshal serving writs was driven off, and more than 500 armed men attacked the fortified home of the tax inspector John Neville.[^ch13-wiki-whiskey]
- **The proclamation.** On September 25, 1794, Washington issued a proclamation declaring that “the moment is now come” to reduce the refractory “to a due subordination to the law,” and he called out the militia of New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia. He rode west at the head of about 13,000 militiamen; the rebel leaders fled and there was no battle.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]
- **The outcome.** About 150 men were arrested, only 20 held for trial, and only two convicted; both were eventually pardoned. The episode demonstrated federal authority and divided opinion about whether it had been exercised justly.[^ch13-wiki-whiskey]
- **The consequence.** The rebellion connected the fiscal program to the frontier: a tax to service the debt had to be enforced by an army. It also linked the western country to the wider party conflict, because Republicans read the militia march as an overreach and Federalists as a necessary display of law.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-check-sources]

## 4. France and party

### 4.1 The French Revolution and the Neutrality Proclamation

- **The claim.** The war between revolutionary France and Britain forced the United States to define neutrality, and that definition became a partisan issue.
- **The war.** After the overthrow of the French monarchy in 1792, revolutionary France went to war with Spain and Britain, and the United States had to decide whether its 1778 treaty with France obliged it to fight.[^ch13-state-genet]
- **The proclamation.** On April 22, 1793, Washington issued a proclamation declaring that the duty and interest of the United States required “a conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerent Powers,” and warning citizens that acts against either side would forfeit the protection of the United States.[^ch13-avalon-neutrality]
- **The division.** Jefferson and the Democratic-Republicans sympathized with the French Revolution; Hamilton and the Federalists preferred ties with Britain. Washington steered a neutral course, fearing that war would expose the weak republic to invasion and economic ruin.[^ch13-state-genet]
- **The consequence.** Neutrality was not a middle position that avoided conflict but a policy that created conflict, because each side read it as favoring the other. It became the first foreign-policy issue to organize domestic politics.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-state-jay]

### 4.2 Genêt’s mission and the democratic societies

- **The claim.** The French minister Edmond Charles Genêt tested American neutrality and helped the opposition organize, until his own government recalled him.
- **The mission.** Genêt arrived in Charleston on April 8, 1793, calling himself “Citizen Genêt,” and began issuing privateering commissions against British shipping and recruiting Americans, with the consent of South Carolina’s governor. In Philadelphia, Jefferson told him that outfitting French privateers in American ports violated neutrality.[^ch13-state-genet]
- **The collision.** Genêt ignored the warnings and allowed the arming of another privateer, the *Little Democrat*; the cabinet demanded his recall, and he was replaced when the French government changed course. He chose to remain in the United States.[^ch13-state-genet]
- **The societies.** Popular Democratic-Republican societies formed in several cities, corresponding with each other and pressing the French cause; Federalists condemned them as factional and foreign-inspired. The societies are the era’s clearest example of organized extra-governmental politics.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** The Genêt affair and the societies widened the split between the cabinet factions into a public contest and gave the emerging opposition a name and a network.[^ch13-state-genet]

### 4.3 The revolution in Saint-Domingue and the limits of American liberty

- **The claim.** The revolution in the French colony of Saint-Domingue exposed the contradiction between American claims of liberty and the defense of slavery, and it shaped American policy toward the Atlantic revolutions.
- **The revolution.** Saint-Domingue was the most profitable colony in the Americas, worked by an enslaved majority. A massive rebellion began on August 22, 1791; Toussaint Louverture emerged as its leading figure, and the colony became a multi-sided civil war in which Spanish and British forces also intervened.[^ch13-state-haiti]
- **American policy.** American leaders, many of them slaveholders, provided limited aid to suppress the revolt and later supported Toussaint’s forces against Britain; the United States did not recognize Haitian independence until 1862. Jefferson favored limited aid to the planters and feared the example of slave revolt.[^ch13-state-haiti]
- **The refugees.** In 1793 the fighting and a fire destroyed much of Cap-Français, and refugees fled to Norfolk, Baltimore, Philadelphia, and New York, including enslaved people brought by their owners. The revolution arrived in American ports as a refugee crisis.[^ch13-state-haiti]
- **The consequence.** Saint-Domingue made the era’s revolutionary language dangerous for slaveholders and tied American politics to Caribbean slavery. The chapter should name the enslaved people whose freedom the revolution won and whose condition American policy sought to protect.[^ch13-state-haiti][^ch13-check-haiti][^ch13-check-slavery]

### 4.4 The cabinet quarrel and the invention of party

- **The claim.** The disputes over credit, neutrality, and the bank turned two factions inside the government into a party system, even though both sides condemned parties.
- **The factions.** Hamilton’s program and Washington’s neutrality drew support from commercial and Federalist interests and opposition from agrarian and Republican ones. Jefferson resigned as secretary of state at the end of 1793, and the contest moved into the press and the public.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-check-sources]
- **The press.** Partisan newspapers, foreign policy, and the societies organized opinion outside Congress and made national politics a public contest rather than a cabinet argument. The chapter should show party as an invention of the decade, not a fixed structure.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-sources]
- **Who was excluded.** The parties argued about the meaning of “the people” while excluding enslaved people, Native nations, and women from the electorate. Both sides worked to preserve existing hierarchies even as the franchise expanded for White men.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-names]
- **The consequence.** By 1794 the contest had organized itself around two competing accounts of the Revolution—one Federalist, one Republican—and around the question of how far the federal government should consolidate power. That question dominates §§5–7.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-state-jay]

## 5. Treaties and the 1796 election

### 5.1 Jay’s Treaty and the price of peace

- **The claim.** Jay’s Treaty with Britain avoided war but conceded most disputed points and provoked the era’s greatest popular protest.
- **The problems.** Britain still occupied the northwestern posts promised in 1783, restricted American trade, and seized American ships and impressed sailors. Washington sent Chief Justice John Jay to negotiate, against Republican objection.[^ch13-state-jay]
- **The terms.** Signed November 19, 1794, the treaty gained the surrender of the posts and “most favored nation” commercial status but restricted access to the British West Indies and left the boundary, debts, and seizures to arbitration. Jay even conceded that Britain could seize American goods bound for France if it paid for them.[^ch13-state-jay]
- **The ratification.** The treaty was immensely unpopular; the Senate approved it 20 to 10 on June 24, 1795, and Washington implemented it as the price of peace and time to consolidate.[^ch13-state-jay]
- **The consequence.** The treaty divided the country, made Washington’s neutrality policy concrete, and deepened the party conflict. Its unpopularity is the background to the 1796 election.[^ch13-state-jay][^ch13-check-sources]

### 5.2 Pinckney’s Treaty and the Mississippi

- **The claim.** A second treaty, with Spain, won navigation of the Mississippi and the port of New Orleans, and it showed how European war could produce American advantage.
- **The negotiation.** Spain’s defeats in war and its fear of an Anglo-American alignment led it to negotiate. Thomas Pinckney, the American minister to Britain, signed the Treaty of San Lorenzo on October 27, 1795.[^ch13-state-pinckney]
- **The terms.** The treaty set the southern boundary at the 31st parallel, granted American ships free navigation of the Mississippi and duty-free transport through New Orleans, and resolved territorial disputes in the present-day Southeast.[^ch13-state-pinckney]
- **The consequence.** The treaty secured the western country’s access to market and made the union more attractive to western settlers, which Washington cited in his Farewell Address. Together with Jay’s Treaty, it defined the decade’s diplomacy.[^ch13-state-pinckney][^ch13-avalon-farewell]

### 5.3 Washington’s Farewell Address

- **The claim.** Washington’s Farewell Address announced his retirement and warned against party and foreign entanglement, but its warnings described conflicts already underway.
- **The address.** Published September 19, 1796, the address declined a third term and warned “in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally,” arguing that party opens the door to foreign influence and corruption.[^ch13-avalon-farewell]
- **The foreign advice.** Washington urged the nation to extend commercial relations while having “as little political connection as possible” with other nations, and to rely on temporary alliances only in emergencies.[^ch13-avalon-farewell]
- **The contradiction.** The address condemned parties in the very act of framing the election as a choice between Federalists and Republicans. Its warnings are evidence of the conflict, not proof that the conflict was avoidable.[^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** Washington’s retirement removed the one figure both sides claimed and left the succession open. That succession is the subject of §5.4.[^ch13-avalon-farewell]

### 5.4 The election of 1796 and the sectional ticket

- **The claim.** The election of 1796 was the first contested presidential succession, and its outcome placed the leaders of the two factions in the same administration.
- **The campaign.** The Federalists backed Vice President John Adams and Thomas Pinckney; the Republicans backed Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr. The campaign was fought through newspapers and correspondence rather than public meetings.[^ch13-wiki-1800][^ch13-check-dates]
- **The result.** Adams won the presidency and Jefferson, as the runner-up in the electoral vote, became vice president. The exact electoral totals must be verified before drafting; the outcome itself—a Federalist president with a Republican vice president—is the point.[^ch13-wiki-1800][^ch13-check-dates]
- **The problem.** The Constitution’s rule of one vote per elector, with no separate designation of president and vice president, produced a divided administration and set up the crisis of 1800. The Twelfth Amendment would later separate the offices.[^ch13-wiki-1800]
- **The consequence.** The election confirmed that the parties would contest national power and that the constitutional machinery had not anticipated organized opposition. Adams inherited Washington’s cabinet and the French crisis.[^ch13-wiki-1800][^ch13-state-xyz]

## 6. Quasi-War, repression, and the 1800 election

### 6.1 The XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War

- **The claim.** A French demand for bribes turned the diplomatic quarrel with France into an undeclared naval war and a domestic crisis.
- **The background.** France resented the Jay Treaty and in 1796 authorized the seizure of American merchant ships. President Adams sent Elbridge Gerry, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, and John Marshall to restore harmony.[^ch13-state-xyz]
- **The XYZ Affair.** The envoys could not meet Foreign Minister Talleyrand directly; intermediaries demanded a low-interest loan, payment of American claims, and a substantial bribe. The American envoys refused, and the publication of the dispatches, with the intermediaries’ names replaced by the letters W, X, Y, and Z, produced outrage.[^ch13-state-xyz]
- **The Quasi-War.** The United States and France fought an undeclared naval war from 1798 to 1800, and the United States built up its navy. Peace was restored by the Convention of 1800, also called the Treaty of Mortefontaine.[^ch13-state-xyz]
- **The consequence.** The crisis strengthened the Federalists’ argument for military preparedness and for suppressing domestic dissent, which produced the Alien and Sedition Acts.[^ch13-state-xyz][^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

### 6.2 The Alien and Sedition Acts and the assault on dissent

- **The claim.** The Federalist Congress used the war scare to restrict immigration and to criminalize criticism of the government, the era’s clearest test of the First Amendment.
- **The four laws.** In 1798 Congress passed the Naturalization Act, extending the residency requirement from five years to 14; the Alien Friends Act, allowing the president to deport non-citizens deemed dangerous; the Alien Enemies Act, allowing the detention or deportation of male citizens of a hostile nation in wartime; and the Sedition Act.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]
- **The Sedition Act.** The law criminalized “false, scandalous, and malicious writing” against the government and its officers, and it was used against Republican editors and politicians. It expired at the end of Adams’s term in 1801.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]
- **The prosecutions.** Matthew Lyon, a Republican congressman from Vermont, was the first person tried under the act, convicted for writings critical of the government. The prosecutions made martyrs and rallied the opposition.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition][^ch13-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** The acts connected the foreign crisis to the domestic contest and gave the Republicans their strongest argument: that the Federalists would use national security to destroy political opposition.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

### 6.3 The resolutions, Fries’s Rebellion, and the peace of 1800

- **The claim.** The Republicans answered with states’-rights resolutions, and a second tax revolt tested the government again before peace with France.
- **The resolutions.** Jefferson and Madison drafted the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions of 1798–99, arguing that the Alien and Sedition Acts were unconstitutional and asserting the states’ role in judging federal overreach. The resolutions advanced a theory of state authority that would be used later for different ends.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition][^ch13-check-sources]
- **Fries’s Rebellion.** In 1799 Pennsylvania German farmers resisted a direct federal property tax; John Fries led the resistance, and federal troops suppressed it. Adams later pardoned Fries and the others, choosing conciliation over severity.[^ch13-check-sources][^ch13-check-dates]
- **The peace.** Adams reopened negotiations with France and accepted the Convention of 1800, ending the Quasi-War. The decision split his own party, since Hamilton and the high Federalists wanted war.[^ch13-state-xyz]
- **The consequence.** Adams’s peace removed the war as a campaign issue but did not heal the party split; it also spared the republic a war it was unprepared to fight. The election of 1800 would decide the rest.[^ch13-state-xyz][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

### 6.4 The election of 1800 and the House crisis

- **The claim.** The election of 1800 produced a tie between Jefferson and Burr and threw the presidency into the House, where the Federalists nearly blocked the transfer of power.
- **The campaign.** The Republicans ran Jefferson and Burr; the Federalists ran Adams and Charles Cotesworth Pinckney. The Republicans carried the electoral vote, 73 to 65 for Adams and 64 for Pinckney, but every Republican elector voted for both Jefferson and Burr, leaving the two tied.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-wiki-1800]
- **The crisis.** Under the Constitution, the tied election went to the House, where each state delegation cast one vote. Federalists opposed to Jefferson maneuvered to elect Burr or to prevent a choice; the contest went to 36 ballots before Jefferson won on February 17, 1801, with 10 states to Burr’s four.[^ch13-wiki-1800][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]
- **The danger.** The crisis brought threats of violence and fears of dissolution, and it exposed the Constitution’s failure to anticipate party tickets. The parties themselves supplied the mechanism of settlement.[^ch13-loc-peaceful][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]
- **The consequence.** The election was decided, but the rules that produced the tie were not yet fixed; the Twelfth Amendment would separate the presidential and vice-presidential votes.[^ch13-wiki-1800]

### 6.5 The transfer of power

- **The claim.** Jefferson’s inauguration in March 1801 was the first transfer of national power between rival parties, and it set a precedent that was not inevitable.
- **The transfer.** Jefferson took office on March 4, 1801, and the Federalists surrendered power without a coup. His inaugural address sought to calm the country: “We are all republicans. We are all federalists.”[^ch13-loc-peaceful][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]
- **The moderation.** Jefferson preserved the Bank of the United States, honored but paid down the national debt, and shrank the army and navy rather than abolishing them, conciliating moderate Federalists while pushing his own program.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]
- **The exclusions.** The transfer extended power to a wider White male electorate but not to women, free Black people, or the enslaved; the calm at the top was purchased in part at the expense of those at the bottom. The chapter must name that limit.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-names]
- **The consequence.** The machinery built in the 1790s—a funded debt, a bank, a revenue system, a frontier army, a party press—passed intact to the opposition. Chapter 14 owns what Jefferson did with it.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-check-ch14]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

- **The fiscal program built a working national state.** Funding, assumption, the bank, and the excise gave the government revenue, credit, and a financial institution, and they created the first organized opposition.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-house-first-bank]
- **Consolidation rested on dispossession.** The war for the Ohio Country and the Treaty of Greenville transferred most of Ohio to the United States and set the pattern for later treaty-and-encroachment.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-myaamia-greenville]
- **Federal authority was enforced.** The militia march against the Whiskey Rebellion showed that the government could and would enforce federal law, and it divided opinion about how far it should.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]
- **The Atlantic revolutions shaped American politics.** Neutrality, the Genêt affair, and the revolution in Saint-Domingue turned foreign policy into domestic conflict and exposed the contradiction between liberty and slavery.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-state-haiti]
- **A party system was invented.** Despite near-universal condemnation of parties, organized contest emerged around credit, neutrality, and the meaning of the Revolution.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-avalon-farewell]
- **The transfer of 1801 was contested.** The election of 1800 produced a tie and a House crisis before Jefferson took office.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-wiki-1800]

### 7.2 What is open

- **How much economic interest drove the parties.** Were the Federalists and Republicans chiefly the agents of competing economic interests, as Beard argued, or of ideas about republicanism, corruption, and the Revolution, as the republican synthesis held?[^ch13-beard-origins][^ch13-s-usih-appleby][^ch13-check-sources]
- **Whether the parties were inevitable or contingent.** Did the Constitution’s structure produce parties, or did particular choices—assumption, the bank, neutrality, the Sedition Act—create them? The chapter should hold the contingency open.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-sources]
- **How to weigh the Federalist achievement.** Did the Federalists build a durable state that their opponents inherited, or did they overreach and destroy themselves, as Elkins and McKitrick argue?[^ch13-goodreads-elkins][^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty][^ch13-check-sources]
- **Whether consolidation was inseparable from dispossession.** Could the fiscal-military state have been built without the western war and the land cessions, or were they the same project?[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-myaamia-greenville][^ch13-check-sources]
- **What the archives can and cannot show.** Enslaved people, Native nations, and women appear mainly through the documents of those who governed, owned, or excluded them; the treasury reports and party press are the records of the winners.[^ch13-state-haiti][^ch13-check-names]
- **How to read the transfer of 1801.** Was it a genuine democratic advance, or the consolidation of a White male democracy purchased by excluding others?[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the 1790s created a more capable national government and, at the same time, an organized politics that excluded most of the people it governed; neither the fiscal achievement nor the exclusions cancels the other.
- **The economic interpretation.** Charles A. Beard’s *Economic Origins of Jeffersonian Democracy* (1915) read the Federalist–Republican struggle as a conflict between agrarianism and fluid capital and extended the class-conflict thesis of his work on the Constitution. It is the origin of the chapter’s question about whose interests the fiscal program served.[^ch13-beard-origins][^ch13-britannica-beard]
- **The political synthesis.** Stanley Elkins and Eric McKitrick’s *The Age of Federalism* (1993) treats the 1790s as a contest over the meaning of the Revolution, driven by political ideas and by the Federalists’ own political ineptitude as much as by interest; it is the standard modern synthesis of the decade.[^ch13-goodreads-elkins][^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty]
- **The republican synthesis and its critics.** Lance Banning and others argued that the Republicans opposed a corrupt “consolidated” government in the name of a classical republican tradition, while Joyce Appleby answered that the Republicans were the party of a new liberal, commercial society rather than of classical virtue. The debate over what the parties believed is the chapter’s central historiographical problem.[^ch13-s-usih-appleby][^ch13-check-sources]
- **Democracy and its limits.** Gordon Wood narrates the era as the rise of egalitarian democracy for ordinary White men; his critics, including Nancy Isenberg and John L. Brooke, argue that this consensus narrative homogenizes Americans and marginalizes women, slavery, and dispossession. The chapter should present both.[^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty][^ch13-cfr-wood]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions say that the 1790s built a fiscal-military state, invented a party system, and extended political voice to White men while defending slavery and taking Native land. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty][^ch13-s-usih-appleby][^ch13-check-sources]

## Legacies

The 1790s created a national government that could tax, borrow, charter a bank, raise an army, and enforce its laws, and it did so while taking Native land and defending slavery. The funded debt, the bank, the revenue system, and the frontier army passed intact to Jefferson in 1801 and became the instruments of later expansion.[^ch13-house-first-bank][^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

The decade also invented the party system. Washington warned against the spirit of party, yet the disputes over credit, neutrality, and the Sedition Act produced organized national parties and a partisan press that outlasted the founders. The election of 1800 proved that power could change hands without violence, but only after a constitutional crisis that the Twelfth Amendment tried to prevent.[^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-wiki-1800]

And it left the chapter’s central contradiction. The government that claimed to secure the blessings of liberty rested on the enslavement of people it counted for representation, on the dispossession of Native nations, and on the exclusion of women and the propertyless from the political nation. The United States did not recognize Haitian independence until 1862, because the example of a successful slave revolution was too dangerous for a slaveholding republic.[^ch13-state-haiti][^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-slavery]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The American 1790s were one part of an Atlantic revolutionary age: the French Revolution, the revolution in Saint-Domingue, and the European wars shaped American neutrality, party conflict, and the fiscal program. The chapter should place the new republic inside that moment, not outside it.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-state-haiti]
- **Archives and silences.** Enslaved people, Native nations, and women appear in these records mainly through the documents of those who governed, owned, or excluded them; the treasury reports, the party press, and the treaty texts are the records of the victors. The chapter should name those limits, and treat the Myaamia account of Greenville as evidence in its own right.[^ch13-myaamia-greenville][^ch13-check-names]
- **Distant connections.** Later chapters return to the fiscal-military state (Ch. 14), the three-fifths clause and slaveholding power (Chs. 16 and 20), the party system and the Twelfth Amendment (Chs. 16 and 20), and the treaty-and-encroachment pattern set at Greenville (Ch. 17).[^ch13-check-ch14][^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-check-slavery]

## Summary

- The First Congress built the departments, courts, and revenue system of the new government, and Washington invented the presidency’s practices in public.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-check-judiciary]
- Hamilton’s *Report on Public Credit* funded the debt at face value and proposed assumption; the Compromise of 1790 passed assumption in exchange for a Potomac capital.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790]
- The bank bill produced the first great argument over implied powers; Washington accepted Hamilton’s reading and signed it in 1791.[^ch13-jefferson-bank-opinion][^ch13-hamilton-bank-opinion]
- In the Ohio Country, the confederacy destroyed St. Clair’s army in 1791, but Wayne’s victory at Fallen Timbers in 1794 and the Treaty of Greenville in 1795 ceded most of Ohio to the United States.[^ch13-myaamia-stclair][^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-avalon-greenville]
- Washington’s militia march against the Whiskey Rebellion proved that the federal government could enforce its laws, and it divided opinion about how far it should.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]
- The Neutrality Proclamation, the Genêt affair, and the revolution in Saint-Domingue turned the Atlantic crisis into American party conflict and exposed the contradiction between liberty and slavery.[^ch13-avalon-neutrality][^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-state-haiti]
- Jay’s Treaty avoided war with Britain at the cost of unpopular concessions, and Pinckney’s Treaty won the Mississippi; Washington’s Farewell Address warned against the parties already forming.[^ch13-state-jay][^ch13-state-pinckney][^ch13-avalon-farewell]
- The XYZ Affair produced the Quasi-War and the Alien and Sedition Acts; the Republicans answered with the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions, and Adams made peace with France in 1800.[^ch13-state-xyz][^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]
- The election of 1800 tied Jefferson and Burr and went to 36 ballots in the House before Jefferson won; the transfer of power in 1801 set the precedent of a peaceful change of party.[^ch13-wiki-1800][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-loc-peaceful]

## Study Questions

1. Read the National Archives account of Washington’s inauguration and the National Park Service page on the inaugural Bible. What does the record establish about the oath, and what does the dispute over “So help me God” show about how historical detail is added after the fact?
2. Using Hamilton’s *Report on Public Credit*, identify his argument for funding at par. Why did Madison object, and what did the “discrimination” alternative propose?
3. Read Jefferson’s and Hamilton’s opinions on the bank. Where exactly do they disagree about the “necessary and proper” clause, and which reading has shaped later American governance?
4. The Compromise of 1790 linked assumption to the capital’s location. What does that bargain reveal about how the new government made decisions, and who paid the price?
5. Read the Treaty of Greenville. What does it cede, what does it promise in return, and what does the document not say about enforcement?
6. Compare the Myaamia account of Greenville with the treaty text. What does each source establish, and what does each leave out?
7. Washington’s proclamation on the Whiskey Rebellion invoked his duty “to take care that the laws be faithfully executed.” How did the government’s response to the excise compare with its response to the Alien and Sedition Acts?
8. The Neutrality Proclamation declared “a conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerent Powers.” Why did a policy of neutrality provoke so much conflict?
9. Read the office of the historian’s account of the Haitian Revolution and the United States. How did American leaders reconcile their revolutionary language with their defense of slavery?
10. The election of 1800 tied Jefferson and Burr. Given the constitutional rules in place, what options did the House have, and how did the crisis produce the Twelfth Amendment?
11. Use Table 1. Which interpretation best explains the emergence of party in the 1790s, and what does each position leave unexplained?
12. The contingency question: if Adams had not made peace with France, or if the House had chosen Burr, how might the 1790s have ended? What evidence would you need to answer?

> *A closing note.* The decade that began on a balcony in New York ended with a peaceful transfer of power in Washington, and the distance between those two moments measures what the new government had built: a funded debt, a bank, a revenue system, a frontier army, a party press, and a habit of organized contest. It also measures what it had not settled. The government that could enforce an excise tax on western farmers had made peace with the confederacy by taking its land, had built its fiscal power on a debt that counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person, and had answered criticism with a sedition law. The men who warned against the spirit of party were themselves its founders. What they created was not a finished republic but a working one, capable of both the transfer of 1801 and the exclusions that transfer preserved.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-check-slavery]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on March 4, 1801, when Thomas Jefferson walked to the Capitol and took the oath as the third president, the first to reach the office by defeating an incumbent party. This chapter has followed the machinery from Washington’s balcony through the fiscal program, the western war, the Atlantic crisis, and the election of 1800; Chapter 14 takes up the government once Jefferson held it—the program of limited government, the Louisiana Purchase, the embargo, and the War of 1812—and asks whether a republic of restraint could expand without enlarging the coercive power it criticized.[^ch13-check-ch14][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]
