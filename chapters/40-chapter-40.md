# Chapter 40. The Great Society and the Rights Revolution (1961–1968)

> “The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time.”[^ch40-great-society-speech]
>
> — Lyndon B. Johnson, remarks at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, May 22, 1964.[^ch40-great-society-speech]

> “Just as the 1960s forgot the failures of the 1930s, we today forget the failures of the 1960s.”[^ch40-shlaes-siegel]
>
> — Amity Shlaes, *Great Society: A New History* (New York: Harper, 2019), as quoted in Fred Siegel’s review.[^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

> “It’s a myth to say the Great Society failed, just as it’s a myth to portray it as a radical left-wing, big-government project.”[^ch40-zeitz]
>
> — Joshua Zeitz, *Building the Great Society: Inside Lyndon Johnson’s White House* (New York: Viking, 2018), adapted in *Politico Magazine*, January 28, 2018.[^ch40-zeitz]

Three voices, three verdicts. Johnson described an ambition: a society that would end poverty and racial injustice, not merely administer relief. Shlaes, writing more than half a century later, argued that the programs’ failures were structural and that the generation that built them had forgotten the failures of an earlier era. Zeitz argued that the same programs were moderate in design, effective in important respects, and misremembered by both friends and critics. They disagree about what the Great Society was, what it achieved, and how it should be remembered. Their disagreement frames the chapter’s question: what could federal rights and social programs achieve, and what constrained them?[^ch40-great-society-speech][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-epigraphs]

On the afternoon of July 30, 1965, a motorcade carried President Lyndon B. Johnson from Kansas City to the Harry S. Truman Library in Independence, Missouri. The president’s daily diary recorded a warm, sunny day. Former President Harry S. Truman and his wife, Bess W. Truman, met the party and walked with Johnson and Lady Bird Johnson into the library.[^ch40-medicare-ceremony]

Shortly before three o’clock, Johnson took the platform in the auditorium. Vice President Hubert H. Humphrey and Missouri’s governor, Warren E. Hearnes, were on the platform. Truman stood during Johnson’s remarks and then sat beside him at the signing table, with Lady Bird Johnson behind the president and Bess Truman behind her husband. Johnson had come to sign the Social Security Amendments of 1965, which created Medicare and Medicaid, and he chose the library to honor the former president who had first asked Congress for national health insurance twenty years earlier. “It was really Harry Truman of Missouri who planted the seeds of compassion and duty which have today flowered into care for the sick, and serenity for the fearful,” Johnson said.[^ch40-medicare-ceremony][^ch40-medicare-senate][^ch40-medicare-lbj]

When he sat down, Johnson signed the bill and gave the pens away: first to Bess Truman, then to Truman, to Humphrey, to Representative Wilbur Mills of Arkansas, and to the other guests on the platform. At the ceremony he enrolled Truman as the first Medicare beneficiary and signed Truman’s application for the optional Part B medical coverage as a witness. The LBJ Library’s object record dates the first two cards to that day; the Truman Library’s account of the daily diary records that Johnson hand-delivered them on January 20, 1966.[^ch40-medicare-ceremony][^ch40-medicare-ssa][^ch40-medicare-cards][^ch40-medicare-lbj]

The record establishes the place, the date, the participants, the law’s two titles, and the enrollment. It does not record what Truman privately thought that afternoon, how Johnson weighed the ceremony’s political uses, or that the ceremony itself produced the statute. On July 30, 1965, no one knew that Medicare would enroll nearly 19 million people when coverage began on July 1, 1966, that Medicaid would become a permanent federal-state program, or that the programs’ costs would become a recurring fiscal fight. What could federal rights and social programs achieve, and what constrained them?[^ch40-medicare-implementation][^ch40-check-scene][^ch40-check-silences]

This chapter covers the years from 1961 to 1968 and asks what federal rights and social programs could achieve and what constrained them. It treats the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 as the legal frame it inherits from Chapter 39, and it does not re-narrate the movement history of those statutes, the Fair Housing Act of 1968, or the urban uprisings. It does not narrate the Bay of Pigs, the Cuban missile crisis, or the Vietnam War, which belong to Chapter 41, the 1968 election and Chicago, which belong to Chapter 42, or the later women’s and gay-liberation struggles, which belong to Chapters 42 and 43. The chapter draws on statutes, executive branch records, agency data, census and program statistics, organizational materials, speeches, oral histories, and the secondary literature.[^ch40-check-scope][^ch40-check-ch39][^ch40-check-ch41][^ch40-check-ch42-43]

The chapter uses several contested names. “Great Society” was Johnson’s phrase for an ambition broader than any single statute; “War on Poverty” named the antipoverty programs and the political project around them, not one agency. “New Frontier” was Kennedy’s campaign phrase for his domestic agenda. “Liberal state” is a scholarly shorthand for the postwar federal role in social provision, regulation, and rights enforcement, and it was never a single institution. “Rights revolution” here means the overlapping claims to legal equality and social provision that extended beyond the civil-rights statutes. “Maximum feasible participation” was the statutory phrase that became a demand and a conflict. “Backlash” describes a political reaction, not a single motive; the chapter uses it for the coalition and rhetoric that opposed further federal action while noting that voters’ reasons were mixed.[^ch40-check-names][^ch40-check-scope]

The available record over-represents presidents, administrators, and national organizations and under-represents the poor people, local officials, unpaid women, and gay and lesbian people whose lives the programs touched. The poverty, enrollment, and cost figures often measure different things, and they are given with their definitions, base dates, and denominators. Where the archive is thin, the chapter names the silence rather than filling it with a composite witness.[^ch40-check-silences][^ch40-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the New Frontier and Kennedy’s death. §2 turns to the 89th Congress and the poverty, health, education, and immigration statutes, and reads Map 31. §3 follows the organizing of women, consumers, environmentalists, and gay and lesbian people. §4 examines the cities and fiscal pressures, and reads an early Medicare card in the In Focus. §5 follows the backlash and the political transition. §6 asks how historians have weighed the completion of New Deal liberalism, the design and implementation of the programs, the pressure from below, and the accounts of backlash. The sections are organized thematically around programs and questions rather than as a single strict chronology, because the legislative, organizational, and electoral stories overlap in time. The chapter begins on July 30, 1965, in Independence, Missouri.[^ch40-check-scope]

## 1. The New Frontier and Kennedy’s death

### 1.1 The claim: a stalled program and a sudden inheritance

**The claim.** Between 1961 and November 1963 Kennedy’s New Frontier proposed a broad domestic agenda but won only part of it from a Congress whose southern Democrats controlled the key committees, and Johnson inherited both the unfinished program and the political opening created by Kennedy’s assassination.[^ch40-jfk-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

The pattern matters. The Great Society extended proposals that predated Johnson: aid to education, medical care for the elderly, urban aid, regional development, and a tax cut. What changed after 1963 was not the idea but the opportunity — a landslide majority, a president who knew how to use it, and the pressure of the civil rights years. The proposals, the votes, and the electoral results are documented. They cannot establish that any single cause produced the legislative burst of 1964 and 1965, and the sequence is best read as the interaction of inheritance and opportunity rather than as the arrival of a wholly new program.[^ch40-jfk-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 The New Frontier and its limits

Kennedy took office in January 1961 in the depths of the fourth major recession since the Second World War. Business bankruptcies had reached their highest level since the 1930s, farm incomes had fallen 25 percent since 1951, and 5.5 million Americans were looking for work. Kennedy pursued tax cuts, protection for the unemployed, an increase in the minimum wage, and measures to stimulate business and housing. He also proposed federal aid to education, medical care for the elderly, urban mass transit, a Department of Urban Affairs, and regional development in Appalachia.[^ch40-jfk-miller]

The results were partial. Kennedy won an increase in the minimum wage, and his administration undertook regional development in Appalachia, an initiative that would reduce poverty in the region over the following decades. A major medical program for the elderly went nowhere. Southern Democrats killed the Department of Urban Affairs because they assumed Kennedy would appoint an African American as its first secretary. The education bill foundered on the question of aid to parochial schools. Kennedy, as a Catholic, opposed such aid to protect his political credibility, while his Protestant successor would be under no such constraint.[^ch40-jfk-miller]

The proposals and their fates are documented. Whether Kennedy would have succeeded after 1964 is a counterfactual, and the chapter treats it as speculation. The New Frontier’s limits were structural as well as personal: the committee system, the southern wing of the Democratic Party, and the church-state dispute over schools set boundaries that no president could simply talk away.[^ch40-jfk-miller][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 1.3 The assassination and the 1964 mandate

Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on November 22, 1963. That evening, Johnson told aides Bill Moyers and Jack Valenti that every issue on his desk had been on it when he entered Congress in 1937: civil rights, health insurance for the elderly and the poor, federal aid to schools, support for higher education, and antipoverty and nutrition programs. In his first State of the Union address, in January 1964, he declared: “This administration today, here and now, declares unconditional war on poverty in America.”[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

Johnson signed the Revenue Act in February 1964 and the Civil Rights Act in July. The tax cut was meant to spur growth, and the civil-rights law ended segregation in public accommodations and employment, following through on Kennedy’s commitment. In November 1964 Johnson defeated the Republican senator Barry Goldwater of Arizona in a landslide. The result returned 68 Democratic senators and 295 representatives, giving Johnson working, and often veto-proof, majorities in both chambers.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

The dates, the statutes, and the election result are documented. They do not prove that the landslide alone produced the 89th Congress’s output. The mandate mattered, but so did the movement pressure that had made civil rights a national crisis and the legislative skill that turned a majority into statutes. The chapter holds the mandate and the organizing together.[^ch40-check-debate-works][^ch40-zeitz]

### 1.4 The “Great Society” and the speech

Johnson first used the phrase “Great Society” at Ohio University on May 7, 1964, and developed it in a commencement address at the University of Michigan on May 22. At Ann Arbor he described the purpose as moving “not only toward the rich society and the powerful society, but upward to the Great Society.” He then supplied the sentence that became the phrase’s public definition: “The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time.”[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-great-society-speech]

The speech named three places to begin: “in our cities, in our countryside, and in our classrooms.” It described cities losing community, countryside losing beauty, and classrooms losing the money and teachers that could free every young mind. It also described “a creative federalism, between the National Capital and the leaders of local communities,” a formula that promised federal purpose without a single federal administration. The speech stated a purpose, not a program. Its content came later, in statutes, budgets, and administrative fights.[^ch40-great-society-speech]

### 1.5 Consequences and the map

By the end of 1964 Johnson had converted an inherited agenda into an electoral mandate, and the 89th Congress was poised to pass a body of social legislation that would be compared to the New Deal. The chapter turns next to that Congress and to the programs it built. Map 31, “Poverty and Great Society programs, 1960–1968,” is the chapter’s registered visual, and it belongs to §2. It will show the geography of measured poverty against the placement of major Great Society programs, distinguishing where need was measured from where programs operated. A map can show the coincidence of need and provision. It cannot show that a program caused a local outcome, and the causal argument stays in the prose.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-check-map31]

## 2. The 89th Congress: poverty, health, education, and immigration

### 2.1 The claim: a legislative system, not a single program

**The claim.** The 89th Congress of 1965–67 assembled a linked system of antipoverty, health, education, and immigration programs, and the system’s reach depended on how each program was designed, funded, and administered, not on the breadth of the president’s rhetoric.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]

The statutes formed a portfolio with different mechanisms. The antipoverty programs offered services, training, and legal aid to poor people; Medicare offered social insurance to the elderly; Medicaid offered federal-state assistance to people with limited income; the education acts sent grants to schools and students; and the immigration act replaced a quota system with preferences for family reunification and skills. Each program had a different constituency, a different administrative structure, and a different political fate. The coherence of the portfolio was partly retrospective: the programs were built in overlapping fights, not from a single blueprint.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-immigration-house][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 2.2 The Economic Opportunity Act and the War on Poverty

The Economic Opportunity Act, signed on August 20, 1964, created the Office of Economic Opportunity under R. Sargent Shriver, who had directed the Peace Corps. It launched the Job Corps, Volunteers in Service to America, and the Community Action Program. The Community Action Program required each local Community Action Agency to include “maximum feasible participation” by residents of the communities it served. That phrase was a design choice with political consequences: community activists read it as a mandate for power, while city officials who had expected to control the agencies read it as an invitation to chaos. Vice President Humphrey tried to mediate, but the conflict divided the Democratic coalition.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-eoa-lbj]

The program’s scale grew quickly. By the end of Johnson’s presidency more than 1,000 Community Action Agencies were in operation, and by 1967 Congress had given local governments the option to take them over. Head Start began as an eight-week summer program for about 500,000 children ages three to five. Legal services and neighborhood health centers extended the antipoverty effort into law and medicine. Federal spending on programs targeted at the poor doubled from about $6 billion in 1965 to about $12 billion in 1968, and the official poverty rate fell from about 20 percent to about 12 percent between 1964 and 1974.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-earnings-history]

The decline in poverty had more than one cause. A booming economy did part of the work, and the poverty rate’s fall continued after the programs’ first years. The statutes, agencies, funding, and poverty series are documented. They cannot isolate any single program as the cause, and the “maximum feasible participation” conflict is best read as a design choice with political consequences rather than as a simple failure of administration.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-check-debate-works]

The architects of the War on Poverty were more moderate than their critics and their defenders sometimes suggest. They rejected a guaranteed family income and a large federal public-jobs program, choosing instead education, training, health care, and food security as the routes to opportunity. That choice made the programs politically defensible in 1964 and vulnerable later, when the economy stopped growing fast enough to carry the poor upward. The design was neither radical nor adequate to every claim made for it.[^ch40-zeitz]

### 2.3 Medicare and Medicaid

The Social Security Amendments of 1965, signed on July 30, 1965, created Medicare as Title XVIII of the Social Security Act and Medicaid as Title XIX. Medicare was a social insurance program for people aged 65 and older. It combined hospital insurance with a voluntary supplementary plan that covered physicians’ services and related care. Medicaid was a federal-state program of medical assistance for people with limited income, financed partly by the national government and administered by the states under their welfare programs.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-medicare-lbj]

The statute ended two decades of failed attempts to enact national health insurance for the aged. Truman had proposed such a program in 1945. Senate Finance Committee chairman Harry F. Byrd of Virginia and the American Medical Association had defeated earlier bills, including a narrow loss in 1962, and the 1964 landslide changed the congressional arithmetic. The final bill was what the Senate historian calls a “three layer cake” of hospital insurance for the aged, physicians’ insurance for the elderly, and expanded federal assistance to state medical payments for the poor.[^ch40-medicare-senate][^ch40-medicare-nara]

The provisions were generous by the standards of the time and bounded by design. Medicare covered up to 90 days of hospital care, 100 days of nursing-home care, and 100 home health visits per year. Enrollees over 65 could pay $3 a month for Part B, which covered 80 percent of doctor bills, laboratory tests, and related services. The program enrolled nearly 20 million beneficiaries in its first three years. Budget funding for Medicare and Medicaid in fiscal year 1966, the program’s first full year, was about $2.2 billion, and more than 2.5 million Americans received hospital care covered by Medicare in the first six months.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-medicare-implementation]

The law also carried an exclusion that revealed its origins in the existing Social Security system: people not covered by Social Security had to swear that they were not members of any Communist organization, a requirement carried over from Section 210a(17) of the Social Security Act. At the same time, the administration used Title VI of the Civil Rights Act to require hospitals to integrate as a condition of reimbursement. More than a thousand inspectors visited hospitals; about 7,000 facilities complied quickly, and another 5,500 followed after inspection, with most of the holdouts in Mississippi, Louisiana, Alabama, and South Carolina. Medicare was thus both a social-insurance achievement and an instrument of the civil-rights settlement.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-ch39]

### 2.4 Education: the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and the Higher Education Act

The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 broke the long deadlock over federal aid to schools by directing funds to school districts with concentrations of low-income students. Title I, which accounted for five-sixths of the act’s authorized funds, sent money to schools and districts with high percentages of poor students. Title II supported libraries and textbooks, and later titles supported supplementary educational centers and adult education. Johnson signed the act on April 11, 1965, at the former Junction Elementary School near Stonewall, Texas, where he had attended school as a child. His first teacher, Kate Deadrich Looney, sat beside him. “As a son of a tenant farmer, I know that education is the only valid passport from poverty,” he said.[^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-esea-remarks]

The Higher Education Act of 1965 expanded scholarships, work-study, and low-interest loans for college students. It combined aid to institutions with aid to students, and it helped accommodate the baby boom generation then entering college. The education statutes did not stand alone. Weeks after the ESEA passed, the Johnson administration used Title VI of the Civil Rights Act to threaten the withholding of federal school funds from segregated districts. Between 1965 and 1968 the share of Black students in the South who attended majority-white schools rose from roughly 2.3 percent to almost 23.4 percent.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]

The aid and the enforcement are best read as one system. The ESEA’s money gave the federal government leverage; Title VI gave it a weapon. The desegregation figure measures exposure to white-majority schools, not the quality of schooling or the end of racial hierarchy, and the record cannot show that federal pressure alone produced the change. It can show that the two statutes were administered together and that the federal role in southern education changed faster than the legal language alone would suggest.[^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-numbers]

### 2.5 Immigration: the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965

The Immigration and Nationality Act, signed at the base of the Statue of Liberty on October 3, 1965, abolished the national-origins quota system and replaced it with preference categories that favored family reunification and needed skills. The bill was introduced by Representative Emanuel Celler of New York, the chairman of the House Judiciary Committee, and managed in the Senate by Senator Philip A. Hart of Michigan. It passed the House 318 to 95. The law set an annual ceiling of 290,000 visas and a limit of 20,000 visas per country per year.[^ch40-immigration-house]

Its consequences exceeded its sponsors’ expectations. The family-reunification provisions admitted far more immigrants than the framers had anticipated: annual immigration rose to nearly half a million, and only about 20 percent came from Europe. The statute, the vote, the quotas, and the flow are documented. What Congress foresaw is a different question, and the record does not show that it foresaw the demographic result. The law’s text and its later consequences are distinct. The act’s sponsors presented it as a reform of a discriminatory quota system; the distinction matters for judging both their intentions and the law’s effects.[^ch40-immigration-house][^ch40-check-debate-works]

## 3. Women, consumer, environmental, and early gay and lesbian organizing

### 3.1 The claim: rights organizing beyond the civil rights statutes

**The claim.** The same years that produced the Great Society’s statutes also produced new organizing among women, consumers, environmentalists, and gay and lesbian people, and these groups used the federal government’s language of rights and its new administrative agencies as instruments of their own demands.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-dob-loc]

The organizing was both a response to and a use of the liberal state. Title VII’s ban on sex discrimination gave women a legal handle on employment; the new consumer agencies gave reformers a target and a platform; the conservation statutes gave environmentalists a body of law to enforce; and the federal security apparatus that policed gay and lesbian employees gave activists a concrete injury to challenge. The groups did not share a single agenda, and their internal differences were as important as their common language of rights.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-check-silences]

### 3.2 Women: *The Feminine Mystique* and the National Organization for Women

Betty Friedan’s *The Feminine Mystique*, published in 1963, named a widespread dissatisfaction among suburban white middle-class women and called it “the problem that has no name.” The book became a best-seller and gave a public vocabulary to women who had been told that domestic life was their fulfillment. It did not create the dissatisfaction it described, and its focus on educated white women left other women’s lives at the margin.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm]

In 1966 Friedan, Pauli Murray, Aileen Hernandez, and others founded the National Organization for Women. Friedan became its first president. NOW demanded that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforce Title VII’s guarantee of equality in employment, and its first action was to demand that the EEOC end sex-segregated help-wanted advertising. The organization’s early leadership drew mainly on white, middle-class, educated women, and later critics faulted it for that focus. The book, the organization, and the demands are documented. They cannot show that the book caused the organizing, and it is best read as a catalyst among several.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-check-silences]

### 3.3 Consumers: Ralph Nader and auto safety

Ralph Nader’s *Unsafe at Any Speed*, published in November 1965, attacked the auto industry for putting style and cost ahead of safety. Nader, then a young lawyer, had written on auto safety and worked as a consultant; his book focused on the General Motors Corvair’s rear suspension. Senator Abraham Ribicoff’s subcommittee held hearings on the federal role in traffic safety in 1965, and the hearings gave Nader’s argument a national stage.[^ch40-nader-fhwa]

The pressure produced two statutes. Johnson signed the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act and the Highway Safety Act on September 9, 1966. The Fair Packaging and Labeling Act, signed on November 3, 1966, was a more modest consumer measure: it required labels to state the identity and net quantity of contents and authorized regulations against deceptive packaging. The book, the hearings, and the statutes are documented. They do not prove that the book alone produced the laws, and Nader was one force among several, including the industry’s own scandals and the public’s growing sense that cars were dangerous by design.[^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-fpla][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 Environment: *Silent Spring* and the conservation statutes

Rachel Carson’s *Silent Spring*, published on September 27, 1962, brought the hazards of DDT and other pesticides to a mass audience. Carson, a biologist and writer, argued that indiscriminate spraying was poisoning the food chain and threatening the birds whose songs gave the book its title. The chemical industry attacked her and the book, and the controversy made environmental protection a public issue.[^ch40-carson-nrdc]

The statutes followed unevenly. The Clean Air Act of 1963 was the first federal legislation to authorize a sustained program of air-pollution control, and the Water Quality Act of 1965 extended federal control over water pollution. The Wilderness Act, signed on September 3, 1964, created the National Wilderness Preservation System and placed 54 areas totaling 9.1 million acres in 13 states into it. Howard Zahniser of the Wilderness Society wrote 66 drafts of the act over eight years and died four months before Johnson signed it. The books, the statutes, and the acreage are documented. They do not prove that *Silent Spring* alone produced the laws; public alarm and the administrative machinery of enforcement are distinct.[^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-cleanair-epa][^ch40-wilderness][^ch40-great-society-history]

### 3.5 Gay and lesbian organizing before Stonewall

The 1950s and early 1960s brought the first sustained public organizing for gay and lesbian rights. The Mattachine Society and the Daughters of Bilitis built organizations and tested the boundaries of public visibility; the Daughters of Bilitis published *The Ladder*. Frank Kameny, a federal employee dismissed in 1957 because he was gay, petitioned the Supreme Court for review in 1960 and co-founded the Mattachine Society of Washington. In 1965 he led small groups in the first public protests for gay rights at the White House and in Philadelphia.[^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-dob-loc]

The Daughters of Bilitis was founded in San Francisco on September 21, 1955, by Rosalie “Rose” Bamberger, Del Martin, Phyllis Lyon, and others. It began as a private social club and grew into a national organization with chapters in Rhode Island, New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles by 1960. From 1956 it published *The Ladder*, the first nationally distributed lesbian magazine. Ernestine Eckstein, a member, joined the 1965 pickets and appeared on the cover of *The Ladder* in 1966.[^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-dob-smithsonian]

On April 21, 1966, members of the Mattachine Society staged a “sip-in” at Julius’ bar in Greenwich Village. They identified themselves as homosexuals and asked to be served, using the tactics of the southern lunch-counter sit-ins against a state rule that allowed bars to refuse service to anyone known to be gay. The action helped end New York State’s practice of prohibiting the serving of alcohol to gay patrons. The organizations, the protests, and the licensing change are documented. They cannot show that these groups represented all gay and lesbian people, and the chapter names the price of visibility: known members were watched by local police and by the FBI and the CIA.[^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-julius-gvshp]

Before the 1969 Stonewall uprising, which belongs to Chapter 42, no participant knew that it would become the movement’s emblem. The chapter does not read the later narrative back into 1965 and 1966. It presents the pickets and the sip-in as early, fragile, and consequential acts of public claim-making, not as the opening of a story whose end was already known.[^ch40-check-ch42-43][^ch40-check-scene]

## 4. Cities and fiscal pressures

### 4.1 The claim: urban policy and the cost of guns and butter

**The claim.** The Great Society expanded federal urban policy through the new Department of Housing and Urban Development and the Model Cities program, but by 1966–68 the cost of the Vietnam War, rising inflation, and the political price of open housing constrained what the programs could do.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-zeitz]

The cities were the place where the Great Society’s promise and its fiscal limits met. The federal government could create a department, authorize a program, and send money, but it could not control local housing markets, employment, or police practice. The constraint was both budgetary and electoral: the war consumed money and attention, inflation ate into the value of appropriations, and the fight over open housing damaged the coalition that had won in 1964. The statutes, the departments, the appropriations, and the surtax are documented. They cannot show that the war alone caused the programs’ limits, and the chapter gives the fiscal and political causes together.[^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 4.2 HUD and Robert C. Weaver

The Department of Housing and Urban Development was created in September 1965. In January 1966 Robert C. Weaver became its first secretary and the first African American to serve in a presidential cabinet. HUD coordinated slum clearance, public housing, and urban redevelopment, the programs that had grown up piecemeal since the New Deal. Weaver had administered the Housing and Home Finance Agency under Kennedy and had helped draft the administration’s housing and urban-renewal programs, including a $7.8 billion housing bill in 1965 that expanded public housing and created a rent-supplement program for low-income families.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

As secretary, Weaver promoted the Demonstration Cities and Metropolitan Development Act of 1966, which created the Model Cities program, and he defended urban renewal against its critics. In 1966 Senators Abraham Ribicoff and Robert F. Kennedy charged that federal urban renewal had failed to stop urban decay. The criticism was a live dispute, not a settled verdict. The department, the programs, and the criticism are documented. They do not show that HUD reduced urban poverty, and the agency’s achievements and failures remain a question that the program’s own records do not settle.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 The guns-and-butter squeeze

Johnson insisted that the country could afford both the Great Society and the Vietnam War, and for several years rapid growth sustained the claim. By 1968 inflation and the war’s costs had forced a temporary income tax surcharge, and the fiscal room for new domestic programs closed. Johnson signed the Revenue and Expenditure Control Act on June 28, 1968. The law enacted a temporary 10 percent income tax surcharge as Public Law 90-364, 82 Stat. 251.[^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

The surcharge was the moment the guns-and-butter bargain was openly acknowledged to be over. In his March 31, 1968, address, Johnson had called the fiscal situation “the sharpest financial threat in the postwar era” and asked Congress to pass the tax bill. Congress did so three months later, after Johnson had withdrawn from the presidential race. The statute and the surcharge are documented. They cannot isolate the war’s fiscal effect from the era’s broader inflation, and the chapter marks that attribution as an interpretation.[^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-withdrawal][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 In Focus: an early Medicare card

**In Focus.** The Medicare card issued to Harry S. Truman is a small rectangle of printed card. Its heading is “Health Insurance,” not “Medicare.” Beneath it, the card reads “SOCIAL SECURITY ACT.” The beneficiary’s name is typed in a field labeled “NAME OF BENEFICIARY,” and the “CLAIM NUMBER” has the format of a Social Security account number. Under the heading “IS ENTITLED TO,” it lists two entitlements, “Hospital Insurance” and “Medical Insurance,” each with the same effective date: 7-1-66. It does not mention the $3 monthly Part B premium, the deductibles, or the Communist-organization oath; those terms lived in the statute and application, not on the card. The card showed that the bearer was entitled, and its design placed that entitlement inside contributory social insurance rather than means-tested welfare.[^ch40-medicare-card-object][^ch40-medicare-cards]

The date is the tell. Johnson signed the law on July 30, 1965, and enrolled Truman as the first beneficiary that day; the card’s effective date is July 1, 1966, when coverage began. The object compresses four moments — the signing, the enrollment, the issuance of the card, and the start of coverage — into one small piece of paper. The library dates the cards to the signing; the Truman Library’s account of Johnson’s daily diary records that he hand-delivered them on January 20, 1966. Reading it means pulling those moments apart. It also means seeing the card as a fiscal document: the entitlement it proved would commit the government to pay hospitals and physicians for years.[^ch40-medicare-ceremony][^ch40-medicare-cards][^ch40-medicare-implementation][^ch40-check-focus]

The description rests on the digitized image and the object record; the original card is held by the LBJ Presidential Library. The box is a textual close reading, not a reproduction, and any future reproduction would require a registered image, an exact object locator, a caption, a source line, and a rights record.[^ch40-check-focus][^ch40-check-rights]

## 5. Backlash and the political transition

### 5.1 The claim: reform and reaction together

**The claim.** The Great Society and the civil rights statutes were met by a white political backlash that reshaped the parties, and by 1966 the Johnson legislative momentum had ended; the result was not the program’s repeal but the beginning of its containment and of a durable Republican resurgence.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-1966-retroreport]

The backlash was a political response to real changes in law and neighborhood life, not a simple verdict on individual voters’ motives. The chapter keeps the movement demands and the reaction in the same frame: the same years that produced open housing, school desegregation, and hospital integration also produced white resistance to those changes. The votes, the election results, and the defeated bills are documented. They cannot show that any one issue caused the shift, and the chapter does not treat an election map as proof of motive.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 Open housing and the white backlash

When the administration proposed a fair-housing provision in 1966, it met a backlash among white voters, particularly in northern communities that usually supported Democrats. Johnson had pressed for a bill to bar racial discrimination in housing sales and rentals. A *Time* correspondent told a White House aide that the “backlash” issue had overtaken Vietnam as the top concern for white voters in Indiana and Ohio. The bill died in Congress.[^ch40-zeitz]

The 1966 defeat was not the end of the issue. The Fair Housing Act passed in 1968, after Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination, and that statute belongs to Chapter 39. The chapter distinguishes the two moments: in 1966 the proposal failed because the political coalition for it was not strong enough; in 1968 it passed after King’s death, when the political and moral pressure made refusal costlier. The proposal, the defeat, and the reporting are documented. They cannot show how individual voters weighed neighborhood and race, and the backlash is a political fact with contested causes.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-ch39][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The 1966 midterms and the Republican resurgence

In the 1966 midterms the Republicans gained 47 House seats, three Senate seats, eight governorships, and 557 state legislative seats. They won a majority of the aggregated national Senate vote and made deep inroads in the South for the first time since Reconstruction. The gains halted Johnson’s legislative momentum and advanced a generation of Republican leaders. Ronald Reagan was elected governor of California in 1966 and became a national conservative figure. The former vice president Richard Nixon campaigned hard for Republican candidates and later called 1966 a crucial step toward the White House.[^ch40-midterm-ashbrook]

The 1966 elections accelerated a regional realignment that had begun with white southern defections from the Democrats in 1964. The realignment is best read as a process over several elections, not as a single referendum on the Great Society. Chapter 42 owns the 1968 election that completed the transition. The 1966 results are evidence of a political shift, not proof that every voter who chose a Republican in 1966 was voting against the Great Society.[^ch40-1966-retroreport][^ch40-check-ch42-43]

### 5.4 The long hot summers and the crises of 1968

The urban uprisings of 1964–68 made urban poverty and policing a national crisis. Federal troops were deployed in Los Angeles, Detroit, and Washington, D.C., between 1964 and 1968. Johnson appointed the National Advisory Commission on Civil Disorders, chaired by Governor Otto Kerner of Illinois, in July 1967. The Kerner Commission reported in March 1968 that the nation was “moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal,” and it blamed white institutions and white racism for the conditions that had produced the uprisings. Johnson largely rejected its findings.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-check-ch39]

By 1968, with his attention on Vietnam, Johnson’s efforts to extend the Great Society had ended. He told the historian Doris Kearns that he had sacrificed “the woman I really loved — the Great Society” to the war. On March 31, 1968, he announced that he would not seek, and would not accept, the nomination of his party for another term. The withdrawal did not end the domestic conflict; it transferred the argument to the presidential campaign, which Chapter 42 narrates. The chapter stops at the transition to the Nixon administration, not at the election’s result.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-withdrawal][^ch40-check-ch42-43]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The 89th Congress enacted a large and durable body of social legislation. The Economic Opportunity Act, Medicare and Medicaid, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, the Higher Education Act, and the Immigration and Nationality Act are documented.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-immigration-house]

Medicare and Medicaid became lasting parts of American health care. The statutes, the enrollment of nearly 20 million beneficiaries in the first three years, and the use of Title VI in hospital desegregation are documented.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-zeitz]

The Great Society reduced measured poverty while leaving it intact. Federal spending on programs targeted at the poor doubled between 1965 and 1968, and the official poverty rate fell from about 20 percent to about 12 percent between 1964 and 1974, but poverty persisted and became more concentrated.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-sugrue]

New organizing among women, consumers, environmentalists, and gay and lesbian people emerged in the same years. *The Feminine Mystique*, NOW, *Unsafe at Any Speed*, *Silent Spring*, the Wilderness Act, and the Mattachine and Daughters of Bilitis actions are documented.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-wilderness][^ch40-dob-loc]

The Great Society was met by a political backlash that reshaped the parties. The defeat of open housing in 1966, the Republican gains of 1966, and the fiscal squeeze of 1968 are documented.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-surcharge-state]

### 6.2 What is open

Did the Great Society complete the New Deal or fall short of it? Zeitz presents the programs as a moderate, largely effective completion of New Deal liberalism; Zelizer emphasizes the interaction of Johnson, Congress, and the movement. Quadagno, Matusow, and Sugrue emphasize the racial and structural limits that kept the welfare state two-tiered. The chapter states each at its strongest.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-zelizer][^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-matusow][^ch40-sugrue]

Was the main problem design or politics? Moynihan argued that the antipoverty programs failed because social-science theory was imposed without clear goals, and Marris and Rein traced the dilemmas of community-action reform. Other accounts stress that the programs were undermined by underfunding, federalism, and the war. Moynihan’s 1965 Labor Department report, *The Negro Family: The Case for National Action*, had already framed the debate over race, family structure, and poverty; its title used the period term for Black Americans, and the report was attacked for locating the problem in family structure rather than in jobs and discrimination. The chapter treats the report as a contested period document, not as verified social science.[^ch40-moynihan-max][^ch40-moynihan-report][^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-matusow]

How much did pressure from below shape the legislation? Zelizer’s account emphasizes the interaction of Johnson, Congress, and the civil rights movement. A president-centered account emphasizes Johnson’s skill and the 1964 landslide. The balance remains contested.[^ch40-zelizer][^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

Did the backlash explain the program’s limits, or did the program’s limits produce the backlash? Accounts of white backlash emphasize northern neighborhood politics and the open-housing fight. Fiscal and administrative accounts emphasize inflation, the war, and the two-tier design. The chapter holds both without collapsing one into the other.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-surcharge-state]

What should be counted as the Great Society’s measure? Supporters point to the poverty decline, the reduction of racial exclusion in southern schools and hospitals, and the durability of Medicare and Medicaid. Critics point to the persistence of poverty, the concentration of urban disadvantage, and the fiscal and political costs. The official poverty rate counts pretax cash income; when noncash benefits such as Medicaid and housing subsidies are included, the measured decline is larger. The chapter says what each measure can and cannot establish.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the Great Society delivered real and durable security — health coverage, school aid, and legal equality in federally funded institutions — while leaving poverty, racial hierarchy, and urban disadvantage intact. The reform’s limits came from design, federalism, race, and war together, not from any one cause.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-quadagno]

Contingency matters. The open-housing defeat of 1966, the surcharge of 1968, and the “maximum feasible participation” conflict were decisions people made and could have made otherwise. None was dictated by the outcome. The counterfactuals are not decorations; they identify where the evidence would have to be strongest to support a claim of inevitability.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

Structure and choice belong together. The racial order, federalism, the postwar boom, and the Cold War set the pressures; the choices made within them determined who got a hearing, who got a statute, and who got a hospital bed. A history that emphasizes only structure cannot explain why Medicare passed in 1965 rather than 1945. A history that emphasizes only choice cannot explain why so many of the choices pointed toward the same exclusions.[^ch40-check-scope][^ch40-zelizer]

The synthesis for this chapter is that between 1961 and 1968 the federal government built the broadest social provision since the New Deal and used it to enforce legal equality in federally funded institutions, and that the same years revealed how much of poverty, racial hierarchy, and urban disadvantage lay beyond the reach of the programs and how quickly the war and the backlash consumed the political room to extend them.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]

## Legacies

The years from 1961 to 1968 left the country a larger and more durable federal role in health, education, and income security. Medicare and Medicaid became permanent, school aid and federal enforcement of desegregation reshaped southern education, and the immigration act of 1965 remade the sources of American immigration. Those changes outlived the political coalition that produced them.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-immigration-house]

The years also left a two-tier welfare state and a politics of backlash. Quadagno argues that racism divided social provision into a universal social insurance tier and a stigmatized means-tested tier; Matusow and Sugrue connect liberal reform and urban racial conflict to the backlash that ended the reform era. The result was a welfare state that grew and a coalition that fractured at the same time.[^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-matusow][^ch40-sugrue]

The years left a memory and a dispute. The Great Society became, for its defenders, proof that government could expand opportunity and, for its critics, proof that it could not. Both verdicts are arguments to be tested against the record rather than settled conclusions. The programs’ durability is part of the evidence, and so is the persistence of the poverty they were meant to end.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

## Summary

- On July 30, 1965, Johnson signed the Social Security Amendments of 1965 at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, creating Medicare as Title XVIII and Medicaid as Title XIX; he enrolled Harry S. Truman as the first Medicare beneficiary, and the LBJ Library dates the first two cards to that day.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-medicare-ssa][^ch40-medicare-nara]
- Kennedy’s New Frontier proposed aid to education, medical care for the elderly, urban aid, and regional development but won only part of it; southern committee chairmen and the parochial-school dispute blocked the rest.[^ch40-jfk-miller]
- Johnson signed the Revenue Act of 1964 and the Civil Rights Act of 1964, declared an “unconditional war on poverty” in January 1964, and won a landslide over Barry Goldwater that brought 68 Democratic senators and 295 representatives.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]
- The Economic Opportunity Act of August 20, 1964, created the Office of Economic Opportunity under Sargent Shriver and launched the Job Corps, VISTA, and the Community Action Program with its requirement of “maximum feasible participation.”[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-eoa-lbj]
- The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 sent Title I funds to districts with concentrations of low-income students, and the Higher Education Act of 1965 expanded scholarships, work-study, and loans; by 1968 the share of Black students in southern majority-white schools had risen from about 2.3 percent to almost 23.4 percent.[^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-zeitz]
- The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, signed on October 3, abolished the national-origins quota system and set family reunification and skills as priorities; it passed the House 318 to 95 and was followed by a jump in annual immigration to nearly half a million.[^ch40-immigration-house]
- The Department of Housing and Urban Development was created in September 1965, and Robert C. Weaver became its first secretary and the first African American cabinet member in January 1966.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- Betty Friedan’s *The Feminine Mystique* appeared in 1963, and Friedan and others founded the National Organization for Women in 1966, whose first action was to demand that the EEOC enforce Title VII.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm]
- Ralph Nader’s *Unsafe at Any Speed* appeared in November 1965 and helped produce the 1966 auto-safety laws; Rachel Carson’s *Silent Spring* appeared in 1962, the Clean Air Act of 1963 and the Water Quality Act of 1965 expanded federal pollution control, and the Wilderness Act of 1964 protected 9.1 million acres.[^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-cleanair-epa][^ch40-wilderness]
- The Mattachine Society and the Daughters of Bilitis built the first sustained public gay and lesbian organizing; Kameny led the 1965 White House pickets, and the April 21, 1966, sip-in at Julius’ bar helped end New York’s ban on serving gay patrons.[^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-julius-gvshp]
- The open-housing bill died in 1966 after a white backlash, and the 1966 midterms brought Republican gains of 47 House seats, three Senate seats, and eight governorships; Johnson signed a temporary 10 percent income tax surcharge on June 28, 1968.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-surcharge-state]
- Federal troops were deployed in Los Angeles, Detroit, and Washington between 1964 and 1968; the Kerner Commission reported in March 1968 that the nation was “moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal,” and Johnson announced on March 31, 1968, that he would not seek reelection.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-withdrawal]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1961 and 1968 the federal government built the broadest system of social provision since the New Deal and used it to enforce legal equality in federally funded schools and hospitals. The system was real, and so were its limits: poverty persisted, the welfare state was divided by race, urban disadvantage deepened, and the war and the backlash consumed the political room to extend it. The chapter’s question — what could federal rights and social programs achieve, and what constrained them? — is answered by that double record of durability and constraint.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-zeitz]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on August 4, 1964 — earlier than the signing in Independence — with the naval reports from the Gulf of Tonkin and the resolution that followed, and it takes up the Vietnam War and the global Cold War. Chapter 41 follows containment into the client state, the escalation, the draft, and the crises of 1968, and it shows how the same years that produced the Great Society also produced the war that helped constrain it. The domestic liberal state and the global garrison state grew together, and the next chapter turns to the second of them.[^ch40-check-ch41][^ch40-check-bridge][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
