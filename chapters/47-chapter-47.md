## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date and the occasion.** On November 4, 2008, Barack Obama was elected the 44th president of the United States, winning 365 electoral votes to John McCain’s 173 and 69,498,516 popular votes to McCain’s 59,948,323; he addressed supporters that night in Grant Park in his home city of Chicago.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-obama-grant-park-2008]
- **The vote itself.** Turnout was 131,313,820 votes cast for president, or 58.23 percent of a voting-age population the Census Bureau estimated at 225,499,000. Obama took 52.93 percent to McCain’s 45.65 percent.[^ch47-fec-2008]
- **The speech.** In the prepared remarks, Obama framed the result as an answer to doubt about American possibility, described “lines that stretched around schools and churches in numbers this nation has never seen,” and told the story of Ann Nixon Cooper, “106 years old,” born “just a generation past slavery.” He warned that “the road ahead will be long. Our climb will be steep.”[^ch47-obama-grant-park-2008]
- **The coalition.** Pew’s analysis of the National Election Pool exit polls found that Obama drew 66 percent of voters under 30, 66 percent of Hispanic voters, and 95 percent of Black voters, while taking 43 percent of the White vote; the electorate that year was 39 percent Democratic and 32 percent Republican, a shift from an evenly divided 2004 electorate.[^ch47-pew-2008-coalition]

- **What the record can show.** It can show the margins, the turnout, the composition reported by the exit polls, and the words of the address. ## 1. The election and coalition (2008–2010)

### 1.1 The claim: a decisive victory that did not settle the country

- **The claim.** The 2008 election gave Obama a clear popular and electoral majority and assembled a coalition of young, minority, urban, and suburban voters; within two years that coalition lost the House and the president’s party suffered its largest midterm defeat in decades. The chapter should treat the victory and the backlash as two parts of one political story, not as a triumph followed by an unrelated reversal.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-pew-2008-coalition][^ch47-fec-2010]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the returns and the coalition through the composition of the electorate, the early relationship with Congress, and the 2010 midterm repudiation, with Map 38.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-check-map38]

### 1.2 The returns and the coalition

- **The returns.** Obama won 365 electoral votes to McCain’s 173, and 69,498,516 votes (52.93 percent) to McCain’s 59,948,323 (45.65 percent); total presidential votes were 131,313,820, or 58.23 percent of the estimated voting-age population.[^ch47-fec-2008]
- **The composition.** Pew’s analysis found Obama took 66 percent of voters under 30, 66 percent of Hispanic voters, 95 percent of Black voters, and 43 percent of White voters; the electorate was 39 percent Democratic and 32 percent Republican.[^ch47-pew-2008-coalition]
- **The caution.** Exit polls are estimates with sampling error and demographic definitions that changed over time; the chapter should present them as the best contemporaneous portrait, not as a census, and reconcile their categories with the actual returns.[^ch47-pew-2008-coalition][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Governing against a filibuster

- **The majority.** Democrats entered 2009 with unified control of the presidency and both chambers, but the Senate’s 60-vote threshold for cloture meant that ordinary legislation usually required a supermajority, and the party’s caucus was itself divided between progressives and moderates.[^ch47-check-sources]
- **The constraint.** The chapter should explain the procedural constraint on the 111th Congress from the Senate’s rules and the actual cloture votes rather than treating “gridlock” as a mood; it should distinguish what the majority chose not to attempt from what the rules prevented.[^ch47-check-sources][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The 2010 backlash

- **The result.** In the November 2, 2010, midterm elections, Republicans gained 63 House seats to win a majority — a swing larger than any since 1948 — and gained six Senate seats while Democrats narrowly retained the Senate; the 112th Congress convened with 242 Republicans and 193 Democrats in the House and a 51–47–2 Democratic-held Senate. Republicans won control of the Senate in the 2014 midterms.[^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-fec-2014]
- **The reading.** The president himself called the result a “shellacking,” and the chapter should quote the press conference from the record; the defeat ended the legislative window for the administration’s domestic program.[^ch47-check-sources][^ch47-fec-2010]
- **The caution.** The swing was not a single movement’s work; it combined the economy, the health-reform fight, and the mobilization of the Tea Party with ordinary midterm losses by the president’s party. The chapter should name each factor and not attribute the whole to one.[^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 Map 38: the changing electoral geography

- **Map 38.** The registered visual for this chapter is `ch47-map-01` (Map 38, “Electoral geography, 2000–2024,” panel through 2016). It must use official election returns matched to census geography, state its years, geographic units, and normalization (percentage points or vote share), and distinguish measured change in the partisan vote from any inference about voter motives. It must not let the 2020 or 2024 panels leak into the 2000–2016 narrative, and it must not read a causal story off the map.[^ch47-check-map38]
- **The measure.** The section should use the map to show that the coalition that elected Obama in 2008 was geographically distinct and did not simply persist; the map is a description of changing margins, not proof that any group changed its mind.[^ch47-check-map38][^ch47-pew-2008-coalition]

## 2. Stimulus, autos, Dodd-Frank, Occupy, and the Tea Party (2009–2011)

### 2.1 The claim: a recovery program that was real, contested, and incomplete

- **The claim.** The administration and the 111th Congress responded to the financial emergency with a large fiscal stimulus, a rescue of the automobile industry, and a rewrite of financial regulation; the measures limited the damage and left a recovery that was slower and less equally shared than its authors promised, while the Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street organized opposite complaints about who the government had saved.[^ch47-arra-2009][^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Recovery Act through the auto rescue and Dodd-Frank to the two movements and the measured recovery, with the unemployment series in the foreground.[^ch47-crs-arra-2009][^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009]

### 2.2 The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act

- **The statute.** The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 became Public Law 111-5 on February 17, 2009, making supplemental appropriations “for job preservation and creation, infrastructure investment, energy efficiency and science, assistance to the unemployed, and State and local fiscal stabilization.”[^ch47-arra-2009]
- **The size.** Congressional Research Service analysis describes a conference agreement of roughly $787 billion — about $308 billion in discretionary outlay increases, $267 billion in mandatory outlay increases, and $211 billion in revenue reductions — below the House version’s nearly $820 billion and the Senate version’s roughly $838 billion.[^ch47-crs-arra-2009]
- **The effect.** The administration and outside economists argued the act raised output and employment relative to a no-stimulus baseline, while critics argued it was too small, too slow, and poorly targeted; the chapter should present the range of estimates, their methods, and their uncertainty rather than a single multiplier.[^ch47-cbo-arra-impact][^ch47-check-numbers][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The counterfactual cannot be observed; statements about jobs “saved or created” are model-based estimates, not counts, and the chapter must say so.[^ch47-cbo-arra-impact][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 2.3 The rescue of the automobile industry

- **The intervention.** In 2009 the federal government, through the Treasury and a presidential task force, provided funds to General Motors and Chrysler as they restructured in bankruptcy; the Treasury later reported that it recovered about $70.4 billion of the $79.7 billion it committed to the automakers and their financing arms, a net loss of roughly $9.3 billion.[^ch47-treasury-auto]
- **The stakes.** Supporters credit the intervention with saving hundreds of thousands of jobs and a domestic industrial base; critics call it an improper use of public money that favored union workers and creditors unequally. The chapter should give the amounts, the recovered share, and the employment claims with their sources and methods.[^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** The net-loss figure and the recovered-share figures depend on how “recovered” and the programs’ boundaries are defined; the chapter should give the definitions and avoid presenting a contested accounting as settled.[^ch47-treasury-auto][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 2.4 The Dodd-Frank Act

- **The statute.** The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act became Public Law 111-203 on July 21, 2010, stating its purposes as promoting financial stability, ending “too big to fail,” protecting taxpayers by ending bailouts, and protecting consumers from abusive practices.[^ch47-dodd-frank-2010]
- **The architecture.** The act created the Financial Stability Oversight Council, a consumer financial protection bureau, and a resolution regime for large failing firms, and imposed new capital, derivatives, and mortgage rules; the chapter should describe the provisions from the statute and note that much of the act’s detail was left to rulemaking.[^ch47-dodd-frank-2010]
- **The debate.** Supporters argue the act reduced the risk of another systemic collapse; critics argue it imposed costs on smaller institutions and left the largest firms advantaged. The chapter should present both and use the rulemaking and later evidence, not assertion.[^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 2.5 The Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street

- **The Tea Party.** The movement took its name and energy from broadcasts and protests in 2009, including Rick Santelli’s February 19, 2009, remarks on CNBC and the April 15, 2009, tax-day rallies held in hundreds of locations; Skocpol and Williamson argue that it was largely a reaction by older, middle-class conservatives to the recession, the stimulus, and the health law, and that it reshaped Republican primaries and pulled the party rightward.[^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012]
- **Occupy Wall Street.** Beginning on September 17, 2011, protesters occupied Zuccotti Park in lower Manhattan and spread the language of “the 99 percent” and “the 1 percent”; the movement had no single set of demands and was cleared from the park that November.[^ch47-check-sources]
- **The symmetry and the difference.** Both movements rejected the government’s handling of the crisis, from opposite directions; the chapter should mark the different objects of their anger — bank bailouts and deficits on the right, bank power and inequality on the left — and not flatten them into one “populist moment.”[^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** Crowd sizes at the 2009 rallies and the 2011 occupations are contested estimates; the chapter should give any figure with its source or describe the events without a precise count.[^ch47-check-numbers][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012]

### 2.6 The measure: a recovery that lagged

- **The series.** The unemployment rate peaked at 10.2 percent in October 2009, the highest since 1983, and remained near or above 8 percent for years; the chapter should give the series with its definitions, seasonality, and source.[^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009]
- **The distribution.** Census data show that real median household income did not regain its 2007 level during the Obama years: the 2015 median of $56,516 was the first annual increase since 2007 and remained 1.6 percent below the 2007 figure in real terms. The chapter should use such series to distinguish aggregate recovery from household experience.[^ch47-census-income-2016]
- **The stakes for §2.** The section’s question is whether the response was proportionate to the emergency and who bore its costs and gains; the chapter should hold the aggregate recovery and its uneven distribution together.[^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009][^ch47-census-income-2016]

## 3. Health reform (2009–2015)

### 3.1 The claim: the largest coverage expansion in decades, carried by one party and fought over for years

- **The claim.** The Affordable Care Act expanded health-insurance coverage through subsidies, an individual requirement to hold insurance, insurance-market rules, and a Medicaid expansion; it reduced the number of uninsured Americans substantially while remaining a partisan fight that moved through Congress, the courts, and the states.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the legislative fight through the design of the law, the In Focus close reading of the “death panel” claim, the litigation, and the coverage results.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-nfib-2012][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]

### 3.2 Making the law

- **The statute.** The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act became Public Law 111-148 on March 23, 2010; a companion reconciliation act, Public Law 111-152, made further changes on March 30, 2010. The chapter should distinguish the two and give the dates precisely.[^ch47-aca-2010]
- **The process.** The law passed without a single Republican vote after a year of negotiation, a special Senate election in Massachusetts in January 2010, and the use of budget reconciliation; the chapter should describe the path from the record and not reduce it to a slogan.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **The design.** The act’s individual requirement, subsidies, guaranteed-issue and community-rating rules, essential-benefit standard, Medicaid expansion, and state exchanges are the machinery of the law; the chapter should describe each from the text.[^ch47-aca-2010]

### 3.3 The In Focus: the “death panel” post

- **The object.** In Focus reads Sarah Palin’s August 7, 2009, Facebook note, in which she wrote that “The America I know and love is not one in which my parents or my baby with Down Syndrome will have to stand in front of Obama’s ‘death panel’ so his bureaucrats can decide, based on a subjective judgment of their ‘level of productivity in society,’ whether they are worthy of health care.”[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009]
- **What the record shows.** FactCheck.org and PolitiFact found that the phrase does not appear in the House bill and that the provision Palin opposed — Section 1233 of H.R. 3200, “Advance Care Planning Consultation” — would have had Medicare pay for voluntary end-of-life counseling; the Congressional Budget Office estimated that provision would cost a net $2.7 billion over 10 years.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009]
- **What the close reading does.** The box should read the post as evidence of how a political claim about a statutory provision moved through social media, not as evidence that death panels existed; it should quote Palin exactly, identify the provision she cited, and separate the claim from the bill’s text.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-check-focus]
- **The caution.** The post is a primary source for a movement’s rhetoric and framing; the chapter must not repeat its factual claim as fact, and should note that Palin published a longer follow-up on August 12, 2009, and that the end-of-life provision was dropped from the final law.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009]

### 3.4 The courts

- **The mandate upheld.** In *National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius*, decided June 28, 2012, the Supreme Court upheld the individual requirement as a valid exercise of Congress’s taxing power while holding that the Medicaid expansion exceeded Congress’s spending power as written and was therefore optional for the states; the chapter should give the holding and the vote and distinguish the two rulings.[^ch47-nfib-2012]
- **The subsidies upheld.** In *King v. Burwell*, decided June 25, 2015, the Court held that tax credits were available on federally run exchanges as well as state-run ones; the chapter should give the holding from the opinion.[^ch47-king-burwell-2015]
- **The stakes.** The litigation did not overturn the law but changed its reach, especially by making the Medicaid expansion a state choice; the chapter should connect the rulings to the actual geography of coverage.[^ch47-nfib-2012][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]

### 3.5 The coverage result

- **The number.** Department of Health and Human Services analysis found the number of uninsured nonelderly Americans fell from about 48 million in 2010 to about 28 million in 2016, before rising again; the uninsured rate reached a historic low in 2016. The chapter should give the series, its survey source, and its definitions.[^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]
- **The caution.** The estimate depends on the survey and the year, and “coverage” is not the same as access, affordability, or health; the chapter should distinguish them and not overstate the law’s effects.[^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021][^ch47-check-numbers]

## 4. Afghanistan, drones, the Arab Spring, Syria, Iran, Paris, and Asia policy (2009–2016)

### 4.1 The claim: a foreign policy of limited wars, strikes, and agreements

- **The claim.** The administration narrowed the war on terror’s stated aims while expanding its instruments — a troop increase in Afghanistan, a drone and targeted-killing program, and a set of diplomatic agreements from the Iran nuclear deal to the Paris climate accord — and it promised a “rebalance” toward Asia; the record combines genuine achievements, unresolved wars, and costs that fell on people far from Washington.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015][^ch47-crs-asia-rebalance-2012]
- **The structure.** The section runs from Afghanistan and the 2009 troop increase through the drone program, the Arab Spring and Libya, the Benghazi attack, the Syrian chemical-weapons framework, the Iran and Paris agreements, and the Asia rebalance.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-awlaki-2011][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015]

### 4.2 Afghanistan and the 2009 troop increase

- **The decision.** In his December 1, 2009, address at West Point, Obama said he had “determined that it is in our vital national interest to send an additional 30,000 U.S. troops to Afghanistan,” announced that “after 18 months, our troops will begin to come home,” and set a goal to “disrupt, dismantle, and defeat al Qaeda in Afghanistan and Pakistan.”[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009]
- **The context.** He described Afghanistan as having “moved backwards,” cited corruption, the drug trade, and a resurgent Taliban, and tied the effort to Pakistan; the chapter should use the address for the administration’s own framing and supplement it with the war’s later course.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009]
- **The caution.** The speech is the president’s case, not a neutral account; the chapter should give the troop figures and the drawdown dates with their sources and mark the counterfactual about whether the surge could have succeeded.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Drones and targeted killing

- **The program.** The administration expanded the use of armed drones and “targeted killing” outside declared battlefields, including the September 30, 2011, strike in Yemen that killed the American-born cleric Anwar al-Awlaki, the first United States citizen knowingly targeted and killed by a drone strike; the chapter should describe the program from documented strikes and official statements.[^ch47-awlaki-2011]
- **The controversy.** Supporters argued the strikes were lawful self-defense against an enemy that could not be reached by other means; critics argued they bypassed due process and courts, killed bystanders, and set a precedent for killing citizens without trial. The chapter should present both at their strongest.[^ch47-awlaki-2011][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **The silences.** The record of a drone strike includes the target and the official rationale and often not the names, ages, or number of the dead; the chapter should name that asymmetry and use independent estimates with their methods.[^ch47-awlaki-2011][^ch47-check-silences][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 4.4 The Arab Spring, Libya, and Benghazi

- **The uprisings.** In 2011 mass protests toppled longstanding governments across the Middle East and North Africa, including in Tunisia and Egypt; the chapter should give each country’s dates and outcomes and not treat “the Arab Spring” as one event.[^ch47-check-sources]
- **Libya.** The United States joined a NATO-led intervention under United Nations Security Council Resolution 1973 in March 2011 that helped remove Muammar Qaddafi and left a fractured country; the chapter should give the mandate, the participants, and the aftermath.[^ch47-check-sources]
- **Benghazi.** On September 11–12, 2012, attackers killed four Americans, including Ambassador J. Christopher Stevens, at the United States facilities in Benghazi, Libya; an independent Accountability Review Board examined the attack and the security failures around it, and the episode became the subject of prolonged congressional inquiry.[^ch47-benghazi-arb]
- **The caution.** The Benghazi investigations produced a large, partly contested record with political stakes; the chapter should rely on the Accountability Review Board and the official inquiries and distinguish their findings from the political claims made about them.[^ch47-benghazi-arb][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 Syria and the chemical-weapons framework

- **The red line and the framework.** After a chemical-weapons attack in Syria in August 2013, the United States and Russia agreed on a framework, announced September 14, 2013, for the elimination of Syria’s chemical weapons under the Chemical Weapons Convention; the chapter should give the framework’s terms and the role of the Organization for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons.[^ch47-syria-framework-2013]
- **The stakes.** The episode tested the administration’s credibility after its “red line” warning while avoiding a military strike; later chemical attacks complicated the record. The chapter should present the sequence and mark what remains contested.[^ch47-syria-framework-2013][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.6 The Iran nuclear agreement

- **The agreement.** On July 14, 2015, the P5+1, the European Union, and Iran reached the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action to ensure that Iran’s nuclear program would be exclusively peaceful; the State Department describes Adoption Day as October 18, 2015, and Implementation Day as January 16, 2016, when the IAEA verified Iran’s implementation and nuclear-related sanctions were lifted.[^ch47-jcpoa-2015]
- **The debate.** Supporters argued the agreement verifiably limited Iran’s program without war; opponents argued it granted relief while leaving Iran a path to a weapon. The chapter should present both and cite the verification record.[^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The agreement’s later status — United States withdrawal in 2018 — belongs to Chapter 48 or later; this chapter describes the 2015–16 agreement and the verification, not the aftermath.[^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-check-ch48]

### 4.7 Paris and the climate framework

- **The agreement.** The Paris Agreement was adopted by 195 Parties at the UN Climate Change Conference in Paris on December 12, 2015, and entered into force on November 4, 2016; its goal is to hold the global average temperature increase “to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels” and to pursue efforts “to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels.”[^ch47-paris-2015]
- **The stakes.** The agreement rested on voluntary national pledges rather than binding emissions targets, and the United States participated through executive action rather than a ratified treaty; the chapter should describe the mechanism and the limits.[^ch47-paris-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 4.8 The rebalance toward Asia

- **The policy.** In the fall of 2011 and early 2012, the administration announced that it would intensify its role in the Asia-Pacific region across diplomacy, economics, security, human rights, and the environment — the “rebalance” or “pivot”; the chapter should describe the announced policy and the resources attached to it.[^ch47-crs-asia-rebalance-2012]
- **The caution.** “Pivot” and “rebalance” are contested labels; the chapter should give the announced measures and distinguish them from claims about their effects.[^ch47-crs-asia-rebalance-2012][^ch47-check-names]

## 5. Marriage, immigration, race, protest, and media (2012–2016)

### 5.1 The claim: rights expanded while the country polarized

- **The claim.** In the administration’s second term, the Supreme Court extended marriage equality nationwide and struck down part of the Voting Rights Act, the president created a deferred-action program for undocumented young people, and the killing of Black Americans by police and vigilantes produced the Black Lives Matter movement — all while Congress failed to pass gun legislation after a school massacre and money and social media reshaped the public sphere.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-shelby-2013][^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]
- **The structure.** The section runs from marriage and voting rights through immigration, Ferguson and Black Lives Matter, the gun debate, and the media and money environment, ending on the distribution of the recovery.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015][^ch47-citizens-united-2010]

### 5.2 Marriage equality

- **The decision.** In *Obergefell v. Hodges*, decided June 26, 2015, the Supreme Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognize same-sex marriages, writing that the petitioners “ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. The Constitution grants them that right”; the chapter should give the holding and the vote.[^ch47-obergefell-2015]
- **The path.** The decision followed the Court’s 2013 ruling in *United States v. Windsor* and a shift in public opinion and state law; the chapter should describe the sequence and the dissent’s arguments fairly.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 Immigration and DACA

- **The memorandum.** On June 15, 2012, Secretary of Homeland Security Janet Napolitano issued a memorandum setting out how the department would exercise prosecutorial discretion toward certain young people brought to the United States as children, creating what became the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals program.[^ch47-daca-2012]
- **The criteria.** The memorandum listed criteria including arrival before age 16, continuous residence for at least five years, being in school or having graduated or served honorably in the military, and not having been convicted of specified offenses; the chapter should give the criteria from the memorandum.[^ch47-daca-2012]
- **The caution.** DACA granted deferred action and work authorization, not legal status or citizenship; the chapter should distinguish the program’s protections from the comprehensive reform that Congress did not pass, and note the program’s contested legal footing.[^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-check-numbers]

### 5.4 Ferguson, Black Lives Matter, and racialized reaction

- **The killing and the protests.** Michael Brown, an 18-year-old Black man, was shot and killed by Ferguson, Missouri, police officer Darren Wilson on August 9, 2014, setting off protests; the Department of Justice’s investigation of the shooting concluded in March 2015 that the evidence did not support federal criminal charges against Wilson.[^ch47-doj-brown-2015]
- **The police investigation.** A separate Department of Justice investigation found “a pattern or practice of unlawful conduct within the Ferguson Police Department” that violated the First, Fourth, and Fourteenth Amendments, shaped by the city’s “focus on revenue rather than by public safety needs.”[^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]
- **The movement.** Three Black organizers — Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi — created the #BlackLivesMatter project in 2013 in response to the acquittal of George Zimmerman in the killing of Trayvon Martin; the movement grew after Ferguson and spread nationally.[^ch47-blm-about][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]
- **The interpretation.** Michael Tesler argues that the Obama years saw White political attitudes become more polarized by racial considerations, not less; the chapter should present the racialization thesis alongside the movement’s own account of persistent structural racism, and test both against the record.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-taylor-2016][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** Exit polls and attitude surveys are estimates; the chapter should not treat a survey trend as proof of individual motives, and should name the silences around the victims and communities the data do not capture.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-check-silences]

### 5.5 Sandy Hook and the limits of legislation

- **The shooting.** On December 14, 2012, a gunman killed 20 children and six adults at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut; the state’s report documents the attack and the shooter’s preparation.[^ch47-sandy-hook-2012]
- **The failure.** A bipartisan background-check bill failed in the Senate in April 2013 despite the shooting, the chapter should give the vote and the reasons recorded, and connect the failure to the era’s pattern of mobilized opposition.[^ch47-sandy-hook-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 5.6 Money, media, and the public sphere

- **The money.** In *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, decided January 21, 2010, the Supreme Court held that limiting independent political expenditures by corporations, unions, and other collective entities violates the First Amendment; the chapter should give the holding and note the subsequent growth of outside spending.[^ch47-citizens-united-2010]
- **The media.** Social media, which had helped organize the Obama campaign and the movements of the period, also accelerated rumor and partisan information — the “death panel” post is one instance; the chapter should connect the earlier In Focus to the section’s argument about the public sphere.[^ch47-palin-death-panel-2009][^ch47-citizens-united-2010]

### 5.7 The measure: recovery, inequality, and the 2016 background

- **The distribution.** The chapter should assemble the income, coverage, unemployment, and wealth series to show how unevenly the recovery was shared, using Census, Bureau of Labor Statistics, and Federal Reserve sources with their definitions.[^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-bls-unemployment-2009][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021]
- **The background to 2016.** The slow recovery, stagnant wages, and regional divergence are part of the policy background that Chapter 48 takes up as the 2016 campaign; this chapter names them and does not narrate the campaign.[^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-check-ch48]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The election and the backlash.** Obama won 365 electoral votes and 52.93 percent of the popular vote in 2008; Republicans gained 63 House seats in 2010 and won control of the Senate in 2014.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-fec-2014]
- **The statutes.** The Recovery Act became Public Law 111-5 on February 17, 2009; the Affordable Care Act became Public Law 111-148 on March 23, 2010; the Dodd-Frank Act became Public Law 111-203 on July 21, 2010.[^ch47-arra-2009][^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010]
- **The decisions.** The Supreme Court upheld the individual mandate in *NFIB v. Sebelius* on June 28, 2012, and the exchange subsidies in *King v. Burwell* on June 25, 2015; it required states to license same-sex marriages in *Obergefell v. Hodges* on June 26, 2015, and struck down the Voting Rights Act coverage formula in *Shelby County v. Holder* on June 25, 2013.[^ch47-nfib-2012][^ch47-king-burwell-2015][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-shelby-2013]
- **The foreign-policy record.** The December 1, 2009, troop increase, the July 14, 2015, Iran agreement, and the December 12, 2015, Paris Agreement are documented; the Benghazi Accountability Review Board examined the September 2012 attack.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015][^ch47-benghazi-arb]
- **The rights and protest record.** DACA was created on June 15, 2012; the Department of Justice found a pattern or practice of unconstitutional policing in Ferguson; Black Lives Matter began in 2013.[^ch47-daca-2012][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015][^ch47-blm-about]
- **The coverage and income results.** The uninsured fell from about 48 million in 2010 to about 28 million in 2016; real median household income did not regain its 2007 level during the period.[^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021][^ch47-census-income-2016]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the recovery program proportionate and effective?** One account holds that the stimulus, the auto rescue, and the financial reform prevented a second Depression; another holds that the response was too small, too slow, and too kind to banks, and that the recovery’s slowness was itself a policy choice. The chapter should present each at its strongest and test it against the series.[^ch47-cbo-arra-impact][^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **How much was structural constraint and how much was choice?** The Senate’s supermajority rule, the opposition, and the divided party limited what was possible; but the administration also chose its priorities and compromises. The chapter should separate what the rules prevented from what the government chose not to attempt.[^ch47-check-sources][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **Was the era defined by racial reaction?** Tesler argues that racial attitudes became more central to White politics; others stress economic anxiety, party identity, and media. The chapter should compare the accounts and their evidence and not present one as settled.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **Did movements from below or decisions from above drive the rights gains?** One account credits litigation and presidential action; another credits the movements that forced the issues. The chapter should state each and use the record of both.[^ch47-taylor-2016][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **Was “post-racial” a promise, a hope, or a myth?** The chapter should treat the term as a contested period label and test it against both the coalition evidence and the racialization literature.[^ch47-tesler-2016][^ch47-pew-2008-coalition][^ch47-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the Obama years produced real and durable changes — a coverage expansion, a financial-reform statute, rights decisions, and international agreements — whose reach and cost were contested, and in which recovery and rights gains coexisted with an organized opposition, racialized reaction, and an unevenly shared economy.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-census-income-2016]
- **Contingency.** The stimulus’s size, the health law’s design, the 2010 midterm losses, the failure of the 2013 gun bill, and the Iran and Paris agreements were all contingent; the chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch47-crs-arra-2009][^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-fec-2010][^ch47-check-debate-works]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter should hold the structural conditions — the financial emergency, the Senate’s rules, the economy’s distribution, and the media environment — together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation.[^ch47-check-scope][^ch47-census-income-2016]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Between 2008 and 2017 the United States elected its first Black president, passed a major economic recovery program and a major health-coverage expansion, rescued its automobile industry and rewrote financial regulation, fought an unfinished war and a covert strike campaign, reached agreements on Iran’s nuclear program and the global climate, extended marriage equality, and saw the rise of a movement against police violence — all while the recovery was unevenly shared and the political system grew more polarized; the chapter’s question — why renewal and recovery coexisted with polarization — is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch47-fec-2008][^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

## Legacies

The recovery program and the health law left institutions and expectations that outlasted the administration that built them. The Affordable Care Act’s coverage expansion, the consumer-protection and resolution machinery of Dodd-Frank, and the precedent of a large countercyclical fiscal response became durable features of American government that later administrations would defend, revise, or attack, and the question of how much government should do in an emergency remained central to American politics.[^ch47-aca-2010][^ch47-dodd-frank-2010][^ch47-arra-2009]

The rights decisions and the movements that pressed them changed the country and its conflicts. Marriage equality, the debate over the Voting Rights Act after *Shelby County*, the deferred-action program, and the rise of Black Lives Matter fixed terms that later politics took up; the chapter should trace these to their sources rather than present them as a settled verdict.[^ch47-obergefell-2015][^ch47-shelby-2013][^ch47-doj-ferguson-2015]

And the era’s polarization became the ground on which the next election was fought. The uneven recovery, the organized opposition, the racialized reaction, and the changed media environment set terms that Chapter 48 takes up as it examines the 2016 campaign and the first Trump presidency.[^ch47-census-income-2016][^ch47-skocpol-williamson-2012][^ch47-check-ch48]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The era’s outcomes were made in Kabul, Cairo, Benghazi, Tehran, Paris, and the global credit and oil markets as well as in Washington. The wars, the strikes, the agreements, and the rebalance all show how international decisions bounded American choices; the chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch47-obama-westpoint-2009][^ch47-jcpoa-2015][^ch47-paris-2015]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, officials, and journalists and under-represents the uninsured, the undocumented, the deportees, the drone-strike victims and their families, the imprisoned, and the poor. The chapter should name these asymmetries in the framing paragraph and here rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch47-check-silences][^ch47-aspe-uninsured-2021][^ch47-awlaki-2011]
