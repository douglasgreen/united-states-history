# Chapter 9. The Crisis of the British Empire (1763–1770)

> “Why does not this imaginary representation extend to America, as well as over the whole island of Great-Britain? If it can travel three hundred miles, why not three thousand? if it can jump over rivers and mountains, why cannot it sail over the ocean?”
>
> — Soame Jenyns, *The Objections to the Taxation of our American Colonies by the Legislature of Great Britain, briefly consider’d* (London, 1765), paragraph beginning “Why does not this imaginary representation”[^ch09-jenyns-objections]

> “That it is inseparably essential to the freedom of a people, and the undoubted rights of Englishmen, that no taxes should be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.”
>
> — The Declaration of Rights and Grievances of the Stamp Act Congress, October 19, 1765, third resolve[^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]

> “Opposition thought, in the form it acquired at the turn of the seventeenth century and in the early eighteenth century, was devoured by the colonists. From the earliest years of the century it nourished their political thought and sensibilities.”
>
> — Bernard Bailyn, *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1967), as quoted in the Pulitzer Prize Board’s spotlight on the book[^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer]

The three quotations disagree about the grounds of political authority and about how to explain the conflict. Jenyns, a member of Parliament and of the Board of Trade, restates the theory of virtual representation: the colonists are represented in Parliament as the unrepresented towns of England are, so Parliament may tax them. The Stamp Act Congress answers that consent must be given personally or by representatives the taxed choose. Bailyn steps back and argues that the colonists argued from a long-absorbed opposition ideology, not from sudden grievance. Together they frame the chapter’s question: how did imperial reform become a crisis of authority?[^ch09-check-epigraphs]

In the early hours of August 14, 1765, someone hanged an effigy from the great elm at the corner of Orange and Essex Streets in Boston’s South End. The figure represented Andrew Oliver, the appointed distributor of stamps for Massachusetts and the brother-in-law of Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson. The letters “A O” were marked on it. The accounts differ on details, including whether more than one figure hung there. By daylight the news had spread through the town.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]

Hutchinson, who was also chief justice, ordered Sheriff Stephen Greenleaf to cut the figure down. The sheriff declined to attempt it. Hutchinson later wrote that “such a spirit then appeared that he durst not attempt [it].”[^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]

The effigy stayed up all day. By nightfall a crowd took it down and carried it through the streets. The procession stopped at Oliver’s dock, where a building rumored to be intended for the stamp office was pulled down; Hutchinson described “two small shops.” The crowd went on to Oliver’s house. Oliver’s family escaped minutes before the crowd arrived.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]

Hutchinson followed with the sheriff and the marshal of the admiralty, but the crowd drove them off, and Hutchinson was bruised. The effigy was burned outside the house. The next day Oliver resigned the distributorship.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]

John Adams, then 30, wrote in his diary on August 15 that the destruction was “a very attrocious [sic] Violation of the Peace and of dangerous Tendency and Consequence,” even as he criticized the concentration of offices in the Oliver and Hutchinson families. The protest was the first of two that month. On August 26 a crowd destroyed Hutchinson’s mansion.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]

The question was not simply whether Parliament had authority in America. It was how that authority would be understood, by whom, and at what cost. How did imperial reform become a crisis of authority?[^ch09-check-scene][^ch09-check-ch8]

This chapter follows the conflict between Parliament and the colonies from the settlement of 1763 to the British withdrawal from Boston and the partial repeal of 1770, the years in which imperial reform became a crisis of authority. It draws on the statutes themselves, the resolutions and declarations of colonial assemblies, merchants’ agreements, newspapers, diaries, letters, and the records of the Boston Massacre trials. It treats “representation,” “sovereignty,” “internal” and “external” taxes, and “virtual representation” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter must establish. It uses historical scholarship for what the records cannot show and names the silences left by archives that recorded crowds and enslaved and Indigenous people mainly through the complaints of officials. It does not present independence as the foreseeable or intended end of the crisis.[^ch09-check-scope][^ch09-check-names]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the war debt and the Grenville program of reform. §2 turns to the Stamp Act, the schedule of duties, the Virginia Resolves, and the Stamp Act Congress. §3 follows the repeal of 1766 and the Declaratory Act’s claim to bind the colonies in all cases whatsoever. §4 explains the Townshend Revenue Act, the Massachusetts Circular Letter, and non-importation. §5 weighs the occupation of Boston and the Boston Massacre of March 5, 1770. §6 asks how historians explain the crisis and what the arguments of the 1760s settled and left open. The chapter begins in Boston, where a crowd hung an effigy of a stamp distributor from an elm and then burned it outside his house.

## 1. Debt and reform

### 1.1 The war debt and the case for colonial revenue

The crisis began as a fiscal problem. Victory in the Seven Years’ War left Britain with an enlarged empire and a debt that made colonial revenue attractive, and the peacetime army the empire kept in America gave the argument a standing rationale.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

Britain’s national debt roughly doubled across the war. The compilations give the debt stock at about £70–75 million in 1756 and about £133–140 million in 1763, with interest consuming several million pounds a year. The figures come from different series and do not agree; they describe a scale, not an exact account.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

Grenville’s predecessor had recommended keeping about 10,000 soldiers in America at an estimated £200,000 a year, and Pontiac’s War made the case for a garrison seem urgent. The colonies, by contrast, paid low taxes relative to Britons at home, and the Board of Trade estimated that they smuggled about £700,000 of merchandise a year. The British case was that the colonists had benefited from the war and should help pay for its consequences.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-jyf-stamp]

Reform was not invented as a plot against liberty. It was a solution to a real problem, advanced by a government that believed it was acting reasonably. That is what made it politically dangerous.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

### 1.2 The Grenville program, 1764–1765

Between 1764 and 1765 Grenville’s ministry moved from trade regulation toward direct taxation, and the shift, not any single duty, produced the crisis.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

The Sugar Act received royal assent on April 5, 1764, and took effect in September. It cut the duty on foreign molasses from sixpence to threepence a gallon, retained high duties on foreign sugar, prohibited foreign rum, and added duties on wine, coffee, and other goods. More than half its text concerned enforcement: bonds, affidavits, Royal Navy searches, and trial in a vice-admiralty court at Halifax rather than by a local jury.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

The Currency Act, passed on April 19, 1764, and effective September 1, banned colonial paper currency as legal tender and required the new duties to be paid in hard money. Together with the Sugar Act, it tightened credit in a postwar slump. The Quartering Act of 1765 required colonial authorities to provide quarters and supplies for British troops, and it became a grievance in New York and later in Boston.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

The program had several parts, and they angered different colonies for different reasons. New England resented the molasses duty and the currency restriction; the southern and middle colonies felt the trade provisions differently. The chapter follows those differences rather than treating the measures as a single blow.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-jyf-stamp]

### 1.3 The crisis in figures

Figure 1 sets two quantities side by side that contemporaries argued about but rarely compared directly: the stock of British public debt and the annual revenue Parliament hoped to raise in the colonies. The debt was a stock that had roughly doubled in seven years. The colonial duties were flows that, even by the ministry’s optimistic estimates, were small. The Townshend duties of 1767 were expected to raise little more than £40,000 a year, and their author treated them as a beginning rather than a solution.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-our-rev-townshend]

Plotted together, the two series show why a small colonial revenue mattered politically out of proportion to its size. The ministry was trying to solve a problem of tens of millions with instruments that yielded tens of thousands. Read carefully, Figure 1 is not a graph of colonial burden; it is a graph of British ambition. It shows the scale of the problem and the modesty of the instruments, which is exactly the mismatch that made the taxes feel like a matter of principle rather than of money.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend][^ch09-check-figure]

The debt figures are compiled estimates that disagree, and the revenue figures are official expectations rather than audited receipts. ## 2. The Stamp Act

### 2.1 The statute and the schedule

The Stamp Act of 1765 was the first general internal tax Parliament laid on the colonies, and its long schedule of duties is the best evidence of what it actually did.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

Parliament passed the act on March 22, 1765, for “granting and applying certain stamp duties, and other duties, in the British colonies and plantations in America, towards further defraying the expences of defending, protecting, and securing the same.” Its duties took effect on November 1, 1765.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]

The schedule taxed court papers, deeds, bonds, probates, licenses, land grants, apprentices’ indentures, pamphlets, newspapers, advertisements, almanacs, playing cards, and dice. It charged three pence on a court pleading, six pence on a bond up to ten pounds, a halfpenny on a half-sheet newspaper and a penny on a full-sheet newspaper, two shillings on an advertisement, a shilling on a pack of cards, and ten shillings on a pair of dice. Documents in a language other than English paid double.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]

The design was to make the stamp unavoidable in ordinary legal and commercial life. Unstamped matter could not be “pleaded or given in evidence” in court, and using unstamped paper carried a ten-pound forfeiture. The tax touched people who never saw a customs officer.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]

The schedule explains the breadth of the protest. The tax fell on printers, lawyers, merchants, tavern-keepers, and card players at once, and it made the newspaper press both a target and a mouthpiece.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

### 2.2 The assemblies answer: Virginia and the Stamp Act Congress

The first formal response came from the assemblies, in the language of English rights, and it produced the first coordinated intercolonial meeting of the crisis.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]

On May 29, 1765, Patrick Henry introduced resolves in the House of Burgesses. They claimed for Virginians the privileges of English subjects born in the realm and concluded that the General Assembly of Virginia “have the only and sole exclusive Right & Power to lay Taxes & Impositions upon the Inhabitants of this Colony.” The House softened the resolves the next day, but the full set had already been printed and circulated.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-jyf-stamp]

Delegates from nine colonies met at the Stamp Act Congress in New York from October 7 to 25, 1765. On October 19 they adopted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances. It affirmed allegiance to the crown and “due subordination” to Parliament, then held that “no taxes should be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives,” and that the colonists could not be represented in the House of Commons.[^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]

In the same weeks, crowds and committees pressed stamp distributors to resign, and merchants adopted non-importation agreements. On October 31, 1765, New York merchants resolved to countermand orders for British goods “unless the Stamp Act be repealed,” and retailers pledged not to buy goods shipped after January 1 unless the act fell.[^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]

By the end of 1765 the colonists had produced three instruments at once: assembly resolves, a congress declaration, and market pressure. They were different tools used by different people, not a single movement.[^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration][^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]

### 2.3 In Focus: a stamp, the paper, and the statute

**In Focus.** Few stamped papers survive from the American colonies, because the act was never fully enforced and the stamps went out of use after repeal. What survives is often a printer’s proof or a loose stamp rather than a document that circulated. The Museum of the American Revolution holds a paper stamp of about 1765, printed in Britain and meant to be attached to taxed documents, and the National Postal Museum holds a proof sheet marked with the red imprint of Saint Edward’s Crown, struck before production began. Set beside the proof, the statute’s schedule explains what the mark meant: a halfpenny on a half-sheet newspaper, a penny on a full sheet, two pence on a single-sheet almanac, a shilling on a pack of cards. Read closely, the objects and the statute tell one story. The stamp was not merely a receipt; it was a condition of legality, since unstamped matter could not be used in court. And the proof sheet shows a tax being manufactured in London to be sold in America, which is why colonists called it a tax imposed without their consent.[^ch09-amrev-stamp-object][^ch09-ev-stamp-proof][^ch09-stamp-act-avalon][^ch09-check-focus]

### 2.4 The crisis of authority in the streets

The August 14 protest, described at this chapter’s opening, ended with the crowd’s dispersal; Oliver resigned the next day. It did not end the violence. On August 26 a crowd attacked the houses of customs officials, including Benjamin Hallowell, and then destroyed Thomas Hutchinson’s mansion. The crowd carried off furniture, silver, and about £900 in cash. Hutchinson estimated his loss at more than £3,000 sterling, including the cash; his detailed accounting of the destroyed property came to about £2,200. He called it the “most barbarous outrage that ever was committed in America.”[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]

Patriots and printers argued about whether the violence helped or harmed the cause. A Boston newspaper conceded that “the Cause of Liberty requires an extraordinary Spirit to support it” yet insisted that “pulling down Houses and robbing Persons of their substance” was “utterly inconsistent with the first Principles of Government.” The same paper distinguished the August 14 assembly from the “unbridled Licentiousness” of the second riot, and Hutchinson believed that “the encouragers of the first mob never intended matters should go to this length.”[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]

The crowd had names and a structure. A shoemaker, Ebenezer Mackintosh, led a South End gang that joined the protest, and better-placed organizers — later associated with the group known as the Loyal Nine — helped prepare it. The committees and crowds that pressed the distributors were coming to be called the Sons of Liberty, though the name covered different groups in different towns. Peter Oliver’s later claim that Mackintosh commanded a “Mob of 2000 men” is a Loyalist accusation, not a neutral count. The evidence shows both popular anger and elite organization, and it does not allow the crowd to be reduced to either.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-check-names]

The crowds made the stamp impracticable and cost the governor the initiative. After August 1765, enforcing the act in Massachusetts required the army, not the sheriffs, which set up the occupation of 1768.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]

## 3. Repeal and the Declaratory Act

### 3.1 The repeal of 1766

Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in March 1766 because of pressure from British merchants and manufacturers, not because it conceded the colonists’ constitutional argument.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-morgan-commonplace]

An act repealing the stamp duties received assent on March 18, 1766, and voided the 1765 statute from May 1, 1766. Its preamble gave the reason: the continuance of the act “would be attended with many inconveniencies, and may be productive of consequences greatly detrimental to the commercial interests of these kingdoms.”[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon]

The repeal followed a campaign by merchants whose American debts and orders were at risk. Repeal calmed the ports but settled nothing. Parliament gave up the tax and kept the principle.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-morgan-commonplace]

### 3.2 The Declaratory Act

On the same day it repealed the Stamp Act, Parliament declared its authority to bind the colonies “in all cases whatsoever,” and that claim became the central constitutional question of the decade.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon]

The Declaratory Act declared that the colonies “have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate unto, and dependent upon the imperial crown and parliament of Great Britain,” and that Parliament had “full power and authority to make laws and statutes of sufficient force and validity to bind the colonies and people of America … in all cases whatsoever.”[^ch09-declaratory-avalon]

The repeal satisfied the colonists’ practical grievance, and the Declaratory Act preserved Parliament’s theoretical claim. The next crisis would test whether the two could be separated, and the answer, for the Townshend ministry, was that they could not.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-check-dating]

### 3.3 What repeal settled and what it left open

1766 produced a truce, not a settlement. Non-importation lapsed, the stamps were withdrawn, and colonial assemblies returned to ordinary business. But the assemblies’ resolves of 1765 had claimed for themselves the exclusive power to tax, and that claim was incompatible with the Declaratory Act.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]

The quarrel was framed as “internal” versus “external” taxation, a distinction colonists such as Benjamin Franklin and John Dickinson drew and the ministry declined to accept as a limit on parliamentary authority. Internal taxes fell on persons and property within a colony, such as stamp duties and land taxes; external taxes fell on trade at the ports. The distinction was plausible and unstable, because a duty on imported glass or tea was external in form but raised revenue, and the colonists argued that the purpose, not the form, made it a tax.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend]

The unresolved question — whether Parliament could tax the colonies at all — returned in 1767 with the Townshend duties, which were deliberately designed to look external.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend]

## 4. Townshend measures

### 4.1 The Revenue Act of 1767

The Townshend Revenue Act of 1767 revived the fiscal project by dressing it as external trade duties, and it created the machinery of enforcement that made the duties harder to evade than the Stamp Act had been.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

The act received assent on June 29, 1767, and took effect November 20. It laid duties on glass, red and white lead, painters’ colors, paper, pasteboard, china, and tea imported into the colonies from Great Britain, and it was accompanied by measures establishing an American Board of Customs Commissioners in Boston and tightening the vice-admiralty courts.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

The preamble named the objects: “the administration of justice, and the support of civil government” and “defending, protecting, and securing, the said dominions.” Townshend intended the duties to pay governors and judges directly, freeing them from dependence on colonial assemblies. The aim was the assemblies’ power as much as their purses.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

The duties were expected to raise little more than £40,000 a year. They were meant as a beginning, and their modest yield is part of the story: the issue was authority, not money. By funding officials directly and stationing the customs board in Boston, the Townshend program concentrated the conflict in Massachusetts.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend][^ch09-townshend-avalon]

### 4.2 The Circular Letter and colonial coordination

Massachusetts turned the new duties into a coordinated constitutional protest, and the ministry answered by threatening the assembly’s charter.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon]

On February 11, 1768, the Massachusetts House adopted a circular letter to the other colonial legislatures, drafted by Samuel Adams and James Otis. It conceded that Parliament was “the supreme legislative power over the whole empire,” but argued that the constitution limited that power, and that acts “imposing duties on the people of this province, with the sole and express purpose of raising a revenue, are infringements of their natural and constitutional rights.”[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon]

The letter asked the other assemblies to harmonize their measures, and several replied. The ministry demanded that Massachusetts rescind it; the House refused by a large majority, and the governor dissolved the assembly. The circular letter was a constitutional argument made in the form of an intercolonial message, and it produced both coordination among the assemblies and a direct confrontation over the charter. It should be distinguished from the crowd actions of 1765.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon][^ch09-check-names]

### 4.3 Non-importation and the occupation of Boston

As the duties took effect and the customs board began its work, merchants and artisans revived non-importation, and the ministry answered with troops, which brought imperial authority and the town face to face.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

Colonial merchants again agreed not to import British goods, and town committees enforced the agreements by naming violators. Non-importation worked because it turned private consumption into a public test of loyalty.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

With the customs board in Boston and customs officers demanding naval protection, the ministry stationed British regiments in the town beginning in 1768. Troops stationed in a town of about 15,000, with about 4,000 soldiers present by 1770, made daily friction routine. The occupation was a policy choice, not an accident, and it made the enforcement of customs law a matter of street-level conflict.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-gilder-revere]

## 5. Occupation and the Boston Massacre

### 5.1 The confrontation of March 5, 1770

On the night of March 5, 1770, a crowd and a squad of soldiers met outside the Custom House on King Street, and the soldiers fired, killing five civilians and wounding six.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]

The evening was cold, with light snow on the ground. Private Hugh White stood sentry at the Custom House; a crowd taunted him and threw objects, and White called for help. Captain Thomas Preston led a small party to his aid, and the crowd surrounded the soldiers. A shot was fired, then several more.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]

Five men died: Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray, James Caldwell, Samuel Maverick, and, days later, Patrick Carr. Six others were wounded. Attucks, a sailor of African and Indigenous ancestry, was described in testimony as “mulatto” or “Indian”; he was the first to fall and became a martyr.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-nps-attucks]

Samuel Adams led a funeral procession on March 8 that drew a reported 10,000 people, about two-thirds of the town. The victims were buried in a common plot at the Granary Burying Ground, and the day was commemorated for years afterward. The shooting was the outcome of a specific confrontation, not a planned massacre, but colonists experienced it as one.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-check-dates]

### 5.2 The trials and the rule of law

The colony tried the soldiers in civil courts, and John Adams helped defend them, winning acquittals that became a claim about law rather than loyalty.[^ch09-masshist-massacre]

Captain Preston was tried first and acquitted. The eight soldiers were tried together in the fall of 1770; John Adams and Josiah Quincy appeared for the defense, while Robert Treat Paine and Samuel Quincy represented the deceased. Six soldiers were acquitted and two were convicted of manslaughter and branded on the hand.[^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]

Adams argued that the soldiers had been attacked by a mob and had fired in self-defense. His willingness to defend them, and the acquittals, became evidence that the colonists wanted law and not vengeance. The trials separated the cause of liberty from the demand for retaliation, which strengthened the patriots’ constitutional claim even as it disappointed those who wanted convictions.[^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-check-names]

### 5.3 The prints and the memory

The Boston Massacre became an enduring image because Paul Revere and others engraved it, and those prints shaped what later generations believed happened.[^ch09-masshist-massacre-visual]

Paul Revere issued “The Bloody Massacre” in 1770, probably based on an engraving by Henry Pelham; Revere got his version to market first. It shows an orderly line of soldiers firing into a defenseless crowd, with a poem and a legend. The scene did not match the testimony, which described a confused struggle, and the print omits or alters details such as the snow.[^ch09-masshist-massacre-visual][^ch09-gilder-revere]

Several versions appeared in Massachusetts and London within two years, each shaped to express outrage and build support. Comparing them shows how quickly an event became an argument. The prints are evidence of how the Massacre was remembered, not of what happened on King Street. The distinction is the chapter’s method in miniature.[^ch09-gilder-revere][^ch09-check-names]

### 5.4 The partial repeal and the uneasy calm

Parliament removed the Townshend duties in April 1770 but kept the duty on tea, and the colonists’ non-importation collapsed, leaving the constitutional question open.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

On March 5, 1770, Lord North moved to repeal all the Townshend duties except the tax on tea. George Grenville argued for repealing the tea duty as well, calling the duties bad for trade, but his amendment failed. The repeal received Royal Assent on April 12, 1770.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal][^ch09-our-rev-townshend]

North kept the tea duty to avoid the appearance of surrendering to colonial protest, which preserved the principle at the cost of the revenue. The compromise divided colonial merchants, who wanted trade to resume, from artisans, who wanted non-importation to continue while any duty stood. The years 1770–73 were quieter but not settled. The tea duty remained as a standing provocation, and it set up the crisis of 1773 that Chapter 10 takes up.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal][^ch09-check-ch10]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Parliament’s measures were a fiscal response to the war debt and the peacetime establishment. The Grenville and Townshend programs sought colonial revenue and tighter enforcement, and their designers understood them as reasonable and necessary.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-our-rev-townshend]

The colonists denied that Parliament could tax them without their consent. Assembly resolves, the Stamp Act Congress, and the Circular Letter all made the same claim in different forms.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration][^ch09-circular-letter-avalon]

Colonial resistance was both popular and organized. Crowds, committees, merchants, and assemblies acted at once and sometimes in tension.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]

Repeal in 1766 settled the practice and not the principle. The Declaratory Act preserved Parliament’s claim to bind the colonies in all cases whatsoever.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-declaratory-avalon]

The crisis did not make independence inevitable. Through 1770 the dominant colonial claim was to English rights and the protection of the crown, not to separation.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon][^ch09-check-ch10]

### 6.2 What is open

Whether ideas or interests drove resistance is open. Did colonists act from an absorbed ideology of liberty, from material grievance, from fear of political slavery and corruption, or from some combination?[^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer][^ch09-nash-retrospective]

How far the crowd acted on its own is open. Were the August riots elite-orchestrated, or did artisans and laborers press leaders into more radical action? The evidence is partial and mostly official.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]

Whether virtual representation was a defensible answer is open. The colonists were not represented in the House of Commons; the question is whether Parliament’s theory of virtual representation could legitimately supply the deficiency. It was a real constitutional theory in Britain, not merely a cynical dodge, and the colonists had to answer it on its merits.[^ch09-jenyns-objections]

What the numbers were is open. The debt, the expected yield of the duties, and the size of the crowds and the losses vary across sources and must be given as estimates with attributions.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-our-rev-townshend][^ch09-check-revenue]

What the archives cannot show is part of the problem. The crowds, the enslaved people they involved, and the Indigenous nations on the frontier appear mainly through the writings of officials and printers, and those mediations belong in the account.[^ch09-check-names]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which structural pressures, competing legal theories, and contingent crowd actions all mattered, and in which no single cause explains the crisis.[^ch09-check-sources]

Bernard Bailyn’s *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (1967) argued that a radical-Whig or Commonwealth ideology, absorbed from English opposition writers, gave the colonists a coherent way to interpret the measures as a conspiracy against liberty. He denied that social and economic conflict by itself explained the Revolution.[^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer][^ch09-amrev-bailyn][^ch09-bailyn-wiki]

Edmund S. and Helen M. Morgan’s *The Stamp Act Crisis* (1953) read the resistance as a principled defense of English liberties and constitutional government, and it remains the starting point for the crisis.[^ch09-morgan-commonplace][^ch09-morgan-jstor]

Gary B. Nash’s *The Urban Crucible* (1979) located the origins of the Revolution in the economic and social transformation of the port cities, where the growth of poverty and inequality produced popular politics and class tension. Nash’s account explains why Boston was the most radical of the towns.[^ch09-nash-retrospective]

Thomas Hutchinson insisted that the colonies must acknowledge the supremacy of Parliament, and he treated the assembly’s denial of it as a dangerous innovation. His position, like Jenyns’s, stated the imperial case from within America.[^ch09-hutchinson-speeches][^ch09-jenyns-objections]

Read together, the positions say that the crisis was at once a contest over ideas, a conflict of interests, and a collision between imperial administration and colonial practice. Later scholarship has questioned a hard dichotomy between ideas and interests, and the debate is better described as overlapping than as teams.[^ch09-bailyn-wiki][^ch09-check-sources]

## Legacies

The crisis left a constitutional question that no act had answered. Parliament gave up the Stamp Act in 1766 and the Townshend duties in 1770, but it kept the Declaratory Act and the tea duty, so the claim to bind the colonies “in all cases whatsoever” outlasted the taxes that had provoked the argument.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

It also left a repertoire. Resolves and declarations, a continental congress, non-importation, committees, and crowd action would be reused in every crisis from 1773 onward, and the memory of the Boston Massacre was institutionalized in annual orations at a time when the shooting itself had become a print.[^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon][^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-check-ch10]

And it left a town under arms. The troops who had occupied Boston after 1768 were withdrawn to Castle William after the Massacre, but the customs board remained, the tea duty remained, and in 1773 the same issues returned at Griffin’s Wharf. The crisis of 1765–70 did not end in independence; it ended in a truce that everyone understood to be temporary.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-check-ch10]

## Optional threads

- **The Atlantic frame.** The stamp duties paid the cost of an empire that stretched from the Ohio Country to India, and the resistance used the language of English, not merely colonial, rights. The crisis belonged to a British imperial and Atlantic debate rather than to a purely American quarrel.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer]
- **Archives and silences.** The crowd, the enslaved people present in Boston, and the Indigenous nations of the interior appear mainly in the reports of officials and printers. Naming those mediations is not a reason to ignore popular or Indigenous agency.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-check-names]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 8 carries the war and the debt to 1763; Chapter 10 carries the tea crisis and independence from 1773; later chapters carry the constitutional language of consent into the Revolution and the founding. The sovereignty question is a through-line of the book.[^ch09-check-ch8][^ch09-check-ch10]

## Summary

- Britain’s debt roughly doubled in the Seven Years’ War, and the peacetime garrison gave the ministry a reason to seek colonial revenue.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- The Grenville program of 1764–65 moved from trade regulation to direct taxation through the Sugar Act, the Currency Act, the Quartering Act, and the Stamp Act.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- The Stamp Act of March 22, 1765, taxed a long schedule of legal papers, printed matter, cards, and dice, and made the stamp a condition of legality.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]
- The Virginia Resolves and the Stamp Act Congress claimed that the colonies could be taxed only with their own consent, given by representatives they chose.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]
- On August 14, 1765, a Boston crowd hung an effigy of Andrew Oliver from the elm that became known as the Liberty Tree and burned it outside his house that night; he resigned.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]
- On August 26, 1765, a crowd destroyed Thomas Hutchinson’s mansion, and Hutchinson estimated his loss above £3,000 sterling.[^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]
- Parliament repealed the Stamp Act on March 18, 1766, for commercial reasons, and on the same day passed the Declaratory Act claiming authority to bind the colonies in all cases whatsoever.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-declaratory-avalon]
- The Townshend Revenue Act of 1767 laid external duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea, funded royal officials directly, and placed a customs board in Boston.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
- The Massachusetts Circular Letter of 1768 made a constitutional argument to the other assemblies, and non-importation became the colonists’ principal economic weapon.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon][^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]
- British troops occupied Boston from 1768, and on March 5, 1770, soldiers fired on a crowd, killing five, including Crispus Attucks.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-nps-attucks]
- John Adams helped defend the soldiers, and the trials ended in acquittals and two manslaughter convictions; Paul Revere’s engraving made the event an enduring image.[^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-masshist-massacre-visual]
- Parliament repealed the Townshend duties in April 1770 except the duty on tea, leaving the constitutional question open on the eve of the tea crisis.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

### The imperial, constitutional, and fiscal history

- **Edmund S. and Helen M. ### Society, the crowd, and the historiography

- **Gary B. Nash, *The Urban Crucible: Social Change, Political Consciousness, and the Origins of the American Revolution* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1979).** The social and economic account of urban resistance. Consulted through a retrospective essay; read the book for its argument.
- **Bernard Bailyn, *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1967), and *The Ordeal of Thomas Hutchinson* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1974).** The ideological interpretation and the Loyalist counterpoint. Consulted through the Pulitzer spotlight and a museum reflection; read the works before presenting their theses as fact.

- **Eric Hinderaker, *Boston’s Massacre* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2017).** The modern account of how the event became a political symbol. Read it alongside the prints and the depositions rather than in place of them.

> *A closing note.* The years from 1763 to 1770 turned a fiscal problem into a crisis of authority. A government that had won a world war asked its colonies to help pay for the peace, and it did so through duties that touched nearly every printed and legal page of colonial life. The colonists answered with resolves, a congress, non-importation, and crowds, and they framed their answer in the language of English rights. Parliament gave up the taxes but kept the principle, first in the Declaratory Act and then in the tea duty that survived 1770; the colonists tasted the power of coordinated resistance and kept the memory of the Massacre. Neither side had surrendered its understanding of who could make law for America, and that unresolved question, not any single tax, is what the chapter leaves standing.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
