## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The day.** On October 26, 1825, the Erie Canal opened after eight years of construction. A cannon at Buffalo, where the canal drew its first water from Lake Erie, fired, and a relay of guns spaced ten to fifteen miles apart carried the signal down the canal and the Hudson to New York City.[^ch15-cere-source-ny][^ch15-ny-canal-history]
- **The boat.** Governor DeWitt Clinton, the canal’s most famous champion, boarded the packet boat *Seneca Chief* at Buffalo and led a flotilla east. The New York State canal history states that Clinton “helmed the inaugural voyage aboard the *Seneca Chief*,” culminating in the “Wedding of the Waters.”[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-cere-source-ny]
- **The symbol.** Before leaving Buffalo, two barrels of Lake Erie water were taken aboard. At the journey’s end, off Sandy Hook at the mouth of New York harbor, Clinton poured Lake Erie water into the Atlantic to symbolize the union of the western waters with the sea.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-cere-source-ny]
- **The journey.** The flotilla reached New York City on November 4, 1825, and was met by a harbor full of decorated vessels. Six hundred diners had toasted the canal at Albany on the way down. The opening was as much a civic festival as an engineering milestone.[^ch15-cere-source-ny]
- **The work.** The canal ran 363 miles from the Hudson to Lake Erie, forty feet wide and four feet deep, rising through eighty-three locks with a total lift of 675 feet. The legislature had authorized about $7 million in 1817. The Bill of Rights Institute’s account states that tolls repaid the construction costs within the first nine years of operation.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]

### The scene’s argument

- **A ditch that became a hinge.** The scene should open on the Buffalo waterfront and let the reader watch a mocked public works project become the most consequential piece of infrastructure in the young republic. The point is not that the canal was a marvel but that it was a public investment, made by a state that had decided to act when Congress would not.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-cere-source-ny]
- **The booster and the claim.** Clinton’s proclamation in Colden’s memoir joins private enterprise, public spirit, and divine favor into a single promise of commerce. The scene should quote the promise and then set it against the people the canal moved and the people it left behind.[^ch15-clinton-proclamation]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did exchange change independence and labor?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that the canal alone remade the country. Within a decade the same logic of borrowed money and speculative land would produce the Panic of 1837. The instruments of connection would also become the instruments of risk.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-dpla-panic1837]
- **The widening.** The scene stands on an October morning in 1825. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader through the canals, mills, cotton, households, and banks that together produced the new market.[^ch15-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, crowd feelings, or dialogue.** The departure, the boat, the cannon relay, the barrels, the arrival, and Clinton’s proclamation are documented; the day’s weather and the crowd’s emotions are not. Do not stage remarks that no witness recorded.[^ch15-check-scene]
- **No unverified menagerie.** One popular account describes a flotilla boat called *Noah’s Ark* carrying a bear, eagles, fawns, and two Native American youths as part of a curated display of the “wild” West. The detail is striking and should be used only if it can be corroborated in Colden’s memoir or a reliable newspaper account; otherwise name it as a reported anecdote the chapter does not retell.[^ch15-check-scene]
- **No claim that the canal network stopped at the Appalachians.** Map 12 and the prose must show that the Erie crossed the Appalachian divide through the Mohawk gap, and that Pennsylvania, Ohio, and other states later built competing canal systems, some of which also crossed the mountains. The claim to avoid is that canals as a technology were confined to the eastern seaboard.[^ch15-check-canal-network][^ch15-check-map]
- **No claim that the celebration reached everyone.** Enslaved people in the South and Native nations being dispossessed in the cotton country were outside the “wedding of the waters”; the chapter names them rather than treating the canal as a universal benefit.[^ch15-check-slavery]

## 1. Canals, roads, steamboats, and railroads

### 1.1 The transportation revolution and the Erie Canal

- **The claim.** Before the 1820s, moving goods overland was so expensive that settlement clung to the coast and the rivers; the transportation revolution collapsed the cost of moving freight and people and pulled the interior into an Atlantic market.[^ch15-bri-erie]
- **The cost of distance.** The Bill of Rights Institute’s account states that it cost as much to ship a ton of freight thirty miles overland as to send it across the Atlantic, and that the center of the American population shifted westward only as turnpikes, canals, steamboats, and railroads appeared. These are the terms on which the chapter should frame the revolution.[^ch15-bri-erie]
- **The Erie Canal.** New York authorized construction on April 15, 1817, approving about $7 million for a 363-mile canal. It opened in 1825 after eight years of work, with eighty-three locks and a total lift of 675 feet. Its economics were decisive: the canal cut freight costs sharply and, according to the Bill of Rights Institute, repaid its construction costs out of tolls within nine years.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]
- **The politics of internal improvement.** Congress considered a national program of roads and canals; Calhoun’s Bonus Bill of 1817 proposed using the federal surplus for internal improvements, and Madison vetoed it on constitutional grounds. The failure of a national system left the states, and their credit, to build the canals, a choice with lasting fiscal consequences.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-cere-source-ny]
- **The consequence.** The Erie made New York City the young republic’s dominant port and pulled the Great Lakes into the Atlantic economy. But its promise of connection was also a promise of dependence: farmers who once grew for themselves now grew for a distant market whose prices they did not control. That tension is the chapter’s through-line.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]

### 1.2 Steamboats, roads, and the first railroads; Map 12

- **The claim.** Canals were only one thread. Steamboats mastered the western rivers, turnpikes and plank roads reached into the countryside, and the first railroads began to break the canal’s monopoly on bulk freight.[^ch15-yawp-cotton][^ch15-bri-erie]
- **Steamboats.** The number of steamboats on western rivers grew from seventeen in 1817 to more than seven hundred by 1837, and by 1860 some 3,500 vessels a year were working in and out of New Orleans. Steamboats made the Mississippi the cotton kingdom’s central artery.[^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- **Railroads.** The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, completed in early 1830 as the country’s first intercity line, was thirteen miles long; the railroad network grew to thousands of miles by 1840 and to more than 9,000 miles by 1850. Railroads were financed mainly by private banks rather than state governments.[^ch15-aar-railroads][^ch15-bri-erie]
- **The map.** Map 12 (`ch15-map-01`) plots canals, roads, and railroads for 1825–1840. It should distinguish completed from projected lines, date every element, show the Erie crossing the Appalachian divide through the Mohawk corridor, and refuse the false generalization that the canal network stopped at the Appalachians. Henry Schenck Tanner’s 1840 map and description of American canals and railroads is the principal contemporary source; John Lauritz Larson’s *Internal Improvement* supplies the national-policy frame.[^ch15-check-map][^ch15-tanner-map][^ch15-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** The network was uneven. Port cities, canal towns, and the newer cotton country boomed; upland and remote districts waited decades for a railroad and paid the price in isolation. Map 12’s job is to show that geography, not merely technology, sorted who got connected.[^ch15-check-map]

### 1.3 Who paid: state debt, migrant labor, and the uneven geography

- **The claim.** The transportation revolution was built with borrowed money and hard labor, and its benefits were distributed as unevenly as its costs.
- **The diggers.** More than 9,000 canal diggers worked on the Erie, most from upstate New York and the rest largely Irish immigrants; they were amateurs without formal engineering training, and the canal’s grade and locks were cut by hand and animal power. The chapter should name the labor behind the “wedding of the waters.”[^ch15-bri-erie]
- **The debt.** States and private investors financed canal after canal; when the projects failed, they triggered a wave of state debt defaults in the 1840s, which pushed state governments out and left railroads to private capital. The Panic of 1837 interacted with this debt overhang.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-dpla-panic1837]
- **The land question.** The canals opened the interior to settlement that displaced Native nations; the same decade that built the Erie saw the cotton frontier pushed across the Southeast and the Old Southwest. The chapter treats internal improvement and Indigenous dispossession as connected, not separate, stories, leaving the removal policy itself to Chapter 17.[^ch15-check-slavery][^ch15-check-ch16]

## 2. Mills and wage labor

### 2.1 Slater and the factory system

- **The claim.** The American factory began in a Rhode Island mill where a migrant English mechanic reproduced British cotton-spinning machinery and put children to work on it.[^ch15-nps-slater]
- **The mill.** Moses Brown, a Providence merchant, hired Samuel Slater in December 1789 to build water-powered cotton-spinning machines; Slater got the machines running in December 1790, and in 1793 the firm of Almy, Brown and Slater built the mill at Pawtucket Falls that became the first successful water-powered cotton-spinning mill in the United States.[^ch15-nps-slater]
- **The labor.** Slater hired children, generally between eight and fourteen years old, and paid them not for what they produced but for the hours they spent under his supervision. Entire families eventually worked and lived in mill villages under the constant control of their employers. The factory system began, in short, with child and family labor, not with independent wage earners.[^ch15-nps-slater]
- **The consequence.** Slater’s mill established the template that the larger Waltham–Lowell mills would elaborate: mechanized cotton spinning, waterpower, and a labor force drawn from a population with few alternatives. The chapter should show the continuity from child labor at Pawtucket to the young women of Lowell.[^ch15-nps-slater][^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

### 2.2 Lowell and the Waltham–Lowell system

- **The claim.** At Lowell, corporations integrated spinning and weaving under one roof and recruited young Yankee women to tend the machines, housing them in company boardinghouses and governing their conduct.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- **The recruitment.** Beginning with Lowell’s first factory in 1823, the textile corporations recruited women from New England farms and small villages, drawing them with monthly cash wages and room and board. The women were generally fifteen to thirty years old, and they came because family farms offered young women few ways to earn money of their own.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- **The company town.** The boardinghouses were corporation property run by a female keeper or a couple; a typical house had eight units with twenty to forty women each, sharing rooms and beds. Keepers enforced curfews and codes of conduct, and the factory bell structured the day. The corporation controlled work and much of life outside it.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- **The consequence.** The “mill girls” gained wages, savings, and a measure of independence, but on terms set by the corporation. The chapter should present Lowell neither as a female Eden nor as a prison but as the first large-scale American experiment in assembling and disciplining a wage labor force.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

### 2.3 The costs: hours, turnover, and the turn-outs

- **The claim.** Mill work was long, unhealthy, and insecure; when wages were cut, or boardinghouse rents raised to cut them, the operatives organized and struck.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- **The hours.** Most textile workers toiled twelve to fourteen hours a day plus a half day on Saturday, and typically worked only nine or ten months a year, leaving the mills during part of the summer. The operatives’ own figure of about seventy-three hours a week in the 1830s and 1840s is widely cited and should be checked against the mill records.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-check-numbers]
- **The protest.** Lowell’s female workers struck twice in the 1830s over wage cuts and conditions, and in the 1840s organized for a ten-hour day; few strikes succeeded, and the workforce remained largely unorganized. The chapter should quote the operatives’ own claim to be “the daughters of freemen” and should trace that phrase to its printed source before setting it as their words.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-check-epigraphs]
- **The writers.** Some operatives published letters and essays in the *Lowell Offering* and later in labor papers like the *Voice of Industry*; Sarah Bagley became a leading labor reformer and the editor of the *Voice of Industry*. The chapter should use the operatives’ own publications to recover their voices rather than ventriloquizing them.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** The turn-outs failed in their immediate aims, but they established that the new wage labor was contested from the start. The chapter’s point is structural: the same market that offered women wages set the terms on which those wages could be reduced.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

## 3. Cotton, credit, and the domestic slave trade

### 3.1 The cotton gin and the cotton boom

- **The claim.** The cotton gin made short-staple cotton profitable inland, and the result was a cotton boom that bound the South, the North, and Britain into a single market built on enslaved labor.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- **The gin.** Eli Whitney patented the cotton gin in 1794. Before it, separating the sticky green seeds from upland cotton was so slow that the crop was barely worth growing beyond the coast; a hand worker separated about a pound a day, while a gin could process roughly fifty times as much.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-dpla-cotton-gin]
- **The boom.** Raw cotton output roughly doubled each decade after 1800. The National Archives records that by 1850 the United States grew three-quarters of the world’s cotton, and that by the mid-nineteenth century the South supplied three-fifths of American exports, most of it cotton. The cotton gin “reduced the labor of removing seeds” but not “the need for enslaved labor to grow and pick the cotton”; it increased the demand for both land and enslaved people.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin]
- **The scale by 1860.** By 1860 enslaved labor produced over two billion pounds of cotton a year, about 75 percent of which was sold internationally, most of it to Britain; American cotton made up about two-thirds of the global supply. Cotton accounted for roughly 60 percent of United States exports on the eve of the Civil War.[^ch15-nps-cotton-economy][^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- **The consequence.** Cotton did not merely enrich the South; it financed the Northern textile mills, the Atlantic shipping trade, and the credit system of the whole country. The chapter should present “King Cotton” not as a southern curiosity but as the engine of the American market.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-yawp-cotton]

### 3.2 The domestic slave trade

- **The claim.** Because the transatlantic slave trade ended in 1808 and the cotton frontier demanded labor, the Upper South sold its enslaved people to the Deep South in a vast internal trade that broke families and moved roughly a million people.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-gilderlehrman-berlin]
- **The scale.** Between 1820 and 1860, as cotton cultivation expanded, roughly one million enslaved people were forced west and to the Deep South. The population growth that resulted gave the slave states about a third more seats in Congress through the three-fifths clause, letting enslavers’ interests dominate national politics.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]
- **The traders.** Isaac Franklin and John Armfield, based in Alexandria, Virginia, were among the most successful traders; one of their ship manifests attested that the people named had “not been imported to the U.S. since January [1808]” and were held “to service in labor as slaves,” language showing their compliance with the law that banned the Atlantic trade.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]
- **The experience.** The trade moved people in coffles, chained and handcuffed; the Searchable Museum quotes Charles Ball’s 1837 account of iron collars fitted “round each of our necks” with a hundred-foot chain “passed through the hasp of each padlock.” The chapter must convey the human reality of the trade, not just its statistics.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]
- **The market sites.** Natchez, at the Forks of the Road, held the second-busiest slave market in the Deep South from 1833 until Federal troops arrived on July 13, 1863; traders had been pushed outside the city limits by a cholera scare in 1832. Tens of thousands of people were brought there from Virginia, Maryland, Kentucky, and the Carolinas.[^ch15-nps-forks]
- **The consequence.** The internal trade made the cotton boom possible and made the enslaved family its casualty. The chapter should treat the trade as the connective tissue between the Upper South’s “surplus” and the Deep South’s demand, and should name the people the ledgers counted.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]

### 3.3 Factors, credit, and the cotton economy

- **The claim.** Cotton moved to market through factors, or commission merchants, who sold the crop, advanced credit for the year’s supplies, and took a lien on the next crop—making the planter’s independence a fiction of the account book.[^ch15-ncpedia-factors]
- **The function.** A factor sold cotton or other crops and arranged the buyer, transportation, insurance, and storage for a commission. Because banks and ready cash were scarce, planters far from towns relied on factors for money and supplies on the promise of payment when the crop was ready, so the factor became the planter’s banker as well as his selling agent.[^ch15-ncpedia-factors]
- **The charges.** One survey of the factorage system reports a commission of about 2.5 percent to sell the crop, 2.5 percent to buy supplies, and 2.5 percent to advance cash or credit, with long-term credit sometimes at 8 percent, plus storage and wharfage. If the crop did not cover the year’s debts, the balance rolled over into the next crop, producing chronic debt.[^ch15-woodman-review]
- **The scale of finance.** Cotton alone accounted for about 50 percent of United States exports in the period. Brown Brothers and similar firms appointed agents in Charleston, Mobile, New Orleans, and Savannah who advanced money on consigned cotton; in the depression after 1837, the firm foreclosed on plantations and enslaved people pledged as collateral. The chapter should show the northern and British capital inside the southern credit system.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade]

### 3.4 In Focus: a cotton factor’s account page

- **In Focus: a cotton factor’s account page.** **In Focus.** The box reads one document from the machinery of the cotton trade rather than a generic ledger: the “account sales” a factor rendered to a planter after selling his crop and paying the season’s advances. The document is a two-sided statement of the market’s logic. On one side are the marks and weights of the planter’s bales, the price the factor got in New Orleans or another port, and the gross proceeds; on the other are the deductions—the factor’s commission, freight, drayage, storage, and the cash advances the planter drew during the year. What the planter receives is the residue, and not infrequently nothing at all, because the advances have consumed the crop. The account page should be read as an instrument of power: it translated people into bales and bales into a balance, and it kept the planter in a cycle of debt to a factor whose capital came from northern and British banks. The chapter’s task is to locate and read one identifiable account page—not to invent a representative ledger. Candidate documents include the account sales rendered by the New Orleans factor firm Reynolds, Byrne, and Company to the planter Marbury Morris, held in the Mississippi Department of Archives and History, and the account sales reproduced in Julia Floyd Smith’s study of antebellum Florida.[^ch15-ncpedia-factors][^ch15-woodman-review][^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-smith-florida]

## 4. Households and work

### 4.1 The household economy and outwork

- **The claim.** Before and beside the factory, much production remained in the household, where families made goods for merchants on piece rates under an older “putting-out” system.
- **The older system.** Families produced cotton and woolen goods, shoes, brooms, and plaited straw and palm-leaf hats in their homes for merchants who paid them by the piece. The putting-out system let merchants cut production costs without building factories and gave farm households cash in the seasons when fieldwork was light.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-check-household]
- **The overlap.** Outwork did not disappear when the mills appeared; it persisted alongside them, and the same women who made goods at home or in small shops also supplied the early mills. The chapter should resist a tidy before-and-after story in which the factory simply replaced the household.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- **The consequence.** The “market revolution” reached into the household long before it built the factory, converting ordinary domestic labor into wage labor at piece rates. This is where the chapter’s question about independence and labor is most intimate.[^ch15-check-household]

### 4.2 Separate spheres and the ideology of domesticity

- **The claim.** As production moved out of the home, a new ideology assigned middle-class women to a “separate sphere” of home and family—an ideal that described and justified the market’s reorganization of work.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]
- **The ideology.** The “cult of domesticity,” defined by the historian Barbara Welter in a 1966 article, held that women were designed for the roles of wife and mother and should cultivate piety, purity, submissiveness, and domesticity, with their influence confined to the home. Catharine Beecher’s *A Treatise on Domestic Economy* (1842) taught that American women held a “lofty and fortunate position” in return for their domestic role.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]
- **The limits of the ideal.** The cult of domesticity was designed for the wives and daughters of the white middle and upper classes. Free women forced into poorly paid work and enslaved women throughout the South were excluded from its protection and often cast as “fallen.” The chapter should use Harriet Jacobs’s narrative to show how the ideal looked from outside it.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]
- **The consequence.** “Separate spheres” was less a description of how most people lived than a prescription that served the new market order. The chapter should present it as ideology under construction, not as a settled fact of American life.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]

### 4.3 Enslaved households, women’s labor, and the limits of the “sphere”

- **The claim.** The ideology of separate spheres had no purchase on enslaved households, where women’s labor in the fields and the quarters was the foundation of the cotton economy.[^ch15-nps-cotton-economy]
- **The work.** On cotton plantations, enslaved women, men, and children worked the crop together, and planters measured each person’s daily yield of picked cotton, punishing those who fell short. The household labor that supported this workforce—cooking, cleaning, child care, gardening—was itself unpaid labor performed after the day’s field work.[^ch15-yawp-cotton][^ch15-check-slavery]
- **The trade and the family.** The domestic slave trade broke enslaved households by design, separating husbands from wives and parents from children. The chapter should read the ideology of domesticity against the reality of the auction block, naming the contradiction rather than smoothing it over.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]
- **The consequence.** The same decades that exalted the middle-class home as a haven from the market made the plantation household a site of commodity production worked by people who could be sold at any moment. The chapter’s account of “households and work” must hold both together.[^ch15-check-household][^ch15-check-slavery]

## 5. Banks and panics

### 5.1 The Second Bank and the Panic of 1819

- **The claim.** The market ran on credit, and credit was unstable; the Panic of 1819 was the young republic’s first nationwide depression.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819]
- **The expansion.** The number of banks exploded from three at the founding to about two hundred by the end of the War of 1812. Federal land sales allowed farmers to buy on credit, and in the cotton country land, cotton, and enslaved people were financed together in a loop that held only so long as the price of cotton stayed high in Liverpool.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819]
- **The crash.** The Second Bank of the United States, chartered in 1816, contributing to the boom through loans from its southern and western branches, then contracted sharply to restore its specie reserves. The currency in circulation fell to about half, and perhaps a third of the country’s banks failed. Cotton prices fell more than 50 percent in 1819.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819]
- **The human cost.** Land sales collapsed, merchant houses failed, and perhaps a fifth of wage earners nationally lost work—a tentative figure that must be given as an estimate. Some 3,500 people were imprisoned for debt in Boston alone between 1820 and 1822.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-check-numbers]
- **The consequence.** The panic discredited the bank, embittered the relief politics of the western states, and intensified southern fears about national power—setting up the political conflicts of the 1820s and 1830s that Chapter 16 owns.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-check-ch16]

### 5.2 The Bank War

- **The claim.** Andrew Jackson’s veto of the Second Bank’s recharter and his removal of the federal deposits destroyed the country’s central bank and left a fragmented, lightly regulated state banking system.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- **The veto.** Congress passed a bill to recharter the Second Bank in 1832; Jackson vetoed it in July, and won reelection that fall over Henry Clay by a wide margin. Jackson’s veto message called the bank unconstitutional and a source of corruption and privilege.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- **The removal.** In late 1833 Treasury Secretary Roger Taney ordered the federal deposits moved out of the bank. Biddle responded by contracting the bank’s lending—loans fell from a high of 53 percent of assets in 1832 to about 40 percent in 1835—causing a minor panic that backfired and strengthened Jackson’s case.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- **The pet banks.** The deposits went to state banks, many of them politically friendly “pet banks.” Without a central bank to discipline them, and with no deposit insurance to make depositors monitor them, the state banks invested heavily in land and public works.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- **The consequence.** By paying off the national debt in January 1835 and distributing the surplus, the government poured money into the state banks just as land speculation peaked—setting the stage for the panic of 1837.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]

### 5.3 The Panic of 1837 and its aftermath

- **The claim.** The Panic of 1837 began in the spring of that year and left the economy depressed into the mid-1840s, wrecking the credit system that had financed the cotton boom.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837]
- **The causes.** After Jackson’s veto, hundreds of new state banks issued paper currency far beyond their specie reserves. British banks began pulling back on lending to the United States in 1836, and Jackson’s Specie Circular required that federal land be purchased with gold and silver rather than paper, draining the banks of coin.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837]
- **The collapse.** In the spring of 1837 overextended banks suspended specie payments, and hundreds collapsed; depositors were left with worthless notes, and prices plunged. The absence of a central bank made the contraction worse, because there was no institution to supply liquidity or coordinate a response.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- **The politics.** Whigs and Democrats each blamed the other, and the crisis reshaped the party conflict that Chapter 16 owns. The chapter should show the panic as the market’s own verdict on the speculation the previous decade had encouraged.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-check-ch16]
- **The numbers.** Bank failures, the fall in currency, the decline of commodity prices, and the length of the depression are variously estimated; every figure must carry its source, year, and definition, and uncertain totals must be given as ranges.[^ch15-check-numbers]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The economy was transformed.** Between the 1790s and the 1840s the United States built canals, roads, steamboats, and railroads; moved production into factories; and connected a continental interior to Atlantic markets.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-ny-canal-history]
- **Cotton and slavery were central.** Cotton became the country’s leading export, and the labor that produced it was enslaved; the domestic slave trade moved roughly a million people to the cotton frontier.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]
- **Wage labor was contested.** The mills offered wages and independence on terms set by corporations, and the operatives struck against those terms almost as soon as the system matured.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- **The market was unstable.** The Panics of 1819 and 1837 showed that the new credit system could produce nationwide collapse, not just growth.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-dpla-panic1837]
- **The market reached the household.** Outwork and the ideology of separate spheres both reorganized domestic labor around the market.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity][^ch15-check-household]

### 6.2 What is open

- **When did the market revolution begin?** Sellers locates it after 1815; Howe and others argue that market relations were already widespread in the eighteenth century. The timing determines whether the transformation was a rupture or an acceleration.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-sources]
- **Was it coerced or chosen?** Sellers describes a majority of farm families driven into market dependence; Howe answers that most “welcomed the chance to buy and sell in larger markets” and were better off. The chapter should weigh the evidence rather than declare a verdict.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]
- **What unifies the story?** Is the Market Revolution a single process, or a bundle of regional and local changes—New England factories, southern slavery, western land—that only looks unified in retrospect?[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-sources]
- **Where is slavery in the story?** For some historians the market revolution is a northern story into which slavery intrudes; for others slavery is the engine of American capitalism. The chapter should test both against cotton, credit, and the internal trade.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-check-slavery]
- **How do the accounts show inequality?** The factor’s account, the mill’s payroll, and the census’s population columns count people in ways that reveal who held power; the chapter must read those sources critically rather than as neutral data.[^ch15-check-focus][^ch15-check-numbers]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the Market Revolution was real but not singular: it connected regions and created new freedoms while shifting risk onto workers, farm families, and enslaved people, and it cannot be told apart from slavery or from the state’s role in building infrastructure.
- **The Sellers synthesis.** Charles Sellers’s *The Market Revolution* (1991) argued that establishing capitalist hegemony over the economy, politics, and culture between 1815 and 1846 was the defining event of the era, a transformation imposed on a largely subsistence farm population. Its explanatory power made it enormously influential and also made it a target.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]
- **The Howe rejoinder.** Daniel Walker Howe’s *What Hath God Wrought* (2007) rejects the term, argues that market relations were earlier and that farm families often welcomed them, and places a “communications revolution” of telegraph, postal service, and print at the center of the age instead. It reads the transformation as progress rather than as tragedy.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]
- **The skeptics and regionalists.** Daniel Feller’s warning that “a monograph that presupposes a market revolution will certainly discover one” captures a broader critique; John Lauritz Larson, Winifred Barr Rothenberg, and Christopher Clark emphasize gradualism, regional difference, and the persistence of older practices. Their work cautions against reading a single national revolution into local evidence.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-sources]
- **Slavery at the center.** Historians of slavery and capitalism—Walter Johnson, Edward E. Baptist, Seth Rockman, and Sven Beckert—argue that the transformation cannot be understood apart from the enslaved labor and the credit built on it. Their work moves the internal slave trade and the factorage system from the margins to the middle of the story.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions say that the Market Revolution was a genuinely new scale of connection whose benefits and costs were distributed by power—by who owned land and enslaved people, who commanded credit, and who could sell their own labor. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not as teams.[^ch15-check-sources][^ch15-check-table]

## Legacies

The market order built between the 1790s and the 1840s outlasted the panic that closed the period. The canals and railroads tied the continent together and made the United States a single national economy; the cotton kingdom became the most valuable export sector in the world, and with it the enslaved population grew from about 700,000 in 1790 to more than 3.9 million by 1860.[^ch15-pmc-slave-growth][^ch15-nps-cotton-economy]

The transformation left a divided inheritance. It created wage work, urban growth, and a national market, and it did so through the dispossession of Native nations, the enslavement of millions of people, and a credit system that periodically ruined the very farmers and workers it had drawn in. The panic of 1837 and the depression that followed showed that the market’s risks, like its rewards, were distributed by power.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-check-slavery]

And the chapter’s central question endured. The same connections that made Americans more interdependent made them more exposed—to distant prices, distant banks, and distant decisions. Chapter 16 takes up the political crisis that the market’s demand for land and slavery reopened.[^ch15-check-ch16][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** British capital and British mills financed and consumed the American cotton crop; the Liverpool price set the terms of the southern planter’s year; and the panic of 1837 began with a British contraction of credit. The chapter should treat the American market as one part of an Atlantic economy.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- **Archives and silences.** The factor’s account, the census column, and the ship manifest count enslaved people as property and as commodities; the chapter should name that accounting as evidence of who held power, not as neutral data, and should seek the voices of the enslaved in narratives, testimony, and the records of the trade itself.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]
- **Distant connections.** The cotton frontier that this chapter describes set up the Missouri crisis (Ch. 16) and the removal of Native nations (Ch. 17); the state-financed canals and the credit panics fed later debates over internal improvements and the money supply (Chs. 16 and 27).[^ch15-check-ch16][^ch15-check-slavery]

## Summary

- The transportation revolution—turnpikes, canals, steamboats, and the first railroads—collapsed the cost of moving goods and connected the interior to Atlantic markets.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- The Erie Canal, authorized in 1817 and opened in 1825, ran 363 miles with eighty-three locks and repaid its roughly $7 million cost in tolls within about nine years.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]
- The factory began at Slater’s Pawtucket mill, which used water-powered machinery and child and family labor, and matured at Lowell, where corporations recruited young women into boardinghouses under company control.[^ch15-nps-slater][^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- Lowell operatives worked twelve to fourteen hours a day, struck twice in the 1830s over wage cuts, and organized for a ten-hour day in the 1840s with little immediate success.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- The cotton gin made inland cotton profitable; output doubled each decade after 1800, and by 1860 enslaved labor produced over two billion pounds a year, about two-thirds of the world supply.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-nps-cotton-economy]
- The domestic slave trade forced roughly one million people from the Upper South to the Deep South between 1820 and 1860, breaking families and enriching traders like Franklin and Armfield.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]
- Cotton moved to market through factors who advanced credit and took a lien on the next crop, keeping planters in chronic debt to capital that came from northern and British banks.[^ch15-ncpedia-factors][^ch15-woodman-review]
- Outwork persisted alongside the factory, while the ideology of separate spheres assigned middle-class women to the home—an ideal that excluded enslaved and poor women.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity][^ch15-check-household]
- The Panic of 1819 and the Panic of 1837 showed the new credit system’s instability; the Bank War destroyed the Second Bank and left a fragmented, lightly regulated state banking system.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- The Market Revolution was real and uneven: it created new freedoms and concentrated new risks, and it cannot be told apart from the slavery and dispossession that financed it.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-slavery]

> *A closing note.* The market that Clinton celebrated on November 4, 1825, was real, and so was its price. The canal, the mill, and the cotton field were joined in a single system that moved goods, people, and money across a continent. It gave farm daughters wages, made New York a world port, and built the most productive export economy of its age. It also moved a million enslaved people from their homes, sent mill girls to the strike list at twenty, and periodically wiped out the savings of farmers and workers through panics that began in distant banks. The chapter’s question is not whether the market liberated or enslaved Americans; it is how a single system could do both, and who decided which would happen to whom.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-dpla-panic1837]
