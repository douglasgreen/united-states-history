## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date and the occasion.** On June 16, 2015, Donald J. Trump announced his candidacy for the Republican presidential nomination in the lobby of Trump Tower at 725 Fifth Avenue in Manhattan, before a crowd gathered for the event and carried live by the cable news networks.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]
- **The filing.** Trump submitted a statement of candidacy (FEC Form 2) with the Federal Election Commission on June 16, 2015, beginning the formal campaign that the FEC’s election-results publication later records.[^ch48-fec-2016]
- **The prepared text.** In the prepared remarks, Trump said, “Our country is in serious trouble,” that “We are not respected by anyone,” and, after attacking “politicians,” that “Ladies and gentlemen, I am officially running for President of the United States.” He named China, Mexico, and the Islamic State as adversaries and promised a comeback.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]
- **The staging.** The circulation of the announcement through live television and, later, clips of the escalator descent is documented; the venue, the date, the participants’ names in the campaign material, and the prepared text are on the record.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]
- **The contested crowd.** Claims about the size and composition of the audience, including later reports that the campaign paid some attendees, are contested and depend on the outlet and the source. The chapter should give any figure as an estimate with its source or describe the event without a precise count.[^ch48-check-scene][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the venue, the filing, and the prepared text. It cannot show what each person in the room thought, how any individual decided, or that the announcement itself caused the outcome that followed.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015][^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** On June 16, 2015, no one on the ground knew that Trump would win the nomination, that he would win the Electoral College while losing the popular vote, that he would sign a large tax cut, that a pandemic would define his final year, or that a mob would attack the Capitol. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch48-check-scene][^ch48-check-dates-appD]

- **An entrance and its limits.** The scene’s claim is not that the announcement decided the election, but that it began a campaign organized around grievance, celebrity, and a claim of direct connection to “the people” against a corrupt establishment. - **The stakes.** The tension between a campaign that attacked political institutions and a presidency that wielded them — the tax law, the travel ban, the two impeachments, the pandemic response, and the attempt to overturn an election — is the chapter’s central problem.[^ch48-check-scope][^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-hres-24-2021]

## 1. Announcement and primaries (2015–2016)

### 1.1 The claim: a crowded field and a candidate the party did not choose

- **The claim.** Trump entered a Republican field that eventually reached 17 declared major candidates, was opposed by much of the party’s national leadership, and won the nomination by winning pluralities in state after state while his rivals divided the rest; on the Democratic side, Hillary Clinton defeated Bernie Sanders after a longer and more divided contest than expected. The chapter should treat the nomination fights as organizational and coalitional events, not as a single national verdict.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015][^ch48-fec-2016]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the announcement and the field through the primaries, the conventions, the general-election campaign, and the electoral geography shown in Map 38.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015][^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-map38]

### 1.2 The announcement and the field

- **The text.** Trump’s prepared announcement identified “politicians” as “the problem,” promised to “Make America Great Again,” and attacked trade with China and Japan and migration across the Mexican border; the record shows the prepared version was shorter than the delivered speech.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]
- **The field.** The Republican field’s size, the early debates, and the sequential withdrawals are documented in the FEC’s candidate and results records and in state primary returns; the chapter should give the field’s size and the delegate arithmetic from the official records rather than from memory.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** The number of “major” candidates depends on the threshold chosen; the chapter should state the criterion it uses and not present a contested count as settled.[^ch48-check-numbers]

### 1.3 The primaries and the conventions

- **The Republican contest.** Trump won pluralities rather than majorities in many early states, and the winner-take-all and proportional rules varied by state; the chapter should describe how the calendar and the rules converted pluralities into delegates.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The Democratic contest.** Clinton and Sanders divided the Democratic primaries, with Sanders strong among younger and more progressive voters and Clinton strong among older and Black voters; the chapter should use the official primary returns and the delegate counts.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The conventions.** Each party’s convention nominated its candidate and adopted a platform; the chapter should give the dates and the results from the official records and not reduce the platforms to slogans.[^ch48-fec-2016]
- **The caution.** Leaked and hacked documents from the Democratic National Committee, released in 2016, are evidence of the documents’ contents and of the leak, not proof of every claim they contain; the chapter should attribute each claim to its document.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The general-election campaign

- **The campaign.** The general election ran from the conventions through three debates in the fall of 2016 and a final month dominated by the release of a 2005 recording of Trump describing sexual assault and by the FBI director’s late-October letter about the Clinton email investigation; the chapter should give the dates and the documented events and mark what remains contested.[^ch48-check-scene][^ch48-check-sources]

- **The caution.** The effect of any single campaign event on the outcome cannot be measured directly; the chapter should present the competing accounts and their evidence rather than assert a decisive cause.[^ch48-check-debate-works]

## 2. The 2016 election (2016–2017)

### 2.1 The claim: a split verdict that a system converted into a win

- **The claim.** Hillary Clinton won the national popular vote by nearly three million votes, but Trump won the Electoral College, and the chapter should hold both results at once rather than treat either as the whole story. The result, the composition of the electorate, the competing explanations, and the foreign interference that accompanied the campaign are separate evidence trails.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-mueller-report-2019]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the returns through the popular-vote and Electoral College split, the competing explanations, the documented Russian interference, and the transition, with Map 38 carried forward from §1.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-mutz-2018][^ch48-autor-2020]

### 2.2 The returns and the split verdict

- **The returns.** The FEC’s official compilation records Clinton with 65,853,514 popular votes (48.18 percent) and Trump with 62,984,828 (46.09 percent), a margin of 2,868,686 votes, while the Electoral College vote was 304 for Trump to 227 for Clinton after a small number of faithless electors; the chapter should give the totals with their source and note that some compilations differ slightly.[^ch48-fec-2016]
- **Turnout and composition.** The chapter should give total votes cast, turnout against a stated voting-age population, and the reported demographic composition of the electorate, treating exit polls as estimates with sampling error rather than a census.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** Popular-vote totals and shares vary among compilations because of write-ins, rounding, and when a canvas is finalized; the chapter should name the series it uses and not silently mix them.[^ch48-check-numbers]

### 2.3 Explaining the result: dislocation, status, and backlash

- **The economic-dislocation account.** David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson, and Kaveh Majlesi find that local labor markets exposed to the rise in Chinese import competition shifted toward the Republican Party and more extreme candidates; the chapter should give the measure of exposure, the period, and the finding.[^ch48-autor-2020]
- **The status-threat account.** Diana C. Mutz argues, from panel data, that changes in financial well-being had little effect on individual vote choice in 2016 while measures of status threat did; Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart argue that a cultural backlash among older social conservatives, rather than economic hardship alone, drove support for authoritarian-populist candidates.[^ch48-mutz-2018][^ch48-norris-inglehart-2019]
- **The place-based account.** Katherine J. Cramer’s study of rural Wisconsin emphasizes a politics of resentment rooted in place and in a perceived urban disregard; the chapter should present it as a complement to, not a substitute for, the other accounts.[^ch48-cramer-2016]
- **The caution.** These accounts use different units of analysis (counties, labor markets, individuals) and different methods; the chapter should not treat an ecological association as proof of individual motive, or a survey coefficient as proof of the whole result.[^ch48-check-debate-works][^ch48-check-numbers]

### 2.4 Russian interference and the investigation

- **The interference.** The special counsel’s report describes a Russian social-media and hacking operation that interfered in the 2016 election “in sweeping and systematic fashion” and identifies “numerous links” between Russians and the Trump campaign; the chapter should quote the report’s executive summary exactly and give the locator.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]
- **The finding on coordination.** The investigation “did not establish that members of the Trump Campaign conspired or coordinated with the Russian government in its election interference activities”; the chapter should give that sentence in full, distinguish it from a finding of no contacts, and mark the obstruction analysis separately.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]
- **The caution.** The report is a redacted prosecutorial document, not a neutral history; it records the evidence gathered under a specific legal standard, and the chapter should say so. The investigation’s origins and conduct are themselves contested and should be presented from the inspector general’s and Senate committee records where available.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 2.5 The transition

- **The handover.** The transition from the Obama to the Trump administration, the president-elect’s early appointments, and the first executive orders in January 2017 are documented; the chapter should give the dates from the official record.[^ch48-check-scope][^ch48-check-dates-appD]
- **The stakes for §2.** The section’s question is what the 2016 result did and did not decide; the chapter should hold the split verdict, the competing explanations, and the foreign interference together rather than choosing one register.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

## 3. Tax, regulatory, and executive policy (2017–2020)

### 3.1 The claim: a governing program carried through executive power and one-party legislation

- **The claim.** With unified Republican control of Congress in 2017–18, the administration and its congressional allies enacted a large tax cut and confirmed a series of judges, then governed increasingly through executive orders, rule changes, and trade actions after the House flipped in 2018; the chapter should distinguish what required legislation from what the executive could do alone.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-supreme-court-appointments]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the tax law through the deregulatory and executive program, the judicial appointments, and the trade and treaty actions, with the courts as a connecting thread.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-supreme-court-appointments][^ch48-paris-withdrawal-2020]

### 3.2 The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act

- **The statute.** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act became Public Law 115-97 on December 22, 2017, as an act of budget reconciliation; it cut the corporate income-tax rate to 21 percent and changed individual rates and deductions, with many individual provisions set to expire after 2025.[^ch48-tcja-2017]
- **The stakes.** Supporters argued the law would raise investment and wages; critics argued it would increase deficits and concentrate its benefits at the top. The chapter should give the enacted provisions, the estimated revenue effects with their methods, and the distributional analyses with their assumptions.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** Projections are not outcomes; the chapter should distinguish the enacted provisions from the later estimated effects and not present a modeled estimate as a settled result.[^ch48-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Deregulation and the executive program

- **The instruments.** The administration issued executive orders including Executive Order 13771, “Reducing Regulation and Controlling Regulatory Costs,” and rolled back or revised numerous environmental, financial, labor, and public-health rules; the chapter should count from a stated register and give the rule’s stage (proposed, final, or rescinded).[^ch48-check-sources]
- **The caution.** Claims about “the largest deregulation in history” depend on how rules, costs, and benefits are counted; the chapter should present the count’s method or avoid superlatives.[^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The stakes.** The section’s question is how far a president can change policy without legislation; the chapter should use the rulemaking record and the court challenges that followed.[^ch48-check-sources]

### 3.4 Judicial appointments and the courts

- **The justices.** Trump nominated Neil Gorsuch in 2017, Brett Kavanaugh in 2018, and Amy Coney Barrett in 2020, each confirmed by the Senate; the chapter should give the nomination and confirmation dates and the votes from the Senate record.[^ch48-supreme-court-appointments]
- **The lower courts.** The administration also confirmed a large number of appeals- and district-court judges; the chapter should give the count and the period from the official records and avoid an unsourced total.[^ch48-supreme-court-appointments][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** The chapter should describe the appointments from the Senate record and connect them to the later decisions that cited them, without presenting a court’s later ruling as what the appointing president knew at confirmation.[^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Trade, treaty withdrawals, and foreign-policy actions

- **Trade.** The administration imposed tariffs on Chinese goods under Section 301 and later reached the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement, which entered into force in 2020; the chapter should give the actions, the legal authority, and the dates from the official records.[^ch48-check-sources]
- **The withdrawals.** The administration announced withdrawal from the Paris Agreement in 2017, gave formal notice in November 2019, and left the agreement in November 2020; it withdrew from the Iran nuclear agreement in 2018. The chapter should distinguish an announced intention, a formal notice, and an effective withdrawal.[^ch48-paris-withdrawal-2020][^ch48-check-scope]
- **The caution.** “Foreign policy” here names executive actions that sometimes required legislation and sometimes did not; the chapter should state the instrument for each action rather than treat them as one category.[^ch48-check-scope][^ch48-check-sources]

## 4. Travel restrictions, border enforcement, and family separation (2017–2020)

### 4.1 The claim: immigration policy as the administration’s sharpest exercise of executive power

- **The claim.** The travel restrictions, the narrowing of asylum, and the family-separation policy were carried out largely through executive action, produced intense litigation and public conflict, and fell hardest on people with the least voice in the record; the chapter should trace each from its instrument to its consequences.[^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-doj-zero-tolerance-2018][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the travel ban and its litigation through border enforcement and asylum, the family-separation policy, and the human costs, ending on the silences in the record.[^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]

### 4.2 The travel ban and its litigation

- **The orders.** Trump signed Executive Order 13769 on January 27, 2017, suspending entry from several countries and the refugee program and producing immediate chaos at airports; a revised order, Executive Order 13780, followed on March 6, 2017, and Proclamation 9645 followed in September 2017.[^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-eo-13780-2017][^ch48-proclamation-9645-2017]
- **The decision.** In *Trump v. Hawaii*, decided June 26, 2018, the Supreme Court upheld Proclamation 9645 as a lawful exercise of the president’s authority under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f) and rejected the Establishment Clause challenge, 5–4; the chapter should give the holding and the vote and distinguish the statutory and constitutional questions.[^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018]
- **The caution.** The administration called the measures national-security tools and critics called them a “Muslim ban”; the chapter should quote each side’s language and test the claims against the orders’ terms and the Court’s reasoning.[^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Border enforcement and asylum

- **The policy.** The administration reduced the refugee-admissions ceiling, terminated or narrowed temporary protected status for several countries, and restricted asylum through rules and metering at the border; the chapter should give each action’s instrument and date.[^ch48-check-sources][^ch48-check-scope]
- **The caution.** “The border” is not a single policy or measure; the chapter should distinguish apprehensions, asylum claims, removals, and the backlog, and give each series with its definition and source.[^ch48-check-numbers]

### 4.4 Family separation and zero tolerance

- **The policy.** Attorney General Jeff Sessions announced a “zero-tolerance” policy for illegal entry on April 6, 2018, directing federal prosecutors to charge all such offenses criminally; because the children of criminally prosecuted parents could not be held with them, the policy separated families, and the administration later ended the practice amid an outcry.[^ch48-doj-zero-tolerance-2018]
- **The count.** The Department of Health and Human Services inspector general reported that, as of December 2018, the department had identified 2,737 children separated from their parents or guardians and required to be reunified under the *Ms. L* court order, and that thousands more may have been separated before the court-ordered accounting; the chapter should give the figure with its cutoff and state that it is a floor, not a total.[^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]
- **The caution.** The number of separated children is genuinely uncertain because the government did not maintain a single tracking system; the chapter should name that uncertainty rather than substituting a round number.[^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The cost.** The section should name the children and parents who could not be heard in the record and use the inspector general’s and the courts’ findings rather than inventing testimony.[^ch48-check-silences]

## 5. Courts, investigations, impeachment, and public information (2017–2021)

### 5.1 The claim: a president under investigation, and a fight over what was true

- **The claim.** The administration was under investigation from its first months, faced a special counsel and a first impeachment over its dealings with Ukraine, and repeatedly contested the factual record, from the size of its inauguration crowd to the path of a hurricane; the chapter should treat the investigations and the information fight as connected exercises in institutional contest.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-noaa-statement-2019]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Russia investigation and the Mueller report through the first impeachment and acquittal, the In Focus close reading of the altered Dorian graphic, and the media environment.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-ukraine-call-2019][^ch48-noaa-statement-2019]

### 5.2 The Russia investigation and the Mueller report

- **The investigation.** The Justice Department appointed Robert S. Mueller III as special counsel in May 2017; the office submitted its report in March 2019 and the department released a redacted version in April 2019.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]
- **The findings.** The report describes “sweeping and systematic” Russian interference, identifies “numerous links” between Russians and the campaign, and states that the investigation “did not establish” that the campaign conspired or coordinated with the Russian government; it also sets out an obstruction analysis that does not exonerate the president. The chapter should quote each finding from the report and give the locator.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]
- **The caution.** The report is one evidentiary record among several, including the Senate Intelligence Committee’s bipartisan report and the inspector general’s review of the investigation’s origins; the chapter should present them together and not treat any single document as the final word.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The first impeachment and acquittal

- **The call.** On July 25, 2019, Trump spoke by telephone with Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelensky; the White House memorandum of the call records Trump asking for a “favor” and for investigations connected to a political rival and to a discredited theory about the 2016 election. The chapter should quote the memorandum exactly and give the page locator.[^ch48-ukraine-call-2019]
- **The articles.** The House adopted H. Res. 755 on December 18, 2019, on two articles — abuse of power and obstruction of Congress; the votes were 230–197 and 229–198. The chapter should give the votes and the conduct each article charged.[^ch48-hres-755-2019]
- **The acquittal.** The Senate acquitted Trump on both articles on February 5, 2020, by votes of 48–52 and 47–53, short of the two-thirds needed; Senator Mitt Romney voted to convict on the abuse-of-power article, the only Republican to do so.[^ch48-senate-acquittal-2020]
- **The caution.** The impeachment record includes the whistleblower complaint, the House depositions, and the Senate trial record; the chapter should use the official documents and distinguish an accusation from a finding.[^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 5.4 The In Focus: the altered Dorian forecast graphic

- **The object.** In Focus reads a dated National Hurricane Center forecast graphic for Hurricane Dorian and the image as displayed in the Oval Office, on which a line appears to have been added in black marker to extend the forecast cone over Alabama.[^ch48-noaa-statement-2019][^ch48-noaa-oig-2020]
- **The sequence.** On September 1, 2019, the president wrote that Alabama would be hit; the National Weather Service office in Birmingham replied that Alabama would see no impacts; on September 4, the president displayed the altered graphic; on September 6, an unsigned NOAA statement rebuked the Birmingham office and supported the president’s original claim. The chapter must keep the September 1 statement and the September 4 display distinct, as the README requires.[^ch48-noaa-statement-2019][^ch48-noaa-oig-2020]
- **The finding.** The Department of Commerce inspector general’s June 2020 report examined the September 6 statement, its origin in a request from the White House acting chief of staff, and the internal and public backlash, and faulted NOAA’s handling; the chapter should use the report’s findings rather than the controversy’s shorthand.[^ch48-noaa-oig-2020]
- **What the close reading does.** The box should read the graphic as evidence of how an official forecast was altered and defended, not as evidence about the storm itself; it should quote the NOAA statement exactly, identify the graphic, and separate the weather record from the political claim.[^ch48-noaa-statement-2019][^ch48-check-focus]

### 5.5 The media and public information environment

- **The environment.** The period saw sustained attacks on the press, the spread of the term “fake news,” the rise of social-media misinformation, and a documented asymmetry in the reach of partisan media; the chapter should describe the media ecosystem from studies of it, not from impression.[^ch48-benkler-2018]
- **The record.** The chapter should connect the information fight to the In Focus and to the later refusal to accept the 2020 election result, while distinguishing a documented claim from a contested one.[^ch48-noaa-oig-2020][^ch48-benkler-2018]
- **The caution.** “The media” is not one actor; the chapter should distinguish the mainstream press, cable news, and social platforms and give each claim a source.[^ch48-check-sources][^ch48-benkler-2018]

## 6. COVID-19, the 2020 election, January 6, and the second impeachment (2020–2021)

### 6.1 The claim: a pandemic, a contested election, and an attack on the transfer of power

- **The claim.** The administration’s final year brought a pandemic that killed hundreds of thousands of Americans, a presidential election held under emergency conditions, a sustained attempt to overturn its result, an attack on the Capitol, and a second impeachment and acquittal; the chapter should treat these as connected events with distinct evidence trails.[^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021][^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-hres-24-2021]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the pandemic through the 2020 election, the attempt to overturn it, the January 6 attack, and the second impeachment and trial.[^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

### 6.2 The pandemic

- **The onset.** The World Health Organization characterized COVID-19 as a pandemic on March 11, 2020, and the president declared a national emergency on March 13, 2020; the chapter should give the dates and the instruments.[^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-national-emergency-2020]
- **The toll.** The CDC estimated 545,600–660,200 excess deaths in the United States from January 26, 2020, through February 27, 2021, and reported that the COVID-19 death toll surpassed 400,000 in January 2021; the chapter should give the series and the method and distinguish excess deaths from deaths attributed to the virus.[^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021][^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020]
- **The response.** The federal response included travel restrictions, guidance disputes, the operation to develop vaccines, and the authorization of the first vaccines in December 2020; the chapter should give each action and date and mark what remains contested about the response.[^ch48-fda-pfizer-eua-2020][^ch48-national-emergency-2020]
- **The silences.** The pandemic’s record over-represents officials and under-represents the dead, the sick, the disabled, and the essential workers who bore the risk; the chapter should name that asymmetry.[^ch48-check-silences][^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021]

### 6.3 The 2020 election

- **The returns.** The FEC’s official compilation records Joseph R. Biden Jr. with 306 electoral votes and Donald J. Trump with 232, and Biden with about 81.3 million popular votes (51.3 percent) to Trump’s about 74.2 million (46.8 percent), on a record total of about 158.4 million votes; the chapter should give the totals with their source.[^ch48-fec-2020]
- **The context.** The election was held during the pandemic, with expanded mail voting and altered procedures; the chapter should describe the changes and the turnout from the official records.[^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** Some widely cited popular-vote totals differ slightly by compilation; the chapter should name the series it uses.[^ch48-check-numbers]

### 6.4 The attempt to overturn the election

- **The challenge.** The president and his allies filed dozens of lawsuits and pressed state officials and legislatures to overturn the result; courts repeatedly rejected the claims, and the chapter should give the number of suits and the outcomes from a documented compilation.[^ch48-post-election-lawsuits]
- **The caution.** The chapter should distinguish a lawsuit’s dismissal on standing from a ruling on the merits, and should not treat either as proof that fraud did or did not occur beyond what the courts found.[^ch48-post-election-lawsuits][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 6.5 January 6 and the second impeachment

- **The attack.** On January 6, 2021, a mob supporting the president breached the Capitol as Congress met to count the electoral votes; the violence injured officers and others, led to deaths in the immediate aftermath, and delayed but did not prevent the certification of Biden’s victory early on January 7. The chapter should build the account from the select committee’s report and the criminal records.[^ch48-j6-report-2022]
- **The prosecutions.** More than 1,500 people were charged in connection with the attack, and leaders of the Oath Keepers and the Proud Boys were convicted of seditious conspiracy; the chapter should give the counts and the convictions from the Justice Department records and note their later legal status.[^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-check-sources]
- **The second impeachment.** The House adopted H. Res. 24 on January 13, 2021, charging incitement of insurrection, by a vote of 232–197; the Senate acquitted Trump on February 13, 2021, by a vote of 57–43, short of the two-thirds needed, with seven Republicans voting to convict.[^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2021]
- **The caution.** The 2022 select-committee report is retrospective evidence, not a 2017–21 scene; the chapter may cite it but must not stage it as contemporaneous testimony.[^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-check-scope]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

- **The elections.** Trump won the Electoral College in 2016, 304–227, while losing the popular vote by 65,853,514 to 62,984,828; Biden won in 2020, 306–232, with about 81.3 million votes to about 74.2 million.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-fec-2020]
- **The statutes and orders.** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act became Public Law 115-97 on December 22, 2017; the first travel order was signed January 27, 2017, and the Supreme Court upheld the third version in *Trump v. Hawaii* on June 26, 2018.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018]
- **The investigations and impeachments.** The special counsel reported that Russia interfered “in sweeping and systematic fashion” and that the investigation “did not establish” campaign coordination; the House impeached Trump on December 18, 2019, and again on January 13, 2021; the Senate acquitted him on February 5, 2020, and February 13, 2021.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2020][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2021]
- **The border record.** The zero-tolerance policy was announced April 6, 2018; the Department of Health and Human Services identified 2,737 separated children as of December 2018 and stated that the number was not the full scope of separations.[^ch48-doj-zero-tolerance-2018][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]
- **The pandemic and January 6.** The WHO characterized COVID-19 as a pandemic on March 11, 2020, and the CDC estimated 545,600–660,200 excess deaths through February 27, 2021; on January 6, 2021, a mob breached the Capitol and delayed but did not stop the certification of Biden’s victory.[^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

### 7.2 What is open

- **Why did Trump win?** One account emphasizes economic dislocation and the trade shock; another emphasizes status threat and cultural backlash; a third emphasizes place-based resentment and party decline. The chapter should present each at its strongest and test it against the returns and the surveys.[^ch48-autor-2020][^ch48-mutz-2018][^ch48-cramer-2016]
- **How much was a party failure and how much a party choice?** The Republican leadership largely opposed Trump’s nomination, yet he won it; whether this shows the collapse of elite coordination or its adaptation is open. The chapter should present the *Party Decides* framework and its critics.[^ch48-rosenfeld-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]
- **Did the media create the misinformation or amplify it?** Benkler, Faris, and Roberts argue that a distinct right-wing media ecosystem, rather than a symmetric collapse of the public sphere, produced the period’s asymmetric misinformation; the chapter should compare that account with market and platform explanations.[^ch48-benkler-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]
- **Was this an episode of democratic backsliding?** Levitsky and Ziblatt argue that democracies die through the slow erosion of norms by elected leaders; others stress the resilience of courts, elections, and the bureaucracy in 2020–21. The chapter should present both and use the institutions’ actual record.[^ch48-levitsky-ziblatt-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]
- **Was Trump a populist, and was the American case comparable?** Müller and Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser define populism as a thin-centered opposition of a pure people to corrupt elites; Kazin locates the American version in a long persuasion. The chapter should test the definitions against the record and against other cases.[^ch48-muller-2016][^ch48-kazin-2017][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which a campaign organized around grievance won the presidency while losing the popular vote, and in which the administration used executive power to cut taxes, appoint judges, restrict immigration, and contest investigations, before a pandemic, a contested election, and an attack on the Capitol tested the institutions that had produced the result.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-j6-report-2022]
- **Contingency.** The nomination, the general election, the tax law, the travel ban, the two impeachments, and the 2020 result were all contingent; the chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch48-check-debate-works][^ch48-check-scope]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter should hold the structural conditions — deindustrialization, polarization, the media environment, and the pandemic — together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation.[^ch48-autor-2020][^ch48-kruse-zelizer-2019][^ch48-check-scope]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Between 2015 and 2021 the United States nominated and elected a candidate who had never held office, cut taxes and remade the federal judiciary, sharply restricted immigration, impeached and acquitted its president twice, suffered a pandemic that killed hundreds of thousands, and watched a mob attempt to stop the transfer of power; the chapter’s question — how a campaign organized around grievance exercised and contested presidential power — is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

## Legacies

The tax law and the judicial appointments outlasted the administration that made them. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act’s corporate-rate cut and its expiring individual provisions shaped federal revenue and later tax debates, and the three Supreme Court appointments reshaped the Court that later decided cases on abortion, administrative power, and presidential immunity; the chapter should trace these to their sources rather than present them as a settled verdict.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-supreme-court-appointments]

The immigration policies and the investigations left institutions and precedents that later administrations would defend, unwind, or extend. The travel ban’s framework, the family-separation record, and the inspector general’s findings on the Dorian statement remained live questions, and the prosecutions of January 6 defendants continued for years after the attack.[^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019][^ch48-noaa-oig-2020][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

And the contest over the 2020 election became the ground on which the next years were fought. The refusal to accept the result, the attack on the Capitol, and the second acquittal set terms that Chapter 49 takes up as it examines the Biden years and the elections that followed.[^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-check-ch49]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The turn was made in a global context of populist and nationalist movements, from Brexit to elections across Europe and Asia; the chapter should compare the American case with others rather than treating it as unique or as a mere copy.[^ch48-muller-2016][^ch48-norris-inglehart-2019]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, candidates, officials, and journalists and under-represents the migrants, the detained and separated families, the sick and the dead, the low-wage workers, and the imprisoned. The chapter should name these asymmetries rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch48-check-silences][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 47 owns the 2008 election and the Obama-era recovery and polarization; Chapter 49 owns the Biden years and the 2024 election. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch48-check-ch47][^ch48-check-ch49]
