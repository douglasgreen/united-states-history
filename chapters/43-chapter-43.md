# Chapter 43. The Seventies: Stagflation, Détente, and Malaise (1973–1980)

> “The threat is nearly invisible in ordinary ways. It is a crisis of confidence. It is a crisis that strikes at the very heart and soul and spirit of our national will.”[^ch43-carter-confidence]
>
> — Jimmy Carter, address to the nation on energy and national goals, July 15, 1979.[^ch43-carter-confidence]

> “As we search through the letters of Paul, and all the other New Testament letters, we see that the church is given no command other than this Great Commission to take the message of Christ to a dying world.”[^ch43-falwell-sermon]
>
> — Jerry Falwell, “Ministers and Marches,” sermon at Thomas Road Baptist Church, Lynchburg, Virginia, March 21, 1965.[^ch43-falwell-sermon]

> “The last days of the Sixties signaled the end of the post-World War II era, with its baby boom and economic boom, its anticommunist hysteria and expansive government, and the beginning of another age, the long 1970s, which defined the terms of contemporary American life.”[^ch43-schulman-excerpt]
>
> — Bruce J. Schulman, *The Seventies: The Great Shift in American Culture, Society, and Politics* (2001).[^ch43-schulman-excerpt]

Three voices, three verdicts. Carter told the nation that its deepest problem was a loss of confidence. Falwell, in 1965, told his congregation that the church had no commission to reform the world through politics; by 1979 he had helped organize a movement to do exactly that. Schulman argues that the decade’s importance lay not in its mood but in a structural break with the postwar order. They disagree about whether the crisis was spiritual, political, or economic, and about whether the 1970s were a decline or a hinge. Their disagreement frames the chapter’s question: what made the postwar settlement harder to sustain?[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-falwell-sermon][^ch43-schulman-excerpt][^ch43-check-epigraphs]

July 15, 1979, was a Sunday. Carter spoke from the Oval Office that evening in a televised address. He began by noting that exactly three years earlier he had accepted his party’s nomination for the presidency. Ten days earlier, he said, he had planned yet another speech on energy — the fifth — and had instead decided to “reach out and listen to the voices of America.” He invited to Camp David “people from almost every segment of our society — business and labor, teachers and preachers, Governors, mayors, and private citizens,” and then traveled to hear others. The *Washington Post* called the interval an “extraordinary 10-day ‘domestic summit conference.’”[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-wapo-confidence]

The speech turned from energy to something deeper. “The true problems of our Nation are much deeper,” Carter said, “deeper than gasoline lines or energy shortages, deeper even than inflation or recession.” The country faced, he said, “a crisis of confidence” visible in “the growing doubt about the meaning of our own lives and in the loss of a unity of purpose for our Nation.” He listed the symptoms: “paralysis and stagnation and drift,” a majority believing the next five years would be worse than the last, two-thirds of the people not voting, declining productivity, and “a growing disrespect for government and for churches and for schools, the news media, and other institutions.” Then he returned to energy, setting a goal that the nation would never use more foreign oil than it had in 1977, announcing import quotas, and proposing an energy security corporation, a solar bank, a mandate that utilities cut their oil use by half within a decade, a conservation program, standby gasoline rationing, and a windfall profits tax.[^ch43-carter-confidence]

The address was initially popular. Hendrik Hertzberg, who wrote speeches for Carter, later recalled that it generated a record volume of positive mail and that Carter’s approval rating rose eleven points overnight; the political damage, he argued, came days later with the cabinet firings. Carter never used the word “malaise” in the speech. The label was applied afterward, and it became the name of the episode. What the record can show is the date, the setting, the text, the preparation method Carter described, and the proposals. It cannot show what every listener felt, how representative the Camp David guests were, or that the speech caused the politics that followed. In July 1979 no one in the room knew that the embassy in Tehran would be seized in November, that Soviet troops would enter Afghanistan in December, or that Carter would lose the 1980 election. The question was already older than the speech: what made the postwar settlement harder to sustain?[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-newyorker-malaise][^ch43-miller-carter-keys]

This chapter covers the years from 1973 to 1980 and asks that question. Its narrative runs from the first oil shock of 1973–74, whose onset belongs to Chapter 42, through the second shock of 1979–80, the inflation and productivity slowdown, the fiscal crisis of the cities, the taxpayer revolts, the diplomacy of détente and its collapse, the Ford and Carter presidencies, and the rights conflicts that divided the nation Carter addressed. It draws on presidential speeches and papers, the *Foreign Relations of the United States* series, Federal Reserve and Bureau of Labor Statistics data, court opinions and constitutional texts, agency histories, contemporaneous journalism, and the secondary literature.[^ch43-check-dates-appD]

Several of its names are contested. “Stagflation” described the combination of inflation and stagnation that the postwar tools had not been built to handle. “Malaise” was a later label for a speech that did not use the word. “Détente” described a managed rivalry, not a friendship. “Deindustrialization” names a pattern of plant closings and employment loss, not a single cause. “Tax revolt” describes a series of ballot measures and political campaigns, not a unanimous public. “The New Right” and “neoliberalism” are analytical labels for movements and ideas that their participants did not always use. Each is a term to define, not a description to assume.

The record over-represents presidents, officials, and journalists. It under-represents the workers who lost their jobs, the poor, women outside the political class, and the Iranian and Afghan peoples whose countries the United States dealt with. Oil prices, inflation rates, and election returns measure different things; each figure is given with its dates, units, and denominators. Where the archive is thin, the silence is named rather than filled with a composite witness.[^ch43-check-silences][^ch43-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements, organized thematically around questions because the economic, diplomatic, and rights stories overlap in time and are not one strict chronology. §1 examines the second oil shock, inflation, the productivity slowdown, the Volcker turn, and deindustrialization, with Map 34. §2 examines the New York City fiscal crisis, the taxpayer revolts, and Proposition 13, with the In Focus on Article XIII A. §3 examines the Helsinki Final Act, the fall of Saigon, the Committee on the Present Danger, and SALT II. §4 examines the Ford and Carter presidencies, Camp David, the Panama Canal treaties, the Iran hostage crisis, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. §5 examines the ERA’s defeat, gay rights, evangelical and New Right organizing, and *Gregg v. Georgia*. §6 asks how historians have weighed failure, transition, structural constraint, and the groundwork for market-oriented governance. The chapter begins in the Oval Office on July 15, 1979, when a president told the nation that its problem was deeper than energy.[^ch43-check-dates-appD]

## 1. Energy, inflation, and deindustrialization (1973–1980)

### 1.1 The claim: three supports of the postwar boom weakened at once

**The claim.** The long postwar expansion had rested on cheap energy, rising productivity, and a stable dollar anchored to the fixed exchange rates of the Bretton Woods system. In the 1970s all three weakened together, producing “stagflation” — high inflation alongside high unemployment — that confounded the policy tools of the postwar settlement.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-bls-cpi][^ch43-fed-volcker]

The section runs from the first oil shock of 1973–74, whose onset Chapter 42 owns, through the second shock that followed the Iranian Revolution, the inflation of 1979–80, the Federal Reserve’s turn in October 1979, and the deindustrialization of the old industrial belt, including the closing of the Youngstown mills in 1977.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-belt-youngstown]

### 1.2 The second oil shock and the energy crisis

The second shock began with a revolution, not a decision in Washington. Iranian oil output fell by 4.8 million barrels a day — about 7 percent of world production — by January 1979, as the revolution ended the reign of Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. Oil prices more than doubled between April 1979 and April 1980. The Federal Reserve’s history argues that fear of further disruption and speculative demand may have mattered more than the lost barrels themselves. The physical shortage, the price response, and the political reaction were three different events, and no single cause explains all three.[^ch43-fed-oil1979]

The gasoline lines were the visible face of the dependence. Carter called the “long lines which have made millions of you spend aggravating hours waiting for gasoline” the direct consequence of imported oil, and he called that dependence the direct cause of the lines and a cause of “the increased inflation and unemployment that we now face.” In the same spring, on March 28, 1979, the Unit 2 reactor at Three Mile Island, near Middletown, Pennsylvania, suffered a partial meltdown. It was the most serious accident in the history of American commercial nuclear power, though the small radioactive releases were judged to have had no detectable health effects on workers or the public. The accident deepened public unease about nuclear power in the same spring as the gasoline lines.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-nrc-tmi]

### 1.3 Inflation and the productivity slowdown

Inflation had already begun to accelerate before the second shock. Consumer inflation rose from below 5 percent in early 1976 to nearly 7 percent by March 1979, and it was running at about 9 percent in July 1979. The largest 12-month increase of the era ran from March 1979 to March 1980, when the consumer price index rose 14.8 percent. Those are 12-month changes, not annual averages, and the index’s base period matters; each figure is given with its series and dates.[^ch43-bls-cpi][^ch43-fed-volcker]

The deeper problem was productivity. The growth of output per hour slowed sharply after the early 1970s. One widely cited estimate puts the fall in nonmanufacturing labor-productivity growth from about 2.95 percent a year in 1948–72 to about 1.29 percent a year in 1972–96. The causes remain disputed: the end of the postwar catch-up, the oil shocks, the shift from manufacturing to services, regulation, and measurement all have their advocates. The slowdown is an estimate with competing explanations, not a single verdict.[^ch43-gordon-productivity]

Why did productivity matter? Slower output per hour made it harder to raise wages without raising prices, and it narrowed the fiscal room that had paid for the postwar expansion. The connection is an argument about mechanism, not a license to read every plant closing or price increase as its result.[^ch43-gordon-productivity][^ch43-check-numbers]

### 1.4 The Volcker turn

In July 1979, as part of a cabinet reshuffle, Carter nominated G. William Miller, the Federal Reserve chairman, to be treasury secretary and nominated Paul A. Volcker to replace him at the Fed. Inflation was then running at about 9 percent. At his confirmation hearing Volcker pledged that the Fed would “have to call the shots as we see them” and made fighting inflation his first priority.[^ch43-fed-volcker]

In October 1979 the Federal Open Market Committee changed how it conducted policy. Instead of targeting the federal funds rate directly, it adopted new operating procedures aimed at controlling the growth of money and bank reserves. The change, often dated to the committee’s meeting of October 6, 1979, led to sharply higher interest rates. The full effect — the recession of the early 1980s and the eventual disinflation — belongs chiefly to Chapter 44. In 1979 the outcome was not known, and the decision cannot be credited with a result it had not yet produced.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-sf-fed-1979]

### 1.5 Deindustrialization: Map 34

On September 19, 1977, Youngstown Sheet & Tube announced that it was closing its Campbell Works, putting about 5,000 people out of work. Within five years roughly 50,000 jobs disappeared from the Mahoning Valley. Local accounts call the day “Black Monday.” The closing was one documented instance of a wider regional change, not the whole of it. It shows what deindustrialization looked like in a place; it does not show that one plant explains a national pattern.[^ch43-belt-youngstown]

Map 34, “Where the work went, 1969–1979,” is this chapter’s registered visual. It will show where employment and population moved, distinguishing absolute employment change, percentage change, and population change so that a reader cannot mistake a small-count area with high percentage growth for the largest gain, or decline for stasis. Its source will be County Business Patterns, supported by App. B, Table 7. The map describes regional outcomes. It cannot establish why any plant closed, attribute motive to any firm, or prove that deindustrialization alone caused the politics that followed.[^ch43-check-map34]

The human record is uneven. It is fuller for firms, unions, and officials than for the workers who lost their jobs and the communities that absorbed the loss. That asymmetry is named rather than spoken over.[^ch43-check-silences]

## 2. Cities, taxpayers, and Proposition 13 (1975–1979)

### 2.1 The claim: the fiscal crisis moved from Washington to the cities and the voters

**The claim.** As inflation and slow growth squeezed public budgets, the fiscal crisis of the mid-1970s was fought out at the level of cities and states. The taxpayer revolts that followed rewrote the terms of urban governance and public finance.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-ppic-prop13]

The section runs from the near-default of New York City in 1975 through the creation of state oversight machinery, to the California ballot measure of June 1978 and the wider taxpayer reaction, with the In Focus on the text of Article XIII A.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-prop13-original]

### 2.2 The New York City fiscal crisis

New York City’s finances had come under increasing strain from rising demand for social programs, job loss, middle-class flight to the suburbs, and heavy reliance on short-term borrowing. In 1975 the strain became a crisis when the Urban Development Corporation defaulted on some of its bonds and the city’s own bonds came under scrutiny.[^ch43-nys-fcb]

The state responded first. In April 1975 it advanced $800 million to keep the city operating. The Legislature created the Municipal Assistance Corporation in June 1975, and in September 1975 it passed the Financial Emergency Act, creating the Emergency Financial Control Board, which took control of the city’s finances and would not release money without approving the city’s budget. In December 1975 President Gerald R. Ford signed the Seasonal Financing Act, authorizing the Treasury to make almost $6 billion available to the city over the next two and a half years. The federal help came after Ford had first signaled his refusal to bail out the city. The *New York Daily News* captured the episode in a headline that became its shorthand: “Ford to City: Drop Dead.” The headline ran on October 30, 1975, the day after Ford’s speech, and it was the newspaper’s wording, not the president’s.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-fcb-about][^ch43-dailynews-drop-dead]

The crisis was the product of the city’s revenue structure, its borrowing practices, its service obligations, and the national economy. It was not evidence that municipal government was simply profligate.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 In Focus: the text of Article XIII A (Proposition 13)

**In Focus.** On June 6, 1978, California voters approved Proposition 13 with about 65 percent support. The measure added Article XIII A to the state constitution. Its first section set the limit that became the measure’s signature: “The maximum amount of any ad valorem tax on real property shall not exceed one percent (1%) of the full cash value of such property.” Section 2 defined “full cash value” as the county assessor’s 1975–76 valuation, or the appraised value at purchase, new construction, or a change of ownership, and allowed the base to rise by no more than 2 percent a year. Section 3 required a two-thirds vote of the Legislature for state tax increases. Section 4 required a two-thirds vote of local electors to approve special taxes. Read together, the sections were a set of limits on the power to tax, not a single rate. The article ran to six sections, and Section 1 alone contained two subdivisions. The current text has been amended — most notably by Proposition 39 in 2000 — so the box quotes the original 1978 text. The initiative was a political act; the article is a legal instrument, and the difference matters.[^ch43-prop13-original][^ch43-prop13-official][^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-check-rights]

### 2.4 The taxpayer revolt beyond California

Proposition 13 was the best-known of a series of tax-limitation measures. Massachusetts voters approved Proposition 2½ in 1980. The California measure was one case within a national movement whose scope, timing, and causes require their own evidence. The limits shifted the cost of local services and reduced the growth of the property tax base, with effects that fell unevenly on schools, cities, and the residents who depended on them. Those effects are described from evidence, not as a foregone conclusion.[^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-mass-prop25]

## 3. Helsinki, Saigon, and détente’s critics (1973–1979)

### 3.1 The claim: détente was both an achievement and a provocation

**The claim.** Détente’s architecture — arms control, the Helsinki framework, and the partial opening to China — produced real agreements, but it also gave its critics a target. By 1979 the critics had the initiative in the Senate and in the emerging foreign-policy debate.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-state-salt2]

The section runs from the Helsinki Final Act of 1975 through the fall of Saigon and its aftermath, to the Committee on the Present Danger and the SALT II agreement of June 1979.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-state-saigon][^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-state-salt2]

### 3.2 The Helsinki Final Act

The Helsinki Final Act was signed by 35 nations on August 1, 1975, concluding the Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe. It was not a treaty and not legally binding. It dealt with its subjects in four “baskets”: political and security principles; economic and trade relations; human rights and freedom of movement; and procedures for future meetings. The Soviet Union sought recognition of the postwar borders in Eastern Europe. Western governments accepted the borders in exchange for the human-rights and freedom-of-movement language of Basket III. Gerald R. Ford later cited the accords among his greatest foreign-policy achievements, and dissidents and monitoring groups used Basket III in the years that followed.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-ford-helsinki]

The accords were an ambiguous instrument. They neither ended the Cold War nor were simply a defeat, and their consequences were uneven across the signatory states. They were a framework that different governments and movements used for different purposes.[^ch43-ford-helsinki][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 3.3 The fall of Saigon and the war’s end

The Paris settlement of January 1973 belongs to Chapter 42. The war continued, and North Vietnamese forces took Saigon on April 30, 1975, ending the Republic of Vietnam. The fall marked the close of the American war and a shock to the postwar claim that American power could shape outcomes abroad at will.[^ch43-state-saigon]

The aftermath included the evacuation of Americans and Vietnamese allies, and the arrival of refugees whose fate the record often leaves to the margin. The record of their evacuation and resettlement is thinner than the record of the American withdrawal.[^ch43-state-saigon][^ch43-check-silences]

### 3.4 Détente’s critics and the Committee on the Present Danger

The Committee on the Present Danger was formed in 1976, announcing itself soon after the presidential election, to promote a strong defense policy. Its creation was prompted especially by skepticism about the arms-control negotiations of the détente era. Its founders included former senior officials such as Paul H. Nitze and Eugene V. Rostow.[^ch43-cpd-oac]

The “Team B” exercise of 1976 gave the critics an official venue. Outside analysts were asked to produce an alternative assessment of Soviet strategic objectives, and they concluded that the intelligence community had underestimated the scope and intensity of the Soviet buildup. The report was an experiment in competitive analysis, not a consensus document. The contest was about the interpretation of evidence, not only about policy preference.[^ch43-team-b-frus][^ch43-cpd-oac]

The critics’ argument deserves its strongest form: verified arms control required a defensible military balance, and the Soviet buildup was real. Whether the critics’ estimates were right, and whether their prescriptions would have produced a safer balance, are separate questions.[^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 SALT II and the limit of détente

Carter and Soviet General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev signed the SALT II treaty in Vienna on June 18, 1979. It capped strategic nuclear delivery vehicles and MIRVed systems and continued the framework begun at Vladivostok in 1974, but it did not achieve the deep reductions some had sought.[^ch43-state-salt2]

Carter transmitted the treaty to the Senate, but after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 the Senate chose not to ratify it, and Carter withdrew it from consideration on January 2, 1980. Ratification was a decision taken within changed international circumstances, not a verdict on the treaty’s merits.[^ch43-state-salt2][^ch43-politico-salt2]

## 4. Ford, Carter, Camp David, Panama, Iran, and Afghanistan (1974–1980)

### 4.1 The claim: notable settlements in a constrained presidency

**The claim.** The Ford and Carter presidencies produced lasting diplomatic settlements — the Egypt-Israel treaty, the Panama Canal treaties, and the SALT II framework — while appearing unable to control energy prices, inflation, or events abroad. The gap between the diplomatic record and the political perception is the section’s subject.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]

The section runs from Ford’s succession and the 1976 election through Carter’s human-rights diplomacy, Camp David and the Panama treaties, the Iranian Revolution and the hostage crisis, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.[^ch43-miller-carter-keys][^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-iran-hostages][^ch43-state-afghanistan]

### 4.2 Ford and the interregnum

Gerald R. Ford became president in August 1974 and pardoned Richard M. Nixon in September 1974; Chapter 42 owns both events. This chapter takes up the Ford presidency as a period of transition, including the “Whip Inflation Now” campaign, the Helsinki signing, the New York fiscal crisis, and the 1976 election, in which Carter narrowly defeated Ford.[^ch43-check-ch42][^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-miller-carter-keys]

The Ford years were not a mere interval. The Helsinki accords, the fiscal-crisis response, and the 1976 campaign set terms Carter inherited.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-nys-fcb]

### 4.3 Carter, human rights, and the Panama Canal treaties

Carter took office in January 1977 promising a foreign policy that emphasized human rights. He pardoned Vietnam War draft evaders, wrote in support of the Soviet dissident Andrei Sakharov, and spoke at Notre Dame in May 1977 of a new direction that put fundamental human rights ahead of anti-communism.[^ch43-miller-carter-keys]

Carter and Panama’s Brigadier General Omar Torrijos signed two canal treaties on September 7, 1977. The Senate ratified the Neutrality Treaty on March 16, 1978, by a vote of 68 to 32, and the Panama Canal Treaty on April 18, 1978, by the same margin. The treaties entered into force on October 1, 1979, and provided for Panamanian control of the canal by the end of 1999.[^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-un-panama]

The treaty fight was a domestic political ordeal as well as a diplomatic settlement. The agreement and the opposition it provoked belong together.[^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 Camp David and the Egypt-Israel treaty

After Anwar Sadat’s November 1977 announcement that he would go to Jerusalem and the deadlock that followed, Carter called a summit at Camp David. It met from September 5 to 17, 1978, and produced two “Framework” documents rather than a formal peace agreement, laying out the principles of an Egyptian-Israeli peace and a formula for Palestinian self-government in Gaza and the West Bank.[^ch43-state-campdavid]

After further negotiations, including Carter’s March 1979 trip to Israel, the Egyptian-Israeli Peace Treaty was signed on March 26, 1979. Camp David was a genuine achievement that left the Palestinian question unresolved, and the treaty was not a comprehensive Middle East settlement.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-egypt-israel-treaty]

### 4.5 Iran: from the “island of stability” to the hostage crisis

The Iranian Revolution toppled the Shah, whose regime Carter had visited in December 1977 and described as “an island of stability” in the Middle East. By early 1979 the Shah had fallen and Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini’s Islamic republic was taking shape, and the disruption helped drive the second oil shock.[^ch43-miller-carter-keys][^ch43-fed-oil1979]

On November 4, 1979, Iranian students seized the United States embassy in Tehran and detained more than 50 Americans, from the chargé d’affaires to junior staff. The State Department records that the diplomats were held for 444 days. The crisis dominated the news, made the administration look weak, and sapped its vitality.[^ch43-state-iran-hostages]

This chapter owns the seizure and the first year of the crisis. The release in January 1981, and therefore the full 444-day span read as a completed episode, belong to Chapter 44. The crisis appears here as it did to Americans in 1979–80, without the knowledge of its ending.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-dates-appD]

### 4.6 Afghanistan and the end of détente

At the end of December 1979 the Soviet Union sent thousands of troops into Afghanistan and assumed control of Kabul and large portions of the country, beginning a decade-long war and marking the only time the Soviet Union invaded a country outside the Eastern Bloc.[^ch43-state-afghanistan]

In an address to the nation on January 4, 1980, Carter said that “Fifty thousand heavily armed Soviet troops have crossed the border and are now dispersed throughout Afghanistan, attempting to conquer the fiercely independent Muslim people of that country.” He called the invasion “an extremely serious threat to peace” and linked it to the ongoing hostage crisis. The administration responded with measures including a grain embargo and a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, and the invasion led Carter to withdraw SALT II from the Senate.[^ch43-app-afghanistan-address][^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-politico-salt2]

The invasion and the American response were events whose consequences — the longer Afghan war, the renewed Cold War, the eventual Soviet withdrawal — were not known in 1980.[^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-check-dates-appD]

### 4.7 The constrained presidency and the 1980 campaign

By the spring of 1980 the administration faced inflation, the unresolved hostage crisis, and the political challenge of the coming campaign. The account ends on the eve of that contest, not on its result.[^ch43-check-ch44]

The next chapter takes up the campaign and the decade that followed. This chapter leaves the president in the Oval Office, the economy under strain, and Americans held in Tehran.[^ch43-state-iran-hostages][^ch43-check-bridge]

## 5. Rights, the ERA struggle, evangelical organizing, and the New Right (1972–1982)

### 5.1 The claim: rights widened and a countermovement organized

**The claim.** The same decade that widened rights for women and gay people produced an organized countermovement that fused religious conviction with a political program and reshaped the Republican coalition. The gains and the backlash are two parts of one story.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-falwell-pbs]

The section runs from the 1972 ERA passage, owned by Chapter 42, through the ratification struggle and the 1977 National Women’s Conference, the gay-rights advances and Harvey Milk’s career and murder, the founding of the Moral Majority, and the Supreme Court’s reinstatement of the death penalty in *Gregg v. Georgia*.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-justia-gregg]

### 5.2 The Equal Rights Amendment and its defeat

The proposed Equal Rights Amendment was fifty-one words long. It was first introduced in 1923 in a form suggested by Alice Paul, and Congress passed it in March 1972 and sent it to the states. The ratification struggle, the extension, and the defeat belong to this chapter.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-check-ch42]

Phyllis Schlafly, a conservative activist and author, founded the Eagle Forum in 1972 and led the STOP ERA campaign. She argued that the amendment would abolish protections for women and threaten the family. She helped organize a counter-rally to the 1977 National Women’s Conference in Houston, a gathering that had infused the ratification effort with new energy.[^ch43-nwhm-schlafly][^ch43-loc-era]

The original deadline was extended to 1982, but no new states ratified during the extension, and the amendment failed, three states short. On the night of June 30, 1982, supporters held vigils, while opponents celebrated what Schlafly called “a great victory for women” and called for “a mighty movement” to “set America on the right path.”[^ch43-loc-era]

The ERA fight was a contest between two organized camps of women, not a simple story of progress delayed. Schlafly’s case drew on a defense of the family and a fear that formal equality would erase protections many women relied on; the amendment’s defeat was a political outcome, not a verdict on women’s equality.[^ch43-check-debate-works][^ch43-nwhm-schlafly]

### 5.3 Gay rights, Harvey Milk, and the Briggs Initiative

Harvey Milk, born in 1930, was elected to the San Francisco Board of Supervisors in 1977 and became the first openly gay elected official in California. He was assassinated on November 27, 1978, together with Mayor George Moscone, by a disgruntled former supervisor, Dan White.[^ch43-glbt-milk]

The statewide Briggs Initiative, which would have barred gay and lesbian teachers and their supporters from public-school employment, was defeated at the polls in November 1978. After White was convicted of manslaughter and given a light sentence, mass protests known as the White Night Riots followed in San Francisco.[^ch43-nara-milk][^ch43-glbt-milk]

The record of the period’s gay-liberation history is uneven and retrospective. Statements are attributed to the witnesses who made them, later recollections are dated, and the roles of people of color and gender-nonconforming people whom early accounts often minimized are noted.[^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-check-silences]

### 5.4 Evangelical organizing and the New Right

Jerry Falwell, a fundamentalist preacher, had declared in 1965 that the church had no commission to take up political reform. He changed course in the 1970s, dismayed by the 1973 *Roe v. Wade* decision and influenced by Francis Schaeffer. He staged “I Love America” rallies that attacked the ERA, homosexuality, pornography, and women’s liberation, and in 1979 he helped found the Moral Majority, which mobilized conservative Christian voters.[^ch43-falwell-sermon][^ch43-falwell-pbs]

The Moral Majority joined tax, race, and family-values organizing to a religious appeal, and it became one of the vehicles by which the New Right entered the Republican coalition. The organizing was the work of identifiable people and institutions, not a spontaneous national mood.[^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-check-debate-works]

The movement’s reach and its effect on the 1980 election are contested. The 1980 contest is named here; its outcome belongs to Chapter 44.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 *Gregg v. Georgia* and the death penalty

In *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, decided July 2, 1976, the Supreme Court upheld Georgia’s revised death-penalty statute against an Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment challenge. The Court held that the new procedures guided the sentencer’s discretion and thus avoided the arbitrary imposition it had condemned in *Furman v. Georgia* (1972). The decision allowed executions to resume.[^ch43-justia-gregg]

*Furman* and *Roe* belong to Chapter 42; *Gregg* and the death-penalty debate of the later 1970s belong here. The decision was a legal holding with a contested aftermath, not a verdict on the morality of capital punishment.[^ch43-justia-gregg][^ch43-check-ch42]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The second oil shock and the inflation that followed are documented. Oil more than doubled between April 1979 and April 1980, and the consumer price index rose 14.8 percent in the 12 months from March 1979 to March 1980. The Federal Reserve changed its operating procedures in October 1979, and interest rates rose sharply.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-bls-cpi][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-sf-fed-1979]

New York City required state and then federal help. The state created the Municipal Assistance Corporation and the Emergency Financial Control Board in 1975, and the federal Seasonal Financing Act followed in December 1975. California voters approved Proposition 13 on June 6, 1978, with about 65 percent support, adding Article XIII A to the state constitution.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-fcb-about][^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-prop13-original]

The Helsinki Final Act was signed on August 1, 1975. It was a non-binding statement among 35 nations organized in four baskets, the third on human rights and freedom of movement. Saigon fell on April 30, 1975, ending the American war in Vietnam. The Committee on the Present Danger formed in 1976, and SALT II was signed in Vienna on June 18, 1979.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-ford-helsinki][^ch43-state-saigon][^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-state-salt2]

Camp David produced frameworks, and the Egypt-Israel treaty followed on March 26, 1979. The Panama Canal treaties were ratified in 1978 and entered into force in 1979. The embassy in Tehran was seized on November 4, 1979, and the hostages were held for 444 days. The Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan at the end of December 1979, and Carter withdrew SALT II on January 2, 1980.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-egypt-israel-treaty][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-un-panama][^ch43-state-iran-hostages][^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-politico-salt2]

The ERA failed to be ratified by its extended 1982 deadline. Harvey Milk was assassinated in November 1978. Jerry Falwell helped found the Moral Majority in 1979, and *Gregg v. Georgia* reinstated the death penalty in 1976.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-justia-gregg]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the decade a failure or a pivot? The “malaise” reading treats the 1970s as a crisis of confidence and decline. The structural-pivot reading treats the decade as the hinge between the postwar order and the present. Schulman and Stein emphasize rupture and transition; accounts closer to the period’s own diagnosis emphasize decline. Each position explains something the other misses.[^ch43-schulman-excerpt][^ch43-stein-pivotal][^ch43-check-debate-works]

How much was structure and how much was choice? One position locates the decade’s outcomes in oil, productivity, and the end of Bretton Woods. Another locates them in decisions — Volcker’s turn, the tax revolts, deregulation, and détente’s management — that could have gone otherwise. The precipitating shocks must be separated from the contingent choices made within them.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-check-debate-works]

Did conservative organizing cause the realignment or reflect it? The movement account treats grassroots conservative organization around taxes, race, and family values as a cause of the political turn. The structural account treats it as a response to economic and social change. The question does not reduce to one causal arrow.[^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-check-debate-works]

Was “malaise” an interpretation or a measurement? Carter never used the word. The label was applied afterward, and the speech was initially well received. “Malaise” is a contested interpretation, not a description of a universal national mood.[^ch43-newyorker-malaise][^ch43-carter-confidence]

What did the decade’s diplomatic achievements accomplish? The Egypt-Israel treaty, the Panama treaties, and SALT II were real agreements with contested legacies. What they settled and what they left open are different questions.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-salt2]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the postwar settlement was eroded by structural forces — energy, productivity, and the dollar — and by contingent choices — a monetary turn, a taxpayer revolt, a diplomatic opening, a revolution, and an invasion. No participant knew how the decade would end.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]

The Volcker turn, the Panama ratification, the Camp David summit, the decision to admit the Shah for medical treatment, and the response to the Afghan invasion were decisions that could have gone otherwise. The alternatives actually considered, and why they were rejected, are part of the record. Structure and choice must be held together, not played off against each other.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-shah-admission][^ch43-check-debate-works]

Between 1973 and 1980 the United States discovered that the abundance on which the postwar settlement rested was not guaranteed, that its government could negotiate lasting settlements and still appear powerless, and that the rights it widened for some of its people were the grievances around which others organized. That synthesis is an argument about how the pieces fit, not a verdict on who was right.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-state-campdavid]

## Legacies

The years from 1973 to 1980 left a more constrained federal government and a more contested presidency. The taxpayer revolts and the New York fiscal crisis began a long renegotiation of who pays for local government and public services. Proposition 13 and the measures that followed fixed limits that still shape state and local budgets, and the fiscal crisis of a great city became an argument about the reach of government itself. These developments connect to the present as a documented change in public finance, not as a political verdict.[^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-prop13-original][^ch43-nys-fcb]

The decade also left the market turn and the religious right. The October 1979 monetary reform opened the long fight against inflation that would define the following decade, and the organizing around taxes, family, and faith built a movement that the 1980 campaign would test. Neither outcome was foreordained in 1979; both were contingent results of choices made under pressure.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-check-ch44]

And it left the hostage crisis as a template and a wound. The seizure of the Tehran embassy and the long captivity that followed fixed a way of talking about American power and vulnerability that outlasted the individuals, and the Afghan invasion reopened a Cold War the decade had tried to soften. Those connections are documented, and the questions they raised about who the nation was and what it could do are the question the book keeps asking.[^ch43-state-iran-hostages][^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-falwell-pbs]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The decade’s politics were made in Riyadh, Tehran, Cairo, and Kabul as well as Washington. - **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, officials, and journalists and under-represents working people, the poor, women outside the political class, and the Iranian and Afghan peoples. The displaced workers of the Mahoning Valley, the gasoline-line motorists, and ordinary Iranians and Afghans appear mostly through the eyes of officials and reporters. ## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1973 and 1980 the United States discovered that the energy, productivity, and confidence on which the postwar settlement rested were not guaranteed. Its government negotiated lasting settlements in the Middle East and Panama and still appeared powerless before gasoline lines, inflation, and a hostage crisis; it widened rights for women and gay people while a countermovement organized around the family and the flag. The chapter’s question — what made the postwar settlement harder to sustain? — is answered by a decade in which structural forces and human choices pressed at once, and in which no participant knew how the pressure would resolve.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on July 17, 1980, when Ronald Reagan accepted the Republican nomination for president in Detroit and asked a nation weary of limits to trust its own values and choose a different course. Chapter 44 takes up the conservative realignment, the recession and deregulation, rearmament and the end of the Cold War, and the response to the AIDS crisis. This chapter ends with a constrained presidency, an economy under strain, and Americans held in Tehran; the next turns to the campaign and the decade that the 1970s made possible.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-bridge][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
