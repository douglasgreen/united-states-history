# Chapter 18. Manifest Destiny and the Mexican-American War (1844–1850)

> “… the fulfillment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions.”[^ch18-osullivan-manifest]
>
> — John L. O’Sullivan, “Annexation,” *The United States Magazine and Democratic Review*, July–August 1845[^ch18-osullivan-manifest]

> “But now, after reiterated menaces, Mexico has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.”[^ch18-polk-war-message]
>
> — President James K. Polk, Special Message to Congress, May 11, 1846[^ch18-polk-war-message]

> “[W]hether the particular spot of soil on which the blood of our citizens was so shed, was, or was not, our own soil.”[^ch18-lincoln-spot]
>
> — Representative Abraham Lincoln, “Spot Resolutions,” introduced in the House of Representatives, December 22, 1847[^ch18-lincoln-spot]

> “[T]he armed conflict between Mexico and the United States from 1846 to 1848 was the product of deliberate aggression.”[^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]
>
> — Jesús Velasco-Márquez, “A Mexican Viewpoint on the War With the United States,” *Voices of Mexico* no. 41 (1997): 49–57[^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]

Four voices, four ways of naming the same war. An editor told the nation that expansion was providence. A president told Congress that Mexico had attacked the United States on American soil. A freshman congressman asked which soil. A Mexican historian called the conflict the product of deliberate aggression. They disagree about whether the war was destiny, defense, theft, or a calamity that Mexico did not choose. They disagree, more fundamentally, about who had the authority to say where one nation ended and another began. The question is not which voice was sincere. It is how a republic founded on consent governed a conquest that enlarged and destabilized it.[^ch18-check-epigraphs]

Rancho de Carricitos lay on the north bank of the Río Grande, about 28 miles upriver from Fort Texas, in a farm field fenced by high, nearly impenetrable chaparral. On the morning of April 25, 1846, Captain Seth Thornton led 63 United States Dragoons toward it, investigating reports that a large Mexican force had crossed the river the day before.[^ch18-nps-carricitos]

The reports were accurate. General Anastasio Torrejón had brought almost 1,600 cavalry and infantry across the river. Warned of Thornton’s approach, he marched east and found the dragoons inside the natural fence. He sent detachments around the field to close the escape routes, then ordered infantry through the narrow gap that served as the only gate.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos]

Thornton’s men charged, and heavy gunfire turned them back. They dismounted and tried to cut through the chaparral; Mexican troops outside the field stopped them. They found no other way out. As Mexican cavalry poured into the clearing, the dragoons surrendered. Eleven United States soldiers lay dead, and 46, including Thornton, were taken prisoner.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos]

The land itself was the dispute. Between the Nueces and the Río Grande lay a strip of dry country that the United States claimed as Texas and Mexico claimed as its own. Mexico held that the boundary was the Nueces; the United States held that it was the Río Grande. Thornton’s patrol was therefore operating on ground that both governments claimed. News reached Fort Texas the same day and Washington on May 10. The next day Polk told Congress that Mexico “has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.” Congress declared war on May 13.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-loc-congress]

The weather that morning, what Thornton said when he saw the infantry in the gate, and what either commander intended beyond the movements the records describe are not preserved. The field, the gate, the numbers, and the competing claims are. The exact site is not: the river has shifted since 1846, and the National Park Service says the spot where the war began remains hidden. What survives is a collision on contested ground and a president’s decision to describe it as an invasion. How did a republic founded on consent govern a conquest that enlarged and destabilized it?[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-check-scene]

This chapter covers the annexation of Texas, the war with Mexico, and the territorial and sectional settlement that followed, from 1844 through 1850. Chapter 17 carried Texas from Anglo colonization through revolution to the annexation controversy. This chapter takes up the annexation decision’s international consequences and the war. It draws on the joint resolution and statutes, presidential messages, congressional debates and votes, the treaty text, military records, the letters and memoirs of participants, the Mexican press, and the scholarship on expansion and the war. It treats “manifest destiny,” “the disputed territory,” “the Mexican Cession” — the territory ceded in 1848 — and “American blood upon the American soil” as the era’s own contested vocabulary, not neutral description. The war was a war of conquest. It killed tens of thousands of people, seized about half of Mexico’s territory, and ended with a treaty whose promises to the conquered were partly struck out before ratification and widely broken in practice. A story that reports only the glory, or only the shame, will be incomplete.[^ch18-check-scope][^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-check-race][^ch18-check-names][^ch18-check-dates]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 follows the election of 1844, the annexation of Texas, the Oregon compromise, and the failed diplomacy that preceded the war. §2 turns to the disputed border, Thornton’s patrol, Polk’s war message, the votes and the dissent, and the Wilmot Proviso. §3 follows the northern campaigns, from Palo Alto and Monterrey to New Mexico and California. §4 follows Winfield Scott from Veracruz to Mexico City and asks what the occupation cost. §5 examines the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, its promises to the conquered, the Californios, the Gold Rush, and the Compromise of 1850. §6 asks how historians explain expansion, the decision for war, and the peace. The chapter begins at Rancho de Carricitos in April 1846, where a patrol on disputed ground gave President Polk his war.[^ch18-check-scope]

## 1. Polk, Texas, and Oregon

### 1.1 The election of 1844 and the annexation of Texas

Texas annexation became the central issue of the 1844 presidential election, and its resolution by joint resolution, on the eve of a war the new president would provoke, set the terms of everything that followed. President John Tyler, lacking a party and seeking reelection, made annexation his cause. A treaty he concluded in April 1844 failed in the Senate. Annexation was finally accomplished by a joint resolution that Tyler signed on March 1, 1845, and Texas was admitted to the Union on December 29, 1845.[^ch18-state-texas-annex]

In 1844 the Democrat James K. Polk defeated Henry Clay in a close contest in which annexation and the “reoccupation” of Oregon were central. Polk understood his victory as a mandate for expansion. The margin was narrow, and the mandate was his reading of it. The election gave him the presidency, not a referendum on every boundary he would claim.[^ch18-state-texas-annex][^ch18-peacehistory]

With Texas came the boundary question. Mexico had never recognized Texan independence and had warned that annexation meant war. It insisted that the southern boundary of Texas was the Nueces, while the United States claimed the Río Grande. Annexation converted a dormant border dispute into an international crisis. The decision was contested at home and taken with full knowledge that it risked war.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-state-texas-annex][^ch18-check-consent]

### 1.2 “54°40′ or fight” and the Oregon compromise

Polk coupled the Texas claim with an Oregon claim, then settled Oregon by compromise. That sequence reveals expansion as a set of negotiable calculations, not a single providential program. Expansionists demanded the whole of Oregon to “54°40′,” the latitude of the southern boundary of Russian Alaska. The slogan “Fifty-four Forty or Fight” framed the demand. Polk proposed a settlement at the 49th parallel.[^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]

Great Britain and the United States signed the Oregon Treaty on June 15, 1846, ending 28 years of joint occupation and setting the 49th parallel as the boundary west of the Rockies. Vancouver Island remained British. The Senate ratified the treaty by a vote of 41 to 14 on June 18, 1846. Polk got less than the slogan demanded and accepted the compromise rather than fight Britain, even as the Mexican crisis reached its climax.[^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]

The contrast is the point. Polk was willing to bargain in the north and to force matters in the south. “Manifest destiny” did not dictate a single outcome. It depended on assessments of power, risk, and cost, and those assessments differed sharply between the Columbia River and the Río Grande. O’Sullivan’s essay had predicted that California would “next fall away” under the “Anglo-Saxon foot.” The phrase was prophecy in the service of a policy, and the policy was made by men who counted armies and votes.[^ch18-osullivan-manifest][^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-check-consent]

### 1.3 The Slidell mission and the failure of diplomacy

Before the fighting began, Polk tried to buy what he wanted. In November 1845 he sent John Slidell to Mexico with authority to settle the Texas boundary and to purchase California and New Mexico. Mexico, whose government was already unstable, refused to receive him.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-state-texas-annex]

Mexico’s refusal was not simply truculence. Between 1841 and 1848 the country passed through one of the most critical periods in the formation of its state. Santa Anna ruled as a dictator from 1841 to 1843. A second Centralist Republic held power until December 1845. Mariano Paredes led a dictatorship for eight months, and the federal republic was restored in 1847, after six presidents had succeeded one another between June 1844 and September 1847. Most of those governments came to power through uprisings and faced opponents eager to overthrow them. The Texas question became a weapon in that internal struggle. The Herrera administration had only precarious support for negotiating with the United States, and the opposition press denounced negotiation as weakness or treason. Mexico also held that annexation violated the 1828 border treaty and the fundamental principles of international law. When Congress approved the joint resolution, Mexico suspended diplomatic relations and treated the act as a declaration of war.[^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]

Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor to move the “Army of Observation” from Corpus Christi to the Río Grande, into territory Mexico claimed, while he prepared a Pacific naval squadron. Diplomacy and coercion proceeded together. Mexico had ordered its own troops to the Río Grande in July 1845 to defend the border it recognized. By the spring of 1846 an American army stood on contested ground, a diplomatic mission had failed, and a clash was probable. Thornton’s patrol produced it.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]

## 2. The disputed border and the war decision

### 2.1 The Nueces Strip and the boundary dispute

The war began over a boundary that both governments claimed and neither had settled. It was a conflict of sovereignty, not a sudden surprise attack. After San Jacinto, the captured Santa Anna had agreed in the Treaties of Velasco to a Río Grande boundary, but Mexico repudiated the agreement as the act of a prisoner. Mexico insisted that the historic Texas boundary was the Nueces. The United States asserted the Río Grande, which carried the boundary far to the south and west.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]

The Río Grande claim mattered because the disputed strip led toward the territories Polk wanted, New Mexico and Alta California. Grant later wrote that the annexationists “wanted more territory than they could possibly lay any claim to.” In his memoirs he put the charge more sharply. Annexation was “a conspiracy to acquire territory out of which slave states might be formed,” he wrote, and Texas “never had exercised jurisdiction over the territory between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande.” Grant was a participant recalling the war years later, not a neutral judge. His judgment still names the question the boundary dispute raised: what, exactly, had the United States annexed?[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-ahc-thornton]

The boundary was a genuine legal and diplomatic disagreement. Polk resolved it by moving an army into the disputed ground. The Mexican government regarded the movement as an outright attack on its territorial integrity. The Mexican newspaper *El Tiempo* described the American government as a bandit who had come upon a traveler. One need not choose between the two governments’ legal theories to see what the record shows: the United States occupied land Mexico claimed and then treated Mexico’s response as an invasion of the United States.[^ch18-velasco-mexican-view][^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-check-consent]

### 2.2 Thornton’s patrol and Polk’s war message

The skirmish at Carricitos gave Polk the incident he used to declare that Mexico had attacked the United States. On April 25, 1846, Thornton’s 63 dragoons were surrounded and captured by a much larger Mexican force. It was the first armed clash of the war. The National Park Service counts 11 dead and 46 captured, with only minor Mexican casualties. The American Battlefield Trust gives 17 American casualties and no Mexican ones. Polk told Congress that “some 16 were killed and wounded.” The counts differ because the definitions do. On May 11 Polk told Congress that Mexico “has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.” He asked Congress to recognize that war existed and to authorize him to call out volunteers.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-check-numbers]

The message assumed precisely what was in dispute: that the Río Grande was the boundary. Polk’s own text elsewhere described the ground more loosely. He told Congress that he had ordered the army to take a position “between the Nueces and the Del Norte,” and he argued that Texas had “extended and exercised” jurisdiction beyond the Nueces. The House’s later account of the episode preserved the phrase “American blood upon America’s soil” as the contested claim it was. The war’s justification rested on a boundary claim, not a settled fact.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-polk-war-message-house]

The Mexican government saw the same events from the other side. On July 6, 1846, President Mariano Paredes issued a decree that the government would “repel the aggression initiated and sustained by the United States of America,” which had “invaded and committed hostilities” in Mexican territory. The decree did not declare war on the United States. It described a defense of national integrity against an invasion already under way. Mexico’s account and Polk’s account could not both be true, and the war would decide which one the United States would write into the map.[^ch18-velasco-mexican-view][^ch18-polk-war-message]

### 2.3 The war vote, the dissent, and the “spot”

Congress declared war by lopsided margins, but a substantial minority, including many Whigs, regarded the war as an unconstitutional and unnecessary aggression. The House voted 174 to 14 on May 11, and the Senate voted 40 to 2 the next day. Polk signed the declaration on May 13. The votes were overwhelming, and they were not unanimous. The opposition was real, articulate, and rooted in the same constitutional tradition the war’s supporters invoked.[^ch18-loc-congress][^ch18-house-work29][^ch18-peacehistory]

Former president John Quincy Adams called it “this most outrageous war,” waged for territorial aggrandizement and the extension of slavery. The Massachusetts legislature denounced it in April 1847 as “a gigantic crime” against a “weak neighbor.” Henry Clay told a Lexington audience on November 13, 1847, “This is no war of defense, but one unnecessary and of offensive aggression.” Henry David Thoreau refused his poll tax in July 1846 and spent a night in jail, a protest he later defended in “Resistance to Civil Government.” On January 3, 1848, the House passed a resolution declaring that the war had been “unnecessarily and unconstitutionally commenced by the president.”[^ch18-peacehistory]

On December 22, 1847, Representative Abraham Lincoln introduced resolutions demanding to know “whether the particular spot of soil on which the blood of our citizens was so shed, was, or was not, our own soil.” In a House speech on January 12, 1848, he argued that Polk’s evidence fell “far short of proving his justification.” Lincoln was a first-term Whig from Illinois, and his questions did not stop the war. They fixed the issue that the war’s opponents would carry forward: the president had asked the country to believe a boundary claim that he had not established.[^ch18-lincoln-spot][^ch18-peacehistory]

The range of opinion ran from abolitionist denunciation to the All of Mexico movement, which arose in 1847 and wanted to annex the entire country. Polk wanted more territory but not all of Mexico. The war’s legitimacy was contested from the first week and remained contested for a century. The dissent deserves its full weight because the dissenters asked the question the war could not answer without the war: on what ground did the United States claim the land?[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-peacehistory]

### 2.4 The Wilmot Proviso and the turn to slavery

Before the war was won, the question of slavery in any territory taken from Mexico split Congress along sectional lines. On August 8, 1846, Representative David Wilmot of Pennsylvania introduced an amendment to a war-appropriations bill prohibiting slavery in any territory acquired from Mexico. It passed the House but failed in the Senate, and it was reintroduced repeatedly without becoming law.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-battlefields-impact]

The vote aligned northern Democrats and northern Whigs against southerners of both parties, shifting politics from party division toward sectional division. The proposal’s defenders often framed free territory as reserved for White settlement, not as a commitment to Black freedom. Wilmot was a free-soil Democrat, not an abolitionist; he wanted to keep slavery out of the West, not to end it in the South. The proviso’s failure did not settle the question. It made the question national.[^ch18-battlefields-impact][^ch18-check-race]

The war had barely begun before its likely fruits became the object of the sectional conflict that would produce the Civil War. The proviso connects the fighting in Mexico to the peace in Washington and to the settlement of 1850. It also marks the moment when the conquest’s territorial question became a question about who would be allowed to live in the new West and on what terms.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

## 3. Northern campaigns

### 3.1 Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma

Taylor’s first battles on the Río Grande blunted the Mexican advance and opened the invasion of northeastern Mexico. On May 8–9, 1846, Taylor defeated General Mariano Arista at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma near the mouth of the Río Grande. The engagements followed Torrejón’s move east after Carricitos.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos]

Among the young officers present was Ulysses S. Grant, who would command in the Civil War. Grant had been stationed at Corpus Christi before the march south, and he called the fighting his first taste of combat. The war that began as a border dispute became the school in which a generation of American officers learned how to fight.[^ch18-army-grant][^ch18-battlefields-scott]

Military success in the north made the war appear quick and cheap to the public while the political questions went unanswered. The battles were small by the standards of later wars, and the victories were real. They did not produce a Mexican government willing to concede the boundary.[^ch18-battlefields-impact]

### 3.2 Monterrey, Buena Vista, and the occupation of northern Mexico

Taylor’s campaign captured Monterrey and defeated Santa Anna at Buena Vista, but the north alone did not bring Mexico to terms. That failure pushed Polk toward the invasion of the Mexican heartland. In September 1846 Taylor’s army assaulted Monterrey in fierce street fighting. In February 1847 his smaller force defeated Santa Anna’s larger army at Buena Vista. Grant, who was with Taylor’s army, later wrote that he served through every major battle except Buena Vista.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-army-grant]

Taylor’s victories made him a national hero and, in 1848, the Whig candidate for president. A general who had won the war’s northern battles became the president who inherited its consequences. The war enlarged Polk’s problems: the general he distrusted became a rival for the presidency, and the north’s success did not produce a peace.[^ch18-nps-1848][^ch18-battlefields-scott]

### 3.3 New Mexico, California, and the Bear Flag Revolt

While Taylor fought in the east, Kearny’s Army of the West and the Pacific Squadron brought New Mexico and California under American control. General Stephen Kearny marched west from Fort Leavenworth and occupied Santa Fe in August 1846 without a major battle, then continued toward California. In June 1846 American settlers rose against Mexican authority in the Bear Flag Revolt at Sonoma. They seized the Californio commander Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo, a Mexican officer born in California who had favored American immigration and was taken prisoner despite not resisting. Commodore John D. Sloat and, later, Commodore Robert F. Stockton and Lieutenant Colonel John C. Frémont secured the territory. Californios rose briefly against the American garrison in Los Angeles before the Treaty of Cahuenga ended the fighting in January 1847.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-homestead-article10]

Vallejo’s story dramatizes the chapter’s argument. He was an extraordinary Californio: soldier, colonizer, and founder of Sonoma, born in Monterey in 1807, military commandant of northern California, and a man who had welcomed the first overland American immigrants. The Bear Flag rebels arrested him in his own home and imprisoned him at Sutter’s Fort. He was released in early August, after the American flag had replaced the Bear Flag, and he later served in the convention that wrote California’s constitution. In the decades that followed, the United States Land Commission put the burden on each grant holder to prove title, and Vallejo lost nearly all of his land. The conquest’s promise of citizenship and property was already broken in practice.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-check-citizenship]

By the time the treaty was signed, American forces occupied New Mexico and California, the prizes the treaty would confirm. The Californios faced an American order that would soon strip many of the rights the treaty promised. The conquest was not only a contest between armies. It was a contest over who would own the land and who would belong to the new state.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-homestead-article10]

### 3.4 The volunteers, the occupation, and the cost

The war was fought substantially by volunteers whose conduct and losses shaped how Americans and Mexicans experienced the conflict. The Regular Army was small. Congress authorized 50,000 volunteers, and about 59,000 of the roughly 90,000 Americans who served came from state volunteer regiments. Many were from the South and West. Their discipline was uneven, and their violence against Mexican civilians was documented.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-ecwc]

The occupation exposed the war’s coercive character. United States forces occupied Mexican towns and, later, the capital, imposing martial law and suppressing guerrilla resistance. Mexican civilians suffered from violence, requisition, and bombardment. Grant wrote from Matamoros that “a great many murders” had been committed against Mexican civilians and that officers had failed to prevent their repetition. Taylor found some volunteer units “more trouble than they were worth.” The war became the first sustained American counterinsurgency abroad.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-ecwc]

The cost was far larger than the brief, victorious battles suggested. Of roughly 90,000 United States soldiers who served, nearly 14,000 died, a death rate of about 15 percent and the highest of any American foreign war. The great majority died of disease, not battle wounds. Mexican military deaths are estimated at about 25,000, and Mexican civilian deaths are uncounted. The war must be measured by its dead as well as its territory.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-numbers]

## 4. Veracruz to Mexico City

### 4.1 The Veracruz landing and siege

Scott’s campaign opened with the first major American amphibious landing. On March 9, 1847, Scott landed about 12,000 troops near Veracruz, supported by Commodore Matthew C. Perry’s squadron. The landing gave the United States a base to march on the Mexican capital. Mexican General Juan Morales held Veracruz with about 3,400 men. A bombardment opened a gap in the city walls, and the city surrendered on March 25 with only about 80 American casualties. Future Civil War commanders, including Lee, Grant, Meade, and Longstreet, took part.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]

The base at Veracruz made the advance inland possible and put Mexico City within reach. It also put the American army in a country whose climate, roads, and politics it did not control. The landing was a triumph of planning. The occupation that followed was a test of endurance.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]

### 4.2 Cerro Gordo and Puebla

Scott’s victory at Cerro Gordo and his bloodless occupation of Puebla opened the road to the capital. Santa Anna entrenched at Cerro Gordo to block the mountain road. Scott, aided by Robert E. Lee’s reconnaissance, flanked the position over difficult terrain and defeated the Mexicans on April 18, 1847. Santa Anna suffered more than 1,000 casualties and some 3,000 captured, against about 400 American casualties. Scott’s army entered Puebla on May 1. The city, Mexico’s second largest, capitulated without resistance and became the American base and supply depot.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]

The road to Mexico City lay open, but the occupation raised the problem of maintaining order and supply in a hostile country. The American army was far from home, dependent on long supply lines, and surrounded by a population that had reasons to resist. Victory at Cerro Gordo did not answer the political question of what the United States would do with the country it had invaded.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-peacehistory]

### 4.3 The Valley of Mexico and the fall of the capital

Scott’s capture of Mexico City in September 1847 ended the conventional war but not the conflict. Mexico refused to concede, and the American occupation had no government willing to treat with it on American terms. Scott fought a series of battles around the capital: Contreras and Churubusco in August, and Molino del Rey and Chapultepec in September. On September 14, 1847, his army entered Mexico City. The American flag rose over the capital, and the Mexican government fled.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-peacehistory]

Hostilities continued in the countryside. Mexico’s leaders debated whether to continue the fight or negotiate, and the American occupation found itself governing a capital it had captured but could not pacify. Military victory did not produce peace on its own. The treaty would depend on diplomacy, on Mexicans willing to sign, and on an American diplomat who defied his own president.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]

### 4.4 Guerrilla war, the San Patricios, and the meaning of the occupation

The occupation exposed the war’s coercive character and the conflicted loyalties it produced. Mexican irregulars and civilians resisted the occupation throughout, and American forces responded with punitive measures that fell on civilians as well as combatants. The war was not only between governments. It was also a struggle among divided loyalties within both nations.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-ecwc]

The Batallón de San Patricio, largely composed of Irish Catholic immigrants who deserted the United States Army, fought for Mexico and was captured. Its soldiers fought at Monterrey, Buena Vista, Cerro Gordo, and Churubusco. After Churubusco, 50 of the San Patricios were executed. Their story raises the question of who belonged to “the people” whose consent the republic claimed to govern by. Catholic immigrants who had enlisted in the American army found themselves fighting a Catholic nation. Some chose Mexico. The choice was treason in American law and a claim of conscience in Mexican memory.[^ch18-check-citizenship][^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-peacehistory]

The occupation revealed that the war was not only a contest of armies but a conflict over citizenship, religion, and belonging. It foreshadowed the conflicts the conquest would create when the United States had to decide who in the new territories would be a citizen and on what terms.[^ch18-check-race][^ch18-ecwc]

## 5. Treaty, land claims, California, and the 1850 settlement

### 5.1 Trist and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo

The peace was made by a diplomat whom Polk had recalled. Nicholas P. Trist, chief clerk of the State Department, negotiated with a Mexican commission at Guadalupe Hidalgo in early 1848. Polk had recalled him in October 1847, but Trist, believing Washington misread Mexico’s situation, ignored the recall and signed the treaty on February 2, 1848. He wrote to his wife that the “decision is altogether my own.” The Mexican commissioners signed a document that gave the United States most of what it had sought. Bernardo Couto told Trist that the moment might be proud for the United States but was humiliating for Mexico. Trist later said that his shame as an American was stronger than theirs as Mexicans.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-velasco-mexican-view]

Mexico ceded about 525,000 square miles, nearly 55 percent of its territory, including California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and Colorado, and parts of New Mexico, Oklahoma, Kansas, and Wyoming. It acknowledged the Río Grande boundary and relinquished Texas. The United States paid $15 million and assumed the claims of American citizens against Mexico. The Senate ratified the treaty on March 10, 1848. The Library of Congress gives the vote as 38 to 14; the National Archives gives 34 to 14. The treaty was ratified, and Article X, which would have guaranteed Mexican land grants, was struck out before ratification.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-loc-congress][^ch18-check-numbers]

The treaty’s terms were those of a conqueror. Its promises to the conquered were enforceable in principle and largely sacrificed in practice. The gap between the two is the subject of the next section.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-check-citizenship]

### 5.2 In Focus: Article VIII and the promise to the conquered

**In Focus.** Article VIII of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848, is a revealing promise. It provided that Mexicans “now established in territories previously belonging to Mexico, and which remain for the future within the limits of the United States … shall be free to continue where they now reside, or to remove at any time to the Mexican Republic, retaining the property which they possess in the said territories …” Those who stayed could “either retain the title and rights of Mexican citizens, or acquire those of citizens of the United States,” but they had to choose “within one year from the date of the exchange of ratifications.” Property “now belonging to Mexicans not established there, shall be inviolably respected.” Article IX promised that those who chose American citizenship would be “admitted at the proper time (to be judged of by the Congress of the United States) to the enjoyment of all the rights of citizens of the United States.” Article X, which would have protected Mexican land grants, was struck out by the Senate. Article XII set the $15 million payment. It promises choice, property, and equal guarantees, and makes citizenship a deadline enforced by the government that had just taken the land. Its promises addressed Mexican citizens, not Indigenous peoples, whose rights it did not recognize. Article XI spoke instead of “savage tribes,” its degrading term for the Indigenous nations whose lands it was transferring, and pledged the United States to restrain their incursions.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-check-focus][^ch18-check-citizenship]

### 5.3 Land claims, the Californios, and the broken promise

The treaty’s property guarantees were undermined by the Senate’s removal of Article X, by a burdensome claims process, and by the land hunger of American settlers. The Senate struck Article X before ratifying the treaty. The promise of property protection survived only in general language, and the property-rights provisions became the subject of decades of litigation.[^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-archives-guadalupe]

Californio and New Mexican landholders had to prove their titles before American commissions under unfamiliar law, at great cost and delay, while squatters and tax burdens eroded their holdings. Vallejo, who had been imprisoned by the American conquerors, lost much of his estate in the decades that followed. The Land Commission put the burden on each grant holder to prove the validity of the grant, and the trials, fees, and financial mismanagement that followed cost him nearly everything. His case was not unique.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-check-citizenship]

The broken promise was a central consequence of the war, not a footnote. The republic that claimed to govern by consent subjected the conquered to a property regime that tested and often denied their rights. The treaty’s text and its implementation diverged, and that divergence shaped the place of Mexican Americans in the United States for generations.[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-check-race][^ch18-avalon-guadalupe]

### 5.4 The Gold Rush and the making of California

Gold was discovered nine days before the treaty’s signing. On January 24, 1848, James Marshall found gold at a sawmill on the South Fork of the American River for John Sutter. News spread despite efforts to keep it quiet, and President Polk confirmed the finds to Congress in December 1848. By the spring of 1849 the largest migration in American history to that point was under way. About 25,000 people arrived that year, and an estimated 140,000 came over the California Trail between 1849 and 1854.[^ch18-nps-goldrush]

The rush transformed the conquered territory into a magnet for migration and sped California toward statehood on American terms. It also devastated Native Californians through disease, starvation, and violence, and it overwhelmed the Californio society the treaty had promised to protect. The mines drew people from the eastern United States, Latin America, Europe, and Asia. California’s non-Native population soared, and the state’s new constitution banned slavery. Gold made California too populous and too important to be governed as a conquered province. It forced the statehood question that drove the settlement of 1850.[^ch18-nps-goldrush][^ch18-howe-controversial][^ch18-check-race][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

### 5.5 The Compromise of 1850 and the settlement of the conquest

The Compromise of 1850 briefly settled the sectional crisis the conquest had created. By 1849 California sought admission as a free state, threatening the sectional balance. Henry Clay proposed a package of resolutions. After seven months of debate, Stephen A. Douglas steered substitute bills through Congress in September 1850. The five statutes admitted California as a free state, organized Utah and New Mexico without a slavery restriction, and adjusted the Texas boundary and assumed part of Texas’s debt. They also abolished the slave trade but not slavery in the District of Columbia, and enacted a stricter Fugitive Slave Act requiring federal and local enforcement and penalizing those who aided escape.[^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

The compromise postponed the sectional reckoning at the price of making the fugitive-slave question national and explosive. It admitted a free California and refused to decide the status of slavery in the rest of the Mexican Cession. It strengthened the law that would send federal marshals into free states after enslaved people who had escaped. Its enforcement is the subject of Chapter 19. It was a settlement, not a solution.[^ch18-check-ch19][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

The southern boundary would be adjusted again in the Gadsden Purchase of 1853–54, a sequel that belongs to Chapter 19 and App. B, Table 3. The war’s main territorial settlement was the one made in 1848 and patched in 1850.[^ch18-check-gadsden]

### 5.6 The peace as a problem in self-government

The conquest enlarged the republic and multiplied the questions of membership it could not answer. The treaty promised citizenship and property. The Senate struck the strongest property guarantee. The Gold Rush made California a free state. The Compromise of 1850 bought time with a harsher fugitive-slave law. Each solution created a new problem because the underlying question was not territorial but political: by what right did a republic of consent rule people who had not consented?[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-check-citizenship]

This is the chapter’s core argument. Military victory did not resolve the disputes over slavery, sovereignty, citizenship, and property that expansion magnified. It relocated and intensified them. The United States won the war and then discovered that the peace required it to decide what the conquered territory was for, who would live there, and whether the people already there would be members of the republic or subjects of it.[^ch18-battlefields-impact][^ch18-check-consent]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The evidence supports several conclusions. The war was provoked on a disputed boundary. The record establishes that American forces occupied territory Mexico claimed, that Thornton’s patrol clashed there in April 1846, and that Polk used the clash to obtain a declaration of war.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-lincoln-spot]

The war was a conquest that seized about half of Mexico. The United States won every major battle, captured Mexico City, and by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo took about 525,000 square miles for $15 million.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-battlefields-scott]

The conquest reopened and intensified the slavery question. The Wilmot Proviso, the sectional votes, and the Compromise of 1850 are documented, and Grant, who had fought in the war, connected it directly to the coming Civil War. He wrote that “the Southern rebellion was largely the outgrowth of the Mexican war.”[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-archives-compromise1850][^ch18-grant-memoirs]

The treaty promised rights that were largely denied. Article VIII promised choice and property, Article IX promised citizenship, and Article X was struck. The implementation fell short of the text.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10]

The cost was heavy. Roughly 14,000 American soldiers died, the great majority of disease, and Mexican military deaths were higher still.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-numbers]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the war Polk’s personal project or a popular movement? Daniel Walker Howe calls the war “bitterly controversial,” supported by the Democratic administration and opposed by the Whigs at the party level. Others stress broad land hunger and racial ideology. The chapter weighs contingency against structure.[^ch18-howe-controversial][^ch18-ecwc]

What was the weight of slavery? Some historians treat the war as a slaveholders’ conspiracy. Others stress Polk’s fixation on California, which was never likely to be a slave state. Grant, Douglass, and Adams connect the war to slavery; the outcome in California complicates the strongest conspiracy version. The 1848 election made the question national: the Free Soil Party nominated Martin Van Buren and opposed the extension of slavery, while Taylor, a slaveholder, carried the Whig banner. Both readings must be tested against the decisions and the result.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-zinn-douglass][^ch18-nps-1848][^ch18-check-race]

Was Mexico bound to lose, and was American victory inevitable? The United States won every battle, but Mexico’s internal divisions, not only American power, shaped the outcome. Mexican historians emphasize Mexico’s own fractured politics and the diplomatic and human costs on the Mexican side. The Mexican government’s instability made negotiation difficult and defense divided. It did not make the American invasion just.[^ch18-velasco-mexican-view][^ch18-peacehistory]

How should the treaty’s broken promises be weighed? The gap between Article VIII’s text and the postwar land regime is documented, but the degree of deliberate dispossession and the persistence of Mexican American rights remain debated. The Land Commission was not a single act of theft. It was a legal process that placed a heavy burden on the conquered and produced widespread loss.[^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-check-citizenship]

Whose voices survive? Presidential messages, treaties, statutes, and court records document the policy and its makers. The dissenting press, memoirs, and Mexican accounts preserve an opposition and a Mexican perspective, while the conquered poor and the enslaved left few direct records. The silence should be named without speaking for the silenced.[^ch18-zinn-douglass][^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the Mexican-American War was a war of conquest provoked on contested ground, legitimated by a racialized doctrine of destiny and mission, opposed by a real minority, and concluded by a treaty whose promises to the conquered were largely unkept. The conquest, far from settling the republic’s questions, intensified the conflict over slavery and membership that soon produced the Civil War.

Historians in the mission-and-destiny tradition distinguished a broad popular “mission” of extending self-government from a narrower, disappointed “manifest destiny.” Frederick Merk and Albert K. Weinberg traced expansionist ideology as a set of nationalist arguments. Read critically, this tradition takes the era’s own civic language seriously but can confuse a claim of consent with consent itself.[^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-check-sources]

The slaveholding-strategy reading argues that annexation and war served the extension of slavery. Grant, Douglass, Adams, and later “Slave Power” scholarship connect the war to slavery’s political economy. The reading can overstate a unified conspiratorial plan, but it correctly identifies the question that made the conquest explosive.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-zinn-douglass]

The conquest-and-race synthesis recovers the ideology, the violence, and the cost. Reginald Horsman’s study of racial Anglo-Saxonism, Amy Greenberg’s *A Wicked War*, Paul Foos’s *A Short, Offhand, Killing Affair*, and Timothy Henderson’s *A Glorious Defeat* strengthen the case for conquest. They can flatten the variety of motives and the contingency of the outcome.[^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-check-sources]

The contingency account restores agency and opposition. Howe and John Schroeder stress the shallowness of popular support and the importance of Polk’s decisions, while Anders Stephanson treats Manifest Destiny as a durable language of national chosenness. This literature can understate structural pressure.[^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-check-sources]

Read together, these positions show that destiny, slavery, and conquest were parts of one history. The debate is best read as overlapping positions, not teams. Each can be tested against the boundary dispute, the votes, the treaty text, and the persistence of Mexican American communities.[^ch18-check-table][^ch18-check-sources]

## Legacies

The peace did not settle the questions the war had raised. The territory won in 1848 reopened the conflict over slavery in the territories. Ralph Waldo Emerson warned that the conquest would poison the republic, and Grant wrote that “the Southern rebellion was largely the outgrowth of the Mexican war.” The war’s most durable result was not the map it drew but the crisis that map produced.[^ch18-digitalhistory-significance][^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

The conquest also created enduring communities and enduring wrongs. Mexican Americans in the Southwest trace their citizenship and their dispossession to the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, whose Article VIII promised property and choice and whose implementation often delivered neither. Californios such as Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo, who had welcomed American settlers and was imprisoned by them, lost much of what the treaty had promised to protect. The treaty’s promises did not extend to Indigenous nations, whose lands and rights it did not recognize.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-check-citizenship]

The chapter’s central question has a double answer. Military victory enlarged the republic and made it a continental power. But it did so on terms that intensified the conflict over slavery, membership, and property, so that the war’s most durable result was the crisis that led, a dozen years later, to the Civil War.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-archives-compromise1850][^ch18-battlefields-impact]

## Summary

- In 1844 annexation became the central issue of the presidential election. Congress annexed Texas by joint resolution on March 1, 1845, and Texas entered the Union on December 29, 1845, over Mexico’s warning of war.[^ch18-state-texas-annex]
- Polk paired the Texas claim with Oregon, then compromised at the 49th parallel in the Oregon Treaty of June 15, 1846, showing that “manifest destiny” was a set of negotiable calculations.[^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]
- After the Slidell mission failed, Polk moved Taylor’s army onto the disputed Nueces Strip. On April 25, 1846, Thornton’s patrol was captured at Rancho de Carricitos, and Polk told Congress that Mexico had shed “American blood upon the American soil.”[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message]
- Congress declared war by 174 to 14 in the House and 40 to 2 in the Senate, over the dissent of Adams, Clay, and later Lincoln, whose “Spot Resolutions” asked which soil had been shed upon.[^ch18-loc-congress][^ch18-lincoln-spot][^ch18-peacehistory]
- The Wilmot Proviso of August 1846 sought to bar slavery from any territory taken from Mexico. It passed the House, failed in the Senate, and shifted politics toward sectional alignment.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-battlefields-impact]
- Taylor won at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma, Monterrey, and Buena Vista. Kearny occupied New Mexico, and Americans seized California in the Bear Flag Revolt while imprisoning the Californio commander Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-sonoma-vallejo]
- Scott landed at Veracruz, won at Cerro Gordo, and captured Mexico City on September 14, 1847. Roughly 14,000 American soldiers died, most of disease, and Mexican deaths were far higher.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-peacehistory]
- Nicholas Trist, recalled by Polk, negotiated the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo on February 2, 1848. Mexico ceded about 525,000 square miles for $15 million, and the Senate struck Article X’s protection of Mexican land grants. Article VIII’s promise of choice and property was largely unkept.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10]
- Gold, found nine days before the treaty’s signing, transformed California and drove the Compromise of 1850, which admitted California free and strengthened the Fugitive Slave Act. The compromise deferred the sectional crisis rather than resolving it.[^ch18-nps-goldrush][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- The debate turns on whether expansion was democratic opportunity, slaveholding strategy, conquest, or Polk’s contingent war, and the honest answer holds several of these together.[^ch18-check-table][^ch18-check-sources]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The United States fought Mexico to settle a boundary it had drawn across land Mexico claimed, and then took half of Mexico’s territory in a peace negotiated by a diplomat its own president had recalled. The treaty promised the conquered freedom of choice, citizenship, and the protection of their property. The Senate struck the strongest guarantee, and the promise was broken in practice. What a republic founded on consent does when it wins a war of conquest is the chapter’s question, and the answer its own history gives is that military victory did not settle its disputes. It magnified them, and the poison Emerson named worked through the politics of the next decade.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-digitalhistory-significance][^ch18-grant-memoirs]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Syracuse, New York, on October 1, 1851, where a crowd rescued the fugitive Jerry from federal custody under the Fugitive Slave Act. That act was the price of the Compromise of 1850, which this chapter has shown was the settlement of a conquest.
