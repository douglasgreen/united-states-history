# Chapter 32. The 1920s: Prosperity, Culture, and Conflict (1920–1929)

> “America’s present need is not heroics, but healing; not nostrums, but normalcy; not revolution, but restoration; …”[^ch32-harding-normalcy]

— Warren G. Harding, address to the Home Market Club, Boston, May 14, 1920.[^ch32-harding-normalcy]

> “We younger Negro artists who create now intend to express our individual dark-skinned selves without fear or shame.”[^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]

— Langston Hughes, “The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain,” *The Nation*, June 23, 1926.[^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]

> “The unmatched prosperity and cultural advancement was accompanied by intense social unrest and reaction.”[^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

— Joshua Zeitz, “The Roaring Twenties,” Gilder Lehrman Institute of American History.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

Harding asked the country to return to what he called normalcy. Hughes answered that a generation of Black artists would not apologize for itself. Zeitz describes a decade in which prosperity and reaction arrived together. The three quotations disagree about whether the 1920s restored an old order, created a new one, or did both at once. That disagreement is the chapter’s subject.[^ch32-check-epigraphs]

On the evening of November 2, 1920, Leo Rosenberg sat before a microphone in a small shack on the roof of a Westinghouse building in East Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. The station was KDKA. It had received the first commercial broadcasting license from the Department of Commerce on October 27, less than two years after the armistice that ended the First World War. Rosenberg began with a sentence the station preserved: “This is KDKA of the Westinghouse Electric and Manufacturing Company in East Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. We shall now broadcast the election returns. We are receiving these returns through the cooperation and by special arrangement with the Pittsburgh Post and Sun.” Then he added a request. Anyone who heard the broadcast should write to the station, because the engineers wanted to know how far the signal had reached and how it had been received.[^ch32-pabook-kdka][^ch32-fcc-radio]

The returns were the Harding–Cox presidential election. The audience was small and scattered: amateur operators with receiving sets, the kind of listeners who had made wireless a hobby before the war. No reliable count survives, and the station’s own request for letters suggests that its engineers did not know who was listening. Radio was still a curiosity. It was not yet an industry, a network, or a national habit. Within four years, some six hundred stations would be broadcasting in the United States, and the medium would be the shared audience of the decade. No one in the Pittsburgh studio could know that on the evening of November 2, 1920.[^ch32-pabook-kdka][^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-check-scene][^ch32-check-dates]

The country was not quite two years past the armistice. The Nineteenth Amendment had been ratified less than three months earlier, when Tennessee became the thirty-sixth state, though its promise was unevenly enforced. The wartime agencies were being dismantled, and the election returns that KDKA read were bringing the political order of the war to an end. A mobilized society was about to be addressed as a mass audience, by a new medium, in a decade of prosperity and conflict. Who belonged to that audience?[^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-check-ch31]

The chapter covers the years from 1920 to 1929 and asks who belonged to the new mass society. It draws on federal statutes and reports, census and commerce statistics, station and broadcast records, newspaper radio schedules, court and commission documents, the records of the Department of the Interior, and the secondary literature on the decade. It treats “normalcy,” “mass society,” “prosperity,” “modern,” “traditional,” “nativism,” “fundamentalism,” and “New Negro” as contested period terms rather than neutral descriptions. It also states plainly that the record over-represents the prosperous, the broadcast, the official, and the documented, and under-represents the rural poor, the immigrant, the migrant, and the Black southerners whose lives the decade remade.[^ch32-check-scope][^ch32-check-names][^ch32-check-silences][^ch32-check-build]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines production, credit, agriculture, and Republican policy. §2 follows radio, film, literature, jazz, and the Washington Naval Conference’s international context. §3 turns to Prohibition. §4 examines immigration restriction, the second Ku Klux Klan, religion, and federal Native policy. §5 traces the Harlem Renaissance and Black politics. §6 asks how historians have explained the decade’s prosperity and its conflicts. It begins on the evening of November 2, 1920, when a Westinghouse announcer in Pittsburgh read election returns into a microphone and a small, scattered audience heard the same voice at once. It ends with an economy whose foundations were already moving.[^ch32-check-scope]

## 1. Production, credit, agriculture, and Republican policy

### 1.1 A prosperity that was real and uneven

**The claim.** The 1920s produced a genuine and broad increase in output and consumption, but its benefits were distributed unevenly across regions, sectors, and households. The decade’s prosperity was a fact and a problem at the same time.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

The boom had three connected stories. Mass-production industries made new goods cheap. A credit system let households buy them ahead of their incomes. Agriculture and other extractive sectors did not share the gains. The three belong to one account. A country that was learning to buy on time was also a country in which millions still lived on farms and in small towns, and in which farm prices had collapsed while industrial profits rose. By 1920, for the first time, slightly more Americans lived in cities than in villages or on farms. The census recorded the shift. It did not record what it felt like to be on the losing side of it.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]

The record can show output, prices, wages, and credit statistics. It cannot show that most households felt prosperous. “Prosperity” is a claim to be tested, not a settled description.[^ch32-check-numbers][^ch32-check-silences]

### 1.2 Mass production and the automobile

The automobile industry was the decade’s leading sector and its model of mass production. Its growth reshaped work, geography, and consumption. In 1913, Henry Ford’s company introduced the moving assembly line. By using conveyor belts to bring parts to workers, Ford cut the time needed to assemble a car from about 12½ hours in 1912 to about 1½ hours in 1914. Falling production costs let the company cut prices repeatedly. By the mid-1920s, a new Ford cost about $290, less than three months’ wages for an average American worker.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]

Ford also changed the terms of employment. In 1914 he introduced a minimum wage of $5 a day, twice what most workers earned, and shortened the workday from nine hours to eight. In 1926 he reduced the work week from six days to five. Ford argued that mass production required mass purchasing power: workers who could afford the goods they made. The argument was also a business strategy. Higher wages reduced turnover and raised productivity.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]

Alfred P. Sloan, president of General Motors from 1923 to 1941, built his company by selling something different. Ford sold a standardized car; Sloan sold status, styling, and a ladder of brands. He set up the nation’s first national consumer credit agency in 1919, introduced the yearly model change in 1927, and advertised cars as symbols of wealth and prestige. The contrast between Ford and Sloan was a change in how goods were sold as much as how they were made. Production had made the car affordable. Marketing made it desirable, and credit made it immediate.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]

The results were extraordinary. In 1919 there were about 6.7 million cars on American roads. By 1929 there were more than 27 million, nearly one car for every household, and one American in five owned a car. In 1929 the industry produced about 12.7 percent of all manufacturing output and employed roughly one worker in twelve. It also doubled the country’s road system, from about 369,000 miles in 1920 to about 852,000 in 1929, and brought congestion, pollution, and nearly 30,000 traffic deaths a year. The car was the decade’s most powerful engine of growth and its most visible source of new risk.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Consumer credit and its costs

The boom rested on installment credit. Manufacturers and banks allowed households to buy cars, furniture, phonographs, radios, and appliances “on time,” paying a little now and the rest later. In 1929 about a quarter of American families bought a car, and roughly 60 percent of car buyers used credit, often at interest rates of 30 percent or more. About 60 percent of all furniture and 75 percent of all radios were purchased on installment plans. By the decade’s end, the practice had become ordinary across the middle class.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

The material setting of the mass audience was also new. By 1930 about two-thirds of American households had electricity and about half had telephones. Electric irons, vacuum cleaners, refrigerators, and washing machines entered middle-class homes, and advertisers promised that labor-saving devices would reduce the burden of housework. They did not shorten the average housewife’s work week. Standards of cleanliness rose with the machines, and the work moved rather than disappeared. The automobile stimulated steel, glass, rubber, road building, gasoline stations, motor lodges, and the roadside economy. Advertising grew into a national industry; by 1929 American companies spent about $3 billion a year on it, five times the amount spent in 1914.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]

The debt was a structural feature of the decade, not a moral failing of individual buyers. It tied household finances to future income and made consumption sensitive to layoffs, falling prices, and bad news. It did not by itself cause the Depression, which belongs to Chapter 33. It did mean that the prosperity of the 1920s depended on confidence as well as output.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-check-ch33]

### 1.4 The farm depression, the tariff, and Republican policy

**The cost.** Republican policy at the federal level encouraged industry and finance while leaving agriculture in a prolonged depression. The decade’s prosperity was built and defended in a way that deepened a regional and sectoral divide.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-coolidge-mellon]

High wartime prices had led farmers to expand. Wheat acreage rose from about 48 million acres to more than 75 million between 1914 and 1919, and Iowa farmland that sold for about $82 an acre in 1910 sold for about $200 in 1920. When European recovery cut demand, American farm exports fell from about $4.1 billion in 1919 to about $1.9 billion in 1922. Commodity prices collapsed, and land values fell sharply. Gross agricultural income dropped from $17.7 billion in 1919 to $10.5 billion in 1921. Farm foreclosures rose from an average of 3.2 per thousand farms between 1913 and 1920 to 10.7 per thousand between 1921 and 1925, and to 17 per thousand between 1926 and 1930. The 1920s farm economy was a depression inside a boom.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-fordney-mccumber]

The federal government chose sides. The Fordney–McCumber Tariff of 1922 raised average duties to about 14 percent on all imports and about 38.5 percent on dutiable imports. It protected manufacturers and some farm products, but it did not raise farm prices, and the prices farmers paid for equipment rose. Farmers who sold into world markets and bought protected goods faced a price squeeze that the tariff did nothing to relieve.[^ch32-fordney-mccumber]

Tax policy moved in the same direction. Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon argued that high wartime rates discouraged investment and that lower rates would produce more revenue by stimulating taxable activity. The top marginal income tax rate fell from 58 percent after the Revenue Act of 1921 to 46 percent in 1924 and to 25 percent in 1926. The cuts chiefly benefited taxpayers in the upper brackets, and they left the farm depression untouched. They were a policy choice about who would carry the adjustment from war to peace, not a neutral recovery.[^ch32-coolidge-mellon][^ch32-taxfoundation-rates]

President Calvin Coolidge vetoed the McNary–Haugen farm relief bills, which would have had the government buy surplus crops at a supported price and sell the surplus abroad. He called the plan price-fixing and warned of “a bureaucratic tyranny of unprecedented proportions.” He argued that it would encourage overproduction, require further subsidies, and stake American agriculture on the actions of foreign governments. The vetoes were consistent with his limited view of government. They also left the farm crisis without the relief its advocates wanted, and it worsened until the New Deal.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-check-ch33]

The political economy of the decade was therefore not a single policy but a set of choices. Tariffs protected industry. Tax cuts favored investors. Farm relief was rejected. The results were a booming stock of durable goods, a depressed countryside, and a federal government that had decided not to decide who would bear the cost of the farm adjustment. That decision would become visible after 1929.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-coolidge-mellon][^ch32-check-ch33]

## 2. Radio, film, literature, jazz, and the world

### 2.1 Broadcasting becomes a mass medium

Radio grew from a few licensed stations into a national medium within a few years, and that growth created both a shared audience and a new regulatory problem. In December 1921 the Department of Commerce issued regulations formally establishing a broadcast service. In 1922 a broadcasting boom followed. By the end of that year there were more than 500 broadcast stations across the country, and within about four years of the KDKA broadcast roughly 600 stations existed. The number of receivers is harder to establish because the series differ, but by 1929, by one estimate, more than twelve million households owned radios, and the industry was selling about $852 million worth of equipment a year.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

Early programming drew on what audiences already knew in theaters, churches, newspapers, and farm offices. Stations carried opera, orchestral music, vaudeville, news, weather, market prices, farm updates, sermons, and children’s stories. The new medium rearranged existing culture rather than inventing one from nothing. It also changed the scale on which that culture traveled. A local soprano, a college professor, or a hotel orchestra could be heard across a region, and a listener in a farmhouse could hear the same program as a listener in a city apartment.[^ch32-americainclass-radio][^ch32-radio-log-1924]

The growth created a regulatory crisis. Before 1927 the Commerce Department regulated radio but lacked the power to deny broadcasting licenses, so stations crowded the airwaves and interfered with one another. The Radio Act of February 23, 1927, created the Federal Radio Commission and gave it the power to grant and deny licenses and to assign frequencies and power levels. Regulation was a response to crowding and interference, not a coherent design. It nonetheless created the institutional foundation for the medium that would carry the New Deal’s fireside chats and the politics of the 1930s.[^ch32-loc-law-radio][^ch32-fcc-radio]

### 2.2 In Focus: one newspaper radio schedule

**In Focus.** The radio page of the *Evening Star* for April 2, 1924, is a schedule, an advertisement, and a map of the medium at once. Under the heading “BY RADIO TODAY” and the subhead “Schedule of Wireless News and Entertainment,” the Washington paper divided its listings into “LOCAL STATIONS” and “Distant Stations.” Each entry began with call letters and a wavelength in meters, then listed the day’s items by the hour: market and weather reports, children’s hours, dance orchestras, religious services, farm talks, time signals. The distant list ran from New York and Philadelphia through Chicago, Kansas City, Atlanta, Fort Worth, and Los Angeles to Havana and Puerto Rico. The page did not present a national network. It presented a nation of separate stations that a listener could tune in one at a time, and it assumed a listener with the leisure, the equipment, and the electricity to try. The smallest entries are the most revealing: a bedtime story, a talk on orchard spraying, a recital by a local soprano. This was not mass culture arriving from nowhere. It was a new distribution of familiar things, addressed to a home audience that the page imagined as curious, solvent, and at home.[^ch32-radio-log-1924]

### 2.3 Film, jazz, and the New Negro arts

Film and recorded music joined radio in creating a national popular culture, and Black artists were central to it. The decade’s culture was therefore both homogeneous and Black-made.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

Hollywood’s transition to sound is usually dated from *The Jazz Singer*, which premiered in New York on October 6, 1927. The film was not the first talking picture, and it was not even mostly a talking picture: it was largely silent, with about two minutes of dialogue and a set of songs recorded on disc. Its success nonetheless announced the end of the silent feature. By the end of 1929 the silent picture was dead, and an entire industry had retooled around sound. The transition was an industrial change as much as an artistic one. It required wiring theaters, rebuilding studios, and writing new contracts, and it destroyed some careers while making others.[^ch32-loc-jazz-singer][^ch32-check-films]

Jazz became the decade’s signature music. It traveled on records and radio and was associated with the dance halls and nightclubs of the cities. It was an African American creation that became a national and international idiom, a form of cultural authority rather than background noise. Langston Hughes called jazz the child of the “low-down folks,” the common people whose joy, religion, and music he set against the respectable imitations of White culture. The claim was partly a literary manifesto. It was also a description of where the music came from.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]

The New Negro movement produced poetry, fiction, and criticism that insisted on Black self-representation. Alain Locke, the Harvard-educated critic who became the movement’s “dean,” described a “spiritual coming of age” in which African Americans transformed “social disillusionment to race pride.” Hughes described the movement’s aim as an “expression of our individual dark-skinned selves.” Together the phrases announced a literature written by Black artists about Black life, for readers of any race, without asking permission.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-hughes-negro-artist][^ch32-check-epigraphs]

### 2.4 The Washington Naval Conference and the world

The United States did not withdraw from the world after rejecting the Treaty of Versailles. It pursued disarmament and Pacific security through the Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22, which is the decade’s international frame. On November 12, 1921, delegates from the United States, Britain, Japan, France, Italy, and other countries met in Washington. Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes proposed to scrap building programs and older ships, and the talks produced three major treaties.[^ch32-nww2-naval][^ch32-state-naval]

The Five-Power Treaty set capital-ship tonnage at 500,000 tons each for the United States and Britain, 300,000 for Japan, and 175,000 each for France and Italy. The 5:5:3 ratio was a compromise between the Japanese preference for 10:10:7 and the American Navy’s preference for 10:10:5. The treaty called on the signatories to stop building capital ships and to scrap older ones. The Four-Power Treaty replaced the Anglo-Japanese alliance with a pledge to consult in a crisis in East Asia. The Nine-Power Treaty internationalized the Open Door in China and committed the signatories to respect China’s territorial integrity, but it had no enforcement mechanism.[^ch32-state-naval][^ch32-nww2-naval]

The settlement had limits. The Five-Power Treaty left cruisers and other classes of ships unrestricted, and a new competition in cruisers led the powers back to the table in 1927 and 1930. Article XIX recognized the status quo of American, British, and Japanese bases in the Pacific but outlawed their expansion. Many American naval officers worried that the freeze would endanger Hawaii, Guam, and the Philippines. The Nine-Power Treaty promised consultation, not action. The agreements were a status quo settlement, not a lasting peace.[^ch32-state-naval]

The conference also belonged to the decade’s racial politics. The Washington settlement and the immigration act pointed in opposite directions. The United States negotiated with Japan as a great power in 1921 and 1922, and then Congress passed an immigration act in 1924 that excluded Japanese immigrants and offended the Japanese government. The State Department’s own account notes that the 1924 law violated the Gentlemen’s Agreement and increased tensions between the two nations. The United States could negotiate with Japan as a great power while refusing to admit Japanese people as immigrants. That contradiction was one of the decade’s central facts.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-state-naval]

## 3. Prohibition

### 3.1 The amendment and the statute

Prohibition was the decade’s most ambitious federal attempt to regulate private behavior, and it failed on its own terms. That failure makes it the clearest test of the limits of moral reform by statute.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]

The Eighteenth Amendment prohibited the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors, and their importation into and exportation from the United States. Congress passed it on December 22, 1917, and sent it to the states. Ratification was completed on January 16, 1919, when Nebraska became the thirty-sixth state. The National Prohibition Act, known as the Volstead Act, passed over President Woodrow Wilson’s veto on October 28, 1919, and provided enforcement. It defined intoxicating beverages as those containing more than 0.5 percent alcohol. Prohibition took effect on January 17, 1920.[^ch32-fjc-prohibition][^ch32-archives-volstead]

The reform coalition had been built over decades. Temperance societies, the Anti-Saloon League, the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union, and Progressive reformers had campaigned against the saloon as a source of family poverty, political corruption, and industrial inefficiency. Factory owners supported the movement because they wanted a disciplined workforce. Women were prominent in it because alcohol was seen as a destroyer of families and marriages. Prohibition was a Progressive reform, not an aberration, and it drew on the same faith in legislation that supported child-labor laws, pure-food laws, and urban reform.[^ch32-archives-volstead]

The reformers were not hypocrites, and the drinkers were not simply defiant by nature. The record supports a more ordinary story: a broad coalition won a constitutional ban, and a much narrower coalition was willing to pay for its enforcement. That gap between approval and appropriation is where the policy failed.[^ch32-check-prohibition][^ch32-archives-volstead]

### 3.2 Enforcement, evasion, and organized crime

Alcohol consumption fell at first. The National Archives lesson reports a drop of about 30 percent in consumption, with hard liquor off about 50 percent, and arrests for drunkenness fell. The price of illegal alcohol rose above what the average worker could afford. Prohibition changed behavior, at least for a time and at least for some parts of the population.[^ch32-archives-volstead]

It also created a vast illicit market. Bootlegging became rampant, and federal and local authorities would not commit the resources to enforce the law everywhere. In New York City alone, by 1925, there were reported to be anywhere from 30,000 to 100,000 speakeasies. The range is wide because the count was never systematic. Maryland refused to pass an enforcement law. Fewer than half the states funded their own enforcement efforts, and many relied on the federal government to enforce a law that was unpopular with many of their residents.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]

Prohibition agents were poorly paid and poorly trained, and bribery and corruption were widespread. Organized criminal gangs, attracted by the profitability of the illegal liquor trade, fought violent turf battles in Chicago, Detroit, and other major cities. The corruption was connected to the enforcement design, not merely to individual vice. A law that criminalized a widespread practice, funded its enforcement poorly, and left the supply to criminal markets produced exactly the incentives that its designers had warned against.[^ch32-crs-prohibition][^ch32-fjc-prohibition]

The record can show consumption statistics, arrest figures, and enforcement budgets. It cannot show that Prohibition increased total drinking. The claim that it did is contested.[^ch32-check-prohibition][^ch32-check-numbers]

### 3.3 The Wickersham findings and the road to repeal

The federal government’s own commission confirmed by 1931 that Prohibition had failed. President Herbert Hoover appointed the Wickersham Commission shortly after taking office in 1929, and the commission reported two years later. It found that “[s]ettled habits and social customs do not yield readily to legislative fiats.” Americans patronized speakeasies, exploited loopholes in the Volstead Act to obtain medicinal liquor and sacramental wine for recreational purposes, and brewed at home, often without significant legal consequences.[^ch32-crs-prohibition]

The commission also found that federal agencies lacked the funds for serious enforcement, that many agents had low salaries and little training, and that corruption followed. Organized gangs fought violent battles in major cities. Although the commission opposed repeal, a few commissioners dissented and advocated revision or elimination. Its report helped encourage public support for repeal, which came with the Twenty-First Amendment in 1933. The politics of repeal belong to the early 1930s and to Chapter 33.[^ch32-crs-prohibition][^ch32-check-ch33]

The dry decade’s lesson is about the reach of federal moral regulation. Prohibition did not prove that law can never change behavior. It did show that a constitutional ban on a widely practiced habit requires enforcement resources, public consent, and a willingness to accept the costs. The reformers had won the first and lost the second and third. That is a political failure, not a moral fable.[^ch32-crs-prohibition][^ch32-check-prohibition]

## 4. Immigration, the Klan, religion, and Native policy

### 4.1 The turn to restriction

The 1920s ended the era of open European immigration and built a race- and nationality-based quota system that governed entry for more than four decades. The Immigration Act of 1917 had imposed a literacy test and an “Asiatic Barred Zone,” and wartime hostility to southern and eastern Europeans fed nativism. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 set the first numerical limits. It capped immigration at about 350,000 a year and set quotas at 3 percent of the foreign-born population of each nationality in the 1910 census. It established no quotas for the Western Hemisphere. President Wilson pocket-vetoed the bill, and President Warren G. Harding called Congress back into special session to pass it. The act was renewed in 1922.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]

The Immigration Act of 1924, known as the Johnson–Reed Act, lowered the quota to 2 percent and moved the base year to 1890, before the great wave of southern and eastern European immigration. That interim formula shifted the quotas toward Britain and northern Europe. The act also changed the permanent basis of the quota from the foreign-born population to the national origins of the whole population, including natural-born citizens, and it excluded from entry any alien who was ineligible for citizenship by race or nationality. The exclusion ended most Asian immigration, including Japanese immigration, and it offended the Japanese government.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]

The State Department distributed visas through embassies, and the Immigration Service admitted only immigrants who arrived with a valid visa. In 1924 Congress created the Border Patrol in response to illegal entries and smuggling, especially along the land borders. Restriction was a system with an inside and an outside, not merely a set of numbers. It defined who could become a member of the national community and who could not.[^ch32-uscis-restriction][^ch32-state-immigration]

### 4.3 The second Ku Klux Klan

The second Ku Klux Klan became a mass movement in the early 1920s, especially in the Midwest, and its rise and fall show that nativism and racial reaction were not confined to the South. The Klan was re-founded in 1915 at Stone Mountain, Georgia, and it spread across the country after the war. Its recruitment peaked in 1923–24. Indiana was said to have the most powerful Klan organization in the nation, with about 250,000 members under Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson. The Klan’s appeal mixed racism, antisemitism, and, above all, anti-Catholicism. In the Midwest it organized on a county-unit basis with political power as its goal.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]

The Klan was a national movement, not a southern relic. It claimed at least five million members by 1925 and controlled politics in Indiana, Texas, Oklahoma, and Colorado, with strength in California and Georgia. In 1924 it was powerful enough to deadlock the Democratic National Convention for 103 ballots, as supporters of New York governor Al Smith, a Catholic, faced supporters of former Treasury Secretary William McAdoo. The convention nominated a compromise candidate, John W. Davis, who lost to Coolidge. The deadlock was the Klan’s high-water mark.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-vcu-klan]

Membership fell sharply after 1925. The Klan failed to deliver good government, Prohibition failed, federal legislation restricted immigration, and prominent leaders were exposed as corrupt. The decisive blow was the conviction of Stephenson in November 1925 for his role in the death of Madge Oberholtzer. He received a life sentence and later leaked damaging information about his involvement in state politics, ending the careers of Governor Ed Jackson, Indianapolis mayor John Duvall, and several others. The decline was the result of the Klan’s own conduct as much as outside opposition.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]

The record can show membership estimates, klavern locations, and the leadership scandal. It cannot give an exact national membership total. The figures are estimates tied to the sources that compiled them. The Klan’s rise is evidence that racial and religious reaction could recruit millions of ordinary people in the North and Midwest. Its fall is evidence that a movement built on secrecy, violence, and corruption could be discredited from within.[^ch32-check-numbers][^ch32-check-race]

### 4.4 Religion, fundamentalism, and the law

The decade’s religious conflict was a struggle over who would define American culture, and the Scopes trial of 1925 made that struggle a national spectacle. Tennessee’s Butler Act, enacted in February 1925, made it unlawful “to teach any theory that denies the story of divine creation as taught by the Bible and to teach instead that man was descended from a lower order of animals.” John T. Scopes, a twenty-four-year-old general science teacher and part-time football coach, agreed to stand as the defendant in a test case organized by local boosters in Dayton. Clarence Darrow joined the defense, and William Jennings Bryan joined the prosecution.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes]

The trial ran from July 10 to July 21, 1925. It was the first trial broadcast live on radio, and reporters from across the country covered it as a contest between modernism and fundamentalism. The high point came when Darrow called Bryan to the stand as a biblical expert and pressed him on the literal truth of scripture. Scopes was convicted and fined $100. The Tennessee Supreme Court later set the conviction aside on a technicality, but the statute remained in force. The trial was a defeat for the defense in the courtroom and a victory in the press.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-umn-scopes-appeal]

The stakes were not simply science versus religion. Bryan’s concerns included, on some accounts, the use of Darwinian ideas to support eugenics, and he also worried that evolution undermined biblical authority. The defense argued for academic and intellectual freedom. The trial became a national event because it staged a larger conflict over who would define the curriculum, the family, and the moral authority of the Bible. It was not a simple verdict about science versus religion.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-check-debate-works]

The decade’s nativism ran through the legal system as well as the culture. Nicola Sacco, a thirty-two-year-old shoemaker, and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, a twenty-nine-year-old fish peddler, went on trial for murder in Boston in 1921. Both were Italian immigrants and avowed anarchists, and the trial took place in the aftermath of the Red Scare. Many Americans found the evidence against them flimsy and believed that they were being prosecuted for their immigrant background and their radical politics. They were convicted on July 14, 1921. A committee appointed by the governor in 1927, which included Harvard president A. Lawrence Lowell, found the trial fair, and the men were executed on August 23, 1927. The case divided the nation and produced an uproar in Europe. The debate over their guilt remains unresolved.[^ch32-mass-sacco][^ch32-gilder-sacco]

### 4.5 Federal Native policy and citizenship

Federal policy toward Native nations in the 1920s combined a formal grant of citizenship with the continuation of allotment and assimilation. The Indian Citizenship Act, approved on June 2, 1924, declared “all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States” to be citizens of the United States. It added a proviso: “the granting of such citizenship shall not in any manner impair or otherwise affect the right of any Indian to tribal or other property.” The act conferred a national citizenship. It did not settle the political status of Native nations, and it did not grant voting rights.[^ch32-indian-citizenship-act][^ch32-narf-citizenship]

The states continued to deny Native people the vote on grounds of reservation residence, tribal enrollment, taxation, or claimed incompetency. Thousands of Native American veterans, including Code Talkers who returned from the Second World War, found themselves unable to vote in the country they had defended. For many Native people and nations, the act’s promise of citizenship was incomplete.[^ch32-narf-citizenship][^ch32-check-native]

The decade’s own survey documented the damage that federal policy had done. The Problem of Indian Administration, known as the Meriam Report, was completed in 1928. It found that “an overwhelming majority of the Indians are poor, even extremely poor.” It described bad health, overcrowded housing, and a diet that was “generally insufficient in quantity, lacking in variety, and poorly prepared.” It found that the provisions for Indian children in boarding schools were “grossly inadequate,” that the Indian Service lacked enough trained personnel, and that individual ownership of land had “for the most part operated in the opposite direction” from the assimilation its designers had intended. The report did not discover a new problem. It documented what Native communities had long known.[^ch32-meriam-report][^ch32-narf-meriam]

The Meriam Report set the stage for the reform of the 1930s, which belongs to later chapters. Its findings also belong to the 1920s’ pattern: a federal government that granted citizenship in law while maintaining a system of land loss, underfunded services, and assimilation in practice. The gap between the grant and the reality restates the chapter’s question: who belonged to the national community, and on what terms?[^ch32-meriam-report][^ch32-check-native][^ch32-check-ch33]

## 5. The Harlem Renaissance and Black politics

### 5.1 Harlem and the Great Migration

The Great Migration made Harlem and other northern cities Black cultural capitals, and the Harlem Renaissance was both an artistic movement and a political assertion. The migration carried roughly six million Black people from the South to the North, Midwest, and West between 1910 and 1970. The first wave, from 1910 to 1940, carried about two million people to cities such as New York, Chicago, Detroit, and Pittsburgh. Harlem, which covers about three square miles of Manhattan, drew nearly 175,000 African Americans and became the largest concentration of Black people in the world.[^ch32-archives-greatmigration][^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]

Map 25, drawn from the 1910 and 1930 censuses and Appendix B, Table 1, shows the migration’s origins, destinations, and city growth for 1910–1930. It distinguishes the movement’s volume from any claim about migrants’ motives. A map can show that people left particular counties and arrived in particular cities. It cannot show why they left, what they found, or what they thought of it.[^ch32-check-map][^ch32-archives-greatmigration]

The census can show the totals and the destinations. It cannot show migrants’ reasons. Letters, oral histories, and the Black press do that, and the record over-represents the documented and the literate. The Great Migration was one of the largest movements of people in American history, and it was made by millions of individual decisions that no single archive contains.[^ch32-check-silences][^ch32-archives-greatmigration]

### 5.2 The New Negro and the Renaissance

The Harlem Renaissance produced a self-conscious literature and art that challenged racist stereotypes and redefined Black identity for a national and international audience. Among the movement’s central figures were the critics and intellectuals W. E. B. Du Bois, Alain Locke, and Marcus Garvey; the writers and poets Zora Neale Hurston, Langston Hughes, and Countee Cullen; the visual artists Aaron Douglas and Augusta Savage; and musicians including Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington. The names are not a canon. They are a map of the networks of institutions, magazines, publishers, and nightclubs that made the movement visible.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]

Locke described the movement as a “spiritual coming of age” in which African Americans transformed “social disillusionment to race pride.” Hughes described its aim as an “expression of our individual dark-skinned selves.” The two claims were not identical. Locke spoke for a cultural awakening that could be presented to the nation. Hughes spoke for a younger generation that refused to make its art respectable for White readers. Together they defined the movement’s ambition and its internal argument.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]

Harlem’s publishing houses, newspapers, music companies, playhouses, and nightclubs made the neighborhood the epicenter of American culture, and the movement’s art circulated nationally and abroad. The literature, music, and fashion created in Harlem defined culture and “cool” for Black and White audiences alike, in the United States and around the world. The Renaissance was Black-made culture that changed the mainstream, not a separate sphere.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]

The movement also had limits. It was urban, class-inflected, and dependent on patrons, publishers, and clubs. It did not reach or represent all Black Americans, and its heyday was cut short by the stock market crash of 1929 and the Depression that followed. Those limits are a qualification, not a dismissal. The Renaissance changed what American culture could be, even if it did not change the daily lives of most Black southerners.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-check-race]

### 5.3 Garvey, Black politics, and the Tulsa massacre

Black politics in the 1920s ranged from the NAACP’s legal and legislative work to Marcus Garvey’s mass nationalism, and the decade also brought one of the worst racial massacres in American history. Garvey, a Jamaican-born organizer, founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association in Jamaica in July 1914. The UNIA aimed to achieve Black nationalism through the celebration of African history and culture, and it pushed a “back to Africa” program. Garvey created the Black Star Line to carry passengers and goods to and from Africa, and he fostered restaurants and shopping centers to encourage Black economic independence. He drew a mass following in the early 1920s and became one of the most consequential Black political figures of the decade.[^ch32-garvey-archives][^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]

Garvey was also a controversial figure. He believed in racial separatism, challenged W. E. B. Du Bois and the integrationist leadership, and met with White supremacists, including the Ku Klux Klan. In 1922 he was charged with mail fraud in connection with a ship on the Black Star Line. He was convicted, imprisoned, and in 1927 deported to Jamaica after President Coolidge commuted his remaining sentence. His movement declined, but his ideas about Black pride, economic independence, and international solidarity outlasted him. He belongs alongside the integrationist organizations as a major political figure, not as a curiosity.[^ch32-garvey-archives][^ch32-check-race]

On May 31 and June 1, 1921, a White mob destroyed the Greenwood district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, the prosperous Black neighborhood known as “Black Wall Street.” The immediate trigger was the arrest of a young Black man, Dick Rowland, after an encounter with a White woman in an elevator, and an inflammatory report in the *Tulsa Tribune*. Armed crowds gathered at the courthouse, shots were fired, and the outnumbered Black defenders retreated to Greenwood. In the early morning of June 1, Greenwood was looted and burned. The 2001 Race Riot Commission found that 35 city blocks were destroyed, that 1,256 homes were burned, that more than 800 people were treated for injuries, that more than 6,000 Black residents were detained, and that credible estimates of the dead range from about 100 to 300. No one was ever prosecuted.[^ch32-tulsahistory][^ch32-check-numbers]

The commission also found that municipal and county authorities failed to protect Greenwood, that some deputies participated in the violence, and that the National Guard helped round up Greenwood’s residents. The event was long called a “riot,” a name that served insurance companies and obscured the attackers’ responsibility. The massacre showed how violent the resistance to full membership remained, and the Renaissance’s optimism did not prevent it. The decade’s Black politics was therefore both a cultural awakening and a record of unpunished destruction.[^ch32-tulsahistory][^ch32-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The prosperity was real and uneven. Output, consumption, and ownership grew substantially, but agriculture remained depressed and the gains were concentrated in industry and the cities.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]

Mass communication created a national audience. Radio grew from a few stations to hundreds within a few years, and film and recorded music joined it in a shared popular culture.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio]

Immigration restriction was racial and national. The 1921 and 1924 acts built a quota system designed to preserve an imagined homogeneity and to exclude Asians.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]

The second Klan was a mass movement. It spread across the North and Midwest, peaked in the early 1920s, and declined after corruption and scandal.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]

Black culture and politics changed the mainstream. The Harlem Renaissance produced lasting art, and Black politics ranged from legal work to mass nationalism.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-garvey-archives]

The decade ended in mounting instability. The boom rested on credit and an unequal economy, and the collapse that followed belongs to the next chapter.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-check-ch33]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the decade chiefly a culture war, or was it a distinctive postwar political economy? One position treats the 1920s as a contest between modernism and tradition. Another treats it as a distinctive postwar period defined by reaction, restriction, and political economy. The chapter states each at its strongest. The historiographical shift is itself part of the story: newer social and cultural histories have supplemented the traditional focus of 1920s scholarship without replacing it.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-goldberg-discontented][^ch32-goldberg-essay][^ch32-dumenil-modern-temper]

Did mass culture liberate or homogenize? One account stresses the new freedoms and shared experiences of the mass audience. Another stresses standardization, advertising, and the narrowing of local cultures. The evidence supports both, in different places and for different people.[^ch32-dumenil-modern-temper][^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]

How central is race to the decade? One account treats race and nativism as central to the decade’s politics. Another treats them as one theme among several. The question is open, and the evidence points both ways.[^ch32-higham-strangers][^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]

Was the Harlem Renaissance a movement or a moment? One position stresses its lasting artistic and political impact. Another stresses its small, urban, and patronage-dependent base. Both are true in part.[^ch32-huggins-harlem][^ch32-lewis-when-harlem]

Did Prohibition fail, or did it change behavior? One account stresses the law’s failure and the rise of organized crime. Another stresses the real decline in consumption and the long temperance legacy. Both belong in the account.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the 1920s simultaneously created a national mass culture and deepened the exclusions that determined who could join it. The same years that produced jazz, radio, and the New Negro produced the quota acts, the second Klan, and the Scopes trial. That coexistence is the chapter’s contribution to the book’s argument about settlements that change the terms of conflict without settling them.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-goldberg-discontented]

The mass audience and the color line were made together. The new national audience was being defined at the same moment its boundaries were being policed. The “who belonged” question is answered partly by the cultural record and partly by the statute books. A history that reads only the culture will miss the quotas, and a history that reads only the statutes will miss the music.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-check-race]

The decade’s deepest question was not whether the country was prosperous but who was included in the prosperity and the culture it produced. The record supports an answer of expanding audience and narrowing membership at once. That is not a paradox to be resolved. It is the shape of the decade.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-check-table2]

## Legacies

The decade’s prosperity left a double legacy. Mass production and installment credit created a consumer economy and a national market, but they also tied household finances to debt and left agriculture behind. The fragility they built would become visible after 1929. Chapter 33 takes up that story.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-check-ch33][^ch32-check-dates-appD]

The immigration regime outlasted the decade. The quota system of 1921 and 1924 governed American admissions for more than four decades, and it shaped the country’s demography and its relationship with Asia and Europe until Congress replaced it in the Immigration Act of 1965. The system’s racial design was not a temporary expedient. It was a durable settlement that later movements had to dismantle.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-uscis-restriction][^ch32-state-1965]

The mass audience outlasted the decade. Radio, film, and recorded music built a shared national culture that would carry the New Deal’s fireside chats and the politics of the 1930s, and the federal broadcast regulation created in 1927 became the institutional foundation of that medium. The audience that KDKA had addressed in 1920 became the audience that Roosevelt would address in 1933.[^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-loc-law-radio]

The Harlem Renaissance’s assertion outlasted the decade. The New Negro’s insistence on self-representation and the political organizing it fed became part of the foundation of the civil rights movement, even as the Tulsa massacre and the quota acts showed how violent and how official the resistance to full membership remained. The decade’s cultural confidence and its political defeats belong to the same history.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-tulsahistory]

- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents the prosperous, the broadcast, the official, and the documented and under-represents the rural poor, the immigrant, the migrant, and the Black southerners whose lives the decade remade. ## Summary

- The 1920s produced real growth in output and consumption, but the gains were uneven and agriculture remained depressed throughout the decade.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]
- Henry Ford’s assembly line cut car assembly time from about 12½ hours to about 1½ hours, and the number of cars on American roads rose from about 6.7 million in 1919 to more than 27 million in 1929.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- Installment credit let households buy cars and appliances ahead of their incomes, with about 60 percent of 1929 car buyers using credit at rates often above 30 percent.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- Farm exports fell from about $4.1 billion in 1919 to about $1.9 billion in 1922; Coolidge vetoed the McNary–Haugen relief bills, and the Mellon tax programs cut the top income tax rate to 25 percent by 1926.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-coolidge-mellon]
- Radio grew from a few licensed stations to more than 500 by the end of 1922 and about 600 within four years of the KDKA broadcast, and the Radio Act of 1927 created the Federal Radio Commission.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-loc-law-radio]
- The Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22 produced the Five-Power, Four-Power, and Nine-Power Treaties, including the 5:5:3 capital-ship ratio.[^ch32-state-naval]
- Prohibition took effect in January 1920, consumption fell at first, but speakeasies, bootlegging, and corruption spread, and the Wickersham Commission confirmed in 1931 that the law had failed.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]
- The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Johnson–Reed Act of 1924 built a racial and national quota system, and Congress created the Border Patrol in 1924.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-uscis-restriction]
- The second Ku Klux Klan peaked in 1923–24, with about 250,000 members in Indiana under D. C. Stephenson, and declined after his 1925 conviction.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]
- The Scopes trial of July 1925 made the fundamentalist–modernist conflict a national spectacle, and Sacco and Vanzetti were executed on August 23, 1927.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-mass-sacco]
- The Indian Citizenship Act of June 2, 1924, granted Native Americans citizenship without guaranteeing the vote, and the 1928 Meriam Report documented the damage of allotment.[^ch32-indian-citizenship-act][^ch32-meriam-report]
- The Great Migration carried about two million Black southerners north between 1910 and 1940, and the Tulsa massacre of May 31–June 1, 1921, destroyed Greenwood and killed an estimated 100 to 300 people.[^ch32-archives-greatmigration][^ch32-tulsahistory]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1920 and 1929 the United States built a consumer economy and a national audience, restricted immigration by race and nationality, banned alcohol and failed to enforce the ban, put the second Ku Klux Klan into the mainstream and then discredited it, made a fundamentalist schoolteacher’s trial a national event, and produced a Black cultural movement that redrew the boundaries of American art. The chapter’s question — who belonged to the new mass society? — is answered by a record in which the audience expanded and the membership narrowed at the same time. The prosperity that made the decade famous rested on ground that would soon move.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-tulsahistory][^ch32-check-ch33]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on December 11, 1930, when depositors lined up outside the Bank of the United States in New York and the bank failed. The uneven boom of the 1920s would become the prolonged catastrophe of the 1930s. Chapter 33 takes up the Great Depression and asks how a financial breakdown became a social disaster.[^ch32-check-ch33][^ch32-check-bridge]
