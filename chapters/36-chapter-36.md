# Chapter 36. The Second World War (1941–1945)

> “Today all of us are in the same boat … and it is a ship which will not and cannot be sunk.”[^ch36-fdr-cable]
>
> — Franklin D. Roosevelt, cable to Winston S. Churchill, December 8, 1941.[^ch36-fdr-cable]

> “The principle then lies about like a loaded weapon, ready for the hand of any authority that can bring forward a plausible claim of an urgent need.”[^ch36-korematsu]
>
> — Robert H. Jackson, dissenting, *Korematsu v. United States*, 323 U.S. 214 (1944), at 246.[^ch36-korematsu]

> “The broad historical causes which shaped these decisions were race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership.”[^ch36-cwric]
>
> — Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, *Personal Justice Denied*, 1983.[^ch36-cwric]

A president told a prime minister that they were in the same boat. A dissenting justice warned that a principle of racial exclusion, once accepted by a court, would lie about like a loaded weapon. A government commission concluded that the wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans had been produced by race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership. The three statements disagree about what the war was: a common democratic cause, a constitutional danger, or a failure of judgment that the emergency made possible. That disagreement frames the chapter’s question: how did coalition warfare remake American power and belonging?[^ch36-fdr-cable][^ch36-korematsu][^ch36-cwric][^ch36-check-epigraphs]

On December 13, 1941, six days after the attack on Pearl Harbor, Prime Minister Winston Churchill boarded the battleship HMS *Duke of York* and crossed the Atlantic for the United States. Escorted by destroyers, the ship reached the Norfolk Navy Yard in Virginia on December 22. President Franklin D. Roosevelt met him at a Washington airfield shortly afterward. The two leaders and their senior military staffs then began a conference, code-named ARCADIA, that ran from December 22 to January 14, 1942. Churchill said its purpose was to “review the whole war plan in light of reality and new facts, as well as the problems of production and distribution.”[^ch36-whha-churchill][^ch36-arcadia]

The conference reaffirmed the earlier “Germany First” principle, the agreement that Germany was the primary enemy in a two-front war, and created the Combined Chiefs of Staff to plan global strategy and direct Anglo-American forces. The British delegation included Admiral of the Fleet Sir Dudley Pound, Air Chief Marshal Sir Charles Portal, and Field Marshal Sir John Dill, who stayed in Washington as the British liaison after the conference. Brigadier General Dwight D. Eisenhower, then serving in the War Department’s War Plans Division, wrote that the Allies had to recommend a scheme for waging “the war against Germany and Japan, enemies which were widely separated geographically.” He concluded that “We had to attack to win.”[^ch36-arcadia]

On December 26 Churchill addressed a joint session of Congress. “I cannot help reflecting that if my father had been American and my mother British instead of the other way around, I might have got here on my own,” he said. He also warned that “Many disappointments and unpleasant surprises await us.”[^ch36-churchill-congress]

The record establishes the dates, the crossing, the conference, and the texts. It does not establish how Americans received the visit, and it does not show what the participants thought the war would become. In December 1941 no one knew that the war would end in 1945, that the Soviet Union would bear the largest share of German casualties, that atomic weapons would be used, or that the Grand Alliance would fracture into the Cold War. The scene ends where the chapter begins: with two allies who had to decide how to fight together, and with a question about what that way of fighting would do to the country they were defending. How did coalition warfare remake American power and belonging?[^ch36-check-scene][^ch36-whha-churchill][^ch36-arcadia]

The chapter covers the years from the American declarations of war in December 1941 to the Japanese surrender in September 1945. It asks how a coalition war, an industrial mobilization, and a set of political choices about who belonged changed the United States and its place in the world. It draws on statutes and executive orders, conference and diplomatic records, military and naval histories, production and labor statistics, court opinions, commissions of inquiry, and the secondary literature. It treats “good war,” “arsenal of democracy,” “unconditional surrender,” “relocation,” “internment,” and “military necessity” as contested period terms rather than neutral descriptions. “Arsenal of democracy” was Roosevelt’s name for an America that armed the Allies; “unconditional surrender” was the demand for complete capitulation; “relocation” was the government’s word for removal to camps; “internment,” strictly, meant the detention of enemy aliens, though it was often used loosely for the camps; “military necessity” was the claim that military conditions required the removal.[^ch36-check-scope][^ch36-check-names][^ch36-check-incarceration-terms]

The chapter also states a limit at the start. The record over-represents presidents, generals, and organized institutions and under-represents the incarcerated, the displaced, the conscripted poor, and the dead of occupied and colonized places. The global mortality figures are estimates produced by different methods, and the chapter gives ranges and names the methods rather than presenting one number as settled. The sources for the Pacific war are fuller on American decision-making than on Japanese experience, and the sources for the Holocaust are fuller on perpetration and rescue policy than on the dead themselves. Those asymmetries are part of the evidence, not gaps to be filled by invention.[^ch36-check-silences][^ch36-check-mortality]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines coalition strategy and the European war. §2 follows the Pacific campaigns and the racialized conduct of that war. §3 turns to industrial mobilization and the home fronts. §4 examines citizenship, incarceration, and refuge, and reads the 1943 leave-clearance questionnaire in the In Focus. §5 traces the Holocaust, the atomic bombings, the surrender, and the founding of the United Nations. §6 asks how historians have explained the war’s coalition, its exclusions, and its endings. The sections are thematic and overlap in time: §§1 and 2 both run from 1942 to 1945, and §§3 and 4 both concern the home front. The chapter begins in December 1941, when Churchill crossed the Atlantic to plan the war with Roosevelt.[^ch36-check-scope]

## 1. Coalition and European strategy

### 1.1 The claim: coalition warfare as bargaining

**The claim.** The Grand Alliance won the war in Europe through constant bargaining among allies with different priorities. The American preference for a direct cross-channel invasion of France competed throughout 1942 and 1943 with the British preference for operations in the Mediterranean. The result was not a plan handed down from Washington. It was a negotiated sequence, and the second-front delay was the alliance’s central political conflict.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences][^ch36-stoler]

The sequence can be traced in conferences and decisions. It cannot be reduced to a single strategic idea. The Americans believed that a large invasion across the English Channel was the shortest road to Germany. British planners, remembering the slaughter of the First World War, preferred to attack the Axis at its edges, where their sea power and colonial bases gave them advantages. The Soviet Union, bearing the heaviest German assault, wanted a second front in France as soon as possible. Each position had a military logic, and each served a national interest. The record shows the decisions, not that any single strategy was obviously correct.[^ch36-war-conferences][^ch36-dday][^ch36-stoler][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 Germany First and the Combined Chiefs

At ARCADIA the two powers reaffirmed “Germany First” and built the Combined Chiefs of Staff, giving the coalition a permanent institution. The conference created the American Joint Chiefs of Staff as a counterpart to the British Chiefs of Staff Committee. The British proposed the combined body, and General George C. Marshall, the Army chief of staff, quickly endorsed it. The Combined Chiefs became the supreme Allied command for planning global strategy and directing Anglo-American forces in the theaters.[^ch36-arcadia]

The institution mattered because it made coalition warfare routine. It gave the Allies a place to argue about shipping, landing craft, aircraft allocations, and the timing of offensives, and it gave those arguments a procedure. It also gave the American chiefs a standing role in foreign policy that outlasted the war. The Joint Chiefs were not a temporary wartime committee. They became a permanent instrument of American power.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-stoler]

“Germany First” was a priority, not a promise to leave the Pacific alone. The campaigns of 1942–44 consumed men, ships, and aircraft that the priority could not fully spare. The question of whether the principle was honored, and at what cost to the Pacific, belongs to the debate in §6. The record shows the principle and the competing demands, not a single balance between them.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences]

### 1.3 The second-front debate and the Mediterranean

In 1942 the Allies chose North Africa rather than a cross-channel invasion. American and British forces landed in French North Africa in November under the command of Eisenhower. The operation gave the coalition a victory, but it also postponed the invasion of France. At Casablanca, from January 14 to 24, 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill resolved to concentrate against Germany, to invade Sicily and the Italian mainland, to intensify the strategic bombing campaign, and to keep pressure on Japan in the Pacific.[^ch36-dday][^ch36-casablanca]

The Mediterranean strategy pleased the British and frustrated the Soviet Union. It helped knock Italy out of the war and tied down German divisions, but it did not relieve the German pressure on the Eastern Front as a cross-channel invasion would have. At Tehran later in 1943, the Big Three confirmed the cross-channel invasion and Stalin promised that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan after Germany’s defeat. The invasion came on June 6, 1944.[^ch36-war-conferences][^ch36-dday]

The decisions and their sequence are documented. What is not documented is the claim that the delay lengthened the war. That claim depends on a counterfactual about what an earlier invasion would have done, and the chapter treats it as open.[^ch36-check-debate-works][^ch36-stoler]

### 1.4 Unconditional surrender and coalition aims

At Casablanca Roosevelt announced the policy of “unconditional surrender.” He said that he and Churchill had decided that the only way to ensure postwar peace was to demand complete victory, while adding that the policy did not mean the destruction of the German people. It meant, in his words, “the destruction of the philosophies in those countries which are based on conquest and the subjugation of other people.”[^ch36-casablanca]

The policy bound the coalition to total victory and made a negotiated peace with a German government impossible to promise. It also made postwar reconstruction harder to describe, because it did not say what would replace the defeated regimes. Some historians argue that it stiffened German resistance and prolonged the war. Others argue that it reassured the Soviet Union and prevented a separate peace. The announcement and its stated meaning are documented. The claim that the policy caused a longer war is not, and the chapter attributes that claim to the historians who make it.[^ch36-casablanca][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 Map 27 and the European campaigns

The European war was won through the campaigns the coalition planned together: the North African and Italian operations, the strategic bombing campaign, the Normandy invasion of June 6, 1944, and the advance into Germany. The Normandy invasion opened the western front that Stalin had demanded since 1941. The advance into Germany ended the war in Europe.[^ch36-dday][^ch36-war-conferences]

Map 27, “American deployments, 1941–1945,” is the chapter’s registered map. It is planned to distinguish the European and Mediterranean theaters from the Pacific, show the major deployment and supply routes, and identify the observation date of each element. Its caption and source line will state that the evidence is official Army and naval histories and National Archives campaign maps, and the production record is `repo-map27`. The map must distinguish coalition theaters and supply routes rather than implying that all American forces moved under one command.[^ch36-check-map27][^ch36-arcadia]

The record can show deployments and campaigns. A map can show where forces went; it cannot show why a landing succeeded, and the strategy stays in the prose.[^ch36-check-map27]

## 2. Pacific campaigns

### 2.1 The claim: a second war with its own logic

The Pacific war followed its own geography and command structure, and its conduct was marked by a racialized hatred on both sides that the European war did not match. The theater was an ocean war of carriers, amphibious landings, and long supply lines. Its distances were measured in thousands of miles, and its islands were stepping stones rather than places where armies could maneuver. Map 27, described in §1.5, is planned to plot the Pacific deployments alongside the European ones and to show how far the war’s supply lines ran. The fighting was also shaped by ideas about race that appeared in propaganda, in orders, and in the treatment of prisoners.[^ch36-pacific-strategy][^ch36-dower][^ch36-check-map27]

The chapter presents the Pacific campaigns together with the racial ideology that shaped them because the two cannot be separated without distorting either. The most influential statement of that view is John W. Dower’s *War Without Mercy*, which argues that the Pacific war was a race war and that racial thinking shaped combat, atrocity, and memory on both sides. The evidence of propaganda and policy is consistent with that argument, though it does not prove that racial hatred alone explains any particular atrocity.[^ch36-dower][^ch36-check-debate-works]

The record can show campaigns, casualties, and propaganda. It cannot show what every combatant believed. It also cannot show that the Pacific war was unique in its racial violence, because the war in Europe also produced racial atrocities on an enormous scale. The difference is one of degree and form, not of kind.[^ch36-check-silences][^ch36-dower]

### 2.2 Midway and the turn of the Pacific war

The Battle of Midway of June 4–7, 1942 was a five-day naval engagement in the central Pacific that changed the course of the Pacific war. It followed the attack on Pearl Harbor and a string of Japanese advances. American intelligence officers had partly penetrated the Japanese naval code, and Admiral Chester W. Nimitz used the warning to place his carriers where they could ambush the Japanese fleet. Japan lost four fleet carriers in a single day, along with more than 100 trained pilots.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-pacific-strategy]

The battle was a turning point, not the end of the contest. It shifted the balance of naval power and allowed the United States to take the offensive, but Japan still held a vast empire and fought on for three more years. Midway changed the direction of the war without settling its outcome.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 The island campaigns and command

From 1943 American forces advanced across the central and southwestern Pacific under two commands. Nimitz commanded the central Pacific, and General Douglas MacArthur commanded the southwest Pacific. The two drives converged toward Japan through the Gilbert, Marshall, and Mariana Islands and along the coast of New Guinea and the Philippines. The arrangement gave each commander a theater and a supply line, and it also produced a rivalry over strategy and resources.[^ch36-pacific-strategy]

The advance was costly. The invasions of Iwo Jima on February 19, 1945 and Okinawa on April 1, 1945 were assaults against entrenched Japanese defenders who fought from caves, tunnels, and fortified ridges. The campaigns brought American forces within range of the Japanese home islands and gave the Army Air Forces bases for attacks on Japan. They also produced casualty figures that shaped estimates of what an invasion of Japan would cost.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-pacific-strategy]

### 2.4 Iwo Jima, Okinawa, and the calculus of invasion

The bloodshed on Iwo Jima and Okinawa shaped American estimates of what an invasion of the Japanese home islands would cost, and those estimates entered the debate over the atomic bomb. The landing forces on Iwo Jima suffered 6,821 killed and 19,217 wounded. Okinawa cost more than 49,000 American casualties, including about 12,000 deaths. About 90,000 Japanese combatants died on Okinawa, and deaths among Okinawan civilians may have reached 150,000.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa]

The figures carried a message to planners. If small islands could cost tens of thousands of casualties, an invasion of the Japanese home islands might cost hundreds of thousands. The estimates varied widely. The chapter does not present a single projection as the number that decided the atomic question.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-check-numbers]

The casualties are documented. What is not documented is the claim that the projected invasion casualties dictated the atomic decision. That claim is an interpretation of motive, and the chapter treats it as an open question in §6. The casualties made the invasion look costly; they did not make the bomb the only alternative.[^ch36-iwo-okinawa][^ch36-check-atomic]

### 2.5 Race, atrocity, and the conduct of the Pacific war

Both sides in the Pacific war dehumanized the other, and the racial character of the fighting shaped atrocity, surrender, and memory. John W. Dower’s *War Without Mercy* documents American propaganda that portrayed Japanese soldiers as vermin and apes, and Japanese propaganda that portrayed Americans as soft, decadent, and barbaric. The images appeared in films, posters, newspapers, and soldiers’ songs. They made it easier to kill and harder to surrender.[^ch36-dower][^ch36-owi]

The racialization of the enemy was connected to the home-front racism that produced the incarceration of Japanese Americans in §4. The same ideas that made Japanese soldiers seem less than human made Japanese American citizens seem like a threat inside the country. The connection does not mean that the same people made both policies. It means that both drew on a shared vocabulary of race and danger.[^ch36-dower][^ch36-eo9066]

The propaganda and the policy are documented. Neither proves that racial hatred alone explains specific atrocities. The chapter distinguishes ideology, orders, and circumstances, because a racist image is not a sufficient explanation of every killing. The evidence supports the claim that race shaped the war’s conduct, not that it determined every act.[^ch36-check-debate-works][^ch36-dower]

## 3. The arsenal and home fronts

### 3.1 The claim: a negotiated mobilization

American victory rested on an industrial mobilization that converted the world’s largest economy to war production, and that mobilization was negotiated among government, corporations, and organized labor rather than simply commanded. The government set goals, allocated materials, and signed contracts. Corporations kept control of production and profits. Unions bargained over wages, hours, and the closed shop. The result was a war economy built through deals, not a command economy imposed from Washington.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-kennedy]

The bargains shaped who benefited from the boom. War contracts went disproportionately to large firms with the capacity to manage them. Workers gained jobs, but they also faced inflation, rationing, and crowded housing. The New Deal’s agencies and precedents mattered, because the government already had experience in planning and spending. The war put millions of people to work, but the recovery began before Pearl Harbor and depended on spending that had already started. The chapter connects the boom to the New Deal without claiming that the war was the only cause of prosperity.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-kennedy][^ch36-detroit][^ch36-check-homefront][^ch36-check-ch34]

### 3.2 Out-producing the enemy

Roosevelt set extraordinary production goals after Pearl Harbor and created the War Production Board in 1942 to coordinate them. He told the country that “Powerful enemies must be out-fought and out-produced.” The goals were staggering: 60,000 aircraft in 1942 and 125,000 in 1943, along with 120,000 tanks and 55,000 antiaircraft guns in the same period. The goals were aspirational, and the totals below measure what was actually produced.[^ch36-war-production]

The output was enormous. In the three years after Midway, the United States built 17 aircraft carriers to Japan’s six. Over the war American industry provided nearly two-thirds of all Allied military equipment, producing about 297,000 aircraft, 193,000 artillery pieces, 86,000 tanks, and two million army trucks. American industrial production, already the world’s largest, doubled in four years.[^ch36-war-production]

The totals are documented. They do not prove that any single agency caused the expansion. The War Production Board coordinated and allocated, but the factories, the managers, and the workers did the producing. The totals measure capacity, not efficiency or fairness.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Women workers and the limits of “Rosie”

More than six million women entered the workforce during the war, taking jobs in factories, shipyards, and heavy industry. Roosevelt’s Executive Order 8802 banned discrimination in defense jobs and opened new roles for women and for Black workers. Companies were encouraged to hire women who had not normally worked, including mothers. Women moved from clerical and domestic jobs into welding, machining, assembly, and testing.[^ch36-rosie]

The “Rosie the Riveter” image obscured as much as it showed. The name came from a popular 1942 song by Redd Evans and John Jacob Loeb, and the “We Can Do It!” poster was a War Production Board image by J. Howard Miller. The image celebrated a white woman in a factory, while the workforce included Black women, older women, and women who had always needed to work. Women faced lower pay and discrimination on the job, and many were pushed out of industrial work when the war ended.[^ch36-rosie]

The wartime opening was temporary for many women, and the postwar return to domesticity was enforced by layoffs, union rules, and public pressure. The chapter presents the opening as real and limited, not as a revolution.[^ch36-rosie][^ch36-check-homefront]

### 3.4 Mexican labor, migration, and the Bracero Program

Facing a farm-labor shortage, the United States and Mexico concluded the Mexican Farm Labor Agreement of August 4, 1942, creating the Bracero Program. The program eventually brought more than four million Mexican workers to American agriculture and railroads between 1942 and 1964. It was a bilateral bargain: Mexico wanted work for its citizens and protections for them, and American growers wanted labor.[^ch36-bracero]

The two governments agreed on protocols meant to protect braceros from discrimination and poor wages. The protections were unevenly enforced. Workers faced surcharges for room and board, deducted pay, and exposure to deadly chemicals. The program outlasted the war and remained a continuing source of cheap labor until it ended on December 31, 1964. Its legacy included both the migrants who stayed and the unauthorized workers who followed.[^ch36-bracero]

The program was not a humanitarian gesture. It was a labor policy shaped by the needs of growers and the diplomacy of two governments, and the gap between its protocols and its practice is the measure of its limits.[^ch36-bracero][^ch36-check-refuge]

### 3.5 Migration, rationing, and the home-front bargain

The war moved millions of Americans toward defense plants and military bases. Families left farms for cities, and Black families left the South for industrial jobs in the North and West. The migration created housing shortages, crowded schools, and new racial conflicts. It also gave many people jobs and incomes they had not had during the Depression.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-detroit]

The civilian population became a partner in the war economy through rationing, war bonds, and higher taxes. Rationing limited goods the military needed, war bonds absorbed savings and financed part of the deficit, and higher taxes, including a lower personal exemption, paid for another part. The bond drives and rationing campaigns also taught Americans to see consumption as a contribution to victory.[^ch36-war-production]

The home front was both opportunity and strain. The boom was not evenly shared, and the racial and regional inequalities it left became conflicts in 1943. The chapter connects the migration to the violence in Detroit and other cities, where housing and jobs were the flashpoints. The home-front bargain was a promise of sacrifice and reward, and the promise was kept unevenly.[^ch36-detroit][^ch36-check-homefront]

### 3.6 The Office of War Information and the “good war” image

The government and the culture industries produced an image of the war as a unified democratic crusade. The Office of War Information, created by executive order on June 12, 1942, coordinated posters, films, radio broadcasts, and other messages. It urged Americans to support the war effort, conserve resources, buy bonds, and keep military secrets. The posters presented the war as a defense of freedom and abundance.[^ch36-owi]

The image later earned the name “the good war.” Studs Terkel used the phrase, in quotation marks, as the title of his oral history of the war, and the label became a way of remembering the conflict as a time of national unity and moral clarity. The label obscured the incarceration, the race riots, the refugee crisis, and the violence of the Pacific war. It also obscured the experience of the defeated and the dead of occupied countries.[^ch36-terkel][^ch36-cwric]

The chapter uses the “good war” label as a remembered description to analyze, not as a neutral verdict. The record can show propaganda and memory, not that most Americans believed the image. The gap between official culture and popular experience is one of the chapter’s subjects.[^ch36-check-silences][^ch36-owi]

## 4. Citizenship, incarceration, and refuge

### 4.1 The claim: belonging tested

The war tested who belonged to the nation, and it did so through sacrifice and exclusion at once. Japanese Americans were incarcerated and their sons were drafted. Black Americans fought a “Double V” campaign at home and abroad. Jewish refugees were largely kept out even as the Holocaust became known. The same state that refused and imprisoned some citizens also demanded their labor and their lives.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-double-v][^ch36-wyman]

The chapter holds service and exclusion together because they belonged to the same history. The government that removed Japanese Americans from the West Coast also called on them to serve. The government that segregated Black soldiers also needed Black labor. The government that restricted Jewish immigration also knew about the killing. These are not contradictions to be resolved. They are the shape of the wartime state.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-double-v]

### 4.2 Executive Order 9066 and the incarceration

On February 19, 1942 Roosevelt issued Executive Order 9066, authorizing military commanders to exclude civilians from designated areas. The order did not name any ethnic group. Lieutenant General John L. DeWitt of the Western Defense Command used it to impose curfews on Japanese Americans and then to force their evacuation on 48 hours’ notice. In the next six months, about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were moved to assembly centers and then to relocation centers; nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens. The National Archives gives about 122,000 for the number moved to assembly centers.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-manzanar]

Families were moved first to temporary assembly centers at racetracks, fairgrounds, and similar facilities, and then to 10 relocation centers in seven states. Manzanar, in California’s Owens Valley, was surrounded by barbed wire and eight guard towers with searchlights and patrolled by military police. By September 1942 more than 10,000 people were crowded into 504 barracks organized into 36 blocks. Each block of 200 to 400 people shared toilets, showers, a laundry room, and a mess hall. A family of eight might be assigned a single room of 20 by 25 feet.[^ch36-manzanar]

The order, the numbers, and the conditions are documented. The claim that the incarceration was a military necessity is not. The Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians later concluded that Executive Order 9066 was not justified by military necessity and that the causes were race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership. The chapter prefers “incarceration” to the government’s “relocation,” and it reserves “internment” for the detention of enemy aliens, because the citizen majority were not enemy aliens.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-cwric][^ch36-check-incarceration-terms]

### 4.3 The In Focus: the 1943 “Application for Leave Clearance”

**In Focus.** In February 1943 the War Relocation Authority and the War Department began registering adults in the camps with a form called the “Application for Leave Clearance.” The War Department wanted to register draft-age men, and the War Relocation Authority wanted a list of adults eligible to leave. Two questions carried the loyalty test. Question 27 asked, “Are you willing to serve in the armed forces of the United States on combat duty, wherever ordered?” Question 28 asked whether the respondent would “swear unqualified allegiance to the United States of America and faithfully defend the United States from any and all attacks by foreign and domestic forces, and forswear any form of allegiance or disobedience to the Japanese Emperor, or any other foreign government, power, or organization.” The form fell differently on different people. Many Issei, the immigrant generation barred by law from naturalized citizenship, feared that answering “yes” would leave them stateless. Many Nisei, the citizen generation, feared that answering “yes” implied they had previously been loyal to the emperor. A significant minority refused, qualified, or answered “no” to both questions. About 12,000 of the 78,000 adults who received the form were eventually segregated at Tule Lake. These respondents were distinct from the separate draft resisters, most of whom answered “yes” to the loyalty questions and refused induction.[^ch36-loyalty-questions][^ch36-no-no][^ch36-cwric][^ch36-manzanar]

The questionnaire was a release instrument and a loyalty test at once, and its wording made loyalty something a person had to prove under duress. The government changed Question 28 for the Issei after the confusion became clear, but the damage was done. The episode shows how a bureaucratic form can turn a legal status into a moral test, and how the tested can resist by refusing the terms of the test.[^ch36-loyalty-questions][^ch36-check-focus]

### 4.4 Nisei service, Korematsu, and the 442nd

Japanese Americans served in large numbers even as their families remained imprisoned. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team was activated on February 1, 1943. Hawaiian-born Nisei made up roughly two-thirds of the regiment, and the rest came from the mainland. The unit fought in Italy and France alongside the 100th Infantry Battalion, and it became the most decorated unit for its size and length of service in American military history. The 100th/442nd suffered 9,846 casualties, and nearly 26,000 Japanese Americans served in the war, by the National Park Service’s count; the National Archives puts the number above 30,000.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-manzanar][^ch36-check-numbers]

The Supreme Court upheld the exclusion in *Korematsu v. United States* (1944). The Court held that the exclusion of a citizen of Japanese descent was constitutional as of the time it was imposed, while acknowledging that all legal restrictions curtailing the rights of a single racial group are “immediately suspect.” Justice Robert H. Jackson dissented. He warned that the Court had validated “the principle of racial discrimination in criminal procedure and of transplanting American citizens,” and that the principle would lie about like a loaded weapon for any later authority that could claim an urgent need.[^ch36-korematsu]

Service did not erase exclusion. The same country that decorated the 442nd had imprisoned its families and upheld the exclusion in court. The chapter presents the service and the exclusion as one history, not as a balance in which one cancels the other.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-korematsu]

### 4.5 Black Americans, the Double V, and the riots of 1943

Black Americans pressed a “Double V” campaign for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home. The campaign began in 1942, when James G. Thompson, a reader of the *Pittsburgh Courier*, wrote a letter to the editor pointing out the paradox of fighting for democracy abroad while being denied basic rights at home. The newspaper adopted the call for a “Double Victory” and promoted it through articles, editorials, and community organizing. The campaign demanded equality in the armed forces, fair employment in defense industries, and an end to segregation and discrimination.[^ch36-double-v]

The wartime migration to industrial cities produced racial violence. In Detroit, a fight at Belle Isle on June 20, 1943 ignited two days of rioting. By the end, 34 people were dead, 25 of them Black, and 17 of the 25 Black victims were killed by police. Another 675 people were injured. The violence grew from overcrowded housing, competition for jobs, and unequal access to public space and services. It was not a spontaneous explosion of ancient hatreds. It was a conflict produced by migration and segregation.[^ch36-detroit]

The war did not produce a single Black politics. Some Black Americans demanded integration, others built separate institutions, and others resisted the draft or the war itself. The chapter presents the wartime demands as varied, and it treats the Double V as one language among several.[^ch36-double-v][^ch36-check-silences]

### 4.6 Refugees, rescue, and American limits

American refugee policy during the war was restrictive, and the government moved slowly and partially even as evidence of the Holocaust accumulated. The United States had no refugee law or asylum policy in the 1930s and early 1940s. The passengers of the *St. Louis* found no refuge in 1939. The immigration quotas established in 1924 were rarely filled, even when Jews were fleeing Nazi persecution. Between 1933 and 1945, 180,000 to 220,000 refugees from Nazism immigrated to the United States, a small number relative to the need.[^ch36-wrb-nwwii]

Roosevelt created the War Refugee Board only on January 22, 1944. By then, more than five million European Jews had already been murdered. The Board’s creation was a response to public and congressional pressure and to fights within the administration. It came late, and it operated within the same restrictive immigration regime that had limited rescue before. The chapter holds the Board’s work and the earlier refusal together, because one does not undo the other.[^ch36-wrb-nwwii][^ch36-wyman]

The policy and its timing are documented. What is not documented is a precise number of lives that a more generous policy would have saved. The counterfactual is an argument, not a count. The chapter presents the strongest case for what more could have been done and the strongest case for the limits that officials faced, and it does not convert either into a certainty.[^ch36-check-refuge][^ch36-check-debate-works]

## 5. Holocaust, atomic weapons, surrender, and the United Nations

### 5.1 The claim: rescue, annihilation, and the founding of a new order

The war’s final year combined fitful rescue of Europe’s Jews, the invention and use of atomic weapons, the unconditional surrender of Germany and Japan, and the creation of the United Nations. Those developments belonged to the same moment. The same government that declined requests to bomb the gas chambers at Auschwitz was building the bomb that destroyed Hiroshima. The same coalition that demanded unconditional surrender was drafting a charter for a permanent peace. The actors faced these problems together, as questions about what the victors owed a shattered world.[^ch36-wrb-ushmm][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-un-charter]

The chapter resists separating the rescue story from the annihilation, and the atomic story from the founding of the United Nations. The separation makes each story cleaner than it was. The rescue was late and partial, the annihilation had already destroyed most of Europe’s Jews, the bomb ended one war and began another, and the charter promised a peace that the victors would soon contest. The chapter tells them together because that is how they happened.[^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-un-charter]

### 5.2 The War Refugee Board and the limits of rescue

The War Refugee Board, established by executive order on January 22, 1944, funneled money and diplomatic pressure into rescue and relief. The killing it was responding to had been industrialized. At Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest killing center, Jews from across Europe were murdered in gas chambers; on some days as many as 10,000 people were killed there. The Board helped send about $12 million overseas for relief projects, ranging from food packages for concentration camp prisoners to funding underground movements in occupied countries. It appointed representatives in Sweden, Switzerland, the Mediterranean, Turkey, Portugal, and Great Britain. Its representative in Turkey, Ira Hirschmann, negotiated the passage of about 8,000 refugees to Palestine, though one ship, the *Mefkure*, was torpedoed at sea.[^ch36-wrb-ushmm][^ch36-auschwitz-bombing][^ch36-wrb-nwwii]

The Board also encouraged neutral countries to keep their borders open. Roosevelt established the Fort Ontario Emergency Refugee Shelter in Oswego, New York, in June 1944, and it housed nearly 1,000 people, most of them Jewish, who came from Allied-occupied Italy. The Board’s work saved thousands and possibly hundreds of thousands, depending on how one counts the people protected by diplomatic papers and relief. It began late, and it could not undo the earlier refusal. The chapter holds the two facts together.[^ch36-wrb-ushmm][^ch36-wyman]

The Board was a response to pressure, not a reversal of policy. Its creation is evidence of what became possible when the government decided to act, and its lateness is evidence of how much had already been lost.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-check-refuge]

### 5.3 The bombing debate and the politics of rescue

Whether the Allies should have bombed the rail lines to Auschwitz became a central historical dispute over American rescue policy. In the spring of 1944 the Allies received more explicit information about the mass murder by gassing at Auschwitz-Birkenau. Jewish organizations made proposals to halt the killing, and some Jewish leaders called for bombing the gas chambers while others opposed it. The War Refugee Board and the World Jewish Congress forwarded requests to bomb Auschwitz to the War Department, and the requests were denied.[^ch36-auschwitz-bombing]

The strongest case for bombing rests on the fact that the killing depended on trains and that the Allies had aircraft capable of reaching Silesia by July 1944. The strongest case against rests on the uncertainty of the results. The gas chambers and crematoria were small targets, the camps held tens of thousands of prisoners, and a raid might have killed the people it was meant to save. On August 14, Assistant Secretary of War John J. McCloy wrote that such an operation could be executed only by diverting considerable air support from decisive operations and would be of “such doubtful efficacy” that it would not warrant the use of resources. Within a week, American aircraft bombed the I.G. Farben works less than five miles from the killing center.[^ch36-auschwitz-bombing]

David S. Wyman’s *The Abandonment of the Jews* made the charge of abandonment into the sharpest version of the argument that more could have been done. The book’s critics argue that the feasibility and effect of bombing were uncertain and that the diversion of air power would not have saved the Hungarian Jews already killed. What the Allies knew and when is documented. What a raid would have accomplished is not.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-auschwitz-bombing][^ch36-check-debate-works]

### 5.4 The Manhattan Project and Trinity

Under the top-secret Manhattan Project, the United States built the world’s first atomic weapons at Oak Ridge, Hanford, and Los Alamos. The project began after Albert Einstein signed a letter to Roosevelt in August 1939 warning that an “extremely powerful bomb” might be constructed. Roosevelt formed the Advisory Committee on Uranium, which met for the first time on October 21, 1939. The project pursued uranium and plutonium paths in parallel and acquired its sites through eminent domain, displacing Tribes, farming communities, and homesteaders. Some residents had 30 days to leave and were minimally compensated.[^ch36-manhattan]

Robert Oppenheimer directed the Los Alamos laboratory and named the test “Trinity,” after the poems of John Donne. The device was detonated on the Jornada del Muerto, a remote corner of the Alamogordo Bombing Range in southern New Mexico, on July 16, 1945. The test was a gamble on an untested design, and it worked. The project had cost about $2 billion, employed 125,000 people at its peak, and produced a weapon that no one had used before.[^ch36-trinity][^ch36-truman-hiroshima]

The bomb was a technical success and a moral problem, and the two descriptions are not in competition. The project and the test are documented. The necessity of the weapon is not.[^ch36-trinity][^ch36-check-atomic]

### 5.5 Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and the surrender

The United States bombed Hiroshima on August 6, 1945. President Harry S. Truman announced that an American airplane had dropped one bomb with the power of more than 20,000 tons of TNT. “It is an atomic bomb,” he said. “It is a harnessing of the basic power of the universe.” The Soviet Union declared war on Japan on August 8. The United States dropped a second bomb on Nagasaki on August 9. Japan offered to surrender on August 10, and the emperor announced the surrender on August 15.[^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-osti-surrender]

The formal instrument of surrender was signed aboard the USS *Missouri* in Tokyo Bay on September 2, 1945. The Japanese envoys Mamoru Shigemitsu and Yoshijiro Umezu signed for the emperor. The text proclaimed “the unconditional surrender to the Allied Powers” of the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters and all Japanese armed forces. General Douglas MacArthur signed for the Allies, and the time was recorded as four minutes past nine.[^ch36-surrender]

The bombings, the statements, and the surrender are documented. The claim that either bomb alone caused the surrender is not. The Soviet declaration of war came between the two bombings, and Soviet forces invaded Manchuria on August 9. The Japanese decision involved the emperor, the military, and a divided cabinet. The chapter presents the timing together and treats the causation as an open question.[^ch36-hasegawa][^ch36-check-atomic]

### 5.6 The United Nations Charter at San Francisco

Even before the war ended, the Allies built a new international organization. Representatives of 50 nations met in San Francisco, signed the United Nations Charter on June 26, 1945, and brought it into force on October 24, 1945. The charter created a General Assembly of all members and a Security Council whose five wartime victors, China, France, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and the United States, held permanent seats and individual veto power.[^ch36-un-charter]

The arrangement reflected the war’s power. The victors wrote a charter that preserved their own authority, and the veto ensured that the organization could not act against a great power. The charter also promised human rights, self-determination, and collective security, and those promises became resources for later movements. The institution was both a great-power bargain and a statement of ideals, and the two were not separable.[^ch36-un-charter][^ch36-check-ch37]

The charter was a promise whose record lay ahead, and the coming confrontation among the victors would test it almost immediately. The chapter presents it as an achievement and as a limit, not as a guarantee.[^ch36-un-charter]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The Grand Alliance fought and won the war through negotiation. The Combined Chiefs of Staff, the Germany-first principle, and the succession of conferences were real institutions and decisions, whatever their cost. The alliance did not dissolve into a single command, and it did not fight according to a single plan. It bargained, and the bargaining shaped the campaigns.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-war-conferences]

Industrial mobilization gave the Allies a decisive advantage. American production of aircraft, ships, tanks, and vehicles far exceeded that of the Axis and provided most Allied equipment. The totals are not in dispute, even when their causes are. The war economy converted the world’s largest industrial base into an arsenal, and the conversion was one of the war’s most important events.[^ch36-war-production]

The incarceration was not a military necessity. The government’s own commission concluded that the broad causes were race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership, and that a grave injustice was done. The commission’s finding does not settle every question about the decision, but it does settle the official claim that military necessity required the exclusion.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-eo9066]

The Pacific war was racialized on both sides. The evidence of propaganda, policy, and atrocity supports treating the Pacific war as a race war, even if race alone does not explain every act. The atomic bombs were used, and the surrender followed. The dates, the strikes, and the instrument of surrender are documented.[^ch36-dower][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-surrender]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the atomic bomb necessary to end the war? One position holds that the bombings avoided a costly invasion of Japan and saved lives. Another holds that Japan was already near surrender and that the bomb was aimed partly at Soviet power. The chapter states each at its strongest, and it distinguishes the question of motive from the question of effect.[^ch36-stinson][^ch36-alperovitz][^ch36-walker]

Did the atomic bombs or the Soviet declaration of war cause the surrender? Tsuyoshi Hasegawa argues that the Soviet entry played the greater role. The orthodox account emphasizes the bombs. The chapter weighs both against the Japanese records, and it notes that the two events came within two days of each other.[^ch36-hasegawa][^ch36-walker]

Did the second-front delay lengthen the war? The British defend the Mediterranean strategy. The Americans and the Soviets believed that a cross-channel invasion should have come sooner. The chapter presents the strategic arguments, not only the outcomes, and it treats the counterfactual as an argument rather than a measurement.[^ch36-stoler][^ch36-war-conferences]

Could the United States have rescued more Jews? Wyman’s abandonment thesis and its critics differ on what was feasible and what officials decided. The chapter presents the strongest version of each and refuses to turn the counterfactual into a count. The documentary record supports the timing and the limits of rescue policy; it does not support a precise number of lives that a different policy would have saved.[^ch36-wyman][^ch36-auschwitz-bombing]

How large was the war’s human cost? Global mortality estimates vary widely and rest on different methods. One common compilation gives about 15 million battle deaths and 45 million civilian deaths, and it warns that the figures vary and that civilian deaths in China alone may have exceeded 50 million. The chapter gives ranges, names the methods, and refuses to present one number as settled.[^ch36-mortality][^ch36-check-mortality]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the United States helped win a coalition war of enormous destructive power and, in the same years, imprisoned tens of thousands of its own citizens, restricted refugees, and used atomic weapons against cities. Neither a victory story nor a betrayal story explains the whole. The victory was real, and so were the exclusions. A history that emphasizes one without the other is incomplete.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-truman-hiroshima]

The second front’s timing, the incarceration, the rescue policy, and the atomic decision were choices taken by people who could have chosen otherwise. None was dictated by the outcome. The counterfactuals are not decorations. They identify where the evidence would have to be strongest to support a claim of inevitability.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-alperovitz]

Structure and choice belong together. Geography, industrial capacity, alliance politics, and racial ideology set the pressures. The choices made within those pressures determined the timing and the form of the war’s events. A history that emphasizes only structure cannot explain why the incarceration happened when it did. A history that emphasizes only choice cannot explain why the war’s exclusions followed a racial pattern.[^ch36-stoler][^ch36-dower]

The synthesis for this chapter is that coalition warfare expanded American power abroad and sharpened the question of who belonged at home. The war’s endings — rescue, annihilation, the bomb, and the United Nations — left a world the victors only partly controlled. That is the answer to the chapter’s question, and it is a double answer: power and belonging changed together.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-un-charter]

## Legacies

The war left the United States a permanent national security state. The agencies, alliances, and deployment patterns built between 1941 and 1945 fed directly into the Cold War institutions that Chapter 37 describes. The military establishment, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, and the network of overseas bases outlasted the coalition that created them. The country did not demobilize its new capacities when the fighting ended; it reorganized them for a longer confrontation.[^ch36-arcadia][^ch36-check-ch37]

The war also left an accounting. The incarceration of Japanese Americans produced a generations-long struggle for redress that ended in a formal apology and a $20,000 payment to each surviving internee under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988. The memory of the camps became a touchstone for later arguments about security and civil liberty. The commission’s report gave those arguments a documentary foundation, and the report’s phrase about race prejudice, war hysteria, and political leadership entered the national vocabulary.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-cwric]

The atomic bombings began an argument that has not ended. The decision to use the bombs, their role in the surrender, and their place in the Cold War arms race remain contested. The chapter notes that the debate is live rather than closed, and it does not use the passage of time as evidence that the decision was right. The debate is about evidence and judgment at once, and the passage of time has not settled it.[^ch36-alperovitz][^ch36-hasegawa]

The “good war” image proved durable and selective. It celebrated the mobilization and the victory while leaving the incarceration, the race riots, and the refugee crisis at the margins. That selectivity is why the chapter treats memory as part of the history rather than a footnote to it. A country’s account of a war is also an account of what it wants to be, and the two accounts are never entirely separate.[^ch36-terkel][^ch36-cwric]

## Summary

- At the ARCADIA Conference of December 1941 the United States and Britain reaffirmed “Germany First” and created the Combined Chiefs of Staff, giving the Grand Alliance a permanent planning institution.[^ch36-arcadia]
- The Battle of Midway of June 4–7, 1942 changed the course of the Pacific war, and the campaigns of 1945 on Iwo Jima and Okinawa produced casualty figures that shaped the debate over invading Japan.[^ch36-midway][^ch36-iwo-okinawa]
- American industry produced nearly two-thirds of all Allied military equipment, including about 297,000 aircraft, 86,000 tanks, and two million army trucks, and more than six million women entered the workforce.[^ch36-war-production][^ch36-rosie]
- The Mexican Farm Labor Agreement of August 4, 1942 created the Bracero Program, which eventually brought more than four million Mexican workers to American agriculture and railroads.[^ch36-bracero]
- Executive Order 9066 of February 19, 1942 led to the incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, nearly 70,000 of them citizens, and the 1943 leave-clearance questionnaire segregated some 12,000 respondents at Tule Lake.[^ch36-eo9066][^ch36-no-no]
- Japanese Americans served in large numbers, and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team became the most decorated unit for its size and length of service, even as the Supreme Court upheld their exclusion in *Korematsu*.[^ch36-442nd][^ch36-korematsu]
- Black Americans pressed a “Double V” campaign for victory abroad and equality at home, and the Detroit riot of June 1943 killed 34 people, 25 of them Black.[^ch36-double-v][^ch36-detroit]
- The War Refugee Board, created on January 22, 1944, funded relief and negotiated rescues, but it began after more than five million European Jews had already been murdered.[^ch36-wrb-nwwii][^ch36-wrb-ushmm]
- The Manhattan Project tested the first atomic bomb at Trinity on July 16, 1945; the United States bombed Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9; Japan signed the instrument of surrender aboard the USS *Missouri* on September 2, 1945.[^ch36-trinity][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-surrender]
- Fifty nations signed the United Nations Charter in San Francisco on June 26, 1945, and it entered into force on October 24, 1945.[^ch36-un-charter]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between December 1941 and September 1945 the United States helped win the largest war in history and, in the same years, imprisoned tens of thousands of its own citizens, turned away refugees, and used atomic weapons against cities. The chapter’s question is how coalition warfare remade American power and belonging, and it is answered by that double record of victory and exclusion, and by endings that left a world the victors only partly controlled.[^ch36-cwric][^ch36-truman-hiroshima][^ch36-un-charter]
