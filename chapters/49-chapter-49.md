# Chapter 49. A Nation Reckoning (2021–October 29, 2026)

> “We hold that *Roe* and *Casey* must be overruled. The Constitution makes no reference to abortion, and no such right is implicitly protected by any constitutional provision …”[^ch49-dobbs-2022]
>
> — Justice Samuel A. Alito Jr., for the Court, *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization*, June 24, 2022.[^ch49-dobbs-2022]

> “The golden age of America begins right now.”[^ch49-trump-inaugural-2025]
>
> — President Donald J. Trump, second inaugural address, Washington, D.C., January 20, 2025.[^ch49-trump-inaugural-2025]

> “Most republics in history don’t last 300 years, and it’s because it’s a really hard thing to do. … It’s a nation that is based on an idea rather than a shared ethnic or religious background. And that means it’s going to be messy.”[^ch49-chervinsky-2026]
>
> — Lindsay M. Chervinsky, interview with Robert Costa, CBS News, January 18, 2026.[^ch49-chervinsky-2026]

Three voices, three ways of describing the same country. Alito’s majority treated the Constitution as silent on abortion and returned the question to elected representatives. Trump described a restoration already under way, a golden age beginning at the Capitol. Chervinsky described a republic that survives by argument and compromise, and therefore remains unfinished. They disagree about whether the period that began in 2021 was a rupture, a restoration, or an unfinished contest. That disagreement is the chapter’s subject.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-trump-inaugural-2025][^ch49-chervinsky-2026][^ch49-check-epigraphs]

On the morning of June 24, 2022, the Supreme Court released its decision in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization*, No. 19-1392. The slip opinion appeared on the Court’s website with the usual notice that it was subject to formal revision. Its syllabus stated the holding plainly: “The Constitution does not confer a right to abortion; *Roe* and *Casey* are overruled; and the authority to regulate abortion is returned to the people and their elected representatives.” The vote was 6–3. Justice Samuel A. Alito Jr. wrote for the majority. Justices Stephen G. Breyer, Sonia Sotomayor, and Elena Kagan signed a joint dissent.[^ch49-dobbs-2022]

The decision ended a half-century constitutional settlement. *Roe v. Wade* in 1973 and *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey* in 1992 had protected a right to abortion before viability. *Dobbs* did not ban abortion. It removed the federal constitutional guarantee and returned the question to the states, which meant that a right that had been national in scope would now vary by jurisdiction. The majority described that result as a return to the constitutional order; the dissent described it as a loss of liberty and equality. Both descriptions pointed to the same structural fact: a question that the Court had kept for itself for nearly fifty years now belonged to legislatures, courts, and voters in fifty states.[^ch49-dobbs-2022]

Seven weeks earlier, on May 2, 2022, Politico had published a draft of what it described as the majority opinion. The publication was an extraordinary breach of the Court’s confidentiality. The Court’s marshal investigated, and no official report named the source. The leak did not change the holding, but it changed how the decision arrived: a draft had circulated in public before the final text was released. The record establishes the date, the text, the vote, the authorship, and the leak. It does not establish what every person outside the building thought that morning, how many gathered, or what the decision would do to American politics. The opinion ended one settlement; it did not announce the next one.[^ch49-dobbs-leak-2022][^ch49-check-scene]

The chapter’s guiding question follows from that limit. What can a history of an unsettled present responsibly establish, and what must it leave open? The question is not whether *Dobbs* was rightly decided — that is a question of constitutional argument — but what the years that followed can be shown to have done, and what remains a matter of interpretation.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-check-scene]

This chapter covers January 2021 through October 29, 2026, the book’s research cutoff. It draws on the enrolled texts of statutes, the *Federal Register*, slip opinions and the *United States Reports*, election returns and state canvasses, agency statistics and inspectors general’s reports, the *Congressional Record*, contemporaneous reporting, and the secondary literature. It treats “the present,” “polarization,” “democratic backsliding,” “the border,” “the economy,” and “the rule of law” as contested terms that require definition rather than neutral description. “The present” here means the period whose outcome is still contested, not simply the current year. “Polarization” names the distance between the parties and their coalitions, not a single cause. “Democratic backsliding” names the erosion of institutions and norms, not the loss of an election. “The border” names a policy regime and a set of places, not a metaphor for national identity. “The economy” names a set of measures that can move in different directions. “The rule of law” names a set of practices and commitments, not a slogan. It states plainly that the record over-represents presidents, justices, officials, and journalists and under-represents the patients and providers affected by *Dobbs*, the detained and deported, the federal workers who left, and the civilians in Ukraine, Gaza, and Iran. Every claim about 2025 and 2026 in this chapter was checked against a dated record where one was available, and the notes mark what remains unread. No event after October 29, 2026, including the November 2026 election, appears here as completed history.[^ch49-check-cutoff][^ch49-check-names][^ch49-check-silences]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the pandemic inheritance, the recovery program, and the industrial, climate, and labor legislation of 2021–2022. §2 examines *Dobbs*, the firearms and affirmative-action decisions, and the presidential-immunity ruling, with a close reading of the *Dobbs* joint dissent. §3 examines the 2022 midterm elections and the 2024 presidential election. §4 examines the second Trump administration’s domestic actions through October 2026, including the tariffs, the fiscal law, the government-efficiency program, immigration enforcement, and domestic deployments. §5 examines the aftermath of the Afghanistan withdrawal, the war in Ukraine, the conflicts in Gaza and Iran, artificial intelligence, and the 250th anniversary. §6 asks what the period settled and what remains open about democratic backsliding, institutional resilience, political economy, and coalition change. The chapter begins at the Supreme Court on June 24, 2022, when the Justices released a decision that ended a half-century settlement.[^ch49-check-scope]

Chapter 48 owns the 2015–21 campaign and the first Trump presidency, and it closes on the contested 2020 election, the January 6, 2021, attack, and the second acquittal. This chapter opens where Chapter 48’s bridge points, at the *Dobbs* release, and states the changed problem in one sentence: two years after an attack on the transfer of power, the Supreme Court announced a decision that ended a half-century constitutional settlement and returned the question to the states. Chapter 50 owns the retrospective synthesis and the Declaration’s prudence clause; this chapter names those developments without re-narrating them.[^ch49-check-ch48][^ch49-check-ch50]

## 1. Pandemic inheritance and the legislative program (2021–2022)

### 1.1 The claim: an uneven recovery and a burst of industrial policy

**The claim.** The Biden administration inherited a pandemic economy, passed a large recovery law and then a series of industrial, infrastructure, and climate laws, and presided over a recovery that lowered unemployment while inflation reached a four-decade high. The chapter holds the recovery, the price surge, and the distribution of both together rather than choosing one register. A history that records only the jobs misses the prices; a history that records only the prices misses who went back to work and who did not.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-iija-2021][^ch49-ira-2022][^ch49-chips-2022][^ch49-bls-cpi-2022]

The section runs from the recovery law through the infrastructure, climate, and semiconductor statutes, the labor actions of 2023, and the geography of industrial investment shown in Map 39. It ends with the workers whose voices are least represented in the record.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-ira-2022][^ch49-uaw-2023][^ch49-check-map39]

### 1.2 The American Rescue Plan and the recovery

The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 became Public Law 117-2 on March 11, 2021. It extended unemployment benefits, funded direct payments, vaccine distribution, school reopening, and state and local aid, and temporarily expanded the child tax credit. It followed the emergency relief measures of 2020 and set the fiscal terms for the recovery.[^ch49-arp-2021]

Supporters argued that the law prevented a deeper slump. Critics argued that it contributed to inflation. The enacted provisions are documented; the estimated cost depends on the budget office’s assumptions, and the later assessments depend on their models. A projection is not an outcome. The American economy in 2021 and 2022 was also shaped by supply-chain disruption, energy prices, the war in Ukraine, and the Federal Reserve’s decisions.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-check-numbers]

The recovery and the price surge ran together. The unemployment rate fell, and the Consumer Price Index rose 9.1 percent over the twelve months ending June 2022, the largest such increase since 1981. Inflation then eased in 2023 and 2024 as supply conditions changed and the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate. The series, their base periods, and the Fed’s rate response belong together; no single law can be credited or blamed for a movement that crossed borders.[^ch49-bls-cpi-2022][^ch49-fed-2022][^ch49-check-numbers]

“The economy” is not one measure. Employment, real wages, prices, and distribution moved differently, and the recovery was uneven by region, sector, and race. The measure matters: employment, real wages, prices, and distribution answer different questions. A falling unemployment rate can coexist with rising rents and food prices; a rising stock market can coexist with household anxiety. The period’s politics was shaped by that coexistence, not by a single index.[^ch49-bls-cpi-2022][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Infrastructure, climate, and semiconductors

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act became Public Law 117-58 on November 15, 2021. It funded roads, bridges, rail, broadband, water systems, and an electric-vehicle charging network, much of it through multiyear formula and grant programs. The law’s authorizations were not the same as its outlays; a multiyear program spends over time, and a dollar authorized is not a dollar spent in the year of enactment.[^ch49-iija-2021]

The Inflation Reduction Act became Public Law 117-169 on August 16, 2022. It provided tax credits and grants for clean energy, allowed Medicare to negotiate some drug prices, and funded Internal Revenue Service enforcement. It was enacted by budget reconciliation with no Republican votes. The law’s climate provisions were designed to work through incentives rather than a carbon tax, and their effects depended on how households and firms responded.[^ch49-ira-2022]

The CHIPS and Science Act became Public Law 117-167 on August 9, 2022. It subsidized domestic semiconductor manufacturing and authorized science and technology programs. The law was an explicit industrial policy: it used public money to attract private investment in a sector that policymakers regarded as strategically vulnerable. Whether it produced the intended capacity, and where, are questions that outlast the enactment.[^ch49-chips-2022]

### 1.4 Labor

In 2023 the United Auto Workers conducted a “stand-up” strike against the three large automakers. The union struck selected plants rather than all of them at once, a tactic designed to disrupt production while preserving its strike fund. The strike ended with contracts that the union described as raising wages and changing the terms for temporary workers. Hollywood writers and actors struck separately, and the writers’ contract addressed generative artificial intelligence. The strikes tested whether a tight labor market and public support could raise wages in sectors facing technological change.[^ch49-uaw-2023]

The settlements are evidence of what the parties agreed, not proof of a new labor order. Union membership remained a small share of the private workforce, and the strikes took place in sectors where unions already had a foothold. The settlements and their later effects are the evidence; a verdict at the time is not. The workers whose voices are least represented in the record — the unorganized, unauthorized immigrant workers, and the care workers who could not strike — belong in the account.[^ch49-uaw-2023][^ch49-check-silences]

## 2. Rights: *Dobbs*, firearms, affirmative action, and immunity (2022–2024)

### 2.1 The claim: a Court remaking rights and the scope of the presidency

**The claim.** In three terms the Supreme Court ended the constitutional right to abortion, expanded the right to carry firearms, struck down race-conscious college admissions, and granted presidents broad immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts. The decisions were related but distinct. They were made by a conservative majority that had been built through three presidential appointments, and they changed the legal ground on which the period’s political conflicts were fought. They did not all point in the same direction: one expanded an individual right, one restricted the use of race in admissions, and one shielded the presidency.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

The section runs from *Dobbs* and its aftermath through the firearms, affirmative-action, and immunity decisions, with the In Focus close reading of the joint dissent at §2.2. It distinguishes a decision’s legal holding from its political interpretation and presents the dissents at their strongest.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

### 2.2 *Dobbs* and the In Focus

*Dobbs* held on June 24, 2022, that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and overruled *Roe* and *Casey*, 6–3. The majority’s reasoning rested on history and tradition: the Court held that a right to abortion was not “deeply rooted” in the nation’s history and was not implicit in the concept of ordered liberty. The opinion also addressed stare decisis, concluding that *Roe* and *Casey* were wrongly decided and that overruling them was consistent with the Court’s precedents. The dissent disagreed on both the history and the law.[^ch49-dobbs-2022]

**In Focus.** The joint dissent begins on its first page with a sentence that states a jurisprudence: “For half a century, *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U. S. 113 (1973), and *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey*, 505 U. S. 833 (1992), have protected the liberty and equality of women.” The sentence is not an ornament. It names two constitutional goods — liberty and equality — and treats their protection as the reason the precedents mattered. The dissent ends on its sixtieth page: “With sorrow — for this Court, but more, for the many millions of American women who have today lost a fundamental constitutional protection — we dissent.” Between those sentences, the dissent makes three moves: it reads the majority’s history as selective, it argues that the majority’s reasoning would unsettle other rights, and it warns that the Court’s legitimacy depends on appearing to decide by law rather than by numbers. The box reads the dissent as a document that states a position and predicts consequences. It does not prove that the predicted consequences followed. A dissent can be prescient, mistaken, or both, and the historian’s task is to test its predictions against the record rather than to treat its anger as evidence.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-check-focus]

### 2.3 The post-*Dobbs* landscape

After *Dobbs*, many states restricted or banned abortion and others protected it. The result was a patchwork, not a national settlement. Kansas held the first post-*Dobbs* state referendum on August 2, 2022, and 59 percent of voters rejected a constitutional amendment that would have removed state protections, according to the reporting. The result was read as a surprise, and it became evidence that abortion could mobilize voters across party lines.[^ch49-ks-referendum-2022]

The count changed as legislatures and courts acted. As of April 27, 2026, KFF counted 13 states with abortion bans, 28 states with gestational limits, and nine states and the District of Columbia without gestational limits. The date and the source matter: a moving count is not a fixed one. A state’s legal status and its practical access were not the same, because clinic closures, travel distances, and waiting periods could restrict access even where abortion remained legal.[^ch49-kff-2026][^ch49-check-numbers]

The consequences for maternal morbidity, travel, and clinical capacity are studied but contested. The estimates depend on model assumptions, incomplete data, and the lag between a legal change and its effects. They carry their methods with them, and a single effect is not established. The silence matters too: the patients and providers who experienced the change are less represented in the record than the officials and advocates who described it.[^ch49-kff-2026][^ch49-check-silences]

### 2.4 Firearms, affirmative action, and presidential immunity

In *New York State Rifle & Pistol Association, Inc. v. Bruen*, decided June 23, 2022, the Court held 6–3 that New York’s requirement of a special need for a concealed-carry license violated the Second Amendment. The majority announced a historical-tradition test: a firearm regulation is constitutional only if it is consistent with the nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation. The decision was followed by a wave of lower-court litigation over statutes that had previously been upheld.[^ch49-bruen-2022]

Congress responded in part with the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, Public Law 117-159, signed June 25, 2022. It was the first major federal gun-safety law in nearly three decades. It expanded background checks for buyers under 21, addressed trafficking, and strengthened domestic-violence provisions. The law was narrower than the advocates who had pressed for it wanted and broader than the opponents who had blocked earlier efforts would accept.[^ch49-bsca-2022]

In *Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College* and its companion case against the University of North Carolina, decided June 29, 2023, the Court held that the universities’ admissions programs violated the Equal Protection Clause. The majority, written by Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr., concluded that the programs lacked sufficiently measurable objectives, used race in a negative manner, and lacked a logical end point. The Court divided 6–2 in the Harvard case, after Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson recused herself, and 6–3 in the North Carolina case, where she dissented. The decision did not prohibit universities from considering an applicant’s discussion of how race had affected their life, but it ended the use of race as a direct factor in admissions.[^ch49-sffa-2023]

In *Trump v. United States*, decided July 1, 2024, the Court held 6–3 that a president has absolute immunity from prosecution for acts within the core of constitutional authority and at least presumptive immunity for other official acts. The decision sent the pending federal election-interference prosecution back to the district court for a fact-specific inquiry into which acts were official. The dissent argued that the ruling placed the president above the law; the majority argued that immunity was necessary to protect the office from politically motivated prosecution. The holding and its effect on the pending prosecutions are on the record; neither the majority’s nor the dissent’s characterization is the historical verdict.[^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

## 3. The 2022 and 2024 elections (2022–2024)

### 3.1 The claim: two elections that confounded expectations

**The claim.** Republicans narrowly won the House in 2022 while Democrats gained a Senate seat, and in 2024 Donald J. Trump won both the Electoral College and the popular vote. The two results confounded different expectations: the 2022 midterms were read as a Republican wave that did not arrive, and the 2024 election ended two decades in which the Republican nominee had lost the popular vote. The chapter treats each election as a distinct event with its own evidence, rather than as a single verdict on the period.[^ch49-fec-2022][^ch49-fec-2024]

The section runs from the 2022 midterms through the 2024 primaries, the change at the top of the Democratic ticket, the campaign, the returns, and the electoral geography shown in Map 38.[^ch49-fec-2022][^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-check-map38]

### 3.2 The 2022 midterms

In 2022 Republicans gained control of the House with a narrow majority, 222 seats to 213, while Democrats retained and slightly expanded their Senate majority, 51–49. The Senate result depended on a small number of contests, including races in Georgia, Nevada, and Pennsylvania. The House majority was smaller than the party had expected, and it gave a faction of the Republican conference leverage over the speaker and the agenda.[^ch49-fec-2022]

The results were read against expectations of a large Republican wave. The competing explanations include abortion, candidate quality, turnout, and redistricting. Each has evidence: the *Dobbs* decision put abortion on the ballot in several states; several Republican nominees were weakened by controversies; and turnout and redistricting shaped the map. The explanations can be tested against the returns; none can be chosen in advance. The returns can show where the vote changed; they cannot by themselves establish why any individual voted as they did.[^ch49-fec-2022][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 3.3 The 2024 campaign

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. had planned a rematch with Trump. On July 21, 2024, he withdrew from the race and endorsed Vice President Kamala D. Harris, who became the Democratic nominee. The withdrawal followed a debate in June that raised questions about Biden’s age and a pressure campaign from within his party. Harris consolidated support quickly, and the campaign turned to a shorter general-election contest against Trump.[^ch49-biden-withdraw-2024]

On July 13, 2024, a gunman attempted to assassinate Trump at a rally near Butler, Pennsylvania. The attack wounded Trump, killed one attendee, and wounded two others. The shooter was killed by the Secret Service. The FBI and the Department of Homeland Security’s independent review examined the security failures. The account rests on those records, not on impression, and it distinguishes the documented event from the interpretations that each campaign placed on it.[^ch49-butler-2024]

The campaign was also shaped by litigation over state election rules and by the prospect of violence. The vote and the verification process are documented in the official records; the result is not evidence of a single cause. A campaign is not a referendum on one issue; it is a contest in which turnout, the Electoral College, the economy, incumbency, and the candidates’ coalitions all interact.[^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 3.4 The 2024 election and Map 38

Trump won 312 electoral votes to Harris’s 226. He won 77,302,580 popular votes, or 49.80 percent, to Harris’s 75,017,613, or 48.32 percent. The total vote was 155,238,302. The FEC’s official compilation gives the state electoral votes and the popular-vote totals; other compilations differ slightly because of write-ins, rounding, and the date a canvas was finalized. The chapter names the series it uses rather than mixing them.[^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-check-numbers]

Trump’s victory was a coalitional victory, not a single-issue referendum. An exit poll is an estimate, not a census, and a shift in a group’s share of the vote can reflect turnout, defection, or both. The result was a realignment in the making, not a completed one.[^ch49-fec-2024]

## 4. The second administration’s domestic actions (2025–2026)

### 4.1 The claim: an executive-centered program and its contestation

**The claim.** The second Trump administration, beginning January 20, 2025, pursued tariffs, a large fiscal and tax law, a government-efficiency and workforce-reduction program, expanded immigration enforcement, and domestic military deployments. Each action met litigation, state resistance, or public protest. The chapter traces each from its instrument to its consequences, and it distinguishes what the administration announced, what it did, and what the courts allowed.[^ch49-tariffs-crs-2026][^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-doge-2025][^ch49-la-guard-2025]

The section runs from the tariffs and the fiscal law through the efficiency program, immigration enforcement, the deployments and protests, and the courts. Map 39 returns at §4.3 to place the border and enforcement geography beside the county economic measures.[^ch49-tariffs-crs-2026][^ch49-check-map39]

### 4.2 Tariffs and the fiscal law

In the first months of 2025, the president imposed tariffs on imports from most trading partners under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and Section 232. The IEEPA tariffs included a 25 percent duty on most Canadian and Mexican imports and a 10 percent duty on most Chinese imports, framed as a response to drug trafficking, and a “reciprocal” tariff of at least 10 percent on imports from all trading partners, with higher rates for dozens of nations. The rates changed repeatedly: the tariff on most Chinese goods reached 145 percent at one point. According to the Congressional Research Service’s timeline, the administration also reached framework agreements with several partners.[^ch49-tariffs-crs-2026][^ch49-learning-resources-2026]

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held 6–3 in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the principal opinion, concluding that the power to tax imports belongs to Congress and that IEEPA’s grant of authority to “regulate … importation” did not include the power to impose tariffs. The judgment vacated the D.C. case and affirmed the Federal Circuit’s decision in the consolidated case. The Court’s opinion was fragmented: parts of the reasoning commanded a majority, while other parts were joined only by Justices Gorsuch and Barrett. The administration’s response and the tariff regime’s status were still developing at the cutoff.[^ch49-learning-resources-2026][^ch49-check-cutoff]

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, was signed July 4, 2025. It extended the 2017 tax provisions, changed deductions and credits, altered Medicaid and other spending, and raised the debt limit. It was enacted through the reconciliation process, which allowed the Senate to pass it with a simple majority. The enacted provisions are documented; the budgetary effects depend on the estimators’ assumptions, and a projection is not an outcome.[^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

The tariff ruling and the fiscal law together illustrated the period’s pattern: the executive acted, the courts and Congress responded, and the resulting policy was neither the administration’s original design nor a simple reversal. Projections are not outcomes. A tariff that is announced may be lowered, exempted, or struck down; a tax provision that is enacted may be changed by a later Congress. The legal uncertainty is part of the record, not a gap to be resolved prematurely.[^ch49-learning-resources-2026][^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 4.3 The efficiency program, the workforce, and Map 39

The administration established the Department of Government Efficiency by executive order on January 20, 2025. The order renamed the United States Digital Service and directed it to modernize federal technology and reduce spending. The administration conducted a deferred-resignation program and large-scale reductions in probationary employees and agency staff, and DOGE served as the effort’s visible center. Elon Musk, its most visible leader, left the administration in May 2025.[^ch49-doge-2025]

The federal workforce’s change is measurable from the Office of Personnel Management’s employment series, and the deferred-resignation program’s cost from a dated audit. A claimed saving is not a settled net figure. The administration claimed billions in savings; the available audits and workforce data did not support a single net number, and the costs of rehiring, litigation, and lost capacity were not fully counted. The program’s legacy was a smaller and more contested federal bureaucracy, not a proven efficiency gain.[^ch49-doge-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

Map 39 at §4 places the border and enforcement geography alongside the county economic measures. It sources border and sector data separately from county GDP, and it must not infer exposure from output concentration alone. A county with a large manufacturing sector is not necessarily exposed to a particular tariff; a border county is not necessarily a site of enforcement. The map is a description of where things happened, not a causal model.[^ch49-check-map39][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 4.4 Immigration enforcement and domestic deployment

The administration expanded detention and removals. The Department of Homeland Security reported more than 605,000 removals since January 20, 2025, as of December 10, 2025. The department also claimed that more than 2.5 million people had “left” the country, a figure that included self-deportations and other departures. Outside analysts used narrower definitions and produced different totals. Each series carries its own definition and cutoff, and they cannot be combined.[^ch49-ice-removals-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

In June 2025 the president federalized California National Guard troops and deployed forces during protests in Los Angeles over immigration raids. Governor Gavin Newsom opposed the deployment, and the state sued. A federal district court blocked the deployment, and the Ninth Circuit upheld the injunction on October 23, 2026, holding that the federalization exceeded the president’s statutory authority and was unconstitutional. The Guard was no longer federalized by the time of the ruling. The decision was six days before the book’s cutoff and remained subject to further review.[^ch49-la-guard-2025][^ch49-la-guard-ruling-2026]

Mass “No Kings” demonstrations on June 14, 2025, and October 18, 2025, drew organizers’ estimates of more than five million and nearly seven million participants. A crowd-counting analysis cited by Reuters estimated the June turnout at four million to six million. Turnout is an estimate with a source, not a precise count. The protests were the largest single-day demonstrations of the period, and they showed that opposition to the administration could mobilize nationally even when it could not control Congress.[^ch49-nokings-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

A federal government shutdown ran from October 1 to November 12, 2025, the longest in United States history. It ended after 43 days when the president signed a funding package. The shutdown disrupted federal services, furloughed or required work without pay for hundreds of thousands of employees, and imposed costs on the economy. The dates are documented; any economic-cost estimate carries its method.[^ch49-shutdown-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

On January 20, 2025, the president granted pardons and commutations to people convicted in connection with the January 6, 2021, attack on the Capitol. The recipients numbered roughly 1,500. The proclamation was one of the administration’s first acts, and it reversed the criminal accountability that the Justice Department had pursued after the attack. The proclamation and the count are on the official record, and the exact number depends on how cases are counted.[^ch49-j6-pardon-2025][^ch49-check-numbers]

The record over-represents officials and under-represents the detained and deported, the federal workers who left, and the communities affected by the deployments. The asymmetries belong in the account; the silenced are not spoken for. The administration’s claims and the independent data are distinct, and a government press release is not a neutral count.[^ch49-check-silences][^ch49-ice-removals-2025]

## 5. Afghanistan, Ukraine, crises, technology, and the anniversary (2021–2026)

### 5.1 The claim: American power tested abroad and a contested homecoming

**The claim.** The period began with the chaotic end of the Afghanistan war and the return of the Taliban, saw Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine and large American aid, a war in Gaza after the October 7, 2023, attack, a direct conflict with Iran by 2026, and a rapid advance in artificial intelligence, all as the nation approached its 250th anniversary. Each development had its own evidence trail, and each tested the American claim to lead a rules-based order while entangled in wars that the public did not want to continue.[^ch49-afghanistan-aar-2023][^ch49-ukraine-aid-2026][^ch49-gaza-ceasefire-2026][^ch49-iran-war-2026][^ch49-eo-14179-2025]

The section runs from Afghanistan through Ukraine, Gaza and Iran, artificial intelligence, and the 250th anniversary. It represents the civilians and the combatants’ societies, not only American decisions, and it gives estimates as ranges with their sources.[^ch49-afghanistan-aar-2023][^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026]

### 5.2 Afghanistan’s aftermath

The United States completed its withdrawal from Afghanistan in August 2021, and the Taliban returned to power as Kabul fell on August 15, 2021. On August 26, 2021, an attack at Abbey Gate killed 13 American service members and scores of Afghan civilians. The evacuation airlifted more than 120,000 people in a matter of days, a logistical achievement intertwined with a chaotic and deadly final week.[^ch49-afghanistan-aar-2023]

The State Department’s after-action review examined the evacuation and identified failures in planning and coordination. The review and the independent reviews are the evidence, not the period’s shorthand. The casualty figures carry their source, and the American deaths, the Afghan deaths, and the people who were left behind are distinct. The withdrawal did not end the war’s consequences; it changed who bore them.[^ch49-afghanistan-aar-2023][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 5.3 Ukraine, Gaza, and Iran

Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022. The United States provided tens of billions of dollars in security assistance and broader economic support. By the Council on Foreign Relations’ count, Congress had passed five major aid bills totaling about $164 billion in spending related to the war by 2026. Other series count only security assistance or only obligations, and the totals differ accordingly. The series and its period matter; one number is not the aid total.[^ch49-ukraine-aid-2026][^ch49-check-numbers]

By 2025 and 2026 the war had settled into attritional fighting with recurring ceasefire proposals and U.S.-mediated talks. On October 21, 2026, the Kremlin said Vladimir Putin had agreed to resume three-way talks with Ukraine and the United States, possibly in Abu Dhabi in November. The talks had not begun by the cutoff. Their state as of October 29, 2026, is the limit of the record; no settlement had been reached.[^ch49-ukraine-ceasefire-2026][^ch49-check-cutoff]

After the October 7, 2023, attack by Hamas and other armed groups on Israel, Israel fought a war in Gaza that killed tens of thousands of Palestinians and displaced most of the territory’s population. A U.S.-brokered ceasefire took effect on October 10, 2025, but it was repeatedly violated, and the humanitarian crisis persisted. Casualty and humanitarian figures are contested and updated; each carries its source and as-of date.[^ch49-gaza-ceasefire-2026][^ch49-check-numbers]

In June 2025 the United States and Israel struck Iranian nuclear sites. On February 28, 2026, the two countries launched a broader war against Iran, striking military and leadership targets and killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. A ceasefire was reached in April, and Iran and the United States signed a memorandum in June, but the conflict was unresolved at the cutoff. Indirect negotiations continued through Qatar. The 2026 war belongs in the chapter as of October 29, 2026, with its dates; its outcome was unresolved.[^ch49-iran-war-2026][^ch49-check-cutoff]

The civilians and the combatants’ societies belong in the account, not only American decisions. Estimates are ranges with sources, and a government’s casualty figure is not neutral. The record over-represents presidents, generals, and diplomats and under-represents the people who fled, the people who died, and the people who stayed.[^ch49-check-silences][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 5.4 Technology and the 250th anniversary

The administration issued successive executive orders on artificial intelligence. On January 23, 2025, Executive Order 14179 removed barriers to development and revoked the prior administration’s order. On December 11, 2025, Executive Order 14365 sought a national policy framework and directed a litigation task force to challenge state laws that the administration regarded as impediments. On September 29, 2026, Executive Order 14434 renamed artificial intelligence “Super Intelligence” in executive-branch usage and directed agencies to use the new term. The orders showed a consistent preference for development over regulation, and they met resistance from states and courts.[^ch49-eo-14179-2025][^ch49-eo-14365-2025][^ch49-eo-14434-2026]

On July 4, 2026, the National Archives marked the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence with a program on Constitution Avenue and a Rotunda display of the Declaration, the Constitution, the Bill of Rights, the Emancipation Proclamation, and the 19th Amendment. The ceremony included a dramatic reading of the Declaration and music; the museum stayed open from 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. The Archives’ own program is the record, not a reconstructed ceremony; Chapter 50 opens there.[^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026][^ch49-archives-july4-2026][^ch49-check-ch50]

The anniversary is a framing occasion, not permission to declare the period settled. The commemoration was an event with its own record and its own contest over memory: who was included in the founding story, which documents were displayed, and what the nation was being asked to celebrate. The commemoration is a historical event, not a verdict on the history it commemorated.[^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026][^ch49-check-scope]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The legislation is settled. The American Rescue Plan Act became Public Law 117-2 on March 11, 2021; the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act became Public Law 117-58 on November 15, 2021; the CHIPS and Science Act became Public Law 117-167 on August 9, 2022; and the Inflation Reduction Act became Public Law 117-169 on August 16, 2022. Their effects are not settled, but their enactment is.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-iija-2021][^ch49-chips-2022][^ch49-ira-2022]

The decisions are settled. *Dobbs* was decided June 24, 2022, 6–3; *Bruen* was decided June 23, 2022, 6–3; *Students for Fair Admissions* was decided June 29, 2023, with a 6–2 division in the Harvard case and a 6–3 division in the North Carolina case; and *Trump v. United States* was decided July 1, 2024, 6–3. Their reasoning and their consequences remain contested, but the holdings are on the record.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

The elections are settled. Republicans won the House in 2022 while Democrats retained 51 Senate seats; Trump won the 2024 election, 312–226, with 49.80 percent of the popular vote to Harris’s 48.32 percent. The interpretations of those results are not settled, but the returns are.[^ch49-fec-2022][^ch49-fec-2024]

The main actions of the 2025 program, and the checks on them, are settled. The second administration imposed broad tariffs under IEEPA, signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act on July 4, 2025, expanded removals, and deployed the National Guard domestically. The Supreme Court held on February 20, 2026, that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, and a federal appeals court held on October 23, 2026, that the Los Angeles deployment was unlawful. The program was real, and so were the checks.[^ch49-tariffs-crs-2026][^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-learning-resources-2026][^ch49-la-guard-ruling-2026]

The abroad record is settled in outline. The United States withdrew from Afghanistan in August 2021; Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022; a Gaza ceasefire took effect in October 2025; and a broader conflict with Iran was under way in 2026. The outcomes of the wars and the negotiations were not settled at the cutoff.[^ch49-afghanistan-aar-2023][^ch49-ukraine-aid-2026][^ch49-gaza-ceasefire-2026][^ch49-iran-war-2026]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the period a democratic backslide or a resilient contest? Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argue that democracies erode slowly through the breaking of norms, and they place the American case in a comparative pattern. Robert C. Lieberman, Suzanne Mettler, and Kenneth M. Roberts, the editors of *Democratic Resilience*, ask whether American institutions have the capacity to absorb a major shock, and they identify countervailing capacities in parties, courts, and civil society. Their position is not that the danger is imaginary; it is that the outcome is contingent on institutions. Both positions have to be tested against the institutions’ actual record. That record shows both erosion and resistance: a president tested the limits of his authority, and courts, states, and voters checked him. Whether that amounts to resilience or a narrow escape depends on which institutions are counted and how much erosion is enough to call a democracy degraded.[^ch49-levitsky-ziblatt-2018][^ch49-levitsky-ziblatt-2023][^ch49-lieberman-mettler-roberts-2022]

What drove the 2024 result? One account emphasizes trade exposure and economic dislocation; another emphasizes status threat and cultural backlash; a third emphasizes place-based resentment and party change. Each has evidence, and each has a blind spot. The trade-shock literature can show that exposed districts shifted toward the Republican Party, but it cannot establish any individual voter’s motive. The status-threat literature can show that survey measures of status threat predicted vote choice, but its measures are contested. The place-based literature can restore the local texture that national accounts miss, but its fieldwork often precedes the election it is used to explain. Much of this literature was built on earlier elections, and its application to 2024 is an inference rather than a direct finding. Each position is strongest when tested against the returns and the surveys, not when asserted alone.[^ch49-autor-2020][^ch49-mutz-2018][^ch49-cramer-2016]

Did the executive gain or lose power? The tariff ruling and the deployment ruling constrained the executive, while the immunity decision shielded it. The answer depends on which instruments are counted. The president’s formal authority expanded in some areas and contracted in others; the informal capacity to act quickly and to test limits may have grown even when the courts said no. The legal rules and the political practice are distinct.[^ch49-trump-v-us-2024][^ch49-learning-resources-2026]

How much can be known about the present? The events of 2025 and 2026 are documented in official records that are incomplete and contested, and some institutions’ records are not yet available. The evidentiary limits are the story, not a verdict. A history written at the cutoff can establish what was done and said; it cannot establish what it will mean.[^ch49-check-cutoff][^ch49-check-debate-works]

Is the American case comparable? Populist and nationalist movements arose across many democracies in the period. The American case is comparable without being unique or a mere copy. The comparison can identify shared mechanisms — media fragmentation, inequality, migration, and the personalization of politics — without erasing the differences in institutions and history.[^ch49-muller-2016][^ch49-levitsky-ziblatt-2023]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which a Court ended a half-century settlement, a second administration tested the executive’s reach, and elections and courts repeatedly checked and contested that reach, while a pandemic’s aftermath, a price surge, and long-run polarization shaped the terrain. The period was neither a single rupture nor a restoration. It was a contest in which the rules themselves were at stake.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-learning-resources-2026][^ch49-autor-2020]

Contingency matters. The 2022 midterm results, the 2024 ticket change, the 2024 result, the tariff ruling, and the deployment ruling were all contingent. A different candidate, a different turnout, or a different judge could have produced a different outcome. The results were not inevitable, and the historian should identify which alternatives were actually available rather than inventing a counterfactual.[^ch49-biden-withdraw-2024][^ch49-check-debate-works]

Structure and choice belong together. Polarization, deindustrialization, the media environment, and global conflict set the terrain, but actors chose within it. The structural conditions and the choices belong together; one register of explanation is not enough. That is the historian’s version of the period’s own argument about agency and constraint.[^ch49-autor-2020][^ch49-check-debate-works]

The synthesis for this chapter is therefore deliberately bounded. Between 2021 and October 2026 the United States ended a half-century abortion settlement, remade its immigration and tariff policy, learned that core parts of that policy exceeded statutory authority, elected a former president to a second term, supported two wars abroad and began a third, advanced artificial intelligence, and marked its 250th anniversary. The chapter’s question — what a history of an unsettled present can responsibly establish — is answered by holding those developments together under a strict evidentiary rule, not by declaring which of them will define the age.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-iran-war-2026][^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026]

## Legacies

The period’s legal decisions outlasted the terms that produced them. The ending of the federal abortion right, the expansion of gun rights, the invalidation of race-conscious admissions, and the broad immunity for official presidential acts reshaped the ground on which later political and legal conflicts were fought. Each decision was made by a majority that had been appointed over two decades, and each will be contested by the generations that inherit it. They have been traced to their sources here, not presented as a settled verdict.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

The period’s economic and administrative choices left institutions and precedents that later administrations would defend, narrow, or unwind. The industrial-policy statutes, the 2025 tariff and fiscal laws, the government-efficiency program, and the expanded immigration enforcement remained live questions at the cutoff. The Supreme Court’s tariff ruling left the tariff regime’s legal basis unsettled. The federal workforce was smaller and more politicized than it had been, and the machinery of immigration enforcement had been built up in ways that would outlast the emergency that justified it.[^ch49-ira-2022][^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-doge-2025][^ch49-learning-resources-2026]

The contest over how to read the recent past became part of the period itself. The disputes over what the 2024 election meant, whether the executive had exceeded its authority, and how the 250th anniversary should be remembered set terms that Chapter 50 takes up as it weighs what the preceding chapters established. The period did not settle the argument about the American past; it made the argument more urgent.[^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-check-cutoff][^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026][^ch49-check-ch50]

## Closing note and bridge
