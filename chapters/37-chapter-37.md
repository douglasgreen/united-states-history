# Chapter 37. The Cold War Begins (1945–1953)

> “From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent.”[^ch37-churchill-iron-curtain]
>
> — Winston Churchill, “The Sinews of Peace,” Westminster College, Fulton, Missouri, March 5, 1946.[^ch37-churchill-iron-curtain]

> “I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
>
> — Harry S. Truman, address to a joint session of Congress, March 12, 1947.[^ch37-truman-doctrine]

> “‘Isolationism’ for America is a denial of its entire cultural tradition of expansion and empire.”[^ch37-williams-tragedy]
>
> — William Appleman Williams, *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy*, 1959.[^ch37-williams-tragedy]

A British statesman described a Europe already divided. An American president announced a global commitment to resist subjugation. A historian charged that the deeper engine of American policy was expansion itself. The three statements disagree about what the confrontation was: a Soviet threat met by a defensive West, a universal principle of freedom, or an American empire that called its expansion freedom. That disagreement frames the chapter’s question: how did a wartime alliance become a permanent security confrontation?[^ch37-check-epigraphs]

On February 22, 1946, the American chargé d’affaires in Moscow, George F. Kennan, sent telegram 511 to the secretary of state. It was marked “SECRET,” timed at 9 p.m. Moscow time, and answered a State Department query of February 3. Kennan said the questions were “so intricate, so delicate, so strange to our form of thought” that he could not compress his answer into one brief message. He would reply in five parts, from the Soviet outlook to the practical deductions for American policy, and he apologized in advance for “this burdening of telegraphic channel.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]

Kennan traced Soviet policy to a “traditional and instinctive Russian sense of insecurity,” not to an objective reading of the world, and he called the Kremlin’s view of foreign affairs “neurotic.” Soviet power, he wrote, was “neither schematic nor adventuristic. It does not work by fixed plans. It does not take unnecessary risks.” It was “Impervious to logic of reason, and it is highly sensitive to logic of force.” The problem was “within our power to solve — and that without recourse to any general military conflict.” But Kennan added a warning that the rest of the chapter would test: “the greatest danger that can befall us in coping with this problem of Soviet communism, is that we shall allow ourselves to become like those with whom we are coping.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]

The record establishes the date, the sender, the recipient, and the text. It does not establish what Secretary of State James F. Byrnes or President Harry S. Truman made of the cable, how quickly it circulated, or that it alone caused the policy that followed. In February 1946 no one knew that the alliance would harden into a forty-year confrontation, that the Soviet Union would test an atomic bomb in 1949, that China would become a communist state, or that the United States would fight in Korea. The scene ends with a diplomat describing a threat and recommending patience, and with a question about how a coalition of victors became two armed camps. How did a wartime alliance become a permanent security confrontation?[^ch37-check-scene]

Chapter 36 closes on the Japanese surrender of September 1945. This chapter takes up the problem that the surrender left unresolved: the common enemy was defeated, and the alliance that had defeated it had no agreed answer to the questions of Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic bomb.[^ch37-check-ch36]

The chapter covers the years from the wartime alliance of 1945 to the Korean armistice of July 1953. It asks how the United States converted a diagnosis of Soviet expansion into a permanent set of foreign and domestic institutions, and what that conversion cost. It draws on diplomatic records, statutes, treaties, court opinions, casualty series, and the secondary literature. It treats “containment,” “the free world,” “the Cold War,” “loyalty,” “subversion,” “the loss of China,” and “police action” as contested period terms. “Containment” was a strategy, a doctrine, and a set of institutions, and the officials who used the word disagreed about whether it meant political pressure, economic aid, or military force. “The free world” was the American name for the anticommunist coalition, not a description of every government inside it. “Loyalty” and “subversion” named political beliefs and associations as security risks. “The loss of China” was a domestic accusation, not a diplomatic event. “Police action” was the administration’s phrase for a war that killed millions.[^ch37-check-scope][^ch37-check-names][^ch37-check-containment-terms][^ch37-truman-police-action]

The chapter also states a limit at the start. The record over-represents presidents, secretaries, and organized institutions and under-represents the investigated, the blacklisted, the Korean and Chinese dead, and the colonized. The sources for Soviet policy are fuller on American beliefs than on Soviet intentions, and the loyalty files are fuller on employees than on the lives they changed. Casualty figures for Korea come from different systems with different cutoffs, and the chapter gives ranges and names the methods rather than presenting one number as settled.[^ch37-check-silences][^ch37-korea-casualties]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the postwar disagreements that divided the allies. §2 follows containment’s institutions: the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, Berlin, NATO, and the security agencies. §3 turns to the loyalty investigations and anticommunism at home, and reads Executive Order 9835’s standard in the In Focus. §4 examines the shocks of 1949, the fall of China and the first Soviet atomic test, and the response in NSC-68. §5 follows the Korean War to the armistice of 1953. §6 asks how historians have explained the origins of the Cold War and its domestic consequences. The sections are thematic rather than strictly chronological: §§1 and 2 overlap in 1947, and §§3 and 4 both concern the years 1947–50. The chapter begins in February 1946, when Kennan sent the Long Telegram from Moscow.[^ch37-check-scope][^ch37-check-dates-appD]

## 1. Postwar disagreements

### 1.1 The claim: an alliance of expedience

**The claim.** The Grand Alliance was a coalition of necessity against a common enemy. Victory removed the thing that had held it together while leaving unresolved the questions of Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic bomb. The Cold War grew from that vacuum and from the choices each side made within it. That is a claim about sequence and choice, not a claim that either side planned the conflict.[^ch37-check-yalta]

The breakdown is best presented as a series of decisions and misreadings. The allies disagreed about what the wartime agreements meant, about who would govern the territories their armies occupied, and about whether the atomic bomb was a secret to be kept, a weapon to be brandished, or a problem to be placed under international control. The Soviet Union had suffered the largest losses of the war and was determined to secure its western border. The United States had the largest economy and the only atomic bomb, and it wanted a postwar order open to American trade and ideas. Neither position was a plan for conquest. Each looked like a threat from the other side.[^ch37-yalta][^ch37-potsdam]

The record can show the conferences, the occupation arrangements, and the communications. It cannot show that either side intended the conflict from the start. The debate in §6 turns on that limit.[^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 Yalta, Potsdam, and the occupation of Europe

The Yalta Conference met in the Crimea from February 4 to 11, 1945. President Franklin D. Roosevelt, Prime Minister Winston Churchill, and Premier Joseph Stalin agreed that Germany would be occupied and that France would have a zone. They agreed that Germany should pay reparations, though not how much. They promised free elections in the territories liberated from Nazi Germany and issued a declaration on Poland that brought communists into the provisional government. They settled the voting formula for the United Nations Security Council, giving the five permanent members a veto. The agreements were compromises whose meaning depended on who enforced them.[^ch37-yalta]

The Potsdam Conference met from July 17 to August 2, 1945. Truman represented the United States, Churchill was replaced mid-conference by Clement Attlee after the British election, and Stalin represented the Soviet Union. The conference confirmed a demilitarized Germany under four zones of occupation and postponed the creation of a national German government indefinitely. It allowed each occupying power to take reparations from its own zone, a decision that favored the Soviets in the east and the Western powers in the west. It created a Council of Foreign Ministers to draft peace treaties. The United States, Britain, and China also issued the Potsdam Declaration, which threatened Japan with “prompt and utter destruction” if it did not surrender.[^ch37-potsdam]

The texts are documented. The good faith is not. American officials read the Yalta promises as a commitment to self-government; Soviet officials read them as a guarantee that neighboring governments would be friendly. The same words carried different expectations, and the arrival of Soviet-backed governments in Poland, Romania, and Bulgaria turned the ambiguity into a crisis. The dispute was over meaning, not a simple record of broken promises.[^ch37-yalta][^ch37-potsdam][^ch37-check-yalta]

### 1.3 The atomic monopoly and the Baruch Plan

The atomic bomb was simultaneously a secret, a bargaining chip, and a threat. The United States had used it against Japan in August 1945 and then retained a monopoly on its production. The monopoly could not be acknowledged as a permanent advantage without making the Soviet Union a permanent inferior, and it could not be shared without giving up the weapon that seemed to guarantee American security.[^ch37-baruch-plan][^ch37-check-atomic]

In January 1946 the United Nations created an Atomic Energy Commission to advise on the destruction of atomic weapons and the peaceful use of atomic energy. An American advisory committee chaired by Dean Acheson, with David Lilienthal among its members, produced a report written largely by J. Robert Oppenheimer that called for an international Atomic Development Authority to control fissile materials and nuclear facilities. On June 14, 1946, the American representative Bernard Baruch presented a revised plan. It gave the authority power to inspect nuclear facilities and required sanctions against violators, and it stripped the permanent members of the Security Council of their veto over those sanctions. The United States would destroy its atomic arsenal only after the system was in place. The Soviet Union rejected the plan, arguing that it preserved an American monopoly and subjected Soviet facilities to inspection while leaving the American arsenal intact. A vote in the commission on December 30, 1946, won ten of twelve members, but the Soviet Union and Poland abstained and the required unanimity failed.[^ch37-baruch-plan]

The plan failed, and the failure did not have a single cause. The American proposal asked the Soviet Union to accept inspections and sanctions before the United States gave up its advantage. The Soviet Union asked the United States to give up the advantage before any system of inspection existed. The chapter does not claim that a different proposal would have succeeded. It claims that the failure left the world without an agreed nuclear regime and made the bomb a permanent feature of the confrontation.[^ch37-baruch-plan][^ch37-check-atomic]

### 1.4 Iran, Turkey, and the Eastern European sphere

The crises of 1946 convinced American officials that Soviet power was expanding by pressure and subversion. Soviet troops remained in northern Iran after the war, and Moscow pressed for an oil concession. The Soviet Union pressured Turkey for base and transit rights at the Straits. It rejected the Baruch Plan. In Greece, a civil war between the government and communist-led insurgents continued, and Britain told Washington in February 1947 that it could no longer carry the burden of supporting the Greek government. Each episode was local. Together they formed a pattern in American eyes.[^ch37-truman-doctrine]

The pattern was not the same as a Soviet plan. The Truman Doctrine’s account of the four setbacks was an American account, and it recorded what American officials perceived. The Soviet Union had its own reasons for wanting a friendly Iran and secure access to the Mediterranean, and Stalin did not support the Greek communists in a way that matched the fear they aroused. The chapter attributes the claim of a coordinated expansion to the officials who made it, and it asks in §6 whether the claim was accurate.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-check-debate-works]

The Eastern European governments were the clearest case. Soviet armies occupied the region, and communist parties backed by Soviet power took control of police, media, and elections. The process was not identical everywhere, and it was not complete in 1946. But by 1948 the governments of Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, and Czechoslovakia were communist-dominated, and the region had become a Soviet sphere. American officials called this a violation of the Yalta promises. Soviet officials called it a defensive buffer against a West that had twice invaded through it.[^ch37-yalta][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 The Long Telegram and the invention of containment

Kennan’s telegram gave American policy a vocabulary. The cable described a Soviet system driven by insecurity and ideology, responsive to force, and vulnerable to its own internal contradictions. It recommended a patient, firm response. In July 1947, writing anonymously as “X” in *Foreign Affairs*, Kennan gave the strategy its famous name: “a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies.” Soviet pressure, he wrote, “can be contained by the adroit and vigilant application of counter-force at a series of constantly shifting geographical and political points.” Soviet society, he added, “may well contain deficiencies which will eventually weaken its own total potential.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-kennan-x-article]

Containment was never a single program. Kennan emphasized political and economic pressure and warned against a purely military response. Officials in the Treasury emphasized economic reconstruction and open markets. The military services emphasized bases, alliances, and rearmament. By 1950, NSC-68 had redefined containment as a global military and political struggle. Kennan later complained that the policy had become more military than he intended. The word survived because it could hold all these meanings at once, and because it named a posture without requiring agreement on its instruments.[^ch37-kennan-x-article][^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-containment-terms]

The telegram’s influence is documented; its causal weight is not. It gave officials a way to explain the Soviet Union to themselves and to the public. It did not by itself produce the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, or NATO. Those decisions responded to specific crises and domestic politics, and the officials who made them disagreed about what containment required. The chapter treats the telegram as an influential formulation, not as a cause that can be measured by its text alone.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-check-scene]

## 2. Containment, the Marshall Plan, Berlin, NATO, and security agencies

### 2.1 The claim: building institutions

**The claim.** Between 1947 and 1949 the United States converted a diagnosis of Soviet expansion into a set of permanent institutions. Economic aid, a military alliance, a reorganized defense establishment, and intelligence and planning bodies outlasted the crises that produced them. The process was cumulative and partly unintended. Each step answered a specific emergency, and each step enlarged the American commitment. By 1949 the United States had a peacetime military alliance in Europe, a global intelligence agency, a National Security Council, and a defense budget that would have been unthinkable in 1945.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-nato-milestone]

The chapter presents institution-building as a sequence of choices rather than a single design. The Truman Doctrine was a request for aid to Greece and Turkey. The Marshall Plan was a program for European recovery. NATO was a treaty for collective defense. The National Security Act was a reorganization of the defense establishment. None of them was drafted as part of a plan for a permanent global role. Together they became one.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-nsa-1947]

The record can show statutes, treaties, programs, and organizations. It cannot show that officials foresaw the enduring structure they were building. The chapter marks that limit because the legacies of these institutions are part of the argument, not a proven intention.[^ch37-check-silences][^ch37-check-institutions]

### 2.2 The Truman Doctrine, March 12, 1947

On February 21, 1947, the British embassy informed the State Department that Britain could no longer support Greece and Turkey. On March 12, Truman addressed a joint session of Congress and asked for $400 million in military and economic assistance for the two countries through June 30, 1948. He framed the request as a choice between “alternative ways of life.” One was based on the will of the majority and free institutions. The other was “based upon the will of a minority forcibly imposed upon the majority.” He concluded: “I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]

The speech was a request for aid, and it became a doctrine. Its language was universal, but its occasion was specific. Greece and Turkey were the immediate objects; the principle could be applied anywhere. That gap between the occasion and the principle was the source of the doctrine’s power and of its danger: it allowed the United States to treat local conflicts as tests of a global struggle. Whether Greece and Turkey were the object or the occasion is an interpretation.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-check-debate-works]

The doctrine also had a domestic purpose. Truman needed congressional support, and he framed the request in terms that a skeptical Congress could accept. The result was a bipartisan commitment to containment that outlasted the emergency. The vote did not end the debate over the commitment’s cost or limits, but it established the principle that the United States would pay to prevent communist victories.[^ch37-truman-doctrine]

### 2.3 The Marshall Plan, April 3, 1948

On June 5, 1947, Secretary of State George C. Marshall spoke at Harvard University. He proposed that the European nations design their own recovery program and that the United States finance it. The proposal was open to the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, but Stalin refused to participate and pressured the satellites to refuse. Congress passed the Economic Cooperation Act of 1948, Title I of the Foreign Assistance Act, and Truman signed it on April 3, 1948. Over the next four years, Congress appropriated $13.3 billion for European recovery.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-nato-milestone]

The aid provided capital and materials for reconstruction. It also opened markets for American goods and supported “stable democratic governments in Western Europe.” Those purposes were not separable. The program was humanitarian and strategic at once, and its strategic purpose was to make Western Europe prosperous enough to resist communist parties and independent enough to trade with the United States. The Soviet refusal to participate hardened the division of Europe. It also made the program a Western bloc, not a continental one.[^ch37-marshall-plan]

The figures carry their own limits. The $13.3 billion was appropriated over four years in nominal dollars, and it should not be mistaken for the total of European recovery. The counterfactual of European recovery without the program cannot be measured. The chapter gives the sum with its period and its limits, and it does not claim that the program alone saved Western Europe.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-check-numbers]

### 2.4 The Berlin blockade and airlift, 1948–1949

In June 1948 the Western powers introduced a new currency into their zones and West Berlin. The Soviet Union responded by blockading land access to the western sectors of Berlin on June 24. The United States and Britain answered with an airlift. The United States launched “Operation Vittles” on June 26, and Britain followed with “Operation Plainfare.” At the height of the campaign, “one plane landed every 45 seconds at Tempelhof Airport.” In September 1948 some 300,000 West Berliners gathered at the Reichstag to demonstrate against Soviet domination. Moscow lifted the blockade on May 11–12, 1949.[^ch37-berlin-airlift]

The airlift was a logistical achievement and a political symbol. It kept the western sectors supplied and made the Western commitment visible to Berliners and to the world. It did not save the city by itself. The Western counter-blockade, the failure of the Soviet pressure to divide the Western allies, and Soviet calculations about the cost of continuing all shaped the outcome. The chapter presents the airlift as one part of a larger contest, not as a single cause.[^ch37-berlin-airlift][^ch37-check-debate-works]

The crisis also settled a question. The Western powers had not withdrawn from Berlin, and the Soviet Union had not forced them out. The city remained divided, and the division became a permanent feature of the Cold War. The airlift showed that the United States would defend a position it had not originally considered vital, and it made Berlin a symbol of the commitment that NATO would formalize.[^ch37-berlin-airlift][^ch37-nato-milestone]

### 2.5 NATO, April 4, 1949

On April 4, 1949, twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington. The founding members were the United States, Canada, Belgium, Denmark, France, Iceland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, and the United Kingdom. Article 5 stated that “an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all.” Each party would take “such action as it deems necessary, including the use of armed force.” The treaty entered into force on August 24, 1949.[^ch37-nato-treaty][^ch37-nato-milestone]

The treaty was the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered outside the Western Hemisphere. Its commitment was also limited. It applied to attacks in Europe and North America, not to colonial conflicts, and it did not automatically obligate any member to go to war. The “such action as it deems necessary” clause left each government to decide what it would do. The alliance was a promise of consultation and common defense, not a unified command with automatic obligations. NATO’s military structure came later, and its credibility depended on the forces the members were willing to provide.[^ch37-nato-treaty][^ch37-nato-milestone]

The United States also began to arm its allies. The Mutual Defense Assistance Program, authorized in 1949, provided about $1.4 billion in military aid. The program made the alliance more than a paper commitment, and it tied the defense of Europe to American weapons and American strategy. The treaty’s deterrent effect cannot be demonstrated, because Article 5 was not invoked during the period. The chapter presents deterrence as a claim about what the alliance prevented, not as a measured result.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 2.6 The security agencies and Map 28

The National Security Act of July 26, 1947, reorganized the American defense establishment. It created the National Security Council to advise the president on the integration of foreign, military, and domestic policy. It created the Central Intelligence Agency to collect and coordinate intelligence. It created a National Military Establishment under a secretary of defense, with separate departments of the Army, Navy, and Air Force, an arrangement that became the Department of Defense. The act did not by itself authorize covert action, but the new machinery made it possible to plan and conduct covert operations with fewer boundaries between intelligence and policy.[^ch37-nsa-1947]

The act was a response to the experience of the war and to the demands of the peace. The war had shown the need for unified direction, and the peace the need for permanent intelligence and planning. The National Security Council, the CIA, and the defense establishment became permanent parts of the government, and their existence changed the terms of every later debate about American power.[^ch37-nsa-1947][^ch37-check-institutions]

Map 28, “Containment’s geography, 1945–1952,” is the chapter’s registered map. It is planned to show the bases, alliance commitments, and changing memberships of the containment system as of the end of 1952. It must distinguish commitments the United States made from territories it occupied or controlled, and it must state the observation date of each element. Its evidence targets are *Foreign Relations of the United States*, NATO accession records, and deployment records, and its production record is `repo-map28`. The map must not imply that it covers the Korean armistice of 1953 or later alliances. No asset exists yet, and the prose describes the planned content rather than a finished map.[^ch37-check-map28][^ch37-nato-milestone]

A map can show where commitments were. It cannot show why they were made or what they cost. The chapter keeps policy in the prose and uses the map to show the geography that the policy created. The geography mattered: a commitment to Greece and Turkey, a treaty with twelve Atlantic nations, an occupation in Germany and Japan, and a war in Korea were not a single system, and the map should show their differences.[^ch37-check-map28]

## 3. Loyalty investigations and anticommunism

### 3.1 The claim: containment comes home

**The claim.** Containment was not only a foreign policy. It became a domestic program of loyalty investigations, blacklists, and prosecutions that treated political belief and association as security risks. The federal loyalty program, the House Committee on Un-American Activities, the Smith Act prosecutions, and McCarthy’s accusations reinforced one another. They made anticommunism a condition of employment, a test of citizenship, and a language for settling political scores.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-hollywood-ten]

The chapter presents the federal program and the private and congressional campaigns together because they belonged to the same history. The loyalty order created a procedure for federal employees. HUAC created a public spectacle for witnesses who refused to cooperate. The Smith Act created a criminal procedure for communist leaders. The blacklist created a private procedure for the entertainment industry. Each used different instruments, and each depended on the others to make the charge of communism credible and dangerous.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-dennis]

The record can show orders, prosecutions, and dismissal figures. It cannot measure the chill the program produced. The chapter presents “chilling effect” as a claim with evidence, not as a count. The evidence includes the number of employees who resigned, the reluctance of witnesses to answer questions, and the testimony of people whose careers ended without a formal finding.[^ch37-truman-loyalty][^ch37-check-silences]

### 3.2 Executive Order 9835 and the loyalty standard

On March 21, 1947, Truman signed Executive Order 9835, creating the first general loyalty program for federal employees. The order applied to applicants and employees. It established investigations by the Federal Bureau of Investigation and other agencies, loyalty boards in each department, a central master index of findings, and an appeal to a Loyalty Review Board of the Civil Service Commission. It required written notice of charges, a hearing, and the right to counsel. It also allowed investigators to rely on confidential information and to withhold the identities of informants in some circumstances. The order was a procedure for deciding who could work for the government, and it made political loyalty a condition of employment.[^ch37-eo9835]

**In Focus.** Part V of the order set the standard: employment could be refused or removed where “on all the evidence, reasonable grounds exist for belief that the person involved is disloyal to the Government of the United States.” The sentence is worth reading closely because it defines loyalty negatively and probabilistically. It does not require proof of disloyalty, an overt act, or a crime. It asks whether “reasonable grounds” for “belief” exist, and it places the judgment in an administrative board rather than a court. The listed grounds included “membership in, affiliation with or sympathetic association with” any organization the attorney general designated as totalitarian, fascist, communist, or subversive. The definition reached association and sympathy as well as action. The order also created procedural protections: written notice, a hearing before a three-member board, counsel, and an appeal. Defenders cited those procedures. Critics cited the standard, the reliance on confidential informants, and the fact that the employee had to prove loyalty rather than the government prove disloyalty. The box reads both halves together, because the procedure and the standard were part of the same instrument.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-check-focus]

The standard was tightened in 1951. Executive Order 10241, signed on April 28, 1951, replaced “reasonable grounds for belief” with “a reasonable doubt as to the loyalty of the person involved.” The change shifted the burden further toward the employee. It also showed that the program was not a fixed rule but a political instrument that could be made harsher as the anticommunist campaign intensified.[^ch37-eo10241]

The program screened more than five million federal workers between 1947 and 1956. It produced an estimated 2,700 dismissals and 12,000 resignations. Those figures are small relative to the number screened, and they are the strongest evidence for the program’s defenders. They are also incomplete. They do not count the people who were investigated and cleared but whose careers were damaged, the people who left before an investigation began, or the people who were never hired. The Truman Library’s account concludes that the program “exerted its chilling effect on a far larger number of employees than those who were dismissed.” That conclusion is a judgment, and the chapter presents it as one.[^ch37-truman-loyalty][^ch37-check-numbers][^ch37-check-image]

### 3.3 HUAC, the Hollywood Ten, and the blacklist

In October 1947 the House Committee on Un-American Activities opened hearings on communism in the film industry. Ten producers, directors, and screenwriters refused to answer questions about their political affiliations. They were Alvah Bessie, Herbert Biberman, Lester Cole, Edward Dmytryk, Ring Lardner Jr., John Howard Lawson, Albert Maltz, Samuel Ornitz, Adrian Scott, and Dalton Trumbo. The House voted them in contempt on November 24, 1947. They were convicted the next year and sentenced to six months to one year in prison. Most were then blacklisted.[^ch37-hollywood-ten]

The hearings made the blacklist a system. Studios could not hire people who had been named, and the names circulated through private lists and industry gossip. Some of the Ten wrote under pseudonyms, and some continued to work without credit. The blacklist was not a law, and it did not need to be. It worked through the fear of publicity, the pressure of sponsors, and the willingness of employers to avoid controversy. The record shows the hearings, the contempt vote, the sentences, and the blacklist. It cannot show that all the witnesses were communists, and it cannot measure how many people were excluded without being named.[^ch37-hollywood-ten][^ch37-check-silences]

The Hollywood Ten became symbols because they refused to cooperate. Their refusal was a political act and a legal strategy: they argued that the committee’s questions violated their First Amendment rights. The courts rejected the argument, and the contempt convictions stood. The episode showed that congressional investigation could punish without a criminal conviction, and it gave the blacklist a public ritual.[^ch37-hollywood-ten][^ch37-dennis]

### 3.4 The Smith Act, Dennis, and the Rosenbergs

The federal government also prosecuted communist leadership criminally. In 1948 the Justice Department indicted eleven leaders of the Communist Party under the Smith Act, which made it a crime to advocate or conspire to advocate the overthrow of the government. The defendants were convicted in 1949. In *Dennis v. United States* (1951), the Supreme Court upheld the convictions 6–2. Justice Hugo L. Black dissented, calling the decision “a virulent form of prior censorship of speech and press.” The Court’s majority held that the government could punish advocacy that created a clear and present danger of overthrow, and it deferred to Congress’s judgment about the danger.[^ch37-dennis]

The Rosenberg case made the courtroom a stage for the espionage side of the campaign. Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were convicted in 1951 of conspiracy to commit espionage for passing atomic information to the Soviet Union. They were executed at Sing Sing on June 19, 1953, after President Dwight D. Eisenhower declined clemency. The case rested on the testimony of accomplices, including Ethel Rosenberg’s brother, David Greenglass, and on documents that the government did not fully disclose. Later archival work has confirmed that Julius Rosenberg passed information and has raised serious questions about Ethel Rosenberg’s role and about the fairness of the trial. The chapter presents the guilt and the fairness as separate questions, and it does not treat either as settled.[^ch37-rosenberg][^ch37-check-debate-works]

The prosecutions showed that anticommunism had a criminal as well as an administrative dimension. The Smith Act cases targeted belief and advocacy. The Rosenberg case targeted espionage. The cost was a body of law and a public memory that treated political dissent and espionage as versions of the same danger.[^ch37-dennis][^ch37-rosenberg]

### 3.5 McCarthy and the politics of accusation

On February 9, 1950, Senator Joseph R. McCarthy of Wisconsin spoke to a Republican women’s club in Wheeling, West Virginia. He claimed to hold “a list of 205” State Department employees known to the secretary of state as members of the Communist Party. The speech launched the most visible phase of the second Red Scare. The precise contents of the speech quickly became contested, and McCarthy’s numbers changed from 205 to 57 as he repeated the charge. The list was never produced as described. The charge was the event.[^ch37-mccarthy]

McCarthy’s power did not come from evidence. It came from his willingness to make accusations that others could not easily disprove, and from a political environment in which the charge of communism was already credible. He attacked the State Department, the Army, and the Voice of America. He made loyalty a test of patriotism and used the Senate’s immunity to escape the consequences of false statements. His colleagues tolerated him for years because he was useful to a larger partisan attack on the Truman administration. The 1954 Army–McCarthy hearings and his censure belong to Chapter 38. This chapter ends with the moment when accusation became a national politics.[^ch37-mccarthy][^ch37-check-ch38]

The chapter does not treat McCarthy as the cause of the Red Scare. He was its most visible beneficiary. The loyalty program, HUAC, the Smith Act, and the blacklist had already built the machinery he used. His contribution was to show how far the machinery could be pushed when a single politician was willing to use it without restraint.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-mccarthy]

## 4. China and the atomic shock of 1949

### 4.1 The claim: a cascade of reversals

**The claim.** In the space of a few months in 1949, the communist victory in China and the Soviet atomic test shattered the assumption that containment was working. The two events were read through each other. The fall of China suggested that American aid could not determine the outcome of a civil war. The Soviet test suggested that the American atomic advantage was temporary. Together they produced a sense of accelerating crisis that made a military buildup seem necessary. In 1950, NSC-68 gave that sense a policy.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-nsc68]

The sequence matters. The events did not occur in a vacuum, and they did not cause a single response. Officials interpreted each event in light of the others and in light of domestic politics. The chapter presents the shocks in sequence and shows how each was read through the others, rather than treating them as isolated facts.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-soviet-bomb]

The record can show the events and the policy documents. It cannot show that either event caused NSC-68. The timing is context, not proof. The chapter marks that limit because the debate over NSC-68 is part of the larger debate over who began the Cold War.[^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.2 The “loss” of China

On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the People’s Republic of China in Beijing. The proclamation marked the communists’ victory on the mainland, and the Nationalists retreated to Taiwan. The United States refused to recognize the new government and continued to recognize the Nationalist government on Taiwan. The refusal lasted beyond the period covered here.[^ch37-chinese-rev]

The Truman administration had seen the defeat coming. In August 1949 it published the “China White Paper,” a collection of documents intended to show that the Nationalist government had lost the civil war because of its own failures and that only Chinese forces could determine the outcome. The paper argued that American aid had been generous and that more aid could not have reversed the result. The argument did not protect the administration from the charge that it had “lost” China. The charge was political, and it was powerful because it combined a real defeat with a suspicion of betrayal.[^ch37-chinese-rev]

The counterfactual is an argument, not a measurement. It is possible that a larger American intervention would have changed the military balance in 1948 or 1949. It is also possible that it would have produced a longer war and a worse outcome. The chapter presents the claim that American aid could have reversed the result as an argument, and it asks what evidence would decide it. The record shows the victory, the retreat, and the American response. It does not show that a different policy would have saved the Nationalists.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 The Soviet atomic test, August 29, 1949

On August 29, 1949, the Soviet Union detonated its first atomic device at the Semipalatinsk test site in Kazakhstan. The Soviet program called the device “First Lightning.” American monitoring detected the test in September. On September 23, Truman announced that the United States had evidence that an atomic explosion had occurred in the Soviet Union. He did not explain how the United States had obtained the evidence, and he stressed the need for international control of atomic energy. The Soviet Union confirmed on September 25 that it possessed atomic weapons.[^ch37-soviet-bomb][^ch37-soviet-announcement]

The test ended the American monopoly. It did not make the two arsenals equal. The United States still had a much larger arsenal and a longer production record, and the Soviet Union had only begun to build a nuclear force. The test changed the political meaning of the American advantage more than it changed the military balance. It showed that the advantage was temporary, and it made the possibility of a nuclear arms race real. The chapter dates the test and the announcement separately and avoids treating the test as a sudden equality.[^ch37-soviet-bomb][^ch37-soviet-announcement]

The detection also raised a question about secrecy. The United States had detected the Soviet test by means it did not disclose. The announcement was a political act as well as a scientific report. It told the public that the atomic monopoly had ended and that the government had known before the public did. The combination of secret knowledge and public disclosure became a template for the nuclear age.[^ch37-soviet-announcement]

### 4.4 NSC-68, April 14, 1950

NSC-68 was presented to Truman on April 14, 1950. The report argued that the Soviet Union sought to impose “its absolute authority over the rest of the world” and that the conflict involved “the fulfillment or destruction not only of this Republic but of civilization itself.” It described a world polarized between freedom and slavery, said that the “assault on free institutions is world-wide,” and concluded that the United States had “no choice but to demonstrate the superiority of the idea of freedom by its constructive application.” It recommended a major military buildup, including increases in conventional forces, nuclear weapons, and defense spending.[^ch37-nsc68]

The report was a statement of purpose as much as a strategy. It gave the Soviet threat a metaphysical scale and made containment a struggle for survival. It also gave the government a rationale for taxing and spending at levels that had seemed impossible in 1949. The report did not specify a budget, and it did not become policy immediately. Truman did not approve it in April 1950. The Korean War, which began two months later, supplied the occasion and the money for the buildup the report recommended.[^ch37-nsc68]

The relationship between NSC-68 and Korea is a case of timing rather than simple causation. The report was written before the war, and its recommendations were adopted after it. The war made the threat concrete and gave the administration a reason to ask for the taxes and troops. The chapter presents the report as a policy framework that the war activated, not as a document that caused the war or the buildup by itself.[^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The China lobby, Taiwan, and the politics of blame

The “loss of China” became a domestic political weapon that tied foreign policy to loyalty. The charge that officials had betrayed China overlapped with the charge that they were disloyal. The China lobby, a loose coalition of Nationalist supporters, missionaries, businessmen, and members of Congress, pressed for aid to Taiwan and for a harder line against the People’s Republic. Its influence was real, but it was not the only force shaping policy. The administration also had to manage the Korean War, the alliance with Britain, and the risk of a wider war with China.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-eo9835]

The defense of Taiwan became a test of anticommunist resolve. On June 27, 1950, Truman announced that he had ordered the Seventh Fleet to prevent an attack on Formosa and had called on the Chinese government on Formosa to cease air and sea operations against the mainland. The order made Taiwan part of the American security perimeter without a treaty commitment. It also made the “loss of China” more difficult to reverse. The chapter connects the fall of China to the domestic investigations because the charge of betrayal was both a foreign policy argument and a loyalty accusation.[^ch37-truman-korea][^ch37-chinese-rev]

The China lobby’s influence is a contested claim. Some historians see it as a decisive force in the making of American China policy. Others see it as one pressure group among many, important because it reinforced a broader anticommunist consensus. The chapter asks what evidence would distinguish the lobby’s effect from the effect of the war and the larger political climate.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-check-debate-works]

## 5. Korea through the 1953 armistice

### 5.1 The claim: containment becomes a shooting war

**The claim.** When North Korean forces attacked South Korea in June 1950, the United States led a United Nations command that fought a three-year war. Korea became the first major battle waged in the name of containment. The war was also a Korean civil and national conflict. Korean actors had their own aims, and the war’s human cost fell heavily on Koreans. The chapter holds the superpower confrontation and the Korean conflict together, because neither explains the war alone.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-newhistory-works]

The war made containment military in a way that NSC-68 had described but not achieved. It globalized the American commitment, expanded the defense budget, and turned the National Security Council’s planning into operational policy. It also exposed the limits of American power. The United States could intervene, but it could not control the outcome, and it could not fight a total war against China without risking a wider conflict with the Soviet Union.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-nsc68]

The record can show the invasion, the command, and the armistice. It cannot show that Moscow ordered the attack or that Beijing’s intervention was inevitable. The chapter attributes those claims to the evidence that supports them and treats them as interpretations where the record is incomplete.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The invasion, June 25, 1950, and the United Nations

After the Second World War, the United States and the Soviet Union divided Korea at the 38th parallel to oversee the removal of Japanese forces. The division hardened into two governments. The Soviet Union backed Kim Il Sung in the north, and the United States backed Syngman Rhee in the south. American forces withdrew from the south in the late 1940s. On June 25, 1950, North Korean troops attacked south across the parallel toward Seoul. The United Nations Security Council condemned the invasion as a “breach of the peace,” and the United States and other member nations sent forces to support South Korea.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]

The American response was rapid and unilateral in form, multilateral in language. Truman ordered American forces into the war without a congressional declaration. At a press conference on June 29, 1950, a reporter asked whether it would be correct to call the intervention “a police action under the United Nations,” and Truman answered, “Yes. That is exactly what it amounts to.” The phrase described a war fought under international authority, and it obscured the fact that the United States was the principal combatant.[^ch37-truman-police-action][^ch37-korea-milestone]

The question of whether American officials had excluded Korea from the defense perimeter is an interpretation, not a documented order. American officials had not made South Korea a key part of their defensive strategy in Asia. Whether that omission invited the attack is disputed, and the chapter asks what evidence would establish it. The North Korean decision had its own history and cannot be reduced to an American signal.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The counteroffensive, the push north, and Chinese intervention

The American-led force pushed the North Korean army back above the 38th parallel and beyond. By the fall of 1950 the United Nations command had crossed the parallel and advanced toward the Yalu River, the border with China. The advance raised the question of whether the war would be limited to the restoration of South Korea or extended to the unification of the peninsula. The United States chose the second course, and China responded.[^ch37-korea-milestone]

The Chinese People’s Volunteers entered the war in late 1950. Their intervention drove the United Nations forces back below the parallel and produced a costly stalemate. The advance to the Yalu had brought the war to China’s border, and the Chinese decision to intervene had its own political and military reasons. Whether the advance caused the intervention, or was one condition among several, is an open question. The chapter presents it as an open question and notes that Chinese documents and American intelligence estimates do not settle it.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

The war then became a contest of attrition. The front stabilized near the 38th parallel, and the fighting continued while negotiators argued over prisoners of war, the demarcation line, and the terms of a truce. The United States did not use atomic weapons, and it did not attack China directly. It fought a limited war for limited aims, and it paid a high price for the limits.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-armistice]

### 5.4 Stalemate and the armistice of July 27, 1953

After two years of truce talks, the Korean Armistice Agreement was signed at Panmunjom on July 27, 1953. The signers were Lieutenant General William K. Harrison Jr. for the United Nations Command and General Nam Il for the Korean People’s Army and the Chinese People’s Volunteers. They signed 18 official copies at 10 a.m., and the truce took effect at 10 p.m. The agreement suspended hostilities, created a demilitarized zone about 4,000 meters wide, and arranged for the exchange of prisoners. It was purely a military document. No nation was a signatory, and no peace treaty followed.[^ch37-armistice]

The negotiations had been long and bitter. The two sides met 158 times over two years and 17 days, the longest negotiated armistice in history. The agreement left the peninsula divided near the original line. It did not settle the political future of Korea, and it did not end the state of war. It created a buffer that has lasted for decades, and it left the two Koreas with armies facing each other across the demilitarized zone.[^ch37-armistice]

The armistice was not a settlement. It was a pause that became permanent. The agreement ended active combat, and it left the political questions that had caused the war unanswered. That is the sense in which the Korean War ended in 1953 and did not end at all.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 The costs, the alliance, and Map 28

The war killed millions of soldiers and civilians. The National Archives describes a three-year conflict that “claimed the lives of millions of soldiers and civilians.” The American figures are more precise but still require care. The Defense Department’s casualty system counts 36,574 American in-theater deaths between June 25, 1950, and July 27, 1953: 33,739 battle deaths and 2,835 other deaths. The National Archives gives 36,576. The same system counts 103,284 wounded and 1,789,000 Americans who served in the theater. A total of 54,246 Americans died worldwide during the war period, a figure that includes 17,672 non-theater deaths. The difference between 36,574 and 54,246 is the difference between in-theater and worldwide counts, not a dispute about the same number.[^ch37-korean-casualties][^ch37-armistice]

Korean and Chinese losses were far larger and are less precisely known. The war killed millions of Korean civilians and soldiers and large numbers of Chinese soldiers. The estimates rest on different methods, different definitions of the war period, and incomplete records. The chapter gives ranges and names the methods rather than presenting one number as settled. It also notes that the American figures count military deaths, while the Korean and Chinese figures include civilians, and that the two kinds of count should not be compared as if they measured the same thing.[^ch37-korea-casualties][^ch37-check-numbers]

The war left the United States with a permanent military presence in Northeast Asia. In 1953 the United States and South Korea signed a mutual security treaty to protect the South, and the American security commitment to Japan and Korea became a lasting part of the containment structure. The commitment outlasted the armistice and the administrations that made it. Map 28’s 1952 snapshot includes the Korean theater and the Northeast Asian commitments, but it must not imply that it covers the 1953 treaty or later events. The map ends where the snapshot ends, and the prose carries the story forward.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-map28][^ch37-check-korea]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The wartime alliance ended and was replaced by open confrontation. The disputes over Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic bomb, and the institution-building of 1947–49, are documented. The Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, the Berlin airlift, NATO, and the National Security Act created durable structures. The domestic loyalty program existed and reached millions: Executive Order 9835 created a federal program that screened more than five million workers between 1947 and 1956. China became a communist state in 1949, and the Soviet Union tested an atomic device the same year. The Korean War ended in an armistice, not a peace.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-armistice]

These facts establish the sequence, the institutions, and the outcomes. They do not establish why the sequence happened or how to weigh its costs.[^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 6.2 What is open

Who began the Cold War? Orthodox accounts emphasize Soviet expansion and broken wartime agreements. Revisionist accounts emphasize American power, economic expansion, and the atomic bomb. Post-revisionist accounts emphasize security dilemmas and mutual misperception. Each position explains part of the record. The chapter states each at its strongest and asks what evidence would decide among them.[^ch37-orthodox-works][^ch37-revisionist-works][^ch37-postrevisionist-works]

Was the atomic bomb a diplomatic instrument? One position holds that the bomb was used and the monopoly held as leverage against the Soviets. Another treats the atomic decision as primarily military and the postwar program as a response to Soviet power. The documents show the timing and the statements. They do not show a single motive, and the chapter does not assert one.[^ch37-check-atomic][^ch37-baruch-plan]

Was the Korean War a superpower proxy or a Korean civil war? A superpower frame reads it as a test of containment directed from Moscow. Bruce Cumings and others center Korean politics and society. The chapter weighs both against the sources and treats the Korean actors as agents, not as instruments.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-newhistory-works]

How costly was the domestic loyalty program? Defenders cite the procedural protections and the small number of dismissals. Critics cite the standard, the informants, and the chilling effect on a far larger number of employees. The chapter presents both with evidence and refuses to reduce the program to either its procedures or its victims.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-domestic-works]

How many died in Korea? Estimates vary widely by method, population, and cutoff. The chapter gives ranges, names the methods, and refuses to present one number as settled. The American figures are comparatively precise, and even they differ by two between the Defense Department and the National Archives.[^ch37-korea-casualties][^ch37-check-numbers]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which American power, Soviet power, ideology, security dilemmas, and human choices all mattered. The domestic costs of containment were as real as its foreign commitments. Neither a story of Soviet aggression met by innocence nor a story of American empire alone explains the record. The strongest accounts hold the external threat and the internal consequences together.[^ch37-postrevisionist-works][^ch37-domestic-works]

The Marshall Plan, NATO, NSC-68, the loyalty program, and the Korean intervention were choices taken by people who could have chosen otherwise. None was dictated by the outcome. The counterfactuals are not decorations. They identify where the evidence would have to be strongest to support a claim of inevitability. The chapter uses them to keep the argument honest, not to replace it.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-nsc68]

Structure and choice belong together. Geography, the power vacuum, the atomic monopoly, and domestic politics set the pressures. The choices made within those pressures determined the timing and the form of the response. A history that emphasizes only structure cannot explain why the loyalty program took the form it did. A history that emphasizes only choice cannot explain why so many of the choices pointed in the same direction.[^ch37-postrevisionist-works][^ch37-check-debate-works]

The synthesis for this chapter is that the United States built a permanent security state abroad and at home between 1945 and 1953. It did so in response to real Soviet power and to its own political needs. The institutions it created outlasted the crises that produced them, and the costs of those institutions fell unevenly. That is the answer to the chapter’s question, and it is a double answer: power and security changed together.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-eo9835]

## Legacies

The years from 1945 to 1953 left the United States a permanent national security state. The National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency, the Department of Defense, and the alliance system built in these years outlasted the crises that produced them, and the military budget that followed NSC-68 and the Korean War reshaped the federal government.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-nsa-1947][^ch37-nsc68]

The domestic loyalty program left a shorter but sharper legacy. The dismissals and resignations were a small fraction of those screened, but the program’s reach and its reliance on informants and broad standards of association became a cautionary precedent for later debates over security and civil liberty. The Supreme Court’s later narrowing of the Smith Act prosecutions did not restore the careers the investigations had ended.[^ch37-truman-loyalty][^ch37-dennis]

The Korean War left an armistice that is not a peace, a demilitarized zone that remains, and a continuing American military presence in South Korea. The war’s unresolved politics and its heavy Korean and Chinese death tolls are part of the chapter’s argument that containment’s costs fell unequally on the people of the places where it was fought.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]

The period also left a vocabulary. “Containment,” “the free world,” and “loyalty” became words later generations used to describe confrontations whose terms had changed, and the words carried the assumptions of the years that made them.[^ch37-check-containment-terms][^ch37-eo9835]

## Summary

- In February 1946 Kennan’s Long Telegram described the Soviet Union as insecure, expansionist, and responsive to force, and urged a patient, firm containment without general war.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]
- The wartime alliance broke down over Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic monopoly, and the Baruch Plan for international control failed when the Soviet Union rejected it in 1946.[^ch37-baruch-plan][^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- On March 12, 1947, Truman asked Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey and announced the policy of supporting “free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- The Marshall Plan, signed on April 3, 1948, appropriated $13.3 billion over four years for European recovery and aimed to build stable Western European democracies.[^ch37-marshall-plan]
- The Soviet blockade of Berlin from June 1948 to May 1949 was answered by the Berlin airlift, and on April 4, 1949, twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty.[^ch37-berlin-airlift][^ch37-nato-treaty]
- The National Security Act of 1947 created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency, and a National Military Establishment under a secretary of defense.[^ch37-nsa-1947]
- Executive Order 9835 of March 21, 1947, created the federal loyalty program; its standard was tightened by Executive Order 10241 in 1951, and more than five million workers were screened between 1947 and 1956.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-eo10241][^ch37-truman-loyalty]
- The Hollywood Ten were cited for contempt of Congress on November 24, 1947, and eleven Communist Party leaders were convicted under the Smith Act, upheld in *Dennis v. United States* in 1951.[^ch37-hollywood-ten][^ch37-dennis]
- On February 9, 1950, McCarthy claimed to hold “a list of 205” State Department employees known as communists, launching the most visible phase of the second Red Scare.[^ch37-mccarthy]
- The People’s Republic of China was proclaimed on October 1, 1949, and the Soviet Union tested its first atomic device on August 29, 1949; NSC-68 of April 14, 1950, called for a military buildup to ensure the survival of “civilization itself.”[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-soviet-bomb][^ch37-nsc68]
- North Korean forces attacked south on June 25, 1950; after Chinese intervention in late 1950 the war stalemated, and the armistice of July 27, 1953, ended active combat without a peace treaty.[^ch37-armistice]
- The war killed millions of soldiers and civilians and left the United States with permanent commitments in Northeast Asia and an unresolved division of Korea.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1945 and 1953 the United States answered a broken wartime alliance with a permanent security state abroad and at home. It built the institutions of containment — the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, NATO, the National Security Council, and the Central Intelligence Agency — and it screened, investigated, and blacklisted its own citizens in the name of loyalty. The chapter’s question — how did a wartime alliance become a permanent security confrontation? — is answered by that double record of commitment and coercion, and by a Korean armistice that ended the fighting without ending the war.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-armistice]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in 1950 in Levittown, New York, where developers were building the first mass-produced suburbs for a growing and largely White middle class. Chapter 38 takes up the affluent society of the 1950s and asks for whom postwar abundance was secure. The security state that Chapter 37 describes did not recede as the fighting in Korea ended; it became the backdrop for the consumer culture and the conformity that the next chapter examines.[^ch37-check-bridge][^ch37-check-ch38]
