## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **Place and time.** Saturday, July 4, 2026, at Monticello, Thomas Jefferson’s plantation in Albemarle County, Virginia. The ceremony began at 9 a.m. on the West Lawn, which the Thomas Jefferson Foundation described as an outdoor courtroom.[^intro-monticello-official] A Chinese gong Jefferson installed on the roof sounded nine times shortly before 9 a.m.; John Charles Thomas, a retired justice of the Supreme Court of Virginia, then read the Declaration of Independence.[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]
- **Weather and setting.** The forecast called for 102 degrees in Central Virginia during a heat wave. The morning was “a little kinder on the mountaintop.” The ceremony was held under a large fan-equipped tent — only the second tent in the event’s 64-year history. Monticello provided complimentary water and root beer floats made with the estate’s own honey-sweetened, sassafras-flavored soft drink.[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress] The official event page also lists complimentary water and root beer floats.[^intro-monticello-official]
- **The ceremony.** The 64th annual Independence Day Celebration and Naturalization Ceremony. The official event page says 74 individuals took the oath; WVIR reports that they came from 36 countries. More than 4,000 people have become U.S. citizens at Monticello since the ceremony began in 1963.[^intro-monticello-official][^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress][^intro-monticello-2026-wvir] Chief Judge Elizabeth K. Dillon of the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Virginia declared the court in session. Senior Judge Michael F. Urbanski administered the oath. The new citizens raised their hands for the oath and placed them over their hearts for the Pledge of Allegiance.[^intro-monticello-2026-cville][^intro-monticello-2026-wvir]
- **The speakers.** Gov. Abigail Spanberger delivered the keynote. Jane Kamensky, president and CEO of the Thomas Jefferson Foundation, welcomed the crowd. Kamensky called the day “a moment of joining for them” and “a moment of renewal for all of us.”[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir] She also noted the triple anniversary: the 250th anniversary of the Declaration, the 250th anniversary of Virginia’s statehood, and the bicentennial of Jefferson’s death on July 4, 1826.[^intro-monticello-2026-cville]
- **The new citizens.** The reporting names several speakers: Omar Basel Bouz Alassal, 19, born in Syria, who left at age four and came to the United States five years earlier; Ana Maria Silva Molina, born in Ecuador; Frank Georg Biller, born in Switzerland; Dunia Lyceth Woodward, who fled poverty in Honduras with her three-year-old son; Florentina Gonzalez of Mexico; Debee Arvind Bouch of India; and Joseph and Chebwa Mutombo of the Democratic Republic of the Congo.[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir][^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress] Cville Right Now reports that eight naturalized citizens spoke, but the reports do not establish that every named speaker was one of the 74, or that all 74 spoke.[^intro-monticello-2026-cville]
- **The words.** Silva Molina said: “I am choosing to become a U.S. citizen because I want my voice to be heard, and I want it to count.”[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir] Bouz Alassal said: “No words can really explain how proud I am in this very special day today to celebrate the 4th of July and also become an American citizen and have the right to call the United States my home.”[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir] Biller said: “It is my wish, my desire and my pledge that I will always be a citizen that gives more to the community and to the country than I take.”[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir] Woodward recalled: “I had to climb on a tree to get me a mango to eat.”[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]
- **The official frame.** Spanberger said: “You have chosen to become Americans.” She challenged the audience to learn from the new citizens’ example, to believe in the founding ideals, and to make them real.[^intro-monticello-2026-cville] Kamensky said: “We’ve all come to look for America, and gosh darn it, you’ve found it.”[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]
- **The contradiction.** Jefferson enslaved more than 610 people during his lifetime; four hundred men, women, and children lived in bondage at Monticello.[^intro-monticello-slavery] The Declaration he drafted declares that “all men are created equal.”[^intro-declaration] In 1802, as president, he signed a naturalization law that began: “any alien, being a free white person, may be admitted to become a citizen of the United States.”[^intro-naturalization-1802] The first federal naturalization law, in 1790, had used the same limit.[^intro-naturalization-1790]
- **The contemporary frame.** Four days before the ceremony, on June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court struck down President Donald Trump’s executive order ending birthright citizenship. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion in *Trump v. Barbara*, No. 25-365; the Court held that children born in the United States to parents unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment’s Citizenship Clause.[^intro-scotus-barbara-scotusblog] Five justices joined Roberts’s opinion. Justice Brett Kavanaugh concurred in the judgment but rejected the constitutional reasoning, and Justices Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito, and Neil Gorsuch dissented, so the judgment was 6–3 while the constitutional holding rested on five votes.[^intro-scotus-barbara-npr][^intro-scotus-barbara-constitution-center] The decision ran 194 pages.[^intro-scotus-barbara-constitution-center] At Monticello, Nazanin Ash, chief executive of Welcome.US, said: “What a week to be having this conversation.”[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]
- **The crowd.** “Nearly 3,000 people attended,” according to a report that attributes the figure to the Thomas Jefferson Foundation.[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress] This is a single-source estimate, not an official count; the official event page does not give an attendance figure.[^intro-monticello-official] The scene can use “nearly 3,000” only with that attribution, or omit the number.

### The scene’s argument

- **A ritual at a contradiction.** The ceremony is a state ritual of belonging at a site of hereditary racial slavery. The camera should hold both facts without resolving them into either celebration or indictment.
- **The new citizens speak in their own words.** Their remarks emphasize voice, gratitude, family, and giving back. They are not asking the book to speak for them. The scene should quote them and then step back.
- **The state’s power to include is the state’s power to exclude.** The oath is administered by a federal court, under a statute. The same legal order that naturalizes 74 people has, for most of its history, restricted who could be naturalized. The scene makes that paradox concrete.
- **The past is not a prologue to the present.** Jefferson did not know the 2026 ceremony, the 1924 Indian Citizenship Act, or *Trump v. Barbara*. The scene must not give him hindsight. The contradiction is visible to the reader, not to him.
- **The question.** The scene ends with the book’s guiding question: How did a continent of Indigenous nations become a republic, a continental empire, and a global power, and who could claim membership, freedom, land, and authority within those changing arrangements?
- **The evidentiary limit.** The scene is reconstructed from local reporting and an official event page, not from a transcript. The official page says a livestream was recorded. Before drafting, review the livestream or a transcript to check sequence, wording, and attribution.[^intro-check-quote-transcription]

### What must not be invented

- No interior thoughts, overheard remarks, composite witnesses, or invented dialogue.
- No sensory details beyond those reported: the heat, the tent, the fans, the water, the root beer floats, the gong, the raised hands, the certificates.

## 1. Why this book

### 1.1 The question

- **The claim.** The book’s question is not “What is America?” but “How was authority constituted, and who could claim membership, freedom, land, and authority within those changing arrangements?” The question is historical, not metaphysical: it asks how particular arrangements were made, defended, contested, and changed.
- **The four nouns.** *Membership* is legal status, social belonging, and political claim. *Freedom* is not the absence of restraint but a set of enforceable capacities and exemptions. *Land* is property, homeland, ecology, and jurisdiction. *Authority* is the power to make and enforce rules, including the power to decide who belongs.
- **The scene’s return.** The Monticello ceremony is a recent, small instance: 74 people take an oath that makes them citizens of a state whose boundaries and terms were made through conquest, treaty, slavery, migration, and law. The scene raises the question; it does not answer it.
- **The scope.** The book is a history of the United States, not a history of everything in North America. It follows the state’s formation and the territory it claimed, while treating Indigenous nations, empires, and neighboring republics as actors with their own histories.
- **The limit.** The question cannot be answered by a single cause — not capitalism, not race, not geography, not culture. The book tests combinations of causes against evidence and against rival explanations.

### 1.2 The wager

- **The provisional thesis.** The United States was repeatedly constituted through settlements that changed the terms of conflict without settling them permanently. Independence, emancipation, industrial growth, the New Deal, and the rights revolution created real possibilities and real exclusions. Neither progress nor betrayal alone explains the record.
- **What “settlement” means.** A settlement is a durable arrangement that redistributes power: a constitution, a treaty, a labor regime, a racial order, an imperial status, a welfare state. It is not the same as a compromise, a victory, or a consensus. It is a settlement because it lasts long enough to structure the next conflict.
- **The double meaning of “in question.”** The title means both that the nation is contested and that the book puts it in question. The book does not assume the nation’s goodness or its bad faith; it asks how the nation was made and what it cost.
- **The rival explanations.** The book tests the thesis against four rivals: progress (the nation as a long march toward inclusion), betrayal (the nation as a promise always broken), inevitability (the outcome as the product of geography, culture, or economic law), and exceptionalism (the United States as a category apart). Each rival explains part of the record; none explains all of it.
- **The test.** A settlement that permanently closed conflict would count against the thesis. A turning point with no plausible alternative would count against contingency. The book looks for both.

### 1.3 Audience and contract

- **The audience.** Attentive general readers, students, and listeners. No prior knowledge is assumed; no condescension is allowed. The book explains terms at first use and keeps the prose concrete.
- **The contract.** The book is not an encyclopedia. It selects, argues, and shows its evidence. It does not claim to cover every event or to settle every controversy. It gives the reader enough to disagree.
- **The limit of neutrality.** The book does not pretend to a view from nowhere. It states its commitments — to evidence, to the dignity of the people it describes, to the seriousness of the questions — and it tests those commitments against the record. The reader should be able to see where the argument is going and why.
- **The voice.** Plain, serious, sometimes wry, never sneering. The book is written to be read aloud. It should sound like a person thinking in public, not a textbook reciting a consensus.
- **Transition.** Before the argument can be tested, the book has to define its unit: what “the United States” means, and when and where it existed.

## 2. What “the United States” means

### 2.1 State, project, place, and people

- **The claim.** “The United States” names four things at once: a legal state, a settler project, a geographic place, and a people. Confusing them produces bad history. The book keeps them distinct and shows how they interact.
- **The legal state.** The Constitution of 1787 created a federal union with limited powers, divided sovereignty, and a promise of republican government. The Fourteenth Amendment’s Citizenship Clause names “citizens of the United States and of the State wherein they reside,” a dual structure that persists.[^intro-fourteenth-amendment] The state’s authority was never uniform: territories, Indian country, the District of Columbia, and overseas possessions have had different relationships to the Constitution.
- **The settler project.** The United States was built through the dispossession of Indigenous nations, the extraction of land and labor, and the settlement of migrants. This is not a moral add-on to the national story; it is a description of how the state acquired and organized its territory.
- **The place.** The book’s geographic center is the territory that became the fifty states. It follows the Atlantic, Caribbean, hemispheric, and Pacific connections that shaped that territory. Mexico, Canada, the Caribbean, Europe, Africa, and Asia enter where their histories explain the center, not as scenery.
- **The people.** “We the People” was a claim before it was a fact. The boundaries of the people were set by birth, naturalization, race, gender, settlement, and conquest. The book treats those boundaries as historical problems, not as background.
- **Transition.** The republic is a late arrival on a continent with a deep human history. §2.2 begins there.

### 2.2 A continent before the republic

- **The claim.** The history of the United States begins long before 1776, and not because the continent was empty. Indigenous peoples built cities, earthworks, agricultural systems, and trade networks; they managed fire, water, and game; they made war and peace. The republic entered a world that was already political.
- **The evidence.** Fossilized human footprints at White Sands National Park show that people were living in North America at least 23,000 years ago; the published calibrated dates include 22,860 ±320 and 21,130 ±250 years ago.[^intro-white-sands] The date is a minimum, not a single arrival. Chapter 1 owns the competing routes and chronologies.
- **The frame.** The book begins with the peopling of the Americas, not with 1607 or 1620. The deep past is not a prelude to the nation; it is the first chapter of a continental history in which the nation is one episode.
- **The politics.** Indigenous nations were not “prehistory.” They were actors with territory, diplomacy, kinship, and law. The book uses nation-specific names where known and explains contested names. Chapter 3 owns the political worlds of the contact era.
- **The transition.** The republic’s history is a history of encounters with these nations, not a story that begins when Europeans arrive.
- **The limit.** The archaeological record is uneven and constantly revised. The book states dates as estimates or minimums, not as settled facts.

### 2.3 Borders, territories, and the problem of the map

- **The claim.** The map of the United States is a claim, not a fact. Borders were made by treaties, purchases, conquest, court decisions, and settlement. They were contested by Indigenous nations, neighboring states, and the people who lived on the ground.
- **The legal geography.** The United States includes states, territories, the District of Columbia, and overseas possessions with different rights and representation. *Downes v. Bidwell* held that Puerto Rico was not “a part of the United States” for the Constitution’s uniformity clause, and the case is central to the insular cases’ distinction between incorporated and unincorporated territory.[^intro-downes] The Constitution did not follow the flag everywhere.
- **The census snapshot.** On April 1, 2020, the resident population of the fifty states and the District of Columbia was 331,449,281; Puerto Rico’s resident population was 3,285,874.[^intro-census-2020-total] The census counts residents, not citizens. It is a portrait of who was counted, not a referendum on who belongs.
- **The book’s center.** The book follows the territory that became the fifty states, but it does not treat the current map as inevitable. It shows the acquisitions, the claims, and the people who were already there.
- **The limit.** The map cannot show jurisdiction, sovereignty, or belonging. A single color on a map can hide a treaty, a reservation, a military base, or a border crosser.
- **Transition.** If the map is a claim, so is the people. §2.4 asks who “the people” were.

### 2.4 Who is “the people”?

- **The claim.** Citizenship is a legal status, a political claim, and a cultural boundary. The book treats all three. The legal status can be granted or withheld; the political claim can be asserted by people who lack the status; the cultural boundary can be policed by ordinary people who have no legal authority.
- **The close reading.** The Introduction’s close reading is a pair of statutes, not an In Focus box. The first federal naturalization law, approved March 26, 1790, began: “any alien, being a free white person, who shall have resided within the limits and under the jurisdiction of the United States for the term of two years, may be admitted to become a citizen thereof.”[^intro-naturalization-1790] The 1802 law, approved April 14, 1802, and signed by Jefferson, began: “any alien, being a free white person, may be admitted to become a citizen of the United States.”[^intro-naturalization-1802] The 1802 law changed the residence and declaration requirements; it kept the racial limit. The close reading should notice both: the mechanics of naturalization and the boundary of whiteness.
- **The constitutional turn.** The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, made “all persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof” citizens.[^intro-fourteenth-amendment] *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* held that a child born in the United States to Chinese parents domiciled here was a citizen under that clause.[^intro-wong-kim-ark] The clause did not settle every boundary: Section 2 of the amendment referred to “Indians not taxed,” and the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 declared “all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States” to be citizens, while preserving tribal property rights.[^intro-indian-citizenship-act]
- **The sovereignty exception.** *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* described tribes as “domestic dependent nations” in a “state of pupilage,” with the relation of “a ward to his guardian.”[^intro-cherokee-nation] The language is a landmark in the legal construction of tribal sovereignty as something other than foreign status. It is also a justification for tutelage. The book treats tribal sovereignty as a distinct legal and political order, not as a waiting room for citizenship.
- **The 2026 frame.** *Trump v. Barbara*, decided four days before the Monticello ceremony described at the opening, rejected the executive order’s attempt to deny birthright citizenship to children of parents unlawfully or temporarily present. Roberts wrote for the majority: “Citizenship, then and now, was the right to have rights—to freely participate in our political community.”[^intro-scotus-barbara-scotusblog] The decision is a reminder that the boundary of the people is still being litigated.
- **The limit.** Legal citizenship is not the same as social belonging. A citizen can be excluded from the polity by race, gender, poverty, or violence; a noncitizen can be a member of a community in every sense except the legal one. The book keeps the distinction visible.
- **Transition.** The boundaries of the people are one of five through-lines. §3 sets out all five and the seven claims they support.

## 3. Five through-lines and seven claims

### 3.1 The five through-lines

- **The claim.** The five through-lines are not separate histories. They are lenses that make different parts of the same history visible. Each chapter usually carries two or three; the book does not assign one lens per chapter.
- **Land and environment.** Settlement, Indigenous land management, agriculture, extraction, infrastructure, and climate. The central tension: land is both a relation and a commodity. The lens follows the conversion of homelands into property and the resistance that conversion provoked. Illustrative chapters: 1–3, 14–18, 25, 34, 49–50.
- **Liberty and unfreedom.** Slavery, emancipation, labor, bodily freedom, punishment, and the limits of legal rights. The central tension: American freedom was defined through, not despite, coercion. The lens follows the legal and social arrangements that made some people free by making others unfree. Illustrative chapters: 4–6, 19–23, 26, 39–40.
- **Pluralism and belonging.** Citizenship, migration, religion, race, gender, and the changing boundaries of “the people.” The central tension: inclusion and exclusion are produced by the same legal and political order. The lens follows the making and remaking of the people. Illustrative chapters: 2–5, 13–17, 29–32, 45–49.
- **Capitalism and inequality.** Production, credit, public subsidy, work, consumption, and the distribution of power and wealth. The central tension: markets are political creations, and they distribute risk unequally. The lens follows the relationship between growth and inequality. Illustrative chapters: 15, 24–28, 33–34, 43–45.
- **Power and the world.** Indigenous diplomacy, European empires, continental expansion, overseas empire, and superpower commitments. The central tension: the United States was made by the world and then made the world in its image. The lens follows the projection of power and the constraints on it. Illustrative chapters: 8, 11, 18, 27, 30–31, 35–37, 41, 46–48.
- **The limit.** The lenses can become a checklist. The book uses them to ask questions, not to force every chapter into five parts. A chapter that is mostly about land may also be about labor, race, and empire; the outline says which lens is primary and why.

### 3.2 The seven claims

The seven claims are the five through-lines stated as arguable propositions, plus the two methodological claims named in the README. This enumeration is the outline’s interpretation of the plan; the README does not list the seven separately.[^intro-check-seven-claims]

1. **Claim 1 — Land and environment.** The republic’s wealth and power rested on the conversion of Indigenous homelands and ecosystems into property, infrastructure, and commodities; that conversion was extensive but never total, and its consequences remain material.
2. **Claim 2 — Liberty and unfreedom.** American liberty was defined through, not despite, slavery, coerced labor, and exclusion; emancipation and civil rights changed the legal order without dissolving the entanglement.
3. **Claim 3 — Pluralism and belonging.** The boundaries of “the people” were repeatedly redrawn through migration, birth, naturalization, and racial and gender rules; belonging was never a single legal status or a stable cultural fact.
4. **Claim 4 — Capitalism and inequality.** Economic growth depended on public authority, credit, expropriated land, and unequal labor regimes; markets distributed risk and reward unevenly, and booms and busts reshaped political coalitions.
5. **Claim 5 — Power and the world.** The United States became a continental empire and then a global power through conquest, treaty, basing, and trade; its external role shaped its domestic arrangements and was shaped by them.
6. **Claim 6 — Settlements remained contested.** Constitutional, territorial, labor, and racial settlements changed the terms of conflict without ending it; each settlement produced new claims and new arenas.
7. **Claim 7 — Outcomes were contingent.** At each turning point, plausible alternatives existed; later outcomes were made by choices, accidents, and resistance, not foreordained by geography, culture, or economic law.

- **How the claims work together.** Claims 1–5 are substantive: they say what kind of history this is. Claims 6–7 are methodological: they say how the book explains change. Claim 6 without Claim 7 becomes a story of eternal conflict; Claim 7 without Claim 6 becomes a story of random accidents. Together they say: settlements are real, and they are reversible.
- **What the claims do not say.** They do not say that every settlement was equally unjust, that every outcome was equally open, or that the United States is uniquely evil or uniquely good. They are propositions to argue, not verdicts to apply.
- **The test.** Each claim must be falsifiable. The book should be able to say what evidence would count against it. §3.3 states the strongest objections.

### 3.3 What would count against the claims

- **Against Claim 1.** The federal government also conserved land, and Indigenous nations retained, reacquired, and co-managed territory. If the conversion were total and one-way, the claim would be false. The book must show both the conversion and its limits.
- **Against Claim 2.** Antislavery and free-labor ideologies were genuine; not every appeal to freedom was a mask for coercion. If American liberty were nothing but a disguise for slavery, the claim would be too strong. The book must show the entanglement without erasing the genuine commitments.
- **Against Claim 3.** Naturalization, birthright citizenship, and the expansion of rights created real inclusion. If belonging were only a story of exclusion, the claim would be false. The book must show inclusion and exclusion as co-produced, not as one or the other.
- **Against Claim 4.** Markets also created mobility, lower prices, and new goods. If inequality were the only result, the claim would be misleading. The book must show distribution, not just growth.
- **Against Claim 5.** The United States built international institutions and alliances that constrained its own power. If power were only unilateral, the claim would be false. The book must show the constraints as well as the projections.
- **Against Claim 6.** Some settlements were durable: the Constitution’s amendment process, the post-Civil War amendments, the New Deal, the Civil Rights Act. If every settlement collapsed, the claim would be false. The book must explain durability without equating it with finality.
- **Against Claim 7.** Not every alternative was viable. Structural constraints, path dependence, and the distribution of power limited the options. If contingency meant “anything could happen,” the claim would be trivial. The book must specify the alternatives and the constraints.
- **The standard.** A claim survives if it explains the pattern better than its rival, not if it explains every case. The Introduction should say this explicitly, and the chapters should show it.

## 4. Periodization

### 4.1 Why periods are arguments

- **The claim.** A period is not a neutral container. It selects events, implies causes, and silences alternatives. “The Jacksonian era” tells a different story from “the age of removal”; “the Cold War” tells a different story from “the American century.”
- **The book’s periods.** The book uses parts and chapters as interpretive groupings, not as date bins. The boundaries are chosen to make a set of changes visible: the consolidation of colonial societies, the break of independence, the re-founding of Reconstruction, the rise of industrial capitalism, the New Deal, the rights revolution, the neoliberal turn.
- **The overlap.** Chapters 23–28 revisit the same postwar decades through Reconstruction, industry, the West, the South, empire, and culture. Chapters 37–43 revisit the Cold War through institutions, abundance, movements, legislation, foreign war, and realignment. The overlaps are intentional; each chapter has a different job.
- **The risk.** Periodization can become teleology. The book resists by returning to contingency: at each boundary, it asks what else was possible.

### 4.2 The spine

The Introduction should give a compact spine, not a chapter list. The spine is a sequence of periods and turning points, ending with a coda:

- **Deep past to 1500 CE.** Peopling, agriculture, cities, earthworks, and regional political worlds.
- **c. 1000–1551.** Norse and Iberian landfalls, contact, conquest, exchange, and the Valladolid debate.
- **1500–1620.** Indigenous political worlds, European footholds, and the first permanent English settlements.
- **1607–1763.** Colonial formation, slavery, empire, and the great war for empire.
- **1763–1801.** Imperial crisis, independence, constitution-making, and the new republic.
- **1800–1848.** Expansion, the market revolution, mass politics, removal, and the Mexican-American War.
- **1850–1877.** The sectional crisis, the Civil War, emancipation, and Reconstruction.
- **1877–1900.** Industrial capitalism, the conquest of the West, the New South, empire, and culture.
- **1900–1945.** Progressive reform, the First World War, prosperity, depression, the New Deal, and the Second World War.
- **1945–1968.** The Cold War, affluence, the civil rights revolution, the Great Society, and Vietnam.
- **1968–1991.** Unraveling, stagflation, the Reagan revolution, and the end of the Cold War.
- **1991–2008.** Globalization, culture wars, 9/11, and the war on terror.
- **2008–October 29, 2026.** The Obama era, the populist turn, the first Trump presidency, the reckoning, and the 250th anniversary.

- **The boundaries.** 1551 marks the Valladolid debate; 1620 marks the Mayflower; 1763 marks the Treaty of Paris; 1877 marks the end of Reconstruction; 1945 marks the end of the Second World War; 1968 marks a rupture in politics and culture; 2001 marks 9/11; 2026 marks the 250th anniversary and the book’s cutoff. These are arguments, not natural joints.
- **The coda.** Chapter 50 is a retrospective conclusion, not a fifty-first chapter. It weighs what the preceding 49 chapters established.

### 4.3 The 2026 cutoff

- **The rule.** The narrative ends with evidence available through October 29, 2026. The 250th anniversary is a framing occasion, not permission to invent a ceremony or predict a result.
- **The election.** The November 2026 election is after the cutoff. The book must not report it as completed history or predict its outcome. The Introduction can say that the election lay ahead at the cutoff; it should not say what it meant.[^intro-check-recent-cutoff]
- **The recent record.** Claims about 2025–2026 require fresh verification. Journalism is a source, not the final word; official documents, court records, and transcripts may appear later. The book should mark recent claims as provisional where the record is thin.
- **The 250th.** The anniversary is an occasion for public memory, not a conclusion about the nation’s meaning. The book uses it as a frame and then steps back.

### 4.4 What this periodization excludes

- **Presidency-by-presidency.** The book does not organize the narrative around administrations. Presidents matter, but they are not the only actors.
- **War-to-war.** The book does not treat wars as the only turning points. Land, labor, migration, and law change in peacetime too.
- **“The American century.”** The book does not assume that the twentieth century was the nation’s culmination. It treats the century as one period among others.
- **“Rise and fall.”** The book does not assume that the nation is rising or falling. It asks how arrangements were made and changed.
- **The cost.** A periodization that foregrounds land, labor, and belonging will underplay some political and military events. The chapters on those events must justify their place, not assume it.

## 5. Sources and silences

### 5.1 The evidentiary base

- **The claim.** The book uses many kinds of evidence because no single archive contains the history. Archaeology, oral histories, archives, law, newspapers, census series, testimony, landscapes, and digital records each answer different questions.
- **Primary and secondary.** Primary sources establish what a document said, what a record counted, or what a witness claimed. Scholarship supplies interpretation and context. A primary source can establish that someone made a claim without establishing that the claim was true.
- **The rule.** Every substantive claim has a named citation note with a precise locator and a “Used for” annotation. The note is part of the argument, not decoration. The reader should be able to follow the trail.
- **The access rule.** Prefer accessible, stable, document-specific sources. Where a source is offline or restricted, identify the edition and locator and state the limitation. Do not invent a URL, a page, or a verification result.
- **The limit.** The book cannot read everything. It selects the evidence that bears on its claims and names the gaps. A source that is merely discovered is not evidence; only a source that has been read and checked supports a checked assertion.

### 5.2 What the archive holds

- **The claim.** The archive is rich, but it is not neutral. It was created by states, institutions, enslavers, courts, and officials. Its categories reflect their interests. The book uses the archive against the grain where it can.
- **Monticello.** The Thomas Jefferson Foundation’s Getting Word project records and preserves the family histories of the over 610 people enslaved by Jefferson.[^intro-getting-word] The project is a model of institutional research and descendant collaboration, and it is also a curated archive. The book should distinguish the foundation’s summary from the underlying records and oral histories.
- **Slavery and the Atlantic.** SlaveVoyages documents more than 36,000 transatlantic voyages between 1514 and 1866 and more than 37,000 intra-American voyages between 1550 and 1887; the project says the voyages relocated more than 12 million enslaved Africans.[^intro-slavevoyages] The database is a remarkable reconstruction, but it records what surviving sources document. It is not a census of the enslaved.
- **Indigenous records.** The Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center gathers records of the school and the thousands of students sent there.[^intro-carlisle] The archive is partial and shaped by the school’s own recordkeeping. The book uses it to recover names and experiences, and it names what the archive cannot show.
- **Official records.** The National Archives, court opinions, statutes, census releases, and presidential documents anchor the book’s legal and statistical claims. They are authoritative for what they record, not for what they omit.
- **Journalism.** Newspapers and local reporting are essential for recent events, including the Monticello ceremony. They are firsthand or near-firsthand accounts, but they are not transcripts. The book attributes quotations to the reporting and checks them against recordings where possible.[^intro-check-quote-transcription]

### 5.3 What the archive does not hold

- **The claim.** Silence is evidence of power, not proof of absence. The archive’s gaps are part of the history the book tells.
- **Enslaved people’s voices.** Many enslaved people left no written record. Their voices survive in court testimony, bills of sale, letters, oral histories, and the records of the people who owned them. The book uses those traces without pretending to recover a full interior life.
- **Indigenous knowledge.** Some knowledge is not public, and some is protected by protocols of permission and reciprocity. The book does not treat a public archive as the whole of Indigenous history.
- **Women’s labor.** Unpaid care work, household production, and reproductive labor are often missing from wage records and census categories. The book reads the sources with that absence in mind.
- **Undocumented migrants.** People without legal status often avoid records or are recorded by the state in categories designed for enforcement. The book does not infer their lives from the state’s categories alone.
- **The recent past.** Records from 2025–2026 are incomplete; archives may be closed, and officials may not have released documents. The book marks recent claims as provisional.[^intro-check-recent-cutoff]
- **The rule.** Where no voice survives, name the silence. Do not ventriloquize, infer consensus from an absent record, or treat an archive’s exclusions as proof of nonexistence.

### 5.4 Numbers and their limits

- **The claim.** Numbers are arguments. They carry definitions, denominators, observation years, and uncertainty. The book states them.
- **The census.** The 2020 Census counted 331,449,281 residents of the fifty states and the District of Columbia. The Post-Enumeration Survey estimated a 3.30% undercount of the Black population, a 4.99% undercount of the Hispanic or Latino population, a 5.64% undercount of American Indian or Alaska Native people living on reservations, and a 1.64% overcount of the non-Hispanic White population.[^intro-census-2020-total][^intro-census-2020-undercount] These are estimates with standard errors, not counts of missing individuals. The categories are census categories, not natural kinds.
- **The slave trade.** The SlaveVoyages figure of more than 12 million is a count of people embarked on documented voyages, not a count of survivors or of all enslaved people in the Americas.[^intro-slavevoyages] The book should not convert it into a population estimate without saying so.
- **The rule.** Every statistic names its source, observation year or period, units, population or denominator, and series or table locator. Rounding and calculations are stated. Inflation-adjusted money names the price index and base year.
- **The limit.** A number can be precise without being accurate. The book prefers a range with a stated uncertainty to a false precision.

## 6. Historiographical map

### 6.1 Why historiography is part of the argument

- **The claim.** History is written from a position. The book’s synthesis is a wager, not a consensus. The reader should know the arguments the book is choosing among.
- **The purpose.** The map is not a parade of schools or a scorecard. It is a set of tools for asking better questions. Each position illuminates something the others miss.
- **The limit.** A label can become a stereotype. The book names positions to clarify differences, not to dismiss the people who hold them. The table’s “limitation” column is an invitation to argue, not a verdict.

### 6.2 From national narrative to professional history

- **The national narrative.** Nineteenth-century historians wrote the nation as the fulfillment of liberty: Providence, progress, great men, and the march of settlement. The narrative was powerful and popular, and it pushed Indigenous nations, enslaved people, and conflict to the margins. The outline should describe this tradition as a foil, not attribute specific arguments to Bancroft or Parkman unless their works are consulted.[^intro-check-table-rows]
- **The Progressive critique.** Turner’s frontier thesis and Beard’s economic interpretation of the Constitution put land, class, and interest at the center. Turner wrote: “The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development.”[^intro-turner] Beard argued that the Constitution reflected the economic interests of its framers and should be read as a document of political economy.[^intro-beard]
- **The consensus turn.** Mid-twentieth-century historians emphasized shared liberal premises and the stability of American politics. Hofstadter’s *The American Political Tradition* (1948) traced a set of shared political ideas across the presidents and helped make the consensus framework visible. Two decades later, *The Progressive Historians* (1968) criticized Turner, Beard, and Parrington for imposing a conflict narrative on the past.[^intro-hofstadter] The consensus account explained stability, but it underestimated slavery, dispossession, and coercion.
- **The social-history turn.** The new social history recovered the agency of workers, enslaved people, women, immigrants, and ordinary communities. Foner’s *Reconstruction* treated emancipation as an unfinished revolution and placed freedpeople’s agency at the center.[^intro-foner] Hall’s “long civil rights movement” argued that the movement should be understood as a longer struggle over the political uses of the past.[^intro-hall]
- **The sovereignty, race, gender, and environment turns.** Dunbar-Ortiz’s *An Indigenous Peoples’ History of the United States* made settler colonialism a central frame.[^intro-dunbar-ortiz] White’s *The Middle Ground* described a space of accommodation and brokerage between Indigenous nations and empires.[^intro-white-middle-ground] Cronon’s *Nature’s Metropolis* showed that Chicago and its rural hinterland were mutually constituted through markets, transportation, and ecology.[^intro-cronon]
- **The capitalist turn and its critics.** The New History of Capitalism argued that slavery and cotton were central to industrial capitalism.[^intro-new-history-capitalism] Olmstead and Rhode criticized its quantitative claims and called for more careful definitions and evidence.[^intro-olmstead-rhode] The debate is a useful case study in how a powerful interpretation can outrun its evidence.

### 6.3 Five positions

- **Progressive political economy.** Core claim: American democracy and the Constitution were shaped by westward expansion and conflicts over economic interest. Contribution: put land, class, and power at the center. Limitation: treated the frontier as empty and inevitable; reduced politics to interest.
- **Consensus/liberal.** Core claim: American politics has rested on a shared liberal consensus; conflict occurs within narrow bounds. Contribution: explained stability and the absence of a mass socialist movement. Limitation: underestimated slavery, dispossession, and coercion; treated a national consensus as universal.
- **Conflict/social history.** Core claim: history from below; race, labor, and grassroots movements drive change; emancipation and civil rights are unfinished. Contribution: restored agency, violence, and the long arc of struggle. Limitation: fragmented the national story; the state and political economy sometimes disappeared.
- **Sovereignty, race, gender, environment.** Core claim: conquest, Indigenous political orders, gender, and ecology are constitutive, not peripheral. Contribution: reframed the nation as a settler-colonial and ecological project. Limitation: synthesis can flatten regional variation; “settler colonialism” can become a single explanatory key.
- **Transnational/critical political economy.** Core claim: slavery and cotton were central to industrial capitalism; the economy was built through global flows of capital, labor, and violence. Contribution: connected domestic inequality to global markets and coerced labor. Limitation: definitional and quantitative disputes; can reduce slavery to a factor of production.

### 6.4 What is settled, what is open, and what this book takes

- **What is settled.** The Constitution was a contested settlement, not a neutral framework. Slavery and dispossession were central, not marginal. Indigenous nations were political actors. The New Deal and the rights revolution changed the country. The recent past is not a simple story of progress or decline.
- **What is open.** The relative weight of economic interest, ideology, race, and contingency. The degree to which the United States was exceptional. The periodization of the twentieth century. The meaning of the 2026 anniversary. The book does not pretend to close these questions.
- **What the book takes.** From the Progressive tradition: power and political economy. From consensus: the importance of durable institutions and shared frameworks. From social history: agency, violence, and the view from below. From sovereignty/race/gender/environment: conquest, ecology, and the politics of difference. From transnational/critical political economy: global flows and coerced labor.
- **What the book rejects.** Inevitability, consensus as a national fact, pure victimhood, pure agency, market fundamentalism, and the idea that the nation is either a promise or a fraud. The book’s synthesis is a set of tested claims, not a blend of schools.
- **The historiographical lesson.** The best history is not the one that picks a side and stops. It is the one that states its position, gives the strongest version of the rival, and shows what the evidence can bear.

## 7. Method

### 7.1 Scene and claim

- **The claim.** Each chapter begins with a documented scene, states its claim, and then shows how the evidence supports it. The scene is not decoration. It poses the problem the chapter will explain.
- **The scene rule.** A scene must be established by evidence: a place, a person, an object, a document, a day. If the record does not support a detail, the book omits it, qualifies it, or chooses another scene. It does not invent thoughts, dialogue, weather, or witnesses.
- **The claim rule.** The chapter’s first subsection states the claim and the structure. The reader should know what the chapter argues before the narrative begins.
- **The turn.** Each section ends on a consequence or a new question, not a repetitive summary. The chapter’s last section, the Debate, states the strongest rival interpretation and answers it.

### 7.2 Evidence and counterevidence

- **The claim.** The book distinguishes evidence, inference, and speculation. It gives the strongest version of the rival argument before answering it.
- **The footnote rule.** Every substantive assertion, quotation, statistic, and caption has a named citation note with a precise locator and a “Used for” annotation. Reused notes support the same passage and qualification.
- **The counterevidence rule.** The book names what each source cannot establish. A mandatory policy can be documented without proving that it was enforced; a speech can be documented without proving that the audience agreed.
- **The uncertainty rule.** The book uses “the record suggests,” “probably,” and “the surviving evidence does not show” when the evidence is inferential. It does not use “arguably” to avoid a judgment.

### 7.3 Causation and contingency

- **The claim.** Historical explanation distinguishes structural conditions, precipitating events, and contingent choices. The book shows the mechanism, not just the correlation.
- **The mechanism.** “In part” is not a causal explanation. If the book says that slavery helped cause the Civil War, it must show how the political system, the economy, and the moral conflict interacted.
- **The counterfactual.** Counterfactuals are disciplined questions, not fiction. The book asks what alternatives were plausible for the actors, given their information and constraints. It does not invent a world in which everything else stays the same.
- **The limit.** Contingency is not randomness. The book shows that outcomes were made, not that they were arbitrary.

### 7.4 Scale and connection

- **The claim.** The book moves between local, national, and global scales. The through-lines connect the scales without erasing their differences.
- **The local.** A chapter may begin with a single place — a courthouse, a factory, a reservation, a kitchen — and then widen. The local scene grounds the argument in evidence.
- **The national.** The book follows the state’s formation, law, and politics, but it does not assume that the state is the only actor.
- **The global.** The book follows empires, markets, migrations, and wars across borders. It does not treat the world as scenery for American decisions.
- **The overlap.** Chapters overlap intentionally. When a later chapter needs an earlier explanation, it uses a cross-reference, not a repetition. The Introduction should say this so the reader does not expect every chapter to be self-contained.

### 7.5 Writing to be read aloud

- **The claim.** The book is written to be read aloud. The prose should have a cadence, a clear main idea per sentence, and a human voice.
- **The rules.** Keep most sentences under 25 words. Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs. Cut “it is important to note,” “arguably,” “of course,” “in many ways,” and “the fact that.” Define technical terms at first use.
- **The test.** Read a representative page aloud, or use text-to-speech. Check for stacked clauses, repeated rhythm, and places where the reader would stumble. Do not claim a listening check that was not performed.
- **The limit.** Reading aloud can encourage overstatement and false intimacy. The book should sound like a person thinking, not a performer seeking applause.

## 8. Names, language, and dates

### 8.1 Names and groups

- **Native American and Indigenous.** Capitalize both. Use the specific nation and its preferred name where known: Haudenosaunee, Wampanoag, Cherokee, Diné. Use “Indian” only in quotation or historical proper names such as the Indian Removal Act, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and Indian Territory. Explain alternate names where needed.
- **Black and White.** Capitalize both when referring to people. Prefer “enslaved people” in the book’s prose; retain historical proper names and exact quotations. Use “enslaver” where it is accurate, and explain the choice.
- **Latino, Latina, Latinx, Hispanic.** Use “Latino” as the general noun and adjective, “Latina” for a woman, and “Hispanic” only when citing census categories or a person’s self-identification. Use “Latinx” only when a source or a person uses it. The book should state this convention once and then follow it.
- **Immigrant, refugee, migrant.** Use “immigrant” for a person who moves to a country, “refugee” for a person who meets the legal definition or is described by a source as a refugee, and “migrant” for a person who moves without specifying status. Use “undocumented” rather than “illegal”; “illegal” appears only in quotation.
- **Gender and sexuality.** Use the terms people use for themselves where known. Explain terms at first use. Do not infer identity from behavior or from a source’s category.
- **The rule.** A name is part of the evidence. Do not modernize a name silently; explain the change in a note.

### 8.2 “America” and “the United States”

- **The claim.** “America” can mean the hemisphere or the United States. The book uses “the United States” for the state and “the Americas” for the hemisphere. “American” is the adjective, with the context made clear.
- **The title.** “A Nation in Question” uses “nation” as a claim, not a neutral description. The book asks what kind of nation the United States is, and for whom.
- **The limit.** The book does not resolve the naming problem by fiat. It explains the choice and uses it consistently.

### 8.3 Dates and numbers

- **Dates.** Use American date order: July 4, 1776. Give full years before 1800 and at first mention; a standalone year is never abbreviated. Write “the 1850s.” Use CE and BCE for the deep past. Explain “years before present” and its reference year when used in scientific dating; radiocarbon dates conventionally use 1950 CE.
- **Ranges.** Use en dashes for ranges and coordinated compounds: 1861–65, Chs. 5–13, North–South. Never use a hyphen between numbers to mean a range.
- **Numbers.** Spell out one through nine in prose; use figures for 10 and above. Exceptions include proper names, dates, statistics, populations, money, and item numbers. Tables, data, and appendices use figures.
- **Statistics.** State the source, observation year or period, units, population or denominator, and series or table locator. State rounding and calculations. Inflation-adjusted money names the price index and base year.
- **The limit.** A date can be precise without being certain. The book uses “c.” and ranges where the evidence is uncertain.

### 8.4 Quotations, titles, and slurs

- **Quotations.** Quote exactly from the consulted edition, checking spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and omissions character by character. Use curly quotation marks and apostrophes. Use square brackets for insertions and spaced ellipsis for omissions. If modernizing or translating, say so and identify the version.
- **Titles.** Italicize books, journals, newspapers, ships, films, and court cases: *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*, *The New York Times*, USS *Arizona*, *Brown v. Board of Education*. Put articles, essays, speeches, sermons, chapters, and titled short documents in quotation marks: “A Model of Christian Charity,” “I Have a Dream,” “Letter from Birmingham Jail.” Acts, treaties, amendments, and official documents are capitalized and roman: the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, the Fourteenth Amendment, the Emancipation Proclamation.
- **Slurs and degrading language.** Slurs appear only in quotation, only when necessary to the historical argument, and with an explanation in the same paragraph of why they are reproduced. Do not use a slur as a section title or a marketing hook.
- **The limit.** Quotation is not endorsement. The book can quote a document’s cruelty without adopting its voice.

## 9. Reader’s guide

### 9.1 How a chapter works

- **The claim.** Each chapter has a repeatable architecture: a documented scene, a framing paragraph, a roadmap, numbered sections, an In Focus box, a Debate, Legacies, a Summary, Study Questions, Further Reading, a closing note, and a bridge. The Introduction is the exception: no epigraphs, no In Focus box, no formal bridge.
- **The route.** The reader can read in order or follow a through-line. The chapters are designed to stand alone, but they assume the previous chapter’s argument. Cross-references point to the section that supplies the missing context.
- **The apparatus.** The notes are part of the argument. The appendices are reference material: A, Documents; B, Tables and Data; C, Maps and Gazetteer; D, Chronology; E, Historiographical Essay and Further Reading.
- **The invitation.** The book is written to be argued with. The Study Questions are prompts for analysis, not quizzes. The reader should test the claims against the evidence.

### 9.2 How to use the notes and appendices

- **The notes.** Use the named footnotes to follow a claim to its source. The first citation of a work gives the full details; later citations give the author, short title, and new locator. The notes also carry qualifications and editorial checks.
- **The appendices.** Appendix A collects documents; B collects tables and data; C catalogs maps and places; D gives the chronology; E surveys the historiography and recommends further reading. The appendices use the same evidence and typography rules as the chapters.
- **The limit.** The notes are not a bibliography of everything the author read. They are the evidence for the claims in this book. Further Reading is a selective guide, not a citation ledger.

### 9.3 The route to Chapter 1

- **The hand-off.** Chapter 1 begins at Fajada Butte, where people marked the sun long before there was a United States. The Introduction’s Monticello scene is a recent frame, not the chronological start. The book moves from the deep past to the present, and the reader should feel the distance.
- **The final sentence.** “The next chapter begins not in 1776 but at Fajada Butte, where a dagger of sunlight once crossed spiral petroglyphs long before anyone called this continent the United States.” This is a hand-off, not a formal bridge.[^intro-fajada] It should be checked against Chapter 1’s actual opening before drafting.[^intro-check-chapter1-opening]
- **The promise.** The Introduction does not answer its question. It gives the reader the terms, the evidence, and the stakes, and then sends them into the history.

## Closing note

The tent on the West Lawn was temporary. The 74 new citizens have their certificates. Monticello still stands above the Virginia piedmont, with its slave quarters, its kitchen, its nail factory, and its carefully written silences. The man who wrote “all men are created equal” enslaved more than 610 people, and the naturalization law he signed in 1802 began with the words “free white person.” The country that naturalized 74 people that morning was the same country whose Supreme Court, four days earlier, had struck down an attempt to end birthright citizenship.

The book’s wager is that these facts belong together. The United States was not a promise and it was not a fraud. It was a series of settlements that changed the terms of conflict without settling them permanently. The settlements were real: constitutions, treaties, emancipation, the New Deal, the rights revolution. They were also incomplete: land still taken, labor still coerced, belonging still policed. The nation is in question because the question was never closed.
