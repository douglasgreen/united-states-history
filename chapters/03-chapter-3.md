# Chapter 3. Native North America in the Age of Contact (1500–1620)

> “If your king have sent me presents, I also am a king, and this my land … Your father is to come to me, not I to him, nor yet to your fort, neither will I bite at such a bait.”
>
> — Wahunsenacawh (Powhatan), as reported by John Smith, in *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New-England, and the Summer Isles* (1624), quoted in Encyclopedia Virginia, “Powhatan (d. 1618)”[^ch03-powhatan]

> “Neither of us will try to steer each other’s vessel as we travel side by side down the river of life.”
>
> — the Haudenosaunee account of the Two Row Wampum, as told by the Onondaga Nation, “Two Row Wampum – Gaswéñdah”[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

> “The middle ground is thus a process of mutual and creative misunderstanding.”
>
> — Richard White, “Creative Misunderstandings and New Understandings,” *William and Mary Quarterly* 63, no. 1 (2006), p. 10[^ch03-white-creative]

Powhatan speaks as a king in his own land. The Haudenosaunee speak as one people addressing another, equal, neither steering the other’s vessel. White speaks of strangers building a workable world out of creative misreading. The three disagree about what contact was.

The belt is a strip of white shell beads. Two purple rows run its length, parallel and equal. In the Haudenosaunee account, one row is a canoe carrying the Haudenosaunee ways — language, songs, dances, government, ceremonies — and the other is a ship carrying the newcomers’ ways. The rows have the same weight. They run off the end of the belt, because the agreement is to last as long as the water flows downhill and the grasses grow green. The Haudenosaunee call it Gaswéñdah, or Kaswentha, a living treaty.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

The account holds three principles: friendship, peace, and forever. It also holds a refusal. When the newcomers proposed that the Haudenosaunee call them “Father” and themselves “Son,” the Haudenosaunee declined, because a father may command a son. They agreed to address one another as “Brothers,” equals. The Dutch would record the agreement with pen and paper; the Haudenosaunee would make a belt for future generations, and the belt would be the memory.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

The tradition places the agreement in 1613, when Dutch traders worked the river the Haudenosaunee called the River that Flows Both Ways. The paper is another matter. The written “treaty” offered to prove the agreement, the so-called Tawagonshi document, surfaced in 1968. In 1987, Charles Gehring, William Starna, and William Fenton judged it a fabrication; later linguists found more than 40 anachronisms and dated its writing to the twentieth century. It named the Mohawk, but the people on the lower Hudson when the Dutch arrived were the Mahican.[^ch03-tawagonshi]

The tradition is real. The paper is not. No 1613 instrument survives to confirm the date; the evidence is the belt, centuries of Haudenosaunee teaching, and the later record of the Covenant Chain. The belt carries a claim that two peoples could travel side by side without one governing the other. How did Indigenous nations interpret and manage newcomers?[^ch03-check-scene]

This chapter follows the Indigenous political worlds of North America from about 1500 to 1620, with its center in the eastern woodlands, the Great Lakes, the Southwest, and the southern Plains, and with Spanish Florida, New France, and the Dutch Hudson valley as narrow footholds. It uses archaeology, wampum and oral tradition, Native nation histories, and Spanish, French, Dutch, and English records. It treats “contact,” “middle ground,” and “conquest” as analytical terms, states the Haudenosaunee founding as a contested range, and does not present Native societies only through newcomers’ eyes.[^ch03-check-names][^ch03-check-dating][^ch03-check-epidemic]

Chapter 2 ended with the Atlantic world in 1551 and a bridge to this belt. This chapter takes up the Indigenous political worlds the newcomers did not erase.[^ch03-check-ch2]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 sets out Tsenacomoco and Powhatan’s paramount chiefdom. §2 turns to Wampanoag country, the epidemic, and Ousamequin’s alliance with Plymouth. §3 examines the Great Law of Peace, the Grand Council, the contested founding date, and the Two Row Wampum. §4 compares the Wendat, Caddo, Pueblo, and Plains political worlds. §5 reads European representations and the Spanish, French, and Dutch footholds. §6 weighs the debate over Indigenous agency, oral tradition, and the limits of “contact” and the middle ground. The chapter begins with a belt, not a landing.

## 1. Tsenacomoco

### 1.1 A paramount chiefdom, not a tribe

Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom, a political alliance of Algonquian-speaking peoples in Tidewater Virginia. It was not a single tribe, and it was not a centralized state. Its ruler, the mamanatowick, held real authority, but councils and the consent of the constituent chiefdoms checked him.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

The origins of that order are not fully known. The historian James D. Rice argues that before about 1300 the region’s peoples were largely hunters and gatherers with little central authority; by 1607 they had organized into chiefdoms and a paramount chiefdom. Population growth, agriculture, the Little Ice Age, competition for good town sites, and increased warfare and trade were interdependent forces. The Little Ice Age made good land scarcer and pushed displaced peoples such as the Susquehannocks into raids.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

Powhatan inherited the core six groups — the Powhatans, Youghtanunds, Mattaponis, Pamunkeys, Arrohatecks, and Appamattucks — and expanded by diplomacy and force until roughly 28–32 lesser chiefdoms and tribes paid him tribute by 1607. Each chiefdom had its own weroance, or chief, and the chiefs sat on a council, or cockarouse, that advised the paramount chief. Priests, or kwiocosuk, also advised the chiefs.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

Tsenacomoco ran from the south bank of the James River north to the south bank of the Potomac, bounded west by the fall line and east by the Chesapeake Bay. The boundaries were not fixed; they reflected the reach of the paramount chief’s power at any moment. The Eastern Shore and the Patawomecks were not always reliably under his control. Still, the English largely co-opted the boundaries as the early, unofficial borders of Virginia.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

The people lived by farming, fishing, and hunting. Women tended corn and beans and gathered wild plants; men hunted and fished. The English saw unfenced, part-cleared land as “unimproved” and therefore open to seizure. That was a fatal misreading of a managed landscape: the people cleared more land than they farmed, and the remaining ground produced wild grains, greens, and medicines.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

The name itself shows how much depends on translation. William Strachey reported that Powhatan’s people called their land “Tsenacommacah.” Scholars have translated it as “densely inhabited place,” “a nearby dwelling-place,” or “our place.” Each translation carries a different assumption about what the word describes.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

### 1.2 Powhatan, the mamanatowick

Wahunsenacawh, whom the English called Powhatan, ruled as mamanatowick and took the lead in diplomacy with outsiders. His power was real, but it was not absolute, and he had no standing army. He was probably born around 1550 and inherited his position through his mother’s line, as the son of a sister of a Powhatan chief. The title meant “powerful spirits” or “powerful shaman,” suggesting a priestly as well as a political role.[^ch03-powhatan]

Powhatan moved populations, installed sympathetic chiefs, and married the sisters of subchiefs, making his sons potential heirs. But the ties binding tribes to his authority were stronger at the center than at the edges, and his standing rose and fell with his people’s welfare. In good times they paid him tribute, mostly corn and beans, which went into storehouses for feasts, trade, ritual, and relief. By one account he received as much as 80 percent of what his people produced, though he did not accept all of it. In famine or defeat, his legitimacy declined.[^ch03-powhatan][^ch03-tsenacomoco]

### 1.3 Managing the English

On April 26, 1607, three ships entered the Chesapeake with 144 men and boys. A landing party at Point Comfort was attacked at dusk, the first hostile contact between the English and the people of Tsenacomoco. By June 1607, 104 men and boys lived at Jamestown. By the end of the winter, all but 38 were dead.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

The colony arrived in the second year of a drought that lasted until 1612. Garden yields fell, the oaks produced fewer acorns, and the deer declined. The English pressed the land with ceaseless demands for food. Powhatan forbade English expansion up the James, probably to prevent an alliance with his Monacan enemies.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

When Opechancanough captured John Smith in December 1607 and brought him to Powhatan, the chief put him through what was probably an “adoption” ceremony to make him a subject weroance. Smith later wrote that Pocahontas saved his life; most historians conclude that the rescue probably did not happen as described, and that the episode was a ritual of mock execution and adoption.[^ch03-powhatan]

In 1608 the English tried to bind Powhatan to the crown. He refused to come to Jamestown. “If your king have sent me presents, I also am a king, and this my land,” he said, or at least so Smith reported years later. The English came to him instead, leaned on his shoulders to bend his knees, and fitted a crown on his head. To the English he was a subject who would not behave as one. To Powhatan, Smith was a weroance acting like a paramount chief.[^ch03-powhatan]

Relations collapsed into the First Anglo-Powhatan War of 1609–14. In April 1613 Samuel Argall kidnapped Pocahontas; her conversion and marriage to John Rolfe in 1614 ended the war. Powhatan died in April 1618, and his successors inherited a weakening polity as the English made their own alliances inside and outside it.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-pocahontas]

### 1.4 What Tsenacomoco was not

Tsenacomoco was neither a tribe nor an empire. Calling it a “confederacy,” an “empire,” or a “nation” each imports a different misleading model. It contained the Chickahominies, who lived in its heart yet were independent of Powhatan’s rule, and the Patawomecks were not reliably under his control. Powhatan had no standing army or absolute power, though some anthropologists have used the word “empire” for the reach of his violence and population moves.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-powhatan]

The population is another contested number. Thomas Jefferson estimated “about 8000 inhabitants” in 1784; Helen Rountree suggested about 15,000 in roughly 6,000 square miles. Other estimates run from 13,000 to just over 22,000, and Rice puts the whole Chesapeake region at more than 30,000 in 1607. These are estimates built on different assumptions, not a census.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-check-population]

The chapter uses the name Tsenacomoco while explaining that its boundaries and numbers were contested. If the Chesapeake was one political order, New England was another. §2 turns to the Wampanoag, whose world was remade by epidemic and who chose an alliance with the English.[^ch03-check-population]

## 2. Wampanoag country, epidemics, and alliance

### 2.1 The Wampanoag world

The Wampanoag were a confederacy of communities and sachems in present-day southeastern Massachusetts and eastern Rhode Island. Their world was organized by kinship, territory, and the authority of sachems, and it was already entangled with European fishermen, traders, and kidnappers before 1620. They lived around Narragansett and Massachusetts bays and the islands, in villages such as Patuxet, later the site of Plymouth.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-great-dying]

In 1614 Captain Thomas Hunt kidnapped a group of Wampanoags from Patuxet to sell into slavery, including Tisquantum, later called Squanto. The kidnapping turned Native wariness of Europeans into something harder and put a Wampanoag who had lived in Europe at the center of the next negotiation.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-pbs-plague]

The Wampanoag left few written records; most detail comes from William Bradford, Edward Winslow, and *Mourt’s Relation* (1622), which means Wampanoag motives are filtered through English purposes.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

### 2.2 The Great Dying, 1616–19

An epidemic swept the New England coast from about 1616 to 1619, killing a large share of the Wampanoag and other coastal peoples. Its cause is not securely identified. It must not be called a smallpox winter without qualification.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-check-epidemic]

Estimates of Wampanoag losses run from at least 75 percent to as much as 90 percent. The village of Patuxet, home to about 2,000 people, lost nearly everyone. Only one survivor is known by name, Tisquantum. The numbers are estimates built from English reports and later reconstruction, not a count made at the time.[^ch03-great-dying]

Candidates have included smallpox, measles, yellow fever, meningitis, bubonic plague, and, in a 2010 study, leptospirosis. The historian David J. Silverman argues that malignant confluent smallpox is most likely, but no diagnosis is settled. A disease that kills quickly can leave no diagnostic trace; the question is a medical inference from scattered descriptions.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

The disease ran down the Maine coast into Massachusetts and struck some communities harder than others. The Narragansett, west of Narragansett Bay, largely escaped, which shifted the regional balance of power. A confederacy that had lost most of its people now faced a neighbor that had lost far fewer.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

Despite the catastrophe, the region’s Native communities were not destroyed. They survived, reorganized, and continued, and the chapter should resist the word “extinction.”[^ch03-great-dying]

The silence is part of the evidence. The epidemic’s toll is known mainly through English accounts and later estimates, and some English readers took the empty houses and untended fields as divine favor.[^ch03-pbs-plague][^ch03-check-epidemic]

### 2.3 Ousamequin’s choice

Ousamequin, called Massasoit by the English, allied with Plymouth in 1621 as a strategic choice under pressure, not from simple goodwill or simple desperation. The epidemic had weakened the Wampanoag while the Narragansett pressed them for tribute. Payment might have bought protection, but it also risked subordination. Ousamequin concluded that an alliance with the English could protect his people and preserve his own authority.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

In March 1621, about four months after the Mayflower’s arrival, the English and Wampanoag opened contact. Samoset, an Abenaki who knew some English, came first. Ousamequin arrived about a week later. Negotiations used Tisquantum as interpreter. The Wampanoag did not stumble into the English camp; they sent a messenger, tested the ground, and then came in person.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

The terms are preserved in *Mourt’s Relation*. The English wrote that Ousamequin “has a potent adversary in the Narragansetts, that are at war with him, against whom he thinks we may be of some strength to him, for our pieces [guns] are terrible to them.” The treaty bound each side to keep peace and to defend the other against attacks by other Indigenous groups. It was a military alliance with a trade relationship inside it.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

Even some Wampanoags disagreed bitterly with Ousamequin’s decision. That dissent shows the alliance was a contested political choice, not a consensus; Ousamequin was making a bet, and some of his people thought it was wrong.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

The Wampanoag gained a temporary ally and the English gained survival skills. The alliance also opened the way for English expansion that would eventually overwhelm the Wampanoag. It was neither a sentimental friendship nor a simple trap.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

### 2.4 The alliance and its afterlives

The 1621 treaty held until after Ousamequin’s death in 1661. It is often described as the only treaty between Native Americans and English colonists honored throughout the lives of all who signed it. Its collapse belonged to a later generation. Ousamequin’s son Wamsutta died in 1662 during land negotiations. Wamsutta’s brother Metacom, called King Philip by the English, claimed Wamsutta had been poisoned, and the escalating conflict became King Philip’s War of 1675–76.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

King Philip’s War belongs to Chapter 6. This chapter ends its Wampanoag story at the alliance and the peace, naming the later war as the outcome the treaty postponed rather than prevented.[^ch03-check-ch4]

Later generations reimagined the alliance as a Thanksgiving of harmony. The feast of 1621 was a brief, contingent peace built on a hard political calculation, not the origin of a permanent friendship; it postponed the deeper questions of land, sovereignty, and power.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

South and west of the Wampanoag, in present-day New York, another confederacy had built a more formal and longer-lasting union. §3 turns to the Haudenosaunee and the Great Law of Peace.

## 3. The Great Law

### 3.1 The Great Law of Peace

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was founded on the Great Law of Peace, a body of law and ceremony attributed to the Peacemaker, his helper Hiawatha (Aionwatha), and the Mother of Nations, Jigonhsasee. It organized five nations into one house with a common council and a common law. The founding story is the constitutional memory of a working government.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt]

The Peacemaker is said to have uprooted the tallest white pine, the Tree of Peace, and buried the weapons of war beneath it. He converted 50 “evil thinking men” into the Hoyane, or chiefs, and gave clan mothers the power to install and remove them. The story explains where authority comes from, who holds it, and how it can be taken away.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt]

The five nations are pictured as one longhouse. The Mohawk and Seneca are the eastern and western door-keepers. The Onondaga are the Firekeepers who tend the council fire at the center. The Oneida and Cayuga stand between them. Each nation keeps its own council; the Grand Council decides matters affecting all. The 50 chiefs are distributed among the nations — nine Mohawk, nine Oneida, 14 Onondaga, ten Cayuga, and eight Seneca — and the clan mothers hold the authority to replace and remove them. This distribution of power, and the balance of Elder and Younger Brothers, is the confederacy’s constitutional form.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]

The Smithsonian’s educator guide describes the Peacemaker story as “the civic and social code of ethics” that governs how Haudenosaunee people treat one another and outsiders and how they run their traditional government. It is a living constitution, not only an origin myth.[^ch03-nmai-guide]

**In Focus.** The Hiawatha Belt is the national belt of the Haudenosaunee and the visual constitution of their confederacy. Five symbols joined together record the moment when five warring nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca — buried their weapons and agreed to live under one law: a single longhouse in which each nation keeps its own council fire but all govern by “one common law, one heart, and one mind.” Read from east to west, the belt names the Mohawk council fire first, then the Oneida, then the Onondaga as the Tree of Peace at the center, then the Cayuga, then the Seneca as the western door-keeper. Image 1 shows the five symbols joined by a single line. The 50 chiefs, the clan mothers’ power to raise and remove them, and the Onondaga duty to tend the council fire are encoded in the belt’s form. The Haudenosaunee say the belt is “extremely old,” created at the beginning of the confederacy and held long before Europeans came; its material date is not established. It is a record of an internal peace, the founding of a government, not a treaty with outsiders. Any reproduction must be authorized and credited in consultation with the Haudenosaunee stewards of the object.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-check-image]

### 3.2 The confederacy at work

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was a participatory government that balanced national autonomy with collective decision-making. Each nation maintained its own council, with chiefs chosen by the clan mothers, and handled its own internal affairs. The Grand Council dealt with matters affecting all, and the Onondaga kept the fire — the council that “never dies.” The main meeting place was, and remains, on Onondaga territory.[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]

Haudenosaunee leaders later urged the English colonies to unite on the confederacy’s example. Canasatego, an Onondaga chief, did so at Lancaster in 1744, and Benjamin Franklin invoked the confederacy in 1751 and in the 1754 Albany Plan of Union. Those borrowings belong to Chapters 8 and 12. They do not prove that the founders copied the Great Law; they prove that the confederacy was visible and admired.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline]

The Haudenosaunee insist the confederacy existed “from time immemorial” and predated European arrival, making it, in their words, “one of the first and longest lasting participatory democracies in the world.” That is a claim of sovereignty and memory, not a neutral chronicle.[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]

### 3.3 Dating the founding

The founding date of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy is contested. Haudenosaunee tradition places it in time immemorial or well before European contact. Scholarly estimates cluster around either 1142 or roughly 1450–1550, and the archaeology of tribal consolidation and the oral tradition do not agree.[^ch03-check-dating]

The tradition says the exact date of the joining of the nations is unknown and “said to be time immemorial.” The Hiawatha Belt was made “at the beginning of our confederacy of peace,” long before the French, Dutch, and English came. For the Haudenosaunee, the confederacy is older than the colonial record and does not need that record’s permission to exist.[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-hiawatha-belt]

The Onondaga Nation’s timeline states that 1142 is the earliest date scholars place on the confederacy’s formation, while the Haudenosaunee believe it was formed much earlier. The Library of Congress repeats the 1142 date. That date appears to derive from an eclipse-based argument, and it is not accepted by all archaeologists.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline][^ch03-loc-1142]

Iroquoian archaeology has generally indicated that the confederacy consolidated between about 1450 and 1550. That later range and the 1142 date cannot both be a single settled fact. One may be right and the other wrong, or they may be answering different questions about different kinds of consolidation. A 1142 founding gives the Haudenosaunee a deep pre-contact constitution; a later founding ties the union to the crisis of the contact era. The chapter reports the positions and prints no single year as fact.[^ch03-check-dating]

### 3.4 The Two Row Wampum and the Covenant Chain

The Two Row Wampum encoded the Haudenosaunee principle that two peoples should travel side by side without one governing the other. Its 1613 date is traditional. The written document offered to prove it is a forgery. The durable relationship is documented later as the Covenant Chain. Those four statements must be kept distinct.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-tawagonshi]

One purple row is the Haudenosaunee canoe, the other the European ship, equal in weight, with friendship, peace, and “forever” as its three principles. The two rows run off the belt’s end to mark that the agreement lasts as long as the world. The belt depicts two vessels traveling in the same direction under their own laws, not one vessel leading the other.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

The forgery is equally clear. The Tawagonshi document, “discovered” in 1968, was judged a fabrication by Gehring, Starna, and Fenton in 1987 and shown by later linguists to contain more than 40 anachronisms. It named the Mohawk, not the Mahican who lived on the lower Hudson.[^ch03-tawagonshi]

The later record is thinner but real. The first known written non-Native record of the Silver Covenant Chain dates from Albany negotiations in 1677, more than sixty years after the claimed Two Row agreement. The Dutch named the relationship with three silver chains for peace, friendship, and forever.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline][^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

The Haudenosaunee still cite the Two Row Wampum as binding. In 1924 they refused the U.S. Citizenship Act on the ground that one government cannot impose laws on the other government and its people.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline] The chapter keeps the tradition, the belt, the 1613 date, and the forgery distinct.[^ch03-check-twowrow]

## 4. Wendat, Caddo, Pueblo, and Plains political worlds

### 4.1 Wendake: the Wendat confederacy

The Wendat, called Huron by the French, were a confederacy of four Iroquoian-speaking nations in Wendake, between Georgian Bay and Lake Simcoe, with a fifth affiliated group that may not have held full political membership. Their government was not centralized; most politics happened at the local and clan level, with collective decision-making among the nations.[^ch03-wendat]

The confederacy began when the Cord nation allied with the Bear nation around 1400. The Deer joined in 1570 and the Rock in 1610. A fifth group, the People of the Marsh, may have joined later without full political membership. The Wendat were matrilineal, and women had substantial influence over who became headmen.[^ch03-wendat]

Wendat society was built on maize agriculture and palisaded villages of longhouses; at its peak the confederacy held about 30,000 people. A series of epidemics in the early 1600s killed somewhere between half and two-thirds of the Wendat and devastated clan leadership. As the pool of potential headmen shrank, women lost influence, and war leaders took on more civil authority. By 1650 about 12,000 Wendat remained.[^ch03-wendat]

In 1648–49 the Haudenosaunee attacked the Wendat, and in 1650 the confederacy chose to disperse. The dispersal belongs to Chapter 6. Here the Wendat appear as one of the great powers of the Great Lakes before the hammer fell.[^ch03-wendat][^ch03-check-ch4]

### 4.2 The Caddo confederacies

South and west, the Caddo were not one tribe but a set of at least 25 closely affiliated groups around the Red River, in present-day Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana, and Oklahoma. Europeans described the Hasinai, Kadohadacho, and Natchitoches confederacies, though these were better understood as kin-based affiliated groups than as states.[^ch03-caddo]

By about 900 CE the Caddo were a ranked, mound-building, maize-farming society with civic-ceremonial centers, elaborate mortuary ritual, and far-reaching trade in bison hides, salt, bois d’arc bows, copper, turquoise, and marine shell.[^ch03-caddo]

Descent ran through the maternal line, and authority rested in a hierarchy. The xinesi mediated with the supreme god Caddi Ayo. The caddi, the principal headman, made political decisions and led councils. The canahas were subordinate headmen and elders. Leaders sought consensus and led by generosity rather than coercion, creating loyalty by redistributing wealth and food.[^ch03-caddo-structure][^ch03-caddo]

The first European description of the Caddo came in 1542 from diarists with the De Soto expedition, then under Luis de Moscoso Alvarado, who described dense populations and abundant corn. The expedition was a violent intrusion, not a census.[^ch03-caddo]

European missions, ranches, and trading posts grew through the eighteenth century, and epidemics between 1691 and 1816 sharply reduced Caddo populations before removal. That later history lies beyond 1620 but names the direction of change.[^ch03-caddo]

### 4.3 The Pueblo world

The Pueblo peoples of the Southwest lived in independent, ancient towns and held authority through hereditary, theocratic leadership rather than a single supreme chief. The towns collectively became known as “Pueblos” only through the Spanish encounter. The word is a Spanish category, not a nation’s name for itself.[^ch03-pueblo]

Acoma, Zuni, and Hopi, among others, are described by anthropologists as hereditary oligarchies in which a core group of related individuals dominated leadership by controlling access to office. Their government was town-based and religious; a Pueblo town needed a council, a ceremonial order, and a way of holding land and labor together, not a paramount chief.[^ch03-pueblo]

Juan de Oñate invaded in 1598 and established Spanish rule by terror. When Acoma resisted, Oñate ordered that one leg be chopped from every man over 15 and the rest of the population be enslaved, setting a pattern that lasted 80 years.[^ch03-pueblo]

Pueblo people endured what the historian Ramón Gutiérrez called an imposed theocratic utopia, backed by armed force and the whip. Catholic missionaries set out to destroy the ancestral Pueblo world in every respect, including what people could believe and how they could marry, work, live, and pray.[^ch03-pueblo]

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 and the Spanish reconquest belong to Chapter 6. This chapter establishes the Pueblo towns and their subjection, not the revolt.[^ch03-pueblo][^ch03-check-ch4]

### 4.4 Plains societies

The peoples of the Great Plains were as culturally varied as any population in North America. There was no single Plains way of life, and the horse — later the stereotype’s defining feature — was a late and transformative addition, not the original condition. The mounted warrior of the nineteenth century was not the Plains society of 1600.[^ch03-plains-nps]

More than 30 separate tribes, each with its own language, religion, and customs, lived between the Mississippi and the Great Basin. Some were mobile bison hunters. Some were settled agriculturalists in river valleys who also hunted bison. Many raided for horses and goods.[^ch03-plains-nps]

People lived on the Plains for about 13,000 years as hunter-gatherers following bison. About 700–900 CE, maize cultivation spread from Mesoamerica, and communities became semi-nomadic, combining permanent agricultural settlements with hunting.[^ch03-plains]

Before the horse, few peoples settled or traveled far outside the major river valleys, because of distance and the difficulty of hunting bison on foot. Mobility and the mounted warrior came with Spanish horses in the sixteenth century and spread after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. This chapter marks the Plains as distinct societies on the eve of that transformation.[^ch03-plains-nps][^ch03-plains]

The Plains complete the chapter’s demonstration that Native North America held many political orders, each adapted to its country. Map 3 places these political worlds side by side, and it shows how many different ways of organizing power the newcomers encountered in one continent.[^ch03-check-map]

## 5. European representations and Spanish, French, and Dutch footholds

### 5.1 Reading European representations

The richest European descriptions of Native North America in this period were produced by people with something to gain. They are indispensable evidence and unreliable guides. A record can be true in its details and false in its frame.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-pocahontas]

The source problem is general. The Wampanoag left few written records, and most of what survives about the 1621 treaty comes from Bradford and Winslow. Tsenacomoco is known largely through John Smith and William Strachey; the Caddo are known first through Spanish diarists. In each case, the Native side is mediated by people trying to justify a claim, sell a voyage, or defend a colony.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-pocahontas][^ch03-caddo]

John Smith and Pocahontas are the clearest case. In his 1624 *Generall Historie*, Smith wrote that Pocahontas saved his life. In a letter written months after the event, he said only that he was feasted and interviewed. Smith wrote the rescue story during the Second Anglo-Powhatan War and gave his book treacherous Natives, a heroic Smith, and one “good” Indigenous figure. The legend grew not from her lifetime but from the 1820s.[^ch03-pocahontas]

Powhatan’s words reach us only through Smith’s later book, edited and translated. The chapter quotes them as a mediated record, not a transcript: evidence of how Smith wanted Powhatan to sound.[^ch03-powhatan]

### 5.2 Spanish Florida: St. Augustine, 1565

St. Augustine was founded on September 8, 1565, by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés. It became the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in what became the United States. But it began as a small enclave beside an existing Native town and depended on the Timucua and other Native peoples. The oldest city was not built on empty land.[^ch03-staugustine]

Menéndez sighted land on August 28, 1565, and named the site after Augustine of Hippo. He disembarked near the Timucua town of Seloy on September 7, and Father Francisco López said a mass as the Spanish came ashore.[^ch03-staugustine]

The Spanish settled beside, not instead of, the Timucua. The Timucua and their neighbors, and later the Guale and Apalachee, were the peoples of Spanish La Florida; the mission system tied them to Spanish rule.[^ch03-staugustine]

St. Augustine predates Jamestown by 42 years and Quebec by 43. The Spanish were not a side plot. They held the earliest durable European foothold in the future United States by relying on Native labor, food, and knowledge.[^ch03-staugustine]

### 5.3 French footholds: Quebec, 1608

Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec in 1608 as the first permanent French settlement in America. The colony survived only through alliances with the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat. The French foothold was a set of Native alliances as much as a town. Without those alliances, Quebec was a small fortified post on a river it did not control.[^ch03-champlain]

Champlain was a soldier, cartographer, navigator, author, and colonial administrator, and his maps and writings were instruments of persuasion as well as exploration. He forged alliances with the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat and joined them in campaigns against the Haudenosaunee. Those relationships proved essential to the colony’s survival and shaped the fur trade. The French needed partners and trade as much as the Wendat needed a counterweight.[^ch03-champlain][^ch03-wendat]

New France at this date was a narrow foothold held by Native consent. Its power was real but dependent, and its maps were built on Indigenous knowledge of the land.[^ch03-champlain]

### 5.4 Dutch footholds: the Hudson valley

In 1609 Henry Hudson, sailing for the Dutch East India Company, entered New York Harbor and traveled up the river that would bear his name, meeting Mahican communities along its banks. He had been sent to find a northern route to Asia; the river led nowhere, but he recorded the region’s resources, its harbor, and its beavers, whose fur was valuable in Europe.[^ch03-dutch-dpla]

The Mahican, an Algonquian-speaking people, lived in small villages along the river, moving inland in the fall and winter; perhaps 2,000–3,000 lived in the area by the early 1600s. The Mohawk, an Iroquoian-speaking Haudenosaunee nation, lived to the west and traded at the same forts.[^ch03-fort-orange]

The Dutch built a series of fur-trading posts. Fort Nassau was built in 1614 as a trading house near present-day Albany. Fort Orange became the first permanent Dutch settlement in the colony. The posts were factories and forts, not farming colonies, and they depended on Native hunters and traders.[^ch03-fort-orange][^ch03-dutch-dpla]

The Dutch-Haudenosaunee relationship is the setting of the Two Row Wampum tradition. The Dutch, like the French and Spanish, held their foothold by trade and Native tolerance, not by settlement alone. The newcomers’ maps and posts were thin lines on a Native landscape.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-dutch-dpla]

### 5.5 The balance by 1620

By 1620 European power in North America consisted of small, dependent footholds — St. Augustine, Quebec, Fort Orange — while Native nations still held the land, the trade, and the military advantage in most places. The Spanish held Florida, the French a post on the St. Lawrence, the Dutch fur-trading forts on the Hudson. The English had attempted Roanoke and held Jamestown and would soon add Plymouth.[^ch03-champlain][^ch03-fort-orange][^ch03-staugustine]

In the Chesapeake, Powhatan’s people vastly outnumbered the English for at least a decade and could have destroyed Jamestown. In New England, the Wampanoag and other nations retained their political organization even after the epidemic. The newcomers had ships, guns, and trade goods, but not the numbers or local knowledge to dictate terms everywhere.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-great-dying]

The footholds were fragile and Native-dependent, but they were also the beachheads of later empires. Their growth, not their weakness, is the story Chapter 4 tells. The balance of 1620 was real, and it was temporary.[^ch03-check-ch4]

By 1620 the newcomers were few, but they had learned the routes, the trade, and the politics. §6 turns to the historical debate over how to explain what came next.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Native nations were political actors. Tsenacomoco, the Wampanoag, the Haudenosaunee, the Wendat, the Caddo, the Pueblos, and the Plains peoples all had governments, diplomacy, and strategies of their own. That sentence overturns a much older story in which Native people were part of the landscape.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-wendat]

Contact was plural. There was no single encounter, no single date, and no single kind of relationship. Each region produced its own pattern of trade, alliance, and conflict.[^ch03-check-names]

Diplomacy, not only disease or force, shaped contact. Ousamequin’s alliance, Powhatan’s management of the English, and the Two Row principle all show Native nations managing newcomers. Disease killed far more people than any army, but it did not do the political work by itself.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-powhatan][^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

The confederacy predated the Europeans. Whatever its exact date, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy and the Hiawatha Belt existed before the French, Dutch, and English arrived. The 1616–19 epidemic killed a large majority of the Wampanoag, whatever its cause. And the Tawagonshi document is a forgery; the 1987 finding is not seriously disputed.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-tawagonshi]

### 6.2 What is open

The confederacy’s founding date is open. The year 1142 and the later archaeological range of about 1450–1550 cannot both be settled fact. The oral tradition and the archaeology have not been reconciled.[^ch03-check-dating]

The Two Row tradition’s documentary basis is open. The tradition is real and long-lived, but no 1613 instrument survives, and the one offered document is a fabrication. The tradition can be asserted as a tradition; the date cannot be asserted as a documentary fact.[^ch03-tawagonshi]

The epidemic’s cause is open. Smallpox, measles, yellow fever, meningitis, leptospirosis, and other diseases have all been proposed. No diagnosis is settled. Silverman’s smallpox argument is a leading view, not a unanimous one.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]

John Smith and Pocahontas remain disputed. Whether Smith’s rescue story happened, and what Pocahontas’s role was, cannot be resolved from the surviving accounts. The 1624 version contradicts Smith’s earlier letter.[^ch03-pocahontas]

Population estimates remain open. Tsenacomoco’s population ranges from about 8,000 to more than 30,000 depending on the estimate and the area counted, and the Wampanoag losses are usually stated as percentages rather than counts.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-check-population]

The reach of the middle ground is open. Whether the middle ground as a process is nearly universal, or as a historical space was confined to the Great Lakes region, is a live disagreement.[^ch03-white-creative]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The choice is not between an Indigenous-centered story and a European one, nor between Native agency and Native catastrophe. The evidence supports a history in which Native nations acted, adapted, allied, and governed, while also suffering disease and dispossession. A history that keeps only the first half becomes heroic resistance without the dead; one that keeps only the second becomes victims without politics.[^ch03-check-sources]

“Contact” is a poor frame. One word cannot carry the Norse, the Spanish, the French, the Dutch, the English, the fishermen, and the traders, or the many Native peoples they met. “Encounter” is not much better; it suggests a meeting of equals and hides the violence.[^ch03-check-names]

Richard White defined the middle ground as “a process of mutual and creative misunderstanding,” built on a rough balance of power, mutual need, and an inability of either side to compel the other. He also warned that the process is common but the durable space it produced was not. He rejected treating Native peoples as “a people of tradition,” outside modernity.[^ch03-white-creative]

Applied to this chapter, the middle ground fits the Great Lakes and the French alliance well. It fits Tsenacomoco and the Wampanoag less well: Powhatan was trying to make the English clients, and Ousamequin was trying to use them against the Narragansett.[^ch03-white-creative]

Oral tradition and dating are different kinds of evidence. The tradition can establish what the Haudenosaunee hold and teach; the archaeology can establish a material chronology. Neither alone settles the founding.[^ch03-check-dating]

The best history gives the strongest version of the rival account and shows what the evidence can bear. Native nations managed newcomers with diplomacy, kinship, trade, and force; they were not passive, and their political worlds survived the first century of contact even as they were transformed.[^ch03-white-creative][^ch03-check-sources]

## Legacies

The Age of Contact did not end Native government. Tsenacomoco fell apart under English expansion, but its descendant communities live on the Mattaponi and Pamunkey reservations and in other Virginia tribes. The Wampanoag survived epidemic and war and remain in their homeland. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy still governs through its Grand Council, and the Hiawatha Belt remains its national belt. The Caddo Nation lives in Oklahoma. The Pueblo towns are among the oldest continuously inhabited communities in North America. The Wendat live on as the Wyandot and Huron-Wendat.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-wendat][^ch03-caddo][^ch03-pueblo][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-hiawatha-belt]

The Two Row Wampum and the Covenant Chain matter beyond the seventeenth century. The Haudenosaunee have continued to cite the Two Row principle against later impositions, and the belt’s teaching — two peoples, equal, neither steering the other — became a language of sovereignty that Native nations still use. The treaties of the contact era were not relics; they are the ground of present claims.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-onondaga-timeline]

The legacies are double, as in Chapter 2. The same century that produced diplomacy, confederacy, and alliance also produced epidemic, dispossession, and the footholds of empire. The chapter ends with the political worlds intact and under pressure, not with a verdict.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-check-ch4]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The Spanish, French, and Dutch footholds connected Native North America to imperial systems in Europe, the Caribbean, and Asia. The book’s fifty-state frame is a political geography, not a cultural one.[^ch03-champlain][^ch03-dutch-dpla]
- **Archives and silences.** The Wampanoag left few written records; Powhatan speaks through John Smith; the Caddo are known first through Spanish diarists.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-caddo]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 4 takes up English settlement; Chapter 6 owns King Philip’s War, the Pueblo Revolt, and the Wendat dispersal.[^ch03-check-ch4]

## Summary

- Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom of roughly 28–32 tributary groups, ruled by the mamanatowick Powhatan, whose power was real but checked by councils and consent.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-powhatan]
- Powhatan managed the Jamestown colonists as a potential subject people and useful source of goods, and misjudged their intransigence and numbers.[^ch03-powhatan]
- An epidemic from about 1616 to 1619 killed at least three-quarters and perhaps nine-tenths of the Wampanoag; its cause is not settled.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- Ousamequin allied with Plymouth in 1621 to protect the Wampanoag against the Narragansett and preserve his own authority; the treaty held until after his death in 1661.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was founded on the Great Law of Peace and organized five nations under a Grand Council, with clan mothers holding the power to install and remove chiefs.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]
- The confederacy’s founding date is contested between a deep 1142 tradition and a later archaeological range of about 1450–1550.[^ch03-check-dating]
- The Two Row Wampum encodes equal, side-by-side sovereignty; its 1613 date is traditional, and the written Tawagonshi document is a twentieth-century fabrication.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-tawagonshi]
- The Wendat, Caddo, Pueblo, and Plains peoples organized power through confederacy, kinship, theocratic towns, and varied regional economies, not a single model.[^ch03-wendat][^ch03-caddo][^ch03-pueblo][^ch03-plains]
- European representations of Native people were shaped by their authors’ purposes; Smith’s Pocahontas story grew into legend long after her death.[^ch03-pocahontas]
- By 1620 the Spanish, French, and Dutch held narrow, Native-dependent footholds, and Native nations still controlled most of the land, trade, and military advantage.[^ch03-staugustine][^ch03-champlain][^ch03-fort-orange]

## Further Reading

This is a selective guide, not a citation ledger. Unless the annotation says otherwise, the works below were consulted for this chapter and are cited in the combined notes. Richter, Merrell, Hämäläinen, White’s 1991 book, Snow, and the Mann–Fields argument were consulted through samples, reviews, or related articles rather than in full.

### Nation sources and primary voices

- **Onondaga Nation, “Two Row Wampum – Gaswéñdah” and “Hiawatha Belt.”** The Haudenosaunee account of the Two Row principle and the national belt. Read them as statements of a living tradition and of sovereignty, not as neutral chronicles.
- **Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, *Haudenosaunee Guide for Educators*.** A concise, community-informed account of the Peacemaker story and Haudenosaunee government. It is an educator’s guide, so it does not carry scholarly footnotes.
- **The Wyandot Nation of Kansas, “Wendat Confederacy.”** A nation source on Wendat origins, government, and dispersal. It supplies the community’s own framing of Wendat history.
- **John Smith, *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New-England, and the Summer Isles* (1624).** The source of the Pocahontas rescue story and of Powhatan’s words. Read it against Smith’s earlier letter and with attention to its wartime purpose.
- **Florida Historical Society, “St. Augustine founded on this date.”** A short institutional account of the September 8, 1565, founding and its Native context.

### Surveys and syntheses

- **Encyclopedia Virginia, “Tsenacomoco (Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom)” and “Powhatan (d. 1618).”** The clearest short syntheses of the Chesapeake polity, its institutions, and its paramount chief. They are encyclopedia entries, not full monographs.
- **Timothy K. Perttula, “Caddo Indians,” *Handbook of Texas Online*.** A detailed account of Caddo archaeology, society, and trade. It is a state historical association entry with a bibliography.
- **Richard White, “Creative Misunderstandings and New Understandings,” *William and Mary Quarterly* 63 (2006).** White’s own retrospective on the middle ground, its scope, and its limits. Indispensable for stating the position fairly.
- **Pekka Hämäläinen, *Lakota America* (2019) and *The Comanche Empire* (2008).** Accounts of Indigenous power and empire in the West. Their cases are later and regional, so they should not be generalized to the eastern woodlands.

### Specialized studies

- **Daniel K. Richter, *Facing East from Indian Country* (2001).** The central statement of Indigenous-centered early American history. It was consulted through reviews and publisher materials for this chapter; page locators must be checked before quotation.
- **James H. Merrell, *The Indians’ New World* (1989).** A classic study of Native adaptation in the colonial Southeast. It was not read in full for this chapter.
- **Bruce G. Trigger, *The Children of Aataentsic* (1976).** The standard history of the Huron to 1660. It is named as a research target, not yet consulted.
- **Dean R. Snow, *The Iroquois* (1994).** A leading archaeological account, including the confederacy’s chronology. Read alongside the Haudenosaunee tradition before fixing any date.

### The debate

- **Richard White, *The Middle Ground* (1991).** The book that named the concept. The 2006 article above is the best short guide to what White meant and did not mean.

> *A closing note.* The two records that anchor the chapter are a belt and a book. The Two Row Wampum survives as a living teaching about two peoples traveling side by side, yet the written “treaty” offered to date it is a twentieth-century forgery, and no 1613 instrument survives. Smith’s *Generall Historie* gives us Powhatan’s voice and Pocahontas’s rescue, but Smith wrote the rescue decades later, in wartime, and contradicts his own earlier letter. One record is a tradition without a paper; the other is a paper without a reliable memory. Together they show that the Age of Contact is documented unevenly and from the newcomers’ side, and that Native nations governed, negotiated, and remembered even where the archive is thin. The chapter ends with the political worlds intact and under pressure, not with a verdict.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-pocahontas]
