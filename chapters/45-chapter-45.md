## Epigraphs

> “My fellow Americans — on this day, with high hopes and brave hearts, in massive numbers, the American people have voted to make a new beginning.”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]

— Bill Clinton, address in Little Rock, Arkansas, accepting election as the 42nd president of the United States, November 3, 1992.[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]

> “If you’re paying $12, $13, $14 an hour for factory workers and you can move your factory South of the border, pay a dollar an hour for labor … there will be a giant sucking sound going south.”[^ch45-perot-sucking-sound]

— H. Ross Perot, presidential debate, 1992, on the North American Free Trade Agreement.[^ch45-perot-sucking-sound]

> “What we may be witnessing is not just the end of the Cold War, or the passing of a particular period of postwar history, but the end of history as such: that is, the end point of mankind’s ideological evolution and the universalization of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government.”[^ch45-fukuyama-1989]

— Francis Fukuyama, “The End of History?,” *The National Interest*, Summer 1989.[^ch45-fukuyama-1989]

- **Why these three.** Clinton named the election as a “new beginning” and promised a government that “offers a hand up not a hand out,” claiming that growth and opportunity could be shared without the older welfare state. Perot named the same economic integration as a transfer of American jobs to lower-wage Mexico. Fukuyama named the end of the Cold War as the ideological victory of liberal democracy, a claim the decade’s conflicts and distributional record would test. The three disagree about what the decade meant: opportunity, loss, or triumph.[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992][^ch45-perot-sucking-sound][^ch45-fukuyama-1989]

Clinton, 1992*, Book II. Perot’s words are quoted from a debate transcript reproduced on Wikipedia and must be collated against the Commission on Presidential Debates transcript of the October 15, 1992, debate; the commission’s transcript renders the phrase “job-sucking sound,” and the wording must be fixed from the primary transcript before quotation. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date and the occasion.** Clinton addressed supporters in Little Rock on the night of November 3, 1992, after the networks projected his election, thanking his family, his Arkansas supporters, and his campaign staff.[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]
- **The speech’s own framing.** Clinton said the people had “voted to make a new beginning” and called the result “a clarion call for our country to face the challenges of the end of the Cold War and the beginning of the next century, to restore growth to our country and opportunity to our people.”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]
- **The promise.** Clinton defined the result as “a victory for the people who are ready to compete and win in the global economy, but who need a government that offers a hand up not a hand out,” and he pledged to “offer those on welfare new opportunity and the challenge to move to work” and to “export American products, not American jobs.”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]
- **The outreach and the coalition language.** Clinton thanked President Bush for “a generous and forthcoming telephone call of real congratulations” and acknowledged Perot’s call to reform the political system, asking all voters “to join with us in creating a ‘Reunited States.’”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]
- **The closing.** Clinton said that if there is “no sense of community, the American dream will continue to wither,” and closed with the line that became the campaign’s emblem: “I still believe in a place called Hope.”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]
- **The context of the returns.** The American Presidency Project records 104,425,014 total votes: Clinton 44,909,326 (43.0 percent) and 370 electoral votes; George H. W. Bush 39,103,882 (37.4 percent) and 168 electoral votes; and Perot 19,741,657 (18.9 percent) and no electoral votes. Perot’s share was the highest for a candidate outside the two major parties since 1912.[^ch45-app-1992]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the place, the text, and the returns. It cannot show what the crowd felt, how individual voters decided, or that the speech caused any outcome. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992][^ch45-app-1992][^ch45-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** On the night of November 3, 1992, no one at the podium knew that the expansion would become the longest on record, that NAFTA would pass a year later, that the Republicans would take Congress in 1994, that Clinton would be impeached, or that the Supreme Court would decide the next election. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch45-check-scene][^ch45-check-dates-appD]

The speech is the period’s own most quoted statement of the bargain it proposed.[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992][^ch45-sotu-1996]
- **The stakes.** The tension between the promised shared growth and the results — a surging economy alongside widening inequality, trade integration alongside displaced workers, a smaller welfare state alongside persistent poverty — is the chapter’s central problem.[^ch45-check-scope][^ch45-cbo-distribution]

## 1. The boom and its distribution (1991–2000)

### 1.1 The claim: the longest expansion on record, and a widening divide

- **The claim.** The 1990s produced the longest peacetime expansion in American history, with low unemployment, low inflation, and a technology-led surge in productivity; the gains, however, were distributed unevenly, and the decade left a public debate organized around the difference between aggregate growth and median experience.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-census-income-1999]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the recession that began in July 1990 through the expansion, the digital and telecommunications economy, the distributional record, and the federal fiscal turn, with Map 36.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-telecom-act][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-check-map36]

### 1.2 The 1990–91 recession and the long expansion

- **The recession.** The recession that began in July 1990 ended in March 1991, and the recovery was slow at first; the unemployment rate peaked at 7.8 percent in June 1992, after the recession’s official end, which is one reason the economy was a central issue in 1992. The business-cycle dating and the peak must be taken from the National Bureau of Economic Research and the Bureau of Labor Statistics, not from journalism.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **The expansion.** Brookings’s retrospective on 1990s economic policy records that between 1993 and 2000 the United States had “the best economic performance of the past three decades”; in 2000 the expansion “surpassed in length the expansion of the 1960s, and thus became the longest on record”; during Clinton’s second term, real economic growth averaged about 4.5 percent per year and unemployment fell to about 4 percent, accompanied by structural budget surpluses and low inflation.[^ch45-brookings-1990s]
- **The causes, stated as claims.** The sources attribute the performance to monetary policy under Alan Greenspan, the fiscal tightening of 1993, the productivity gains of the late 1990s, and the investment boom in information technology; the chapter should present these as competing and overlapping explanations, not as a settled single cause, and should note that economists still dispute the sources of the late-1990s productivity acceleration.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** Give every figure with its series, dates, and definitions; an unemployment rate is not the same as a payroll count, and a real growth rate depends on the deflator used.[^ch45-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Median income and the distribution of the gains

- **The median.** The Census Bureau’s report on 1999 income records a median household income of about $42,000, up 7.7 percent from 1989 in real terms after adjusting for inflation; the chapter should give the comparison years and the price index used.[^ch45-census-income-1999]
- **The top.** The Congressional Budget Office found that between 1979 and 2007 income grew by about 275 percent for the top 1 percent of households, far faster than for the middle and the bottom of the distribution; a 2003 analysis of the same data records the average after-tax income of the top 1 percent tripling between 1979 and 2000. The chapter should give the 1979 baseline and the endpoints explicitly, and must not present a post-2000 figure as though it were a 1990s measurement.[^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-cbpp-2003][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **The mechanism.** The chapter should distinguish the distributional effects of the tax changes, the asset-price gains of the late 1990s, the effects of trade and immigration, and the effects of changes in labor-market institutions and the minimum wage, rather than assigning the whole shift to one policy.[^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-check-debate-works]
- **The counterevidence.** Within the decade, real wages rose across the distribution in the tight labor market of the late 1990s, and the poverty rate fell; the chapter should state both the widening top share and the late-decade gains at the bottom and explain why they are not contradictory.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-census-income-1999]

### 1.4 Technology, the new economy, and the bubble

- **The policy turn.** The Telecommunications Act of 1996 was the first major overhaul of American communications law in almost 62 years; the Federal Communications Commission described its goal as letting “anyone enter any communications business” and letting “any communications business compete in any market against any other,” while critics noted that it also imposed new regulation.[^ch45-telecom-act]
- **The boom.** Investment in the Nasdaq Composite rose sharply from 1995 to its peak in March 2000 before falling far below that level by late 2002. The chapter should describe the internet and telecommunications build-out as documented investment, not as a transformation whose effects were known at the time.[^ch45-dotcom-goldman][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** The date and level of the Nasdaq peak, the size of the decline, and the wealth destroyed are contested and unit-sensitive; give each figure with its index, dates, and source, and label estimates as estimates. The chapter should not read the 2000–02 bust back into the mid-1990s as inevitable.[^ch45-dotcom-goldman][^ch45-check-dates-appD]

### 1.5 The federal balance and Map 36

- **The fiscal turn.** The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993, signed on August 10, 1993, raised the top federal income-tax rate from 31 percent to 39.6 percent and cut spending in an effort to reduce the deficit; the combined effect of the 1993 law, the 1990 budget agreement, and the 1990s growth moved the federal budget from deficit to surplus by the end of the decade. The chapter should give the revenue and outlay figures from the Office of Management and Budget or the Congressional Budget Office, in current and inflation-adjusted terms, and distinguish annual balances from the accumulated debt.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **Map 36.** The registered visual for this section is `ch45-map-01` (Map 36, “Where the boom landed, 1992–2000”). It must show county-level population and employment growth, state its dates and units, and distinguish measured growth from areas the data do not cover; it must not read a causal story off the map.[^ch45-check-map36]
- **The measure.** Where the text gives a growth rate, it should also give the base year and the geographic unit, and should point to Appendix B, Table 7 for the supporting series.[^ch45-check-map36][^ch45-check-numbers]

## 2. NAFTA, the WTO, and Seattle (1992–1999)

### 2.1 The claim: integration delivered aggregate gains and concentrated losses

- **The claim.** The decade’s trade and investment agreements integrated the North American and global economies and produced aggregate gains in output and lower consumer prices, while concentrating displacement in specific industries and communities and shifting authority over investment disputes to tribunals beyond the ordinary courts.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-epi-nafta]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1992 election’s trade debate through NAFTA’s passage and entry into force, the investor-state provisions, the creation of the World Trade Organization, the Seattle protests, and the evidence on jobs and wages.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-wto-marrakesh][^ch45-wto-seattle][^ch45-epi-nafta]

### 2.2 NAFTA: negotiation, passage, and entry into force

- **The dates, kept distinct.** The Congressional Research Service records that NAFTA was signed by President George H. W. Bush on December 17, 1992, approved by Congress on November 20, 1993, and implemented by the NAFTA Implementation Act signed by President Clinton on December 8, 1993 (Public Law 103-182); it entered into force on January 1, 1994. The implementing act was H.R.3450, introduced on November 4, 1993.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-nafta-impl-act]
- **The votes.** The House passed the implementation act 234–200 on November 17, 1993, and the Senate passed it 61–38 on November 20, 1993. The chapter should give the roll calls and note that the winning coalition depended heavily on Republican votes.[^ch45-nafta-votes]
- **The opposition.** Perot’s 1992 warning that NAFTA would produce “a giant sucking sound” of jobs moving south became the popular shorthand for the agreement’s critics, and organized labor and environmental groups opposed it; the chapter should present the critics’ strongest argument — that the agreement would accelerate the relocation of manufacturing to lower-wage, lower-standard jurisdictions — before testing it.[^ch45-perot-sucking-sound][^ch45-epi-nafta]

### 2.3 Chapter 11 and the In Focus

- **The provision.** The investor-state dispute provisions of NAFTA Chapter 11 permit an investor of one party to seek money damages, through arbitration, for measures of another party that allegedly violate the chapter’s investment obligations; the chapter includes standards for national treatment, a minimum standard of treatment, and expropriation and compensation.[^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-nafta-ch11-state]
- **The novelty.** NAFTA was among the first agreements to make investment-protection standards enforceable through investor-state arbitration, giving foreign investors direct access to binding international tribunals and making the agreement a template for later trade pacts. The chapter should explain the mechanism — who may bring a claim, what it may claim, and where — rather than treating Chapter 11 as a symbol.[^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-nafta-ch11-state]
- **In Focus.** The close reading reads the chapter’s expropriation and compensation provision and the investor-claim article from the agreement text. It should quote the requirement that compensation be “equivalent to the fair market value of the expropriated investment immediately before the expropriation took place,” explain what “measure tantamount to expropriation” and “fair market value” put in dispute, and show how a private investor’s claim bypasses the domestic courts. It must cite the actual article numbers and must not assign to the provision effects the record does not establish.[^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-check-focus]
- **The stakes for §2.** The provision is the chapter’s clearest case of the decade’s larger question: whether the integration of markets also moved decisions beyond the reach of the voters the 1992 campaign had addressed.[^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-check-scope]

### 2.4 The WTO and the Seattle protests

- **The WTO.** The Marrakesh Agreement, signed on April 15, 1994, concluded the Uruguay Round of trade negotiations that ran from 1986 to 1994 and established the World Trade Organization, which entered into force on January 1, 1995, replacing the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade as an institution.[^ch45-wto-marrakesh]
- **Seattle.** The Third WTO Ministerial Conference was held in Seattle from November 30 to December 3, 1999, and met with large protests — commonly estimated at 30,000 to 50,000 people — that came to be called the “Battle of Seattle,” alongside opposition from developing-country delegations inside the hall; the conference failed to launch a new round of negotiations.[^ch45-wto-seattle]
- **The participants and the portents.** The chapter should present the protests as the product of identifiable coalitions — labor, environmental, farm, and consumer groups — rather than a spontaneous national mood, and should note that the meeting’s failure owed as much to disagreements among member governments as to the streets. The chapter must not overstate the protests’ causal effect.[^ch45-wto-seattle][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 2.5 The distributional record

- **The claim at its strongest.** Defenders of the agreements argued that they lowered prices for consumers, expanded export markets for American producers and farmers, and anchored liberalization in Mexico and elsewhere; the chapter should present that case before the counterevidence.[^ch45-nafta-crs]
- **The counterevidence.** Analyses critical of NAFTA recorded large net losses in manufacturing employment concentrated in specific industries and regions, while other economists argued that the job changes reflected broader technological and macroeconomic forces and could not be attributed to the agreement alone. The chapter should give the competing estimates with their methods and years and should not present either side’s figure as settled.[^ch45-epi-nafta][^ch45-piie-nafta][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** No single net job number captures both the gains to consumers and exporters and the losses to displaced workers; the chapter should separate distribution from aggregate effect and mark the limits of each estimate.[^ch45-epi-nafta][^ch45-piie-nafta][^ch45-check-debate-works]

## 3. The Third Way, welfare, and crime (1993–1996)

### 3.1 The claim: a centrist program that reduced the safety net and expanded the punishing state

- **The claim.** The Clinton administration’s domestic program combined deficit reduction and a claim to have ended “the era of big government” with a welfare law that ended the federal entitlement to cash assistance and a crime law that expanded federal punishment; the section holds those choices together as one governing strategy.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-sotu-1996][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-crime-bill]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1993 budget through the 1994 crime act, the failed health-care effort, the 1996 welfare law, and the rights record, with attention to who benefited and who bore the costs.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-check-silences]

### 3.2 Deficit reduction and the “Third Way”

- **The 1993 law.** The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 raised the top marginal rate and other taxes and reduced spending, passing without a single Republican vote; the chapter should give the revenue and deficit figures from the Office of Management and Budget and should present the argument that the deficit reduction lowered interest rates and enabled the later expansion, as a claim to be tested rather than assumed.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-debate-works]
- **The governing formula.** In his 1996 State of the Union address, Clinton said, “the era of big government is over,” adding that the country could not “go back to the era of fending for yourself” and had to “go forward to the era of working together.” The chapter should treat the phrase as the decade’s central claim about the role of government and should test it against what the federal government did.[^ch45-sotu-1996]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give the deficit and surplus figures by fiscal year and note that a surplus is a balance, not a level of debt or of spending.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-check-numbers]

### 3.3 The 1994 crime act

- **The statute.** The Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, H.R.3355, became Public Law 103-322 on September 13, 1994. It funded community policing, created the federal death-penalty expansion of the Federal Death Penalty Act of 1994, and imposed mandatory life imprisonment for a third violent felony, and it included a ban on certain semiautomatic assault weapons.[^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-justice-death-penalty]
- **The consequences.** The law expanded federal criminal jurisdiction and punishment and was part of the longer rise of mass incarceration whose scale and racial incidence the following chapters treat; the chapter should give the statutory provisions and the incarceration data from the Bureau of Justice Statistics, and should distinguish the law’s text from its measurable effects.[^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **The strongest case and its limit.** The chapter should present the argument for the law — that violent crime imposed real harm on affected communities and that police funding and tougher penalties would reduce it — before testing it against the crime and incarceration record, and should not present the law as the single cause of later outcomes.[^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 Welfare reform

- **The statute.** The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, H.R.3734, became Public Law 104-193 on August 22, 1996; it replaced the Aid to Families with Dependent Children program with the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant, imposed work requirements, and set a federal lifetime limit on assistance.[^ch45-prwora]
- **The terms.** The law’s work requirements, time limits, and restrictions on benefits to immigrants and to some families with additional children recast the safety net as a time-limited program tied to employment. The chapter should state the provisions from the statute or the congressional summary, not from a later characterization.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-check-numbers]
- **The record and the debate.** Caseloads fell sharply after 1996, and the chapter should distinguish the contributions of the law, the strong labor market, the minimum-wage and tax-credit expansions, and state policy choices; it should give poverty and employment figures with their sources and should report the disagreement about how much the law improved or worsened the lives of the poor.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Rights, health care, and the uninsured

- **The rights record.** The administration’s record on gay and lesbian rights was double-sided: the “Don’t Ask, Don’t Tell” policy of 1993 allowed gay and lesbian service members to serve only if they concealed their identity, and the Defense of Marriage Act of 1996 barred federal recognition of same-sex marriages and allowed states to refuse recognition of others’ marriages. The chapter should give both the statutes and the administration’s stated reasons, and should name the costs plainly.[^ch45-dadt-doma]
- **The health-care defeat.** The administration’s 1993–94 effort at universal health coverage failed, and the number of uninsured Americans was a persistent feature of the decade; the chapter should present the proposal, the opposition, and the outcome from the record rather than from memory.[^ch45-check-sources]
- **The caution.** The record over-represents organized, media-visible advocates and under-represents the uninsured, the poor, and the incarcerated. Name the asymmetry.[^ch45-check-silences]

## 4. Rights, organizing, impeachment, and the 2000 election (1994–2000)

### 4.1 The claim: a conservative revival, a personal scandal, and an election decided by a court

- **The claim.** The decade’s political history runs from the Republican takeover of 1994 through the budget confrontations, the domestic-terror bombing of 1995, the impeachment of 1998, and the disputed election of 2000, in which the presidency was decided by a divided Supreme Court and a margin of 537 votes in Florida; the section asks how a strong economy and a booming decade produced so unsettled a politics.[^ch45-contract-america][^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-app-2000]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Contract with America through Oklahoma City, the impeachment, and the 2000 election, with the conservative movement and the culture wars as the organizing frame.[^ch45-contract-america][^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-bush-v-gore]

### 4.2 The 1994 takeover and the budget confrontations

- **The Contract.** Republican House candidates signed the Contract with America on September 27, 1994, pledging a set of reforms and floor votes in the first 100 days; the party won control of both chambers in November 1994 for the first time in four decades, and Newt Gingrich became Speaker.[^ch45-contract-america]
- **The confrontation.** The new majority’s budget and welfare proposals produced confrontations with the president, including a shutdown of the federal government; the chapter should give the sequence and the terms of the disputes from the record.[^ch45-contract-america][^ch45-check-sources]
- **The movement frame.** The chapter should present the takeover as the work of an organized conservative movement, not a spontaneous national mood, and should connect it to the culture wars the decade’s school, family, and sexuality conflicts expressed.[^ch45-contract-america][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Oklahoma City and domestic terrorism

- **The attack.** On April 19, 1995, Timothy McVeigh detonated a truck bomb outside the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in Oklahoma City, killing 168 people, including 19 children, and injuring several hundred more; the FBI calls it “the worst act of homegrown terrorism in the nation’s history.” McVeigh was arrested about 90 minutes after the bombing and was later convicted and executed.[^ch45-fbi-okc]
- **The context.** The chapter should place the bombing within the decade’s antigovernment and militia movements and should not attribute it to any broader group the evidence does not implicate. It should also note that many Americans initially assumed the attack was foreign, a fact that belongs to the record of the period’s fears.[^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 The impeachment of 1998

- **The articles.** The House adopted two articles of impeachment against Clinton — perjury before a grand jury and obstruction of justice — in December 1998, arising from the investigation of his relationship with a White House intern and the independent counsel’s inquiry; the Senate acquitted him on February 12, 1999. The chapter should give the articles and the votes from the congressional and presidential records.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-impeachment-congress]
- **The votes.** On the perjury article the Senate split 45–55 for conviction and on the obstruction article 50–50, both short of the two-thirds required; the chapter should give the votes and the constitutional threshold.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-impeachment-congress]
- **The stakes.** The chapter should present the episode as a constitutional confrontation over the meaning of the impeachment standard, as well as a political fight, and should avoid both minimizing and inflating the charges.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The 2000 election and Bush v. Gore

- **The returns.** The election of 2000 produced a popular-vote plurality for Al Gore and an Electoral College majority for George W. Bush — 271 to 266, with one District of Columbia elector abstaining — and an outcome decided in Florida.[^ch45-app-2000][^ch45-archives-2000]
- **The recount.** Florida initially reported Bush ahead by 1,784 votes; a machine recount reduced the margin to 327, and after the litigation the state certified Bush the winner by 537 votes out of more than 5.9 million cast. The chapter should give each margin with its stage and source.[^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-constcenter-2000]
- **The decision.** In *Bush v. Gore*, 531 U.S. 98 (2000), the Supreme Court held that the Florida recount’s varying standards violated the Equal Protection Clause and that no constitutionally valid recount could be completed within the federal deadline, effectively ending the recount and leaving Bush the winner. The chapter should present the holding and the dissent, and should distinguish the Court’s reasoning from the political outcome.[^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-check-2000]
- **The hand-off.** Bush’s accession is the immediate prehistory of Chapter 46; the chapter should end there and must not narrate September 11.[^ch45-check-ch46][^ch45-check-bridge]

## 5. Rwanda, the Balkans, and American predominance (1992–1999)

### 5.1 The claim: a superpower that intervened unevenly

- **The claim.** In the decade after the Cold War, the United States intervened in Somalia, Bosnia, and Kosovo but did not intervene to stop the genocide in Rwanda, and the contrast between the interventions and the non-intervention is the section’s problem; the chapter asks what governed the choices and what they cost.[^ch45-state-somalia][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-dayton-state][^ch45-nato-kosovo]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Somali intervention through the Rwandan genocide, the Bosnian war and the Dayton settlement, and the Kosovo air campaign, and closes on the debate over the “unipolar moment.”[^ch45-state-somalia][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-dayton-state][^ch45-nato-kosovo]

### 5.2 Somalia

- **The intervention.** The United States began Operation Restore Hope in December 1992 under President George H. W. Bush as part of a United Nations humanitarian mission, and the operation transitioned to UNOSOM II in March 1993; the warlord Muhammad Farah Aideed’s forces shot down two Black Hawk helicopters in a battle on October 3, 1993, that killed 18 American soldiers.[^ch45-state-somalia]
- **The turn.** Clinton addressed the nation on October 7, 1993, defended the mission, and set a date for withdrawal; the chapter should give the casualties, the policy change, and the withdrawal timeline from the record, and should present the episode as the event that set the political limits of later interventions.[^ch45-clinton-somalia][^ch45-state-somalia]

### 5.3 Rwanda

- **The genocide.** After the April 6, 1994, shooting down of President Juvénal Habyarimana’s plane, Rwandan military and militia forces began an organized slaughter that killed hundreds of thousands — estimates run to about one million — in roughly 100 days, targeting Tutsi and moderate Hutu.[^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]
- **The American response.** The National Security Archive’s document collection, “The U.S. and the Genocide in Rwanda 1994: Evidence of Inaction,” records that the United States discouraged a stronger United Nations response and avoided describing the killings as genocide while they continued; the chapter should use the declassified documents and should distinguish what officials knew from what they said publicly. It should note the policy review that restricted peacekeeping commitments, and should not reduce the episode to a single official’s choice.[^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]
- **The apology.** On March 25, 1998, in Kigali, Clinton said, “We did not immediately call these crimes by their rightful name: genocide,” and pledged American support for survivors. The chapter should quote the apology exactly and present it as a later acknowledgment, not as a policy the administration pursued in 1994.[^ch45-clinton-kigali][^ch45-check-rwanda]

### 5.4 Bosnia and Kosovo

- **The Bosnian war.** The war in Bosnia and Herzegovina from 1992 to 1995 killed more than 200,000 people and displaced about two million; in July 1995, Bosnian Serb forces overran the United Nations “safe area” of Srebrenica and killed up to 8,000 Bosniak men and boys, which both the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia and the International Court of Justice later ruled was genocide.[^ch45-un-srebrenica][^ch45-dayton-state]
- **Dayton.** After the Srebrenica massacre and a NATO air campaign, the parties negotiated at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base near Dayton, Ohio, opening on November 1, 1995, and signed the General Framework Agreement for Peace in Paris on December 14, 1995, ending the war and deploying a NATO-led force.[^ch45-dayton-state]
- **Kosovo.** NATO’s Operation Allied Force ran from March 24 to June 10, 1999, a 78-day air campaign of more than 38,000 sorties flown without a single Allied fatality, after the failure of talks at Rambouillet and Paris; the campaign ended after the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia accepted terms and the United Nations endorsed a political settlement.[^ch45-nato-kosovo]
- **The caution.** The chapter should give the casualty figures and the legal findings from the tribunals and the United Nations, distinguish those from political claims, and present the interventions as the product of decisions by many governments, not of American power alone.[^ch45-un-srebrenica][^ch45-nato-kosovo][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 The unipolar moment

- **The claim.** The decade left the United States without a rival great power, and officials and commentators debated whether the resulting predominance should be used to enlarge democracy, to keep a narrow account of the national interest, or to withdraw; the chapter should present the debate fairly and connect it to the interventions and the non-intervention.[^ch45-fukuyama-1989][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-nato-kosovo]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give force-structure, deployment, and defense-budget figures with their sources where it makes claims about predominance, and should not treat the “unipolar moment” as a settled description.[^ch45-check-numbers][^ch45-check-debate-works]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The 1992 result.** Clinton won 370 electoral votes and about 43.0 percent of the popular vote; Bush took 168 electoral votes and about 37.4 percent, and Perot about 18.9 percent with no electoral votes.[^ch45-app-1992]
- **NAFTA’s dates.** NAFTA was signed on December 17, 1992, approved by Congress on November 20, 1993, implemented by Public Law 103-182 on December 8, 1993, and entered into force on January 1, 1994.[^ch45-nafta-crs]
- **The WTO’s creation.** The Marrakesh Agreement was signed on April 15, 1994, and the World Trade Organization entered into force on January 1, 1995; the Seattle ministerial ran from November 30 to December 3, 1999.[^ch45-wto-marrakesh][^ch45-wto-seattle]
- **The domestic statutes.** The 1994 crime act became Public Law 103-322 on September 13, 1994, and the 1996 welfare law became Public Law 104-193 on August 22, 1996.[^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-prwora]
- **The economy’s strength.** Between 1993 and 2000 the United States had its best economic performance in three decades, and the 1990s expansion became the longest on record; unemployment fell to about 4 percent and the budget moved to surplus.[^ch45-brookings-1990s]
- **The distribution.** Income growth at the top far outpaced the rest of the distribution after 1979, and the top 1 percent’s share rose over the following decades.[^ch45-cbo-distribution]
- **The Oklahoma City bombing killed 168 people on April 19, 1995; the House impeached Clinton in December 1998 and the Senate acquitted him on February 12, 1999; and the 2000 election was decided in Florida and by the Supreme Court.**[^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-bush-v-gore]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the decade’s growth broadly shared or top-heavy?** The aggregate record — low unemployment, rising median income, falling poverty — coexisted with a rising top share; the chapter should present the strongest versions of the “shared prosperity” and “winner-take-all” readings and weigh them against the distributional series rather than choosing a mood.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-cbpp-2003]
- **Did trade cause the manufacturing losses?** One account attributes large net job losses to the agreements; another attributes the changes to technology and macroeconomic forces; the chapter should compare their methods and years and should not present either figure as settled.[^ch45-epi-nafta][^ch45-piie-nafta][^ch45-check-debate-works]
- **Was welfare reform a success?** Falling caseloads and rising employment are read by supporters as proof the law worked and by critics as the product of a strong economy and a reduced safety net; the chapter should state each at its strongest and use the poverty and employment record.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-check-debate-works]
- **How much did the interventions matter?** The decade’s interventions and its most glaring non-intervention support rival accounts centered on interests, on domestic politics, and on the constraints of a post-Cold War military; the chapter should present the alternatives fairly.[^ch45-state-somalia][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-nato-kosovo]
- **Has history ended?** Fukuyama’s 1989 proposition and its critics disagree about whether the decade confirmed the triumph of liberal democracy or exposed its limits; the chapter should weigh the proposition against the decade’s own conflicts and its distributional record.[^ch45-fukuyama-1989][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the post-Cold War settlement produced real and widely enjoyed growth whose gains were nevertheless concentrated, in which integration created aggregate benefits and localized losses, and in which American power was used selectively and its costs fell heavily on people with little voice in the record.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]
- **Contingency.** The 1993 budget, NAFTA’s passage, welfare reform, the crime act, the intervention choices, and the 2000 outcome were all contingent; the chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-nafta-votes][^ch45-bush-v-gore]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter should hold the structural conditions — the end of the Cold War, the technology boom, the global integration of markets — together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation over the other.[^ch45-check-scope][^ch45-brookings-1990s]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Between 1991 and 2000 the United States enjoyed the longest expansion on record, integrated its economy with its neighbors and the world, ended its federal cash-assistance entitlement, expanded its prisons, acquitted an impeached president, decided a presidential election in court, and intervened in some humanitarian crises while standing aside from the worst; the chapter’s question — who gained, and what was left out — is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]

## Legacies

The decade left an economy that had integrated with its neighbors and the world and a politics organized around the difference between aggregate growth and the median experience. The trade and investment agreements of the 1990s fixed a framework that later governments would defend, revise, or abandon, and the distributional widening that the period’s own data recorded became a permanent feature of the political debate. The chapter should connect these to the present as documented changes, not as a verdict.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-cbo-distribution]

The decade also left a smaller cash-assistance safety net and a larger prison system. The welfare law of 1996 and the crime act of 1994 reset the terms of both debates for a generation; the falling caseloads and the rising incarceration counts of the following decades are the measurable consequences the chapter should trace to their sources.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-crime-bill]

And it left the United States with an unmatched position in the world and an unsettled rule for when to use it. The interventions in Somalia, Bosnia, and Kosovo and the non-intervention in Rwanda left the question of American purpose in the post-Cold War order open; Chapter 46 takes up the answer the following year forced.[^ch45-dayton-state][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-check-ch46]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The decade’s outcomes were made in Mexico City, Geneva, Seattle, Kigali, Sarajevo, and Belgrade as well as in Washington. The trade negotiations, the WTO, the genocide, and the Balkan wars all show how international decisions bounded American choices; the chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate the diplomatic history.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-wto-marrakesh][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, officials, investors, and journalists and under-represents the poor, the uninsured, the incarcerated, the displaced, and the victims of the decade’s interventions. The chapter should name these asymmetries in the framing paragraph and here rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch45-check-silences][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-crime-bill]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 44 owns the end of the Cold War and the Gulf War; Chapter 46 owns September 11 and the war on terror. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch45-check-ch44][^ch45-check-ch46]
