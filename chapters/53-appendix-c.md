## 1. How Appendix C Works

### 1.1 Purpose and reader-facing form

- Appendix C is a concise catalog of the book’s registered visuals and a gazetteer of the places the narrative returns to. It is not a second atlas, a production ledger, or a substitute for the chapters’ evidence.[^app-c-source-register]
- The appendix has four reader-facing parts: a map catalog of 39 entries, a figure catalog of nine entries, an image catalog of eight entries, and one gazetteer table. The catalog says what each visual shows, why the book needs it, and where its evidence comes from. The gazetteer says where each place is, which chapter discusses it, and what name the book uses.
- Each reader-facing catalog entry will run about 25 to 40 words: a display number, a short title, the approved HTML caption, and a compressed source line. The production fields — image content, argument, alt text, credit, and checks — remain in this manuscript file and the README register; they are not printed in the catalog. The caption and alt-text schedules in §§2.7, 3.3, and 4.5 are the authoritative strings for the EPUB.
- The catalog is written for a reader who has finished the book or is using it as a reference. It should answer three questions without repeating a chapter: What does this visual show? Why is it here? What is it not allowed to claim?
- The gazetteer is a table, not a narrative. Each row will identify a place, its present jurisdiction, the chapter or chapters that use it, a short note on its historical role, and the name policy applied. The table is registered as App. C, Table 1.

### 1.2 Numbering, ownership, and cross-references

- Maps, figures, and images have separate book-wide sequences: Map 1–39, Figure 1–9, Image 1–8. Tables are numbered within their owning unit, so the gazetteer is App. C, Table 1.
- Display numbers are frozen at release. A shared visual has one owner and one number. Map 9 belongs to Chapter 10 and is continued, not duplicated, in Chapter 11. Map 38 belongs to Chapter 47 and is reused with dated panels in Chapters 48 and 49. The catalog records the reuse; it does not create a second number.[^app-c-shared-visuals]
- Each entry names its owning chapter and section. The catalog’s cross-reference is to the section where the visual does its argument, not to every passing mention. If a chapter moves a visual, the catalog and the README register move with it.
- The catalog does not print the editorial draft-number concordance. The legacy labels in the README register are production records, not reader-facing numbers.
- The gazetteer’s chapter column is a reading aid. It is not evidence by itself. Every historical note must either summarize a claim already cited in that chapter or carry its own note in this appendix.

### 1.3 Page budget and compression rule

- The allowance is five text pages, or about 2,500 words at the book’s 500-word measure. Reference tables set more densely than prose, so the practical target is about 2,700 to 3,000 words including the gazetteer’s rows and the catalog’s source lines.[^app-c-page-budget]
- The arithmetic is tight. Fifty-six catalog entries at 25 to 35 words apiece consume about 1,400 to 1,950 words. Seventy-six gazetteer rows at eight to twelve words consume about 600 to 900 words. Apparatus, group introductions, and the note on names consume another 400 to 600 words. The total falls between about 2,400 and 3,450 words.
- The compression rule follows from that arithmetic. Map entries get one clause on content and one on purpose. Figure and image entries get one clause on the object and one on the reading. Gazetteer notes are identifications, not mini-essays. Longer explanations belong in the chapters.
- If the finished catalog cannot be set legibly within five pages, request a reallocation to six pages rather than cutting definitions, source lines, or the Indigenous-name notes. The README budget totals 1,021 pages, about 21 pages above the 1,000-page floor, although the current PDF measures 954 pages; a one-page increase would not by itself break the budget, but the measured shortfall is a separate production issue. Record the change in the README budget and status table.[^app-c-page-budget]
- The appendix does not count image space or note space against the five pages. Images and their captions sit outside the text-page goal. The combined notes are estimated separately, as the book plan requires.

### 1.4 Source hierarchy and verification status

- **Base geometry.** Original maps use public-domain base data: Natural Earth for global and continental geometry, the Census Bureau’s cartographic boundary files for current U.S. boundaries, and the Newberry Library’s Atlas of Historical County Boundaries for historical county and state boundaries.[^app-c-natural-earth][^app-c-census-cartographic][^app-c-newberry]
- **Historical geography.** Historical boundaries, routes, and places come from the owning chapter’s cited sources: archives, libraries, museums, tribal nations, government records, and peer-reviewed scholarship. The catalog does not invent a boundary because a modern base map makes it easy to draw.
- **Indigenous geography.** Native Land Digital is a finding aid and a prompt for community consultation, not an authority for legal boundaries. The catalog uses it only with that warning and checks names and territories against the relevant nation’s own publications.[^app-c-native-land]
- **Place names and coordinates.** The gazetteer uses the U.S. Board on Geographic Names and the Geographic Names Information System for official federal names and coordinates. Those names are not automatically the book’s preferred names; where a nation uses a different name, the book prints the nation’s name first and the federal name in parentheses or in a note.[^app-c-usgs-gnis]
- **Data.** Figures use the official series named in the owning chapter and Appendix B. A chart is not evidence for a causal claim. The caption states the series, units, observation years, denominators, and any break in the series.
- **Status.** The catalog entries in this outline are `planned`. The objects are `planned` or `sourced` only when the entry says so. No entry is `produced` or `verified`. The image entries distinguish a located object from a cleared reproduction; a catalog record is not a license. Two source files are located: `images/hiawatha-wampum-belt.jpg` (public domain as a 1909 publication) and `images/aids-quilt.jpg` (permission pending; not the first panel).
- **What “consulted” means.** A source marked “consulted” was opened and read for this outline. A source marked “discovered” was located but not read. The outline does not present a discovered source as verified support. In the notes, “Accessed” records the date the URL was checked; it does not by itself mean the source was read in full. The limitations field says what remains unread or unresolved.

### 1.5 What the appendix must not do

- It must not claim that a map, figure, or image exists because a chapter describes it. Every chapter that references an unproduced visual says so; the catalog must say the same.
- It must not treat a historical map as a neutral window. A map made by a colonial surveyor, a railroad company, or a state tourism board carries the purposes of its maker. The catalog names the maker and the purpose when they matter.
- It must not color Indigenous homelands as empty space, use a single color for “Native Americans,” or imply that a treaty boundary was a settled fact on the ground.
- It must not use a map to prove a causal claim. A map can show where something happened; it cannot show why, who benefited, or what people believed.[^app-c-check-causation]
- It must not reproduce a third-party image because it is easy to find online. Each image needs an object identifier, a credit line, and a rights basis. Community-held objects need community authorization.

### 1.6 Kindle caption and alt-text rules

- Every visual has two HTML strings that never appear inside the image: a caption for `<figcaption>` and alt text for `<img alt>`. The caption states what the visual shows, its date, and its limits. The alt text is 140 characters or fewer and describes the subject and scope, not every label.
- The figure number, title, caption, source line, credit, and alt text are HTML. Map labels, legends, scale bars, axis labels, and data labels may be inside the image because they are part of the visual. A separate figure title, caption, source line, or credit may not.
- The schedules in §§2.7, 3.3, and 4.5 give the approved strings for all 56 registered visuals. The owning chapter, the README register, and the production file must use the same strings. Do not reword a caption or alt text in one chapter without updating the schedule and the register. The schedules are production records; they are not printed in the reader-facing catalog and do not consume its five-page allowance.

Use a real `<figure>` element with an `<img>` that carries the approved alt text in its `alt` attribute and a `<figcaption>` that begins with the figure number and title, then the approved caption, source line, and credit. The exact HTML pattern is in [`docs/kindle.md`](../docs/kindle.md), §7.2; the appendix does not repeat the markup because the checker treats literal attribute quotes as manuscript quotation marks.

The following rules apply to every visual:

- Do not use “see the map below,” “click here,” or a file name as the alt text. Do not put the caption in the image because a reader who enlarges the type cannot enlarge the caption, and a screen reader cannot read it.
- The source line and credit are separate HTML paragraphs after the caption, or a second `<p>` inside the `<figcaption>`. They are never part of the PNG. The caption and alt text are content, not decoration; every visual must also be cited by number in the chapter prose.

## 2. Map Catalog

Every entry below names its image content, argument, evidence, production, and check. The image content is a production description, not the caption. The HTML caption and alt text for each map are in §2.7. Do not put the figure number, title, caption, source line, or credit inside the map; only map labels, legends, scale bars, and data labels belong there.

### 2.1 Deep history and contact (Maps 1–3)

- The first three maps establish a continent that was already inhabited, managed, and connected before European arrival. They must not function as a prologue in which “history” begins at contact.
- The group uses archaeological chronologies, Indigenous political geography, and the documentary record of the first voyages. The maps distinguish a site’s date of occupation from its date of abandonment, a documented route from a reconstructed one, and a paper claim from a recognized boundary.

**Map 1. Cultural complexes around 1200 CE.** Owner: Ch. 1, §4.1.
- **Image content (not caption text).** A continent of regional cultural complexes around 1200 CE: the Mississippi Valley and Southeast, the Southwest, the Pacific Northwest, the Great Plains, the Eastern Woodlands, and the Arctic. The map marks active centers, abandoned earthworks, and the areas beyond them where people lived without monumental architecture.
- **Argument.** The map makes the case that North America was not a wilderness waiting to be discovered. It was a managed landscape with cities, farms, trade routes, and political centers. It also refuses the opposite error of treating every earthwork as part of one “mound builder” civilization.
- **Evidence.** Poverty Point’s earthworks date to roughly 1700–1100 BCE; Chaco’s great-house system flourished from about 850 to 1250 CE; Cahokia peaked between roughly 950 and 1350 CE; Serpent Mound’s date is disputed between a Late Precontact construction around 1100 CE and an Adena-period construction centuries earlier.[^app-c-map01-sites]
- **Production.** Use a physical base with rivers and relief, not a modern state map. Mark the dated centers with symbols that distinguish occupation from construction. Show the earlier Hopewell and Poverty Point earthworks in a separate, muted key. Do not use one color for “mound builders.” State the date of each complex in the map labels or legend; the caption notes that the dates are approximate.
- **Check.** The Serpent Mound date must be printed as a dispute, not a verdict. Cahokia’s population must be a range. The map must not imply that a site’s monumental phase was its only phase.[^app-c-check-dates]

**Map 2. Norse voyages and Atlantic exchange, c. 1000–1551.** Owner: Ch. 2, §2; reused §5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** Two panels. The first traces Norse voyages and the evidence for a short-lived settlement at L’Anse aux Meadows. The second shows the Atlantic world after 1492: Iberian and other European claims, the Tordesillas line, the routes of conquest and exchange, and the movement of people, plants, animals, and pathogens.
- **Argument.** The map distinguishes a voyage, a settlement, a claim, and a conquest. It shows that the Atlantic was an Indigenous world before it became a European arena, and that the Tordesillas line was a paper division between two crowns, not a border that other powers or Indigenous nations recognized.
- **Evidence.** Parks Canada’s L’Anse aux Meadows record documents the Norse site and its dating; the Avalon Project prints the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas. The routes of Cabeza de Vaca, De Soto, and Coronado remain disputed and must be drawn as uncertain where the evidence is uncertain.[^app-c-map02-norse]
- **Production.** Label the Tordesillas line as a claim. Distinguish documented Norse remains from saga narratives and from speculative routes. Do not draw a single arrow from Scandinavia to the Americas. Use a dated key for the post-1492 panel and show the Indigenous political units that the European claims ignored.
- **Check.** The first-landfall identity and the pre-contact population estimates remain open. The map must not settle either by cartographic convention.

**Map 3. Indigenous political worlds, c. 1600.** Owner: Ch. 3, §4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The approximate territories and language families of the major nations and confederacies discussed in Chapter 3: Tsenacomoco and its Siouan and Iroquoian neighbors, the Wampanoag and Narragansett, the Haudenosaunee, the Wendat, the Caddo groups, the Pueblo towns, and selected Plains peoples.
- **Argument.** The map presents Indigenous North America as a set of political worlds, not a cultural blanket. It shows confederacies, alliances, and rivalries, and it makes the point that a nation’s territory could change without a European treaty.
- **Evidence.** The map’s political geography comes from the *Handbook of North American Indians*, nation-specific histories, and the Native Land Digital finding aid. Native Land is used to locate a nation and to prompt consultation, not to fix a legal boundary.[^app-c-map03-native]
- **Production.** Use approximate boundaries with a visible uncertainty key. Name nations in their preferred forms where known. Do not fill the continent with one color or label the interior “unclaimed.” Show the Haudenosaunee and Wendat as confederacies, not as single tribes.
- **Check.** Every boundary and name needs a nation-specific source. The map must not imply that a nation’s territory was fixed for all time.[^app-c-check-indigenous]

### 2.2 Colonial worlds (Maps 4–8)

- The colonial maps place English, Dutch, French, and Spanish settlements inside Indigenous homelands. They show trade and migration as circuits, not as arrows of “settlement” moving into empty space.
- The group also tracks the shift from chartered settlements to imperial warfare. By Map 8, the contest is no longer only between colonies and nations; it is between empires, with Indigenous powers choosing sides and setting terms.

**Map 4. Virginia and New England, 1607–1640.** Owner: Ch. 4, §§1–5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The Chesapeake and New England settlements within the homelands of the Powhatan, Patawomeck, Nansemond, Wampanoag, Narragansett, Pequot, and neighboring nations. It marks Jamestown, Plymouth, Massachusetts Bay, the Pequot homelands, and the areas of the 1622 and 1637 wars.
- **Argument.** The map shows that English settlement was a narrow, fragile intrusion into a densely used landscape. It also shows that the settlers’ maps routinely erased the nations whose land they claimed.
- **Evidence.** Jamestown Rediscovery and the National Park Service document the Virginia site; Plimoth Patuxet documents the Wampanoag and English contexts; the chapter’s sources carry the 1622 and Pequot War evidence.[^app-c-map04-chesapeake]
- **Production.** Label colonial boundaries as approximate and dated. Show Indigenous territories and settlement sites, not only English forts. Distinguish a trading post from a plantation from a town. Mark the 1622 attack area and the Pequot homelands with dates.
- **Check.** The map must not merge “Virginia” and “New England” into one settlement system or treat the colonial charters as actual control on the ground.

**Map 5. The English Atlantic, c. 1680.** Owner: Ch. 5, §2.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The circuits that connected Barbados, Jamaica, the Chesapeake, the Middle Colonies, West Africa, and England: sugar, tobacco, provisions, manufactured goods, and captive labor. It distinguishes trade routes from territorial claims.
- **Argument.** The map connects racial slavery to the broader Atlantic economy. It shows that the colonies were not separate experiments but parts of one commercial and coercive system.
- **Evidence.** The SlaveVoyages database supplies the transatlantic voyage data and its limits; the chapter’s sources carry the Barbados, Chesapeake, and Middle Colonies evidence.[^app-c-map05-slavevoyages]
- **Production.** Use one line style for trade routes and another for territorial claims. Show the Middle Passage as a set of aggregated routes, not a single arrow. State the date and the approximate nature of the boundaries. Do not imply that every colony was equally dependent on slavery.
- **Check.** The map must not treat the slave trade as a natural flow. It must show the coercion and the destinations, and it must note that the database records voyages, not all trafficking.

**Map 6. War, alliances, and empire, 1675–1713.** Owner: Ch. 6, §4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The theaters of Metacom’s War, the northern frontier wars, the Haudenosaunee Covenant Chain and its neighbors, and the territorial changes of 1713. It distinguishes colonial and Indigenous frontiers and claims.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the late seventeenth-century wars were not a single “Indian war.” They were a series of conflicts in which Indigenous nations, colonies, and empires made and broke alliances.
- **Evidence.** The National Park Service’s Roger Williams material documents Metacom’s War; the chapter’s sources carry the Covenant Chain and the 1713 settlement. The Treaty of Utrecht’s territorial clauses still need a proper edition and collation.[^app-c-map06-kingphilip]
- **Production.** Use a dated key for each war. Show the Covenant Chain as a diplomatic relationship, not a territory. Mark the 1713 changes with the instruments that made them and distinguish a European claim from an Indigenous reality.
- **Check.** The map must not color all Indigenous nations as allies of one empire. It must not imply that the 1713 treaty settled the interior.

**Map 7. Colonial society and the Atlantic economy, 1692–1750.** Owner: Ch. 7, §§1–3.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The staple regions (tobacco, rice, indigo, naval stores, provisions), the major ports, the Scots-Irish and German migration streams, Whitefield’s itineraries, and the sites of Salem (1692), Stono (1739), Enfield (1741), and New York (1741).
- **Argument.** The map connects settlement, labor, credit, and religion. It shows that the colonies were integrated into an Atlantic economy and that revival, migration, and slavery were part of the same world.
- **Evidence.** The chapter’s sources carry the staple and migration evidence; the Library of Congress and the David Rumsey collection supply period maps and views for orientation.[^app-c-map07-colonial]
- **Production.** Use a 1750 base and a dated key for the event sites. Show the migration streams as broad bands, not precise routes. Distinguish a staple region from a colony’s political boundary. Do not imply that the Great Awakening was a single unified movement.
- **Check.** The map must not place Salem, Stono, Enfield, and New York in one causal frame. They are sites of distinct episodes.

**Map 8. War for Empire, 1754–1763.** Owner: Ch. 8, §§3–5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The principal campaigns and theaters of the Seven Years’ War in North America, the forts and the forks of the Ohio, Quebec and Montreal, and the Proclamation Line of 1763. It distinguishes imperial claims from Indigenous homelands.
- **Argument.** The map shows how a contest over the Ohio Valley became a world war and how the peace redrew the map without consulting the nations who lived on it.
- **Evidence.** The National Park Service documents Fort Necessity and the war’s opening; the Avalon Project prints the Royal Proclamation of 1763. The chapter’s sources carry the campaign and treaty evidence.[^app-c-map08-fortnecessity]
- **Production.** Mark the campaign routes with dates. Show the Proclamation Line as a claim, not a surveyed border. Distinguish the British, French, Spanish, and Indigenous territories. Do not label the interior “French” or “British” without a date and a source.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that the 1763 settlement was accepted by the nations it divided. It must not use the Proclamation Line as a modern state boundary.

### 2.3 Revolution and early republic (Maps 9–12)

- The revolutionary maps show a war fought across a continent and an ocean, not a single “American” front. The early-republic maps show claims to western land that were asserted before they were surveyed, ratified, or recognized by the nations who held the land.
- The group distinguishes a military campaign from a territorial claim, a treaty cession from an actual survey, and a ratification vote from a settled jurisdiction.

**Map 9. War for Independence, 1775–1783.** Owner: Ch. 10, §3; continued in Ch. 11, §§2–6.
- **Image content (not caption text).** A two-panel object. The northern panel covers Boston, New York, Saratoga, and the Canadian campaign. The southern and alliance panel covers the Carolinas, Georgia, Yorktown, and the French alliance. Both panels mark the date and the uncertain reconstruction of each route.
- **Argument.** The map shows that independence was won in a long, multi-front war that depended on French naval power and on Indigenous and enslaved people’s choices. It is not a story of one army marching from victory to victory.
- **Evidence.** The National Park Service’s Yorktown material and the Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation’s American Revolution Museum supply the Yorktown geography; the chapter’s sources carry the campaign and alliance evidence. The Library of Congress’s military map collections are the base for the period geography.[^app-c-map09-yorktown]
- **Production.** Keep the two panels as one object with one number. Mark the 1783 settlement only where it is shown. Distinguish British, American, French, and Indigenous forces. Use a dated key and an uncertainty line style. Do not imply that the war ended at Yorktown; the peace took two more years.
- **Check.** The map must not merge the northern and southern campaigns into one arrow. It must show the French fleet’s role and the war’s Caribbean and Atlantic dimensions where the panels allow.

**Map 10. Western claims and ratification, 1781–1791.** Owner: Ch. 12, §§1 and 5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The western land claims of the states and the Confederacy, the cessions that created the public domain, and the ratification of the Constitution. An inset shows the state ratifying conventions and the dates of their votes.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the Constitution’s jurisdiction was built on land claims that were contested by Indigenous nations and by the states themselves. Ratification was a political process, not a single national act.
- **Evidence.** The National Archives documents the ratification process and the Documentary History of the Ratification of the Constitution; the chapter’s sources carry the state claims and cessions.[^app-c-map10-ratification]
- **Production.** The main map stops at 1791. Later cessions belong in App. B, Table 3 or a clearly labeled later panel. The inset uses actual ratification dates, not a seaboard/backcountry stereotype. Distinguish a state claim from a federal cession from an Indigenous title.
- **Check.** The map must not show the 1783 treaty boundary as a settled jurisdiction. It must not imply that ratification was unanimous or that the western land was empty.

**Map 11. Louisiana and the expeditions, 1803–1806.** Owner: Ch. 14, §2.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The Louisiana Purchase’s claimed boundaries, the Lewis and Clark route, and the Pike expedition routes. It distinguishes the treaty’s claims from the routes actually traveled.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the purchase was a diplomatic claim to a vast and poorly known territory, not a survey. The expeditions were reconnaissance and diplomacy, not a completion of the claim.
- **Evidence.** The Avalon Project prints the 1803 treaty; the University of Nebraska’s Journals of the Lewis and Clark Expedition and the National Archives’ Pike edition supply the routes. Pike’s southwestern route still needs a direct source in the chapter.[^app-c-map11-louisiana]
- **Production.** Use a separate line style for treaty claims and traveled routes. Mark the date of each expedition segment. Do not draw a single boundary line through the Rocky Mountains as if it were surveyed. Show the Spanish and Indigenous presence.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that the purchase’s boundaries were accepted by Spain or by the nations who lived there. It must not include Pike without a source.

**Map 12. Canals, roads, and railroads, 1825–1840.** Owner: Ch. 15, §1.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The completed and projected canals, roads, and railroads of the 1820s and 1830s, with the Erie Canal crossing the Appalachian divide through the Mohawk corridor. It distinguishes completed from projected lines and dates every element.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the market revolution was a construction project with winners and losers. It also shows that the idea of an “American system” was a patchwork of state and private projects, not a single plan.
- **Evidence.** Henry Schenck Tanner’s 1840 *Description of the Canals and Rail Roads* and his 1840 map supply the period geography; the David Rumsey collection holds the map, and the Internet Archive holds the text.[^app-c-map12-tanner]
- **Production.** Date every line and distinguish completed, under construction, and projected. Show the Erie’s Mohawk crossing. Do not imply that the entire canal network stopped at the Appalachians; Pennsylvania and Ohio canals also crossed the mountains.
- **Check.** The map must not use a modern railroad map as a stand-in for the 1840 network. It must not treat a projected line as a completed one.

### 2.4 Expansion, Civil War, and the West (Maps 13–20)

- The maps in this group cover the period in which the United States acquired a continental empire, fought a civil war over slavery, and then abandoned Reconstruction. They show coerced movement, military geography, and the spatial pattern of political violence, and they close with the West as a set of competing land regimes.
- The group’s common problem is the difference between a legal claim and a lived reality. A removal route, a reservation boundary, a military district, and a county under martial law are not the same kind of object.

**Map 13. Removal routes and Indian Territory, 1830–1846.** Owner: Ch. 17, §2.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The origin nations, principal removal routes, and destinations of the southeastern nations, with the dates of the removals. It shows Indian Territory as a place that was already home to other nations.
- **Argument.** The map shows that removal was a coerced migration across a continent, not a transfer of empty land. It also shows that Indian Territory was not a blank destination; it was a crowded and contested political space.
- **Evidence.** The National Park Service’s Trail of Tears National Historic Trail and the Cherokee Nation’s history supply the routes and the Cherokee experience; the chapter’s sources carry the broader removal evidence.[^app-c-map13-trailoftears]
- **Production.** Distinguish documented routes from reconstructed directions. Mark the date of each removal. Show the nations already living in Indian Territory. Do not draw a single “Trail of Tears” line for all nations.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that removal was a single event or that the routes were chosen by the people being removed.

**Map 14. Mexican war and cession, 1846–1848.** Owner: Ch. 18, §§2–5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The disputed Nueces-versus-Río Grande boundary, the principal campaigns, and the 1848 cession. It dates its boundaries and distinguishes documented routes from reconstructed directions.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the war began in a disputed border zone and ended with a vast cession. It makes the land transfer visible without treating it as a natural expansion.
- **Evidence.** The National Park Service’s US–Mexican War materials document the campaigns; the National Archives’ Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo page documents the cession. The chapter’s sources carry the boundary dispute.[^app-c-map14-mexicanwar]
- **Production.** Show both the Nueces and the Río Grande claims. Mark the campaigns with dates. Omit the Gadsden Purchase except as clearly labeled post-1848 context. Distinguish the cession from the prewar Mexican states and Indigenous territories.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that the Río Grande boundary was settled before the war or that the cession included only “empty” land.

**Map 15. Kansas and the territories, 1854–1859.** Owner: Ch. 19, §3.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The Kansas and Nebraska territories after the 1854 act, the repealed Missouri Compromise line, the competing proslavery and free-state governments, and the routes of settlement and violence.
- **Argument.** The map shows that “popular sovereignty” produced competing governments and a local war, not a neutral vote. It also shows that the territorial boundaries were drawn by Congress, not by the people living there.
- **Evidence.** The National Archives’ Kansas-Nebraska Act milestone documents the statute; the chapter’s sources carry the Lecompton and Bleeding Kansas evidence.[^app-c-map15-kansas]
- **Production.** Date the boundaries and distinguish the federal territory from the competing governments. Mark the repealed Missouri Compromise line. Show the routes of settlement as broad bands, not precise trails.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that Kansas was a single political community or that the violence was spontaneous.

**Map 16. Election and secession, 1860–1861.** Owner: Ch. 20, §§1–4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The 1860 presidential election results and the stages of secession through the spring of 1861. It separates the February 1861 stage from later secession and distinguishes slave states, seceding states, and border states.
- **Argument.** The map shows that secession was a process with different timing in different states, not a single vote. It also shows that the election’s geography did not map neatly onto the secession’s geography.
- **Evidence.** The Library of Congress’s 1860 election resource guide and period maps supply the political geography; the chapter’s sources carry the returns and the secession ordinances. The map’s returns must be collated against state records, not a modern compilation alone.[^app-c-map16-election1860]
- **Production.** Use a dated sequence for secession. Distinguish the February 1861 stage from later secession. Do not use “slave state,” “seceding state,” and “border state” as synonyms. Show the election returns by state, not by county, unless the county returns are complete.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that Lincoln’s election alone caused secession or that all slave states seceded.

**Map 17. Civil War theaters, 1861–1863.** Owner: Ch. 21, §§2–3 and 6.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The eastern and western theaters, the Mississippi River line, and the movement of armies through 1863. It marks the major battles and the dates of the campaigns.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the war was won and lost across several theaters, not only in Virginia. It also shows how geography shaped supply, recruitment, and emancipation.
- **Evidence.** The National Park Service’s Civil War resources and the Library of Congress’s Civil War maps supply the campaign geography; the chapter’s sources carry the battle and emancipation evidence.[^app-c-map17-civilwar]
- **Production.** Distinguish the theaters with color and line style. Mark the Mississippi River line and the dates of the major campaigns. Do not imply that geography determined the outcome.
- **Check.** The map must not reduce the war to a single eastern arrow or omit the Trans-Mississippi theater.

**Map 18. The final campaigns, 1864–1865.** Owner: Ch. 22, §§1–2.

### 2.5 Empire, reform, and world wars (Maps 21–28)

- The maps in this group show the United States as an empire, a reforming state, and a world power. They distinguish annexation, occupation, and control; they show the geography of the world wars; and they show the alliances and deployments of the early Cold War.
- The common problem is legal form. A territory annexed by joint resolution, a base leased by treaty, a country occupied by marines, and an alliance commitment are not the same kind of possession.

**Map 21. American empire, 1898–1902.** Owner: Ch. 27, §§4–5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The territorial acquisitions and occupations of 1898–1902: Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Cuba, with the legal instruments that distinguished annexation, occupation, and control. It does not extend to the later base leases and treaty powers, which belong to Map 23.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the United States became an overseas empire through different legal instruments, not a single policy. It also shows that “empire” was a patchwork of sovereignty, occupation, and informal influence.
- **Evidence.** The Avalon Project prints the 1898 Treaty of Paris; the National Archives documents the Hawaii annexation; the chapter’s sources carry the Cuban and Philippine evidence.[^app-c-map21-empire]
- **Production.** Use a dated key and separate symbols for annexation, occupation, and treaty control. Mark contested boundaries. Do not color the entire Caribbean and Pacific one imperial color.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that Cuba was annexed or that the Philippines was a state.

**Map 22. Woman suffrage by state, 1916.** Owner: Ch. 29, §5.2.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The status of woman suffrage by state as of 1916: full suffrage, partial or presidential suffrage, and no suffrage. It states the state laws behind each category.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the franchise was a patchwork before the Nineteenth Amendment. It also shows that “suffrage” was not one thing; state law could grant presidential, municipal, school, or full voting rights.
- **Evidence.** The Library of Congress’s National American Woman Suffrage Association records and the chapter’s sources carry the state laws. A ca. 1919 map cannot stand in for 1916.[^app-c-map22-suffrage]
- **Production.** Separate full, partial, and no suffrage. Date every category and cite the state law. Do not merge municipal and school suffrage with full suffrage. Use a period base map with state boundaries.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that the Nineteenth Amendment was inevitable or that western suffrage was uniform.

**Map 23. American Caribbean, 1898–1917.** Owner: Ch. 30, §1.4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The bases, occupations, and treaty powers that made up the U.S. position in the Caribbean: Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Panama Canal Zone, Haiti, the Dominican Republic, and the Danish West Indies. It distinguishes a leased base from a customs receivership from a military government.
- **Argument.** The map shows that American power in the Caribbean was a set of different legal arrangements, not a single colony. It also shows that “informal empire” had formal instruments.
- **Evidence.** *Foreign Relations of the United States* and the treaty texts supply the instruments; the chapter’s sources carry the interventions. The 1917 Danish West Indies convention is a useful test case for the difference between purchase and sovereignty.[^app-c-map23-caribbean]
- **Production.** Use separate symbols for bases, occupations, customs receiverships, and treaty rights. State the date of each arrangement. Do not imply that all Caribbean interventions were identical.
- **Check.** The map must not use “American empire” as a single color or omit the local governments and resistance.

**Map 24. The AEF in France, 1918.** Owner: Ch. 31, §2.4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The major engagements of the American Expeditionary Forces in France in 1918: Cantigny, Belleau Wood, Château-Thierry, Saint-Mihiel, and the Meuse-Argonne. It distinguishes American sectors from Allied sectors and dates each engagement.

- **Evidence.** The American Battle Monuments Commission’s Meuse-Argonne materials and the National Archives’ World War I records supply the geography; the chapter’s sources carry the campaign and casualty evidence.[^app-c-map24-aef]
- **Production.** Mark the engagements with dates. Distinguish American and Allied sectors. Do not shade the whole Western Front one color. Use a period base map with the 1918 front.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that the AEF won the war alone or that the Meuse-Argonne was the only American campaign.

**Map 25. The Great Migration, 1910–1930.** Owner: Ch. 32, §5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The origins, destinations, and city growth of the Great Migration between 1910 and 1930. It distinguishes the movement’s volume from any claim about migrants’ motives.
- **Argument.** The map shows a great migration of Black people from the rural South to cities in the North, Midwest, and West. It also shows that the migration was a demographic transformation, not a single journey.

**Map 26. New Deal landscape, 1933–1939.** Owner: Ch. 34, §§1.5 and 3.5.

- **Argument.** The map shows that the New Deal’s reach was uneven and that the geography of protection was not the same as the geography of need. It also shows that migration was part of the New Deal landscape.

- **Check.** The map must not merge the European and Pacific wars into one arrow or omit the Mediterranean.

**Map 28. Containment’s geography, 1945–1952.** Owner: Ch. 37, §2.

- **Argument.** The map shows that containment was a global system of bases, treaties, and alliances, not only a diplomatic doctrine. It also shows that the system was incomplete and contested.
- **Evidence.** NATO’s member-country record and *Foreign Relations of the United States* supply the alliance and base evidence; the chapter’s sources carry the Korean War and domestic politics.[^app-c-map28-containment]
- **Production.** Use a 1952 snapshot. Distinguish NATO members, bilateral treaties, occupied territories, and bases. Do not imply that the map covers the 1953 Korean armistice or later alliances.
- **Check.** The map must not treat all commitments as equivalent or show later NATO members as 1952 members.

### 2.6 Postwar and recent United States (Maps 29–39)

- The postwar maps show a country reshaped by migration, suburbanization, rights movements, deindustrialization, and a changing world role. They also show the limits of the map as evidence: a county-level pattern cannot prove a motive or a cause.
- The group uses census, election, business, and lending data. Each map must state its geographic unit, observation year, denominator, and data limitations.

**Map 29. Migration and metropolitan growth, 1940–1960.** Owner: Ch. 38, §2.5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** Interstate migration, metropolitan growth, and the Great Migration between 1940 and 1960. It distinguishes total population change from Black population change.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the postwar United States was remade by movement: from the South to the North and West, from cities to suburbs, and from farm to factory. It also shows that the movement was not one direction.
- **Evidence.** The Census Bureau’s regional and county series and Appendix B, Table 1 supply the population data; the chapter’s sources carry the policy and cultural evidence.[^app-c-map29-metro]
- **Production.** State the observation years and geographic units. Distinguish interstate migration, metropolitan growth, and the Great Migration. Do not infer individual motives from aggregate movement.
- **Check.** The map must not treat “the suburbs” as a single place or assume that all population loss was white flight.

**Map 30. Civil rights campaigns and registration, 1960–1968.** Owner: Ch. 39, §§1–6.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The geography of sit-in cities, freedom-ride routes, and voter-registration sites, and the uneven change in Black registration. It distinguishes campaign geography from registration results.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the civil rights movement was a national geography of local campaigns and that voting rights changed unevenly. It also shows that protest and registration were related but not the same.
- **Evidence.** The Voter Education Project records and the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights series supply the registration data; the chapter’s sources carry the campaign evidence. The registration denominator is the relevant Black voting-age population, with exact base dates.[^app-c-map30-vep]
- **Production.** State the registration denominator and base dates. Distinguish campaign sites from registration rates. Do not extend the series past 1968 without a labeled panel. Use a period base map with county boundaries.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that a campaign caused a registration change without a documented mechanism.

**Map 31. Poverty and Great Society programs, 1960–1968.** Owner: Ch. 40, §2.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The geography of measured poverty against the placement of major Great Society programs, distinguishing where need was measured from where programs operated.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the War on Poverty was designed and administered in a geography that only partly matched the geography of need. It also shows that program placement was a political and administrative choice.
- **Evidence.** The Census Bureau’s poverty reconstruction, Office of Economic Opportunity records, and Appalachian Regional Commission records supply the data; Appendix B, Table 7 supplies the economic context.[^app-c-map31-poverty]
- **Production.** Identify the retrospective poverty definition and threshold. State the observation years and geographic units. Distinguish need from provision. Do not infer individual benefit from aggregate geography.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that a program’s presence proved its effect or that poverty was evenly distributed.

**Map 32. Southeast Asia and supply routes, 1954–1968.** Owner: Ch. 41, §4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The borders of North Vietnam, South Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, the major bases, and the Ho Chi Minh Trail. It states the map’s date and distinguishes the trail from other supply routes.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the Vietnam War was fought in a region of porous borders and external supply lines. It also shows that the American bombing campaign could not close those routes.
- **Evidence.** The Pentagon Papers and *Foreign Relations of the United States* supply the documentary record; the chapter’s sources carry the military and political evidence.[^app-c-map32-vietnam]
- **Production.** State the date of the borders and bases. Label the Ho Chi Minh Trail and distinguish it from other routes. Do not imply volume or success of infiltration from a boundary line.
- **Check.** The map must not treat Laos and Cambodia as incidental or imply that the trail was a single road.

**Map 33. Elections of 1968 and 1972.** Owner: Ch. 42, §1 and Legacies.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The 1968 three-way presidential race and the 1972 landslide side by side, with popular and electoral votes distinguished.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the same electorate produced a narrow three-way result and a landslide in four years. It also shows that electoral geography is not a map of motives.
- **Evidence.** The MIT Election Data and Science Lab’s county returns and the Federal Election Commission’s official results supply the vote data; Appendix B, Table 2 supplies the election series.[^app-c-map33-elections]
- **Production.** State the years, units, and normalization. Distinguish popular from electoral votes. Do not imply that Wallace voters became Nixon voters.
- **Check.** The map must not read a coalition off the returns or treat county totals as individual behavior.

**Map 34. Where the work went, 1969–1979.** Owner: Ch. 43, §1.
- **Image content (not caption text).** County-level employment change in the 1970s, with absolute change, percentage change, and population change distinguished.
- **Argument.** The map shows that deindustrialization and growth were uneven and that a small county with high percentage growth was not necessarily the largest absolute gain.
- **Evidence.** County Business Patterns and Appendix B, Table 7 supply the employment data; the chapter’s sources carry the political economy.[^app-c-map34-cbp]
- **Production.** State the dates and geographic units. Use separate panels for absolute change, percentage change, and population change. Do not let a small-count area dominate the color scale.
- **Check.** The map must not read a causal story off the pattern or compare incompatible county boundaries.

**Map 35. Eastern Europe and the USSR, 1989–1991.** Owner: Ch. 44, §§3 and 5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The different paths and dates of regime change in Eastern Europe and the USSR between 1989 and 1991, with state boundaries, declared independence, and uncertain reconstructions distinguished.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the end of the Cold War was a sequence of different national paths, not a single collapse. It also shows that the map of Europe was being redrawn while the outcome was still open.

- **Check.** The map must not imply that the outcome was known in advance or that all Soviet republics followed the same path.

**Map 36. Where the boom landed, 1992–2000.** Owner: Ch. 45, §1.
- **Image content (not caption text).** County-level population and employment growth in the 1990s, with measured growth, estimates, and areas the data do not cover distinguished.

- **Evidence.** County Business Patterns and the 2000 census supply the data; Appendix B, Table 7 supplies the economic context.[^app-c-map36-cbp]
- **Production.** State the dates and geographic units. Distinguish population growth from employment growth. Do not read a causal story off the pattern.
- **Check.** The map must not let a small-count area with high percentage growth masquerade as the largest absolute gain.

**Map 37. Housing boom and crisis, 2000–2008.** Owner: Ch. 46, §5.
- **Image content (not caption text).** The geography of mortgage lending and housing exposure in the 2000–2008 window, with the data series and geographic units stated.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the housing boom and crisis had a geography: some places lent and borrowed more, and some places were more exposed to foreclosure. It also shows that the map cannot by itself explain why.
- **Evidence.** Home Mortgage Disclosure Act data and the American Community Survey supply the lending and population data; dated foreclosure records supply the outcome evidence. The chapter’s sources carry the policy narrative.[^app-c-map37-hmda]
- **Production.** State the dates, geographic units, and data series. Distinguish measured lending from areas the data do not cover. Do not infer individual risk from a county average.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that subprime lending was the only cause or that every high-lending county experienced the same outcome.

**Map 38. Electoral geography, 2000–2024.** Owner: Ch. 47, §1; reused in Chs. 48–49.
- **Image content (not caption text).** Three dated panels of presidential election geography: through 2016 in Chapter 47, through 2020 in Chapter 48, and through 2024 in Chapter 49. It shows changing spatial coalitions, not voter motives.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the parties’ geographic bases changed over two decades. It also shows that an election map is an aggregated result, not a transcript of why people voted.
- **Evidence.** The MIT Election Data and Science Lab’s county returns, the Federal Election Commission, and state canvasses supply the vote data; census geography supplies the joins.[^app-c-map38-mit]
- **Production.** Keep the panels dated and do not let later results leak into an earlier chapter’s panel. State the years, geographic units, and normalization. Distinguish a change in the vote from a change in the electorate.
- **Check.** The map must not imply that a county’s shift proves a voter’s motive or that the 2024 panel explains 2016.

**Map 39. County economies and the border, 2023.** Owner: Ch. 49, §§1 and 4.
- **Image content (not caption text).** County-level economic output and the border or trade exposure measures that the chapter discusses, with sector and tariff measures sourced separately.
- **Argument.** The map shows that the border economy is not a single thing: some counties depend on trade, some on manufacturing, some on services, and some on public employment. It also shows that county output alone does not establish a “tech corridor” or a trade shock.
- **Evidence.** The Bureau of Economic Analysis’s county GDP data and County Business Patterns supply the output and employment data; the chapter’s sources carry the trade and border evidence.[^app-c-map39-bea]
- **Production.** Source sector and tariff or border measures separately. State the year and geographic units. Do not infer exposure from output alone.
- **Check.** The map must not treat a county’s GDP as a measure of household income or a tariff’s effect.

The table is a reading aid, not a second history. It will carry the caption “App. C, Table 1. Gazetteer of places” and will be cited in the appendix text as Table 1.

The book does not use a colonized name as the default when a nation’s name is known and appropriate. The README inventory lists some entries as “Guam/Guåhan,” “Hawaii/Hawaiʻi,” or “San Juan Pueblo/Ohkay Owingeh”; the table prints the nation’s name first and the alternate in parentheses.
- Locations and federal names come from the U.S. Board on Geographic Names and the Geographic Names Information System. The gazetteer does not print coordinates for every row; it prints the present jurisdiction and, where useful, a short locational phrase. Coordinates are reserved for the production record.[^app-c-gaz-gnis]
- Historical notes summarize a claim already argued in the cited chapter. They do not introduce a new interpretation or a new statistic. Where a note needs a fact not in the chapter, it carries its own footnote.
- The gazetteer distinguishes a present-day city from a historical settlement, a nation from a reservation, and a region from a legal jurisdiction. “Cherokee Nation” is a sovereign government, not a generic place. “Indian Territory” was a legal and political space, not a single tribe’s homeland. “Dinétah” is a homeland, not a reservation boundary.
- The table’s source line will name the Gazetteer’s base source, the Indigenous-name sources, and the chapter cross-references. It will not claim that a single source supports every row. The place name in each row’s leading cell will be bold, as the book’s table convention requires.

### 5.3 Names, dates, and changing meanings

- The gazetteer uses the book’s date policy. Julian dates through September 2, 1752, and Gregorian dates from September 14, 1752. A date that a source records in a different calendar is given as the source records it, with a note when the difference matters.
- The gazetteer does not convert a colonial place name into a modern one silently. “New Amsterdam” becomes “New York” in 1664; the entry says so. “San Juan Pueblo” becomes “Ohkay Owingeh” in 2005; the entry says so. “Indian Territory” becomes Oklahoma in 1907; the entry says so.
- Indigenous names are not synonyms for colonial names. Where the book uses an Indigenous name, it gives the nation and the source. Where the book uses a colonial name, it says why. The gazetteer does not treat a name as a legal claim.
- The gazetteer distinguishes a nation from a place. “Cherokee Nation” is a sovereign government with a capital at Tahlequah; “Cherokee” is also a people and a language. “Dinétah” is a homeland, not a reservation. “Tsenacomoco” is a paramount chiefdom, not a tribe.
- The gazetteer does not print a single date for a place as if the place had one founding. A place can be a Indigenous settlement, a colonial town, a battlefield, and a modern city. The note gives the relevant dates for the chapter’s argument.

## 6. Production, Rights, and Access

### 6.1 Base mapping and projection

- Every original map will state its projection in the production record. The printed caption need not name the projection unless the projection changes the argument. A world map, a continental map, and a city map cannot share one projection without distortion.
- The base geometry is public domain: Natural Earth for global and continental maps, Census cartographic boundaries for current U.S. maps, and the Newberry Atlas for historical county boundaries. The production record names the version and download date.[^app-c-projection]
- Historical maps that are redrawn will credit the original map and the repository. A redrawn map is an original compilation, not a facsimile. The catalog says “drawn for this volume from” and names the sources.
- The maps will use a consistent palette and type family. Color will not be the only carrier of meaning; patterns, labels, and line styles will also distinguish categories. This is an accessibility requirement, not a decorative preference.

### 6.2 Captions, source lines, and alt text

- Every map, figure, and image gets a short HTML caption, a source line, a credit line, and alt text. The caption states what the visual shows and its date. The source line names the data or object and the repository. The credit line names the maker or institution. The alt text describes the subject and scope, not the file name.[^app-c-caption-policy]
- The caption, source line, credit, and alt text are HTML, not image content. The figure number and title may begin the `<figcaption>`; the source and credit follow in a separate `<p>` or a second paragraph inside the `<figcaption>`. Map labels, legends, scale bars, axis labels, and data labels may be inside the image; a separate title, caption, source line, or credit may not.
- A caption is not a title. “Map 12. Canals, roads, and railroads, 1825–1840” is a title. The caption adds the argument: “Completed and projected routes show the Erie Canal crossing the Appalachian divide through the Mohawk corridor, while Pennsylvania and Ohio canals also crossed the mountains.”
- The source line distinguishes a primary source from a compilation. “Drawn for this volume from Henry Schenck Tanner’s 1840 map and description” is a source line. “Library of Congress” alone is not.
- Alt text is 140 characters or fewer. It begins with the subject, then describes the pattern or scope. It does not repeat the caption or invent inaccessible detail. For a map with many labels, describe the regions and the key, not every label.[^app-c-alt-text]
- Use the approved caption and alt text in §§2.7, 3.3, and 4.5. Do not reword a string in one chapter without updating the schedule, the chapter, and the README register.
- Every visual is cited by number in the chapter text. The catalog repeats the number and the owning section. A visual that is never cited in the text is a production error, not a decoration.

### 6.3 Rights and community authorization

- Original maps, charts, and comparison tables are drawn for the volume. Their copyright is held by the publisher or author as the contract provides. The underlying data may carry its own terms; the production record states them.
- Third-party maps, photographs, and facsimiles require a rights review. A public-domain determination for one object does not cover a repository’s photograph of it, a modern facsimile’s editorial matter, or a compilation’s annotations.[^app-c-rights-policy]
- Community-held objects require community authorization. The Hiawatha Belt is the clearest case: the Onondaga Nation’s account is a source for its meaning, not a license to reproduce the object. The same rule applies to Indigenous photographs, songs, and designs.
- The AIDS Memorial Quilt panel requires the maker’s or caretaker’s consent and a credit line. The Louisiana literacy test requires an exact provenance and a rights basis. The Dorian display image requires a rights-cleared photograph, not a news screenshot.
- If permission fails, withdraw the image in the README register and the chapter, and retain a sourced textual close reading where appropriate. Do not substitute an unregistered image.

### 6.4 Production sequence

- **Step 1. Freeze the list.** Confirm the 39 maps, nine figures, eight images, and one table against the README register. No new visual enters the appendix without a register entry.
- **Step 2. Build the data record.** For each map and figure, save the source series, transformations, definitions, and date of access. For each image, save the object identifier, rights basis, and credit line.
- **Step 3. Draw a proof.** Produce a low-resolution proof with labels and a legend. Check the proof against the chapter’s argument, not only against the data.
- **Step 4. Check the caption and alt text.** Read the caption aloud. Confirm that it states the date, the units, and the limitation. Confirm that the alt text describes the pattern.
- **Step 5. Clear rights.** Complete the rights record before the final art is placed. A proof is not a publication license.
- **Step 6. Inspect the rendered page.** Check the visual at the final trim size, in grayscale, and at the intended screen resolution. Confirm that the labels are legible and that the note links work.
- **Step 7. Record the result.** Update the README register to `produced` or `verified`, with the date and the remaining limitation. Do not mark a visual `verified` because the file exists.
