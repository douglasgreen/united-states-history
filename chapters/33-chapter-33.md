# Chapter 33. The Great Depression (1929–1933)

> “We were challenged with a peace-time choice between the American system of rugged individualism and a European philosophy of diametrically opposed doctrines …”[^ch33-hoover-rugged]
>
> — Herbert Hoover, campaign address in New York City, October 22, 1928.[^ch33-hoover-rugged]

> “Regarding the Great Depression. You’re right, we did it. We’re very sorry. But thanks to you, we won’t do it again.”[^ch33-bernanke-remarks]
>
> — Ben Bernanke, remarks at the Conference to Honor Milton Friedman, University of Chicago, November 8, 2002.[^ch33-bernanke-remarks]

> “We argue that the most important barrier to actions that would have arrested or reversed the decline was the mentality of the gold standard.”[^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
>
> — Barry Eichengreen and Peter Temin, “The Gold Standard and the Great Depression,” *Contemporary European History*, 2000.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

A president said the American system rested on individual initiative and must not be replaced by paternalism. A later central banker said the Federal Reserve’s own leaders caused the disaster and would not repeat it. Two economic historians said the decisive obstacle was an idea — the gold-standard mentality — that made leaders afraid to act. The three quotations disagree about whether the collapse came from individual character, from a failure of central banking, or from an international monetary doctrine. That disagreement is the chapter’s subject.[^ch33-check-epigraphs]

On December 11, 1930, the Bank of the United States, a commercial bank despite its name, ceased operations. It was the fourth-largest bank in New York City, and its deposits of about $200 million made it the largest bank failure in American history to that date. The bank had been negotiating a merger with another institution, and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York had helped search for a partner. When the negotiations broke down, depositors rushed to withdraw their funds, and New York’s superintendent of banking closed the institution.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

The run had begun with a rumor. A depositor at a Bronx branch asked the bank to buy back shares it had sold him. When the manager refused, the depositor left and spread word that the Bank of the United States was insolvent. The bank had expanded from four Manhattan branches to fifty-seven branches across the boroughs, and it served immigrant depositors whom other banks often ignored. Its name sounded official, and outside New York many people associated it with the federal government. Within hours, thousands of depositors lined up outside the Bronx branch. In the following days, thousands more withdrew their savings across the city.[^ch33-gotham-bank]

Other banks did not come to the rescue. One banker explained the refusal by saying the Bank of the United States “catered to immigrants.” The phrase is quoted because it explains why help was withheld, not because it describes the bank accurately. It mixed ethnic contempt with the conviction that the bank’s depositors were not worth saving. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York hesitated, and the merger talks collapsed. The failure revived a panic that had begun in November, when the collapse of Caldwell and Company and its Bank of Tennessee affiliate in Nashville had spread runs through the South.[^ch33-gotham-bank][^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-race-bank]

The date, the institution, its size, the failed merger, the run, and the state closure are documented. The weather, the bank’s interior, the queue, and what any depositor said are not. No such account is cited here. In December 1930 no one knew that the failure would become an emblem of a decade-long depression, how many more banks would fail, or that deposits would eventually be insured.[^ch33-check-scene]

How did a financial breakdown become a prolonged social catastrophe?

The chapter covers the years from 1929 to 1933. It asks how a contraction that began in the summer of 1929 became the deepest economic disaster in American history, and it follows the collapse through the banking panics, the international gold standard, the loss of farms and jobs, the relief crisis, Herbert Hoover’s response, and the election of 1932. It draws on federal statutes and agency records, Federal Reserve and banking histories, census and labor statistics, farm-mortgage and foreclosure studies, period newspapers, presidential papers, and the secondary literature on the Depression. It treats “depression,” “panic,” “sound money,” “voluntarism,” “relief,” and “recovery” as contested period terms that require definition rather than neutral descriptions. By “depression” the chapter means a prolonged contraction of output and employment, not a single bad year. It also states plainly that the statistical record was built by agencies that counted some people and not others: the unemployment series excluded farmers, foreclosure counts depended on legal definitions, and the poorest and most marginal left the fewest records.[^ch33-check-silences]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the uneven boom and the crash of 1929. §2 follows the banking panics and the international transmission of deflation under the gold standard, and presents Figure 7. §3 turns to unemployment, farm foreclosures, hunger, and the first drought, and reads a trustee’s sale notice in the In Focus. §4 examines Hoover’s responses and their limits. §5 traces the political crisis and the election of 1932. §6 asks how historians have explained the causes and transmission of the Depression. The chapter begins on December 11, 1930, when depositors rushed the Bank of the United States and the largest bank failure in American history to that date began. It ends in March 1933, when the banking system stood on the edge of shutdown and the New Deal had not yet begun.

## 1. The boom and crash

### 1.1 A crash that punctuated a contraction

The stock market crash of October 1929 is the event most Americans associate with the start of the Great Depression. It was not the beginning. The contraction began in the summer of 1929, before the market broke, and the crash was both a symptom of the weakening economy and an accelerant of its decline. The banking panics that began in November 1930 did far more than the crash to turn a severe recession into a decade-long catastrophe.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-frh-banking-panics]

Three connected stories belong to this account: the uneven prosperity of the 1920s, the market boom and crash, and the year of contraction that followed. They are not three separate stories. The first explains the setting, the second explains the shock, and the third explains why the shock became a depression.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-check-ch32]

Index levels, margin rates, production, and employment are measurable. They cannot show that the crash “caused” the Depression, and causation is a question for §6, not a premise. A chronology can establish that two events happened in sequence. It cannot establish which one caused the other.[^ch33-check-numbers]

### 1.2 The uneven boom

The prosperity of the 1920s was real and unequal. By 1928 the top 1 percent of families received 23.9 percent of all pretax income, while the bottom 90 percent received 50.7 percent. The figures come from federal tax data and define income as pretax cash market income; they describe families, not individuals. They do not by themselves prove that inequality caused the crash. They do show that the gains of the decade were concentrated at the top, and they frame the question of whether the mass market could absorb what mass production turned out.[^ch33-pew-inequality][^ch33-check-inequality]

Agriculture had been in depression for much of the decade. Farm mortgage debt rose from about $3.2 billion in 1910 to about $9.6 billion by 1930, even as the number of farms fell from about 6.4 million to about 6.3 million. Farmers who had expanded during the First World War faced falling commodity prices, high debt, and tariffs that protected the goods they bought more than the crops they sold. Chapter 32 follows that story. Here it is the inheritance: rural distress preceded the 1929 crash, and it made the farm belt vulnerable when credit tightened.[^ch33-usda-farm-history][^ch33-check-ch32]

Installment buying and margin accounts tied household and investor finances to borrowed money. Consumers bought cars, furniture, and appliances on time; investors bought stocks with loans secured by the stocks themselves. The debt was not a moral failure. It was a structural feature of the boom that made spending and asset prices sensitive to bad news. It was the material inheritance of the 1920s, not a single cause of the Depression.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-check-ch32]

### 1.3 The crash of October 1929

The bull market was extraordinary. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose from 63 in August 1921 to 381 in September 1929. Purchasers of stock on margin typically put down about 10 percent and borrowed the rest, and the borrowed money pushed prices higher. On October 16, 1929, the economist Irving Fisher told a dinner audience that stock prices had reached “what looks like a permanently high plateau.” The quotation is a period artifact, not a verdict on Fisher’s career; it shows how credible the boom still seemed to experts.[^ch33-frh-crash]

The collapse came in two days. On Black Monday, October 28, 1929, the Dow declined nearly 13 percent. On Black Tuesday, October 29, it fell nearly 12 percent. By mid-November the Dow had lost almost half its value. It continued to fall through the summer of 1932, when it closed at 41.22, its lowest value of the twentieth century and 89 percent below its peak. The market did not return to its pre-crash heights until November 1954.[^ch33-frh-crash]

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York responded. It bought government securities on the open market, lent through the discount window, and lowered its discount rate. The actions were controversial at the time because they exceeded what some officials thought the New York Fed’s authority allowed. In hindsight they helped contain the immediate crisis: the market collapsed, but banks near the center of the storm remained open. The contrast with what followed is the point. The New York Fed acted in 1929; the system did not act as a lender of last resort after 1930.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-check-sources]

Prices, dates, margin rates, and official actions are documented. What a given investor or saver felt is not. The gap between the market record and the experience of loss remains.[^ch33-check-silences]

### 1.4 From crash to contraction

After the crash the economy contracted. Industrial production and durable-goods output fell, firms slowed production, and unemployment rose. The downturn was severe, but it initially resembled earlier recessions, and by the fall of 1930 recovery still appeared possible. The Federal Reserve’s own history notes that the previous three contractions had lasted an average of fifteen months and that the downturn that began in summer 1929 had lasted fifteen months by late 1930. A rapid recovery was anticipated. Recovery meant a return to the output and employment of 1929, not merely the end of the decline.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-frh-crash]

Policy had already made the recession worse. The Federal Reserve raised interest rates in 1928 and 1929 to curb speculation in the stock market. Because the international gold standard linked monetary policies, the increases forced foreign central banks to raise their own rates, and tight money tipped economies around the world into recession. The Fed’s leaders did not intend a world depression. They intended to restrain a stock boom. The consequence was a decision with international effects that its authors did not foresee.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-state-gold]

The turning point came in November 1930. The collapse of Caldwell and Company, the largest financial holding company in the South, and the failure of its Bank of Tennessee affiliate in Nashville set off runs that spread through correspondent networks across Tennessee, Kentucky, Arkansas, and North Carolina. The Bank of the United States failed in December. The banking crises had begun, and they changed the trajectory of the contraction.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

## 2. Banking and international contagion

### 2.1 A credit system and a monetary idea

The Depression deepened through two connected failures. The first was domestic: a banking system that could not stop runs once they began. The second was international: a gold standard that forced governments to defend their currencies by tightening credit and cutting wages in the middle of a slump. The two failures reinforced each other. A banking panic shrank the money supply and pushed prices down; the gold standard made governments treat the resulting gold outflows as a reason to tighten further.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

The banking system’s weaknesses were structural, not a story of individual recklessness. When the crises began, more than 8,000 commercial banks belonged to the Federal Reserve System, and nearly 16,000 did not. Nonmember banks operated in an environment similar to the one before the Fed’s creation. They counted checks in the process of collection as cash reserves, so the same funds appeared on the books of two banks. Bankers called these “fictitious reserves.” Country banks kept much of their reserves as deposits in correspondent banks in reserve cities, and those correspondents kept reserves with other correspondents. The pyramid limited country banks’ access to cash in a panic: when a bank needed funds, its correspondent might be facing runs of its own, and its reserves might consist of checks in the mail.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics]

The Federal Reserve was supposed to be the lender of last resort, but its leaders disagreed about what that meant. Some subscribed to a doctrine similar to Walter Bagehot’s dictum that a central bank should lend freely to solvent institutions in a panic. Others followed the “real bills” doctrine, which held that credit should expand with production and contract with demand, and some carried it to a “liquidationist” extreme: let weak banks fail so the system could be purified. The system was decentralized, and district banks pursued different policies. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta lent aggressively to member banks and encouraged them to lend to nonmember correspondents; the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis refused to rediscount for nonmember banks. The differences mattered: after the first crisis, the Atlanta district recovered faster, and the St. Louis district suffered more failures.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-frh-banking-panics]

Failures, deposit losses, currency hoarding, and money-supply figures are documented. They cannot show, from the aggregates alone, which failures were caused by panic and which by insolvency. The Bank of the United States is the clearest case: some scholars attribute its failure to the panic, while others believe it was insolvent when it closed. That dispute remains unresolved.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-debate-works]

### 2.2 Figure 7: banking, unemployment, and prices

Figure 7 plots three series for 1929 through 1933: annual bank suspensions, two measures of unemployment, and the fall of prices. The bank-suspension series uses the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation’s counts: about 1,350 suspensions in 1930, about 2,300 in 1931, about 1,450 in 1932, and about 4,000 in 1933. The unemployment series are the Lebergott and Darby measures, plotted side by side because they answer different questions. The price series shows the consumer and wholesale indexes, with their base years stated in the caption.[^ch33-check-figure7][^ch33-check-figuredata][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-stlouisfed-impact]

The three series together make the collapse legible. Bank suspensions rise in waves, unemployment climbs, and prices fall. The alignment is the mechanism by which a banking crisis became a deflationary spiral: failures destroyed deposits, depositors hoarded cash, banks accumulated reserves, the money stock fell, and prices followed. The figure cannot prove the direction of causation. It can show that the three movements belonged to one event.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-figure7]

### 2.3 The banking panics, 1930–1933

A banking panic begins when depositors at many banks try to withdraw their funds at once, because each fears what the others will do. The first crisis began in November 1930. The Bank of Tennessee closed on November 7; affiliates in Knoxville and Louisville failed within days. Runs spread through Tennessee, Kentucky, Arkansas, and North Carolina. There were 256 suspensions in November and 352 in December; about 1,350 banks suspended across 1930. About a third of the suspended banks reopened within a few months, but most were liquidated.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

The Bank of the United States failed on December 11, and its failure revived the panic. Depositors withdrew from other banks, and the fear spread beyond the neighborhoods the bank served.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-gotham-bank]

A second crisis ran from April to August 1931 and was concentrated in the Federal Reserve districts of Chicago, Minneapolis, Cleveland, and Kansas City. There were 563 suspensions. A third crisis began after Great Britain left the gold standard on September 21, 1931. Unlike the first two, it was nationwide: the banking system faced an external drain of gold and an internal drain of currency. Between September and October, 817 banks suspended. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York raised its discount rate on October 9 to defend the dollar, and the higher rates added to the pressure on domestic banks.[^ch33-fdic-1930s]

The fourth crisis came in the winter of 1933. About 4,000 banks suspended during the year, most of them before the middle of March. By the time of Roosevelt’s inauguration on March 4, 48 states had declared a statewide bank holiday or restricted withdrawals. The banking system was in disarray, and the new administration had not yet taken office.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-dates]

Suspension counts, dates, and deposit losses are documented. What a depositor thought while waiting in line, or what a bank officer said to a regulator, is not. That silence is part of the record.[^ch33-check-silences]

### 2.4 The gold standard and international contagion

The interwar gold standard was the second failure. Under the gold standard, a country losing gold had to restrict credit and reduce prices and wages to restore its external balance. In the politicized labor markets of the 1920s and 1930s, wages were less flexible than they had been before the war. The adjustment therefore fell on output and employment instead of on pay. The result was deflation, bankruptcy, and unemployment.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

The system had been restored after the First World War, but it was fragile. The United States, Britain, France, and other countries held reserves and made policy in ways that drained gold from weaker economies. The United States held about 40 percent of the world’s monetary gold after the First World War, and France’s undervalued franc attracted gold after 1928. Countries that lost gold tightened credit to keep their currencies convertible.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-state-gold]

Britain left the gold standard on September 21, 1931. The United States stayed on gold until 1933. The London Economic Conference of 1933 failed to reach a collective agreement, and the depression dragged on. The chronology matters: the countries that left gold earlier generally recovered earlier, and the countries that stayed on gold suffered longer. That pattern is the core of Eichengreen and Temin’s argument.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

The domestic link is the clearest case of an international monetary rule producing a domestic disaster. When Britain left gold in September 1931, speculators turned to the dollar. The Federal Reserve raised its discount rate on October 9 to defend the dollar’s parity, and the increase intensified the domestic banking crisis. The Fed was not acting out of ignorance. It was acting within a framework that made defending the currency the first duty of central banking. The gold-standard mentality was not a personal flaw; it was a set of ideas that made the crisis worse. Defenders called the commitment to gold “sound money”; critics called it deflation by another name. The gold standard was one transmission channel among several, not the single cause of the Depression.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-check-gold]

### 2.5 Money, credit, and deflation

The banking collapse shrank the money supply. From the fall of 1930 through the winter of 1933, the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent; one estimate puts the fall at 30.9 percent from its 1929 level. The fall was not a single decision. It was the result of bank failures, depositor hoarding, and banks’ decisions to hold excess reserves rather than lend.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-smiley-econlib]

Deflation followed. Consumer prices fell about 25 percent and wholesale prices about 32 percent between 1929 and 1933. Deflation increased the real burden of every debt. A farmer who owed $10,000 owed the same number of dollars, but those dollars bought more grain, more livestock, and more labor. Firms and banks faced the same arithmetic. Deflation forced bankruptcies, distorted decisions, reduced consumption, and increased unemployment.[^ch33-stlouisfed-impact][^ch33-frh-great-depression]

Bank failures also destroyed credit intermediation. Banks held local knowledge about who was creditworthy, and when they failed, that knowledge disappeared. Even solvent firms could not obtain credit at any price. The credit channel is distinct from the money-supply channel, and it is one reason the collapse was so deep.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-debate-works]

The output fall was enormous. Real gross national product fell about 30.5 percent between 1929 and 1933, and real gross domestic product fell about 29 percent. The two series are not the same, and “output fell by a third” is not a single number.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-stlouisfed-impact][^ch33-check-numbers]

## 3. Unemployment, farms, hunger, and early drought

### 3.1 Catastrophe measured and unmeasured

The Depression’s human cost fell unequally, and it cannot be fully read from the statistics. The counts themselves were built with definitions that excluded some of the suffering. The unemployment series did not count farmers as unemployed. Foreclosure counts depended on legal definitions and on which sales were recorded. The poorest and most marginal left the fewest records. Unemployment, farm loss, hunger and relief, and the first drought were overlapping faces of one catastrophe, not separate social problems.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-check-unemployment]

Rates, counts, and acreages are documented. How families managed, what they ate, and how they understood their losses are not. Letters, testimony, and newspaper records do that where they exist, and the silence remains where they do not.[^ch33-check-silences]

### 3.2 Unemployment and its measurement

At the height of the Depression in 1933, 24.9 percent of the total work force — about 12,830,000 people — was unemployed, and farmers were not counted among the unemployed. That is the Lebergott convention, and it is the figure most often quoted. It is not the only defensible measure.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment]

The Lebergott series rises from about 3.2 percent in 1929 to about 25.2 percent in 1933. The Darby series, which counts people on work relief as employed, rises from about 5.9 percent to about 19.2 percent. The difference is not a data error. It is a definitional choice about whether a person who is working for a relief agency is employed or unemployed. Both measures belong in the account, because a single unqualified “25 percent” conceals the choice.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment][^ch33-check-unemployment]

The base of each rate is the civilian labor force; the Lebergott convention did not count farmers as unemployed, and the figures are annual averages, not a single date. A rate without a denominator is not a measurement.[^ch33-margo-unemployment][^ch33-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Farms and foreclosures

Farm families had suffered through the 1920s, and the Depression drove a wave of foreclosures that ruined owners and tenants alike. The farm foreclosure rate was especially high from 1932 through 1934, peaking at nearly 39 foreclosures per 1,000 farms in 1933. An average of more than 100,000 farm mortgages entered foreclosure each year from 1926 to 1940. The numbers are large, but they are also legal counts: they record the farms that entered foreclosure, not every farm that was lost or every family that left.[^ch33-usda-farm-history][^ch33-wheellock-moratoria][^ch33-check-numbers]

The regional pattern was uneven. In Iowa, about 13 percent of farmland was foreclosed on one or more times between 1921 and 1932. The figure shows how far back the distress ran and how heavily it fell on one state. It is state-level evidence, not a national rate.[^ch33-alston-foreclosures]

Farmers organized against foreclosure sales. They crowded auctions to discourage bids, and sometimes they stopped a sale altogether.[^ch33-producers-news-auctions] States imposed temporary foreclosure moratoria. The moratoria reduced foreclosures in the short run, but they also made credit scarcer and more expensive afterward, because lenders could not easily recover their money. They were a measure with costs as well as benefits, not a simple success.[^ch33-wheellock-moratoria]

### 3.4 In Focus: one trustee’s sale notice

**In Focus.** *The Claude News* of January 12, 1932, carried a trustee’s sale notice on its second page. The notice was a formula, and the formula is the artifact. It recited that on July 24, 1923, “FLORENCE O. WILSON, a widow,” had conveyed the land in trust to T. G. Oldham as trustee to secure a debt to the Northwestern National Life Insurance Company; that the deed was recorded in Volume 12, page 393, of the Armstrong County deed records; that default had occurred and the debt was “now wholly due”; and that the substitute trustee, J. O. Jensen, would sell at the county courthouse door on Tuesday, February 2, 1932, between ten in the morning and four in the afternoon. The property was “all of survey number 206, Block B-4,” containing “652.8 acres, more or less.” The sale was to satisfy a note for $15,000. The notice did not say why the debt had gone unpaid, what the land grew, or who would bid. It said only what the law required: who owed, who held the paper, where the land lay, and when the auction would begin. A farm of more than 650 acres had become a legal description and a date, printed in a county weekly beside the bank statement and the school news. What did it mean for a community that such notices became routine?[^ch33-claude-notice][^ch33-check-focus-claude]

The notice is evidence of a process, not a portrait of a family. It shows that a widow in Armstrong County had borrowed against her land, that an insurance company held the debt secured by the deed of trust, that a substitute trustee had been appointed, and that the sale was scheduled for the first Tuesday in February. It does not show whether the land was good, whether the widow had tenants, or whether anyone bid. The formula establishes only so much, and the rest remains unnamed.[^ch33-claude-notice][^ch33-check-image]

### 3.5 Hunger, relief, and the first drought

With no federal system of direct relief — cash or goods to people who had no income — the hungry depended on local charities and city governments that were themselves collapsing. Cities such as Chicago and Philadelphia ran emergency relief organizations that could not meet the demand. By the summer of 1932, Philadelphia had discontinued relief, and Chicago’s organization was on the verge of collapse. The burden fell on private charity and local government until federal relief loans arrived in 1932. Relief was a patchwork that varied by city and state.[^ch33-erca][^ch33-check-relief]

The drought of 1930 added environmental disaster to economic collapse. Dry conditions touched 30 states from the Appalachians to the Rockies, with the worst effects in the lower Mississippi and Ohio valleys. The early drought was distinct from the later Dust Bowl, whose migration and photography belong to Chapter 34. The drought did not cause the Depression, and the Depression did not cause the drought. They arrived together, and each made the other harder to bear.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-check-ch34]

Relief appropriations, drought extent, and migration estimates are documented. Hunger’s distribution is not, and hunger and homelessness were counted imperfectly.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-check-numbers]

## 4. Hoover’s responses

### 4.1 An active president inside a constraining philosophy

Herbert Hoover was not a do-nothing president. He used federal power more than any predecessor had in a depression. He summoned business leaders to the White House, ordered federal construction accelerated, asked governors to expand public works, and proposed a tax cut. He created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation and signed the Emergency Relief and Construction Act. But he refused direct federal relief to individuals, and he relied on voluntarism. The result was a response too small for the crisis.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-oertx-hoover]

Hoover’s philosophy was not an afterthought. He believed the American system rested on “rugged individualism,” and he feared that federal relief would undermine individual character by making recipients dependent. He preferred “voluntary organization and community service,” and he asked businesses to maintain wages and dividends. The philosophy was consistent with his life story and with the economic thinking of his generation. It was also a choice with consequences.[^ch33-hoover-rugged][^ch33-oertx-hoover]

The evidence of activity is real. In November 1929 he summoned business leaders and secured commitments to maintain wages and to spend about $1.8 billion on construction. He ordered federal departments to speed their construction projects, asked governors to expand public works, and proposed a $160 million tax cut in 1929. The activity was real. The question is whether it was adequate.[^ch33-hoover-archives]

The conferences, the public-works orders, the loans, and the tax measures are documented. They cannot show that a larger federal response would have ended the Depression on a given timetable. The judgment that Hoover’s response was inadequate does not require a precise counterfactual.[^ch33-check-debate-works][^ch33-check-relief]

### 4.2 Voluntarism and the high-wage policy

Hoover’s attempt to hold wages and dividends steady by agreement kept real wages high as prices fell. In earlier depressions, wages had fallen and more workers had kept their jobs. In this contraction, manufacturing firms held wage rates nearly constant into 1931. With prices falling, real hourly wages rose sharply, and firms responded by laying off workers rather than cutting pay. The policy was well intended. It was also consequential.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-hoover-archives]

The consequences were concentrated in durable manufacturing. Production there fell about 36 percent between the end of 1929 and the end of 1930, and another 36 percent the following year. Unemployment rose as firms cut payrolls. The wage policy shifted the adjustment from wages to employment; it was not a moral failure.[^ch33-smiley-econlib]

The tariff belonged to the same strategy. Hoover proposed a limited tariff revision to help farmers in 1929, lost control of the bill, and signed the Smoot–Hawley tariff on June 17, 1930, after the Senate passed it on June 13. The tariff raised duties on dutiable imports from about 40.1 percent to about 53.21 percent. It was meant to protect the firms that held wages steady. It did not cause the Depression. It did provoke retaliation and freeze international trade, and it became one of the decade’s most durable political symbols.[^ch33-senate-smoot-hawley][^ch33-smiley-econlib]

The conferences, the pledges, and the wage and employment series are documented. They cannot show that wage cuts would have prevented the Depression. The counterfactual is open.[^ch33-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Federal lending and the instruments of response

Hoover’s most substantial responses were federal credit institutions that lent to banks, railroads, and farmers rather than to unemployed people. The Agricultural Marketing Act of 1929 created the Federal Farm Board to stabilize farm prices by buying and holding surpluses, with a $500 million revolving fund. The Federal Home Loan Bank Act of July 22, 1932, created a system to lend to mortgage lenders. These were efforts to support credit, not to relieve poverty.[^ch33-frh-rfc][^ch33-usda-farm-history][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

The Reconstruction Finance Corporation was the largest peacetime federal intervention in the economy to that date. Hoover signed the RFC Act on January 22, 1932. The RFC lent to banks and other financial institutions, including those outside the Federal Reserve, and began with $500 million in Treasury stock and $1.5 billion in bonds. It was modeled on the War Finance Corporation of the First World War, and Eugene Meyer, the governor of the Federal Reserve Board, had urged its creation.[^ch33-frh-rfc][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

The Banking Act of 1932 widened the collateral the Federal Reserve could accept and, for a time, supported open-market purchases. The Emergency Relief and Construction Act of July 27, 1932, provided about $300 million in relief loans to states and $322 million for public works. The relief loans were the first large-scale federal public welfare program. Hoover welcomed them as loans, not grants, because loans preserved the principle that relief was temporary and repayable. The distinction mattered to him and to the states that had to repay.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-erca][^ch33-check-relief]

The National Credit Corporation, announced on October 13, 1931, was a private consortium of banks intended to lend to illiquid banks. It was ineffective, and the RFC soon replaced it. The sequence shows Hoover’s preference: organize private institutions first, use federal credit second, and direct federal relief last or never.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-relief]

### 4.4 The 1932 tax increase and the limits of the response

Fearing deficits, Hoover recommended and Congress enacted a large tax increase in 1932. The lowest marginal income-tax rate rose from about 1.125 percent to 4.0 percent, and the top marginal rate rose from 25 percent on taxable income above $100,000 to 63 percent on income above $1 million. Exemptions were cut sharply to widen the tax rolls. The increase reduced disposable income and deepened the contraction.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-relief]

The tax increase was also, in part, a defense of the gold standard and of confidence. Hoover had to defend the dollar’s gold parity, and that commitment limited his room to inflate or spend. The fiscal and monetary constraints were connected. The choices were real, and so were the constraints. Hoover could not have overcome a divided Congress and an international monetary system by an act of will.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

The statutory rates, the veto fights, and the loans are documented. They cannot show that a different fiscal policy would have ended the Depression. The judgment that the response was inadequate stands without that claim.[^ch33-check-debate-works][^ch33-check-gold]

## 5. The political crisis and the 1932 election

### 5.1 A crisis of legitimacy

By 1932 the Depression had become a political crisis. Millions of people no longer believed that the government could or would help them. The election of 1932 registered that loss of confidence. Protest, the Bonus Army, the campaign, the results, and the interregnum belong to one story about legitimacy.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-oertx-hoover]

Votes, protest counts, and official statements are documented. What individual voters meant by their ballots is not. An election result is not a policy mandate. A landslide can show that voters rejected an incumbent. It cannot show that they endorsed a program they had not been given.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-app-1932]

### 5.2 Protest and the Bonus Army

The Bonus Army’s march and its forcible dispersal became the emblem of the government’s failure to understand the desperation of ordinary people. In 1924 the government had promised veterans a bonus payable in 1945. In 1932 jobless veterans marched on Washington to demand earlier payment. About 400 gathered in Portland, Oregon, by May 17 under the leadership of a fellow veteran, Walter M. Waters. By June 1 some 1,500 were in Washington, camping at sites including the 30-acre Anacostia Flats. The crowd later grew to somewhere between 10,000 and 20,000.[^ch33-nps-bonus-army]

The House voted to pay the bonus; the Senate rejected it, and Hoover promised a veto. On July 28, 1932, the administration sent the army and police to expel the marchers. Troops led by General Douglas MacArthur cleared the camps with tear gas, bayonets, and torches. One veteran was killed in the police firing near the Capitol. Hoover’s public statement described the marchers as including “communists and persons with criminal records.” Read alongside the record of the marchers’ circumstances, the characterization was a claim, not a finding. The marchers included veterans and nonveterans, and they cannot be reduced to a single motive.[^ch33-hoover-bonus-statement][^ch33-nps-bonus-army]

The dates, the counts, the camps, and the official statements are documented. The marchers’ interior lives are not. A diverse group cannot be reduced to a single motive.[^ch33-check-silences]

### 5.3 The campaign and the election of 1932

Franklin D. Roosevelt defeated Hoover in a landslide. Roosevelt won 472 electoral votes and about 57.4 percent of the popular vote, with about 22,818,740 votes. Hoover won 59 electoral votes and about 39.6 percent of the popular vote, with about 15,760,425 votes. The totals are compiled returns, and compilations differ slightly.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-check-numbers]

Roosevelt campaigned on a “New Deal” and on relief of unemployment, but he refused to endorse Hoover’s policies or to commit to a specific program. The refusal increased uncertainty during the interregnum. The campaign changed the leadership, but the policy content remained open. That is not a criticism of Roosevelt’s tactics. It is a description of what the campaign actually settled.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-frh-great-depression]

The returns and the campaign rhetoric are documented. How many voters were moved by which issue is not. The landslide registered rejection, not a detailed mandate.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-app-1932]

### 5.4 The interregnum and the banking collapse

Between the election and the inauguration, the banking system collapsed. In February 1933, Michigan declared a statewide banking holiday after negotiations to rescue Detroit banks broke down. State holidays spread. By the time of Roosevelt’s inauguration on March 4, 1933, the banking system was in disarray, depositors were hoarding cash, and 48 states had declared a statewide holiday or restricted withdrawals. The outgoing and incoming administrations left the response unclear.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

Roosevelt issued the national bank holiday proclamation on March 6, 1933. Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act on March 9, and Roosevelt gave his first banking fireside chat on March 12. Those acts belong to Chapter 34. The collapse and the unresolved question end here; the recovery belongs to the next chapter.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-ch34]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The contraction was deep and long. Real output fell by about 29 to 30 percent between 1929 and 1933, and unemployment reached its highest recorded level, whether measured by the Lebergott or the Darby series.[^ch33-stlouisfed-impact][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment]

The banking panics were central. Bank failures disabled credit, shrank the money supply, and forced deflation that deepened the collapse.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-frh-great-depression]

The gold standard transmitted deflation. Fixed exchange rates and the defense of gold parity linked the world’s economies and constrained policy in the crisis.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-state-gold]

Hoover intervened, but not enough. He used federal credit and public-works powers more than any predecessor, while refusing direct relief to individuals.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-erca]

The crash was not the whole cause. The 1929 market collapse began a contraction, but the banking crises turned a recession into a depression.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-frh-banking-panics]

The collapse produced a political reckoning. The election of 1932 registered a loss of confidence in the administration’s response.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-nps-bonus-army]

### 6.2 What is open

Was money the cause or the consequence? One position holds that the Federal Reserve’s failure to prevent the collapse of the money stock caused the Depression. Another holds that the money stock fell in response to a decline in spending that originated in the real economy. Each has a strong version. The first explains the depth and length; the second explains the initial downturn and the role of household behavior. The evidence supports a combined account in which both channels operated, but the weights remain disputed.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-debate-works]

How central was the gold standard? One account makes the gold standard the master cause of the worldwide collapse. Another treats it as one transmission channel among several, alongside banking structure and demand. The evidence supports both, in different proportions. The gold standard explains why the depression was international and why recovery followed gold leaving. It explains less well why the American banking system was so fragile in the first place.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-state-gold]

Was Hoover’s failure philosophical or structural? One account blames an ideology of voluntarism and balanced budgets. Another stresses the real constraints of the gold standard, a divided Federal Reserve, and a Congress that would not fund direct relief. The evidence supports both. Hoover’s ideas mattered, and so did the institutions that limited what any president could do.[^ch33-oertx-hoover][^ch33-check-debate-works]

Did inequality make the crash dangerous? One account ties the concentration of income to underconsumption and fragility. Another treats inequality as background rather than a trigger. The question remains open. The 1928 income shares are striking, but inequality alone did not set the timing of the crash.[^ch33-pew-inequality][^ch33-check-inequality]

What did unemployment really reach? The gap between the Lebergott and Darby series is a definitional dispute about work relief, not merely a data error. Both measures belong in the record, and each measures something different.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which no single cause — a crash, a monetary rule, an ideology, or an inequality — is sufficient. The Depression deepened at the intersection of a fragile banking system, an international monetary standard, and a set of policy ideas that made leaders slow to act.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

The outcome was contingent. The Depression was the result of decisions taken by people who could have acted differently, not an earthquake. Eichengreen and Temin’s point is precisely that the outcome depended on human choices within an inherited framework. The framework was real; so were the choices.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

Structure and choice belong together. The structural vulnerabilities — correspondent banking, the gold standard, unequal incomes — set the stage. The contingent choices — the 1928–29 rate rise, the 1931 defense of gold, the 1932 tax increase, the refusal of direct relief — determined how the crisis unfolded.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-smiley-econlib]

The synthesis is that a financial breakdown became a social catastrophe because the institutions meant to absorb shocks — the banking system, the lender of last resort, the gold standard, and the relief apparatus — all failed at once, and because the ideas that guided policy made each failure harder to correct.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-table1]

## Legacies

The collapse left a political legacy that outlasted the decade. It discredited the idea that markets would correct themselves and that private charity could meet a national emergency. It also made the federal government responsible, in the public’s expectation, for the health of the economy and the security of the old and unemployed. The New Deal’s programs belong to Chapter 34, but the political demand for them was created here.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-oertx-hoover]

The banking collapse left a durable institutional legacy. The failure of thousands of banks and the loss of depositors’ savings produced the demand for federal deposit insurance and securities regulation, which reshaped American finance for generations. The transition from the RFC and the 1932 banking act to the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation and the Securities and Exchange Commission belongs to the New Deal chapters, but the argument for it was written by the failures of 1930–33.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-frh-great-depression]

The international legacy was ambivalent. The Depression’s transmission under the gold standard taught policymakers to prefer domestic recovery to exchange-rate orthodoxy. The failure at London in 1933 seemed to prove that cooperation was impossible. The United States would later build international institutions on the premise that the 1930s must not be repeated, a story that belongs to the Cold War chapters.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-check-dates-appD]

The decade’s measurement legacy persists. The unemployment and foreclosure series created to describe the Depression still shape how Americans argue about hard times. The gap between the Lebergott and Darby measures is a reminder that the way a society counts unemployment is itself a political choice.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment]

## Summary

- The contraction began in the summer of 1929, and the stock market crash of October 1929 accelerated it; the Dow fell nearly 13 percent on Black Monday and nearly 12 percent on Black Tuesday, and lost almost half its value by mid-November.[^ch33-frh-crash]
- The banking panics that began in November 1930 turned the recession into the Depression; about 1,350 banks suspended in 1930, about 2,300 in 1931, about 1,450 in 1932, and about 4,000 in 1933.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-frh-banking-panics]
- The Bank of the United States failed on December 11, 1930, then the largest bank failure in American history, after a merger attempt collapsed and depositors ran.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- The money supply fell nearly 30 percent from the fall of 1930 to the winter of 1933; consumer prices fell about 25 percent and wholesale prices about 32 percent between 1929 and 1933.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-stlouisfed-impact]
- The interwar gold standard transmitted deflation, and the Federal Reserve raised its discount rate in October 1931 to defend the dollar after Britain left gold, which deepened the domestic banking crisis.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-state-gold]
- Unemployment reached about 24.9 percent, or about 12,830,000 people, in 1933 on the Lebergott measure, and about 19.2 percent on the Darby measure that counts work relief as employment.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment]
- Farm foreclosures peaked at nearly 39 per 1,000 farms in 1933, and states imposed mortgage moratoria that reduced foreclosures while making credit harder to get.[^ch33-wheellock-moratoria]
- Herbert Hoover relied on voluntarism, secured business pledges to hold wages, created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation in January 1932, signed the Emergency Relief and Construction Act in July 1932, and then raised taxes in 1932.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-frh-rfc][^ch33-erca][^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- The Bonus Army marched on Washington in 1932 and was dispersed by the army on July 28.[^ch33-nps-bonus-army]
- Franklin D. Roosevelt won the election of 1932 with 472 electoral votes and about 57.4 percent of the popular vote; Hoover won 59 electoral votes.[^ch33-app-1932]
- In February and March 1933 the banking system collapsed, and Roosevelt’s national bank holiday, the Emergency Banking Act, and his first banking fireside chat, on March 12, 1933, begin Chapter 34.[^ch33-fdic-1930s]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1929 and 1933 the United States suffered the deepest contraction in its history. A stock market crash was followed by a series of banking panics; a gold standard meant to guarantee stability transmitted deflation around the world; a banking system without deposit insurance turned local runs into a national collapse; and a president who believed in voluntarism used federal credit but refused direct relief. By early 1933 real output had fallen by nearly a third, millions were out of work, farm families were losing land in waves, and the banking system stood on the edge of shutdown. The chapter’s question — how did a financial breakdown become a prolonged social catastrophe? — is answered by a record in which a fragile system, an international rule, and a set of guiding ideas failed together.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on March 12, 1933, when President Franklin D. Roosevelt spoke to the nation by radio in his first fireside chat and explained, in plain language, what his administration had done to reopen the banks. Chapter 34 takes up the New Deal and asks what kind of security it created. The bank holiday proclamation of March 6 and the fireside chat are separate acts, and this bridge keeps them so.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-ch34]
