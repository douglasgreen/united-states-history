>
> — Tecumseh, as reported by the American Battlefield Trust[^ch14-tecumseh-speech]

The four quotations disagree about what the era’s republicanism was for. Jefferson promised honest friendship with all nations. The same president privately concluded that acquiring Louisiana lay beyond the Constitution. Tecumseh, as later reporters had him say, refused the premise that a country could be sold. Hickey answered that the war fought to vindicate American rights produced few concrete gains. The chapter keeps all four in view, because each names something the others leave out: restraint, constitutional evasion, dispossession, and a victory assembled in memory. The question is not which voice was sincere. It is how a republic that promised frugality and peace became an expanding state that bought a province, fought a war in the Mediterranean, enforced an embargo with its army and navy, and broke an Indigenous confederacy.[^ch14-check-epigraphs][^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-tecumseh-speech][^ch14-hickey-pbs]

Washington, March 4, 1801. Thomas Jefferson came out of Conrad and McMunn’s boarding house at New Jersey Avenue and C Street and walked to the unfinished Capitol. Virginia militia officers, District of Columbia marshals, and a group of congressmen went with him. He was 57, tall, and dressed, the *Alexandria Times* reported, in the clothes “of a plain citizen without any distinctive badge of office.” His predecessors had worn elegant suits and swords and arrived in liveried coaches. Jefferson walked.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]

Later accounts put him on horseback. The Thomas Jefferson Encyclopedia states that the story is incorrect, citing a rebuttal in *Harper’s New Monthly Magazine* in 1888. The documented account is the walk, and this scene follows it.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration][^ch14-check-horse]

The Senate Chamber was “so crowded,” one observer wrote, that “not another creature could enter—there was near a thousand persons within the walls.” John Adams, the outgoing president, had left town in the middle of the night. Chief Justice John Marshall, Jefferson’s distant cousin and political opponent, administered the oath. Jefferson then read an address that one observer said was “delivered in so low a tone” as to be barely audible. He had given an advance copy to a printer that morning, and the words reached the country through print.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration][^ch14-check-scene]

The address promised “a wise and frugal Government, which shall restrain men from injuring one another, shall leave them otherwise free to regulate their own pursuits of industry and improvement, and shall not take from the mouth of labor the bread it has earned.” It promised “peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations, entangling alliances with none,” and declared, “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists.” Margaret Bayard Smith wrote that changes of administration elsewhere had “most generally been epochs of confusion, villainy and bloodshed,” while in this country they took place “without any species of distraction, or disorder.”[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-monticello-inauguration]

The ceremony turned a contested transfer into an ordinary morning. Federalists had feared a coup; the transfer happened without one. But the reconciliation had limits. Enslaved people, Native nations, and women took no part in choosing the government, and Federalists in Congress had tried to block the transfer only weeks before. The question is not whether the ceremony was sincere. It is whether a republic of limited government could expand without enlarging the coercive power it criticized.[^ch14-check-ch13][^ch14-check-names]

This chapter follows the government from Jefferson’s inauguration in 1801 to the peace of 1815. It draws on the First Inaugural and Jefferson’s messages and letters; the Louisiana treaty and the constitutional debate over it; Jefferson’s instructions to Lewis and the expedition journals; the naval and diplomatic records of the Barbary War; the statutes and enforcement records of the embargo; the treaties and letters of the western war; the text of the Treaty of Ghent; and the newspapers and memorials of the war’s campaigns. It treats “restraint,” “economy,” “empire,” “neutral rights,” “impressment,” “the embargo,” and “the West” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter establishes in use. It names the people the expansion displaced and enslaved. Native testimony reaches the record largely through American documents, and the chapter says so rather than letting the silence stand as consent. Its dates are kept precise and reconciled with Appendix D (Chronology).[^ch14-check-scope][^ch14-check-names][^ch14-check-dates]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines Jefferson’s program of retrenchment and the conflict with the Federalist judiciary that produced *Marbury v. Madison*. §2 turns to the Louisiana Purchase, the constitutional doubt it exposed, and the expeditions that carried American power west. §3 follows the Barbary War, the embargo, and the Atlantic crisis that led to war in 1812. §4 sets the war for the western country inside Tecumseh’s and Tenskwatawa’s confederacy at Prophetstown. §5 examines the War of 1812 and the settlements at Ghent, Hartford, and New Orleans. §6 asks how historians explain Jeffersonian restraint, continental expansion, and the meaning of the war. The chapter begins on a March morning in 1801, when a plainly dressed president walked to the Capitol.

## 1. Jefferson’s program and Marbury

### 1.1 The promise of a frugal government

Jefferson’s program was a real attempt to shrink the federal government’s domestic footprint, not a disguise for expansion. He reduced the number of employees subject to presidential appointment, cut the Army to two regiments of about 3,500 men, pressed Congress to abolish the direct tax of 1798, and pardoned the ten people still imprisoned under the Alien and Sedition Acts. The Navy was reduced as well. Even after paying $15 million in cash for Louisiana, the national debt fell from about $80 million to about $57 million during Jefferson’s two terms.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic]

Those choices had costs and limits. The army that shrank would be needed on the frontier. The navy that shrank would fight Tripoli. The taxes that were repealed had paid for government, and the debt that fell was still a claim on future revenue. The point is not that restraint was fraudulent. It is that restraint was a program with bills to pay, and the bills came due in the West and on the Atlantic.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]

Jefferson’s inaugural language made the program sound like a moral economy: “economy in the public expense, that labor may be lightly burthened,” “the honest payment of our debts and sacred preservation of the public faith,” “encouragement of agriculture, and of commerce as its handmaid.” The language was not merely rhetorical. It set tests by which the administration could be judged when it bought Louisiana, fought Tripoli, or enforced an embargo. A government that promised to leave labor alone would have to explain why it was seizing cargoes, calling out militia, or taxing households for a war.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]

The program also had a constitutional dimension. Jefferson had spent the 1790s opposing what he called consolidation, the transfer of power from the states to a distant national government. Retrenchment was supposed to reverse that transfer by reducing the offices, taxes, and military establishment through which the federal government reached into ordinary life. The contradiction of his presidency was that the same government would soon reach further than ever, not because Jefferson abandoned the theory but because the opportunities of expansion demanded instruments the theory had not planned for.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-check-scope]

### 1.2 The judiciary, the midnight judges, and Marbury

Jefferson’s program ran into a judiciary that he could not appoint away. On February 13, 1801, the lame-duck Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801. It created a new tier of circuit judges, relieved Supreme Court justices of circuit riding, and reorganized the federal courts. Adams appointed the new judges in his final weeks. Secretary of State John Marshall, who was also chief justice, raced to deliver the commissions and failed to distribute some on Adams’s last day because there were too many documents to carry.[^ch14-fjc-midnight]

The Republican Congress repealed the act, with the repeal signed January 22, 1802, and passed the Judiciary Act of 1802, which abolished the new courts and restored circuit riding. The divisions survived in *Stuart v. Laird* (1803), in which the Court held that the Constitution did not restrain Congress’s power to reorganize the courts. The decision was a defeat for the Federalist judges and a victory for the administration, but it did not settle the larger question of what the courts could do to acts of Congress.[^ch14-capitol-repeal][^ch14-fjc-midnight]

William Marbury, Dennis Ramsay, Robert Townsend Hooe, and William Harper moved in December 1801 for a writ of mandamus compelling Secretary of State James Madison to deliver their commissions as justices of the peace. In February Term 1803, Chief Justice Marshall held that Marbury had a right to the commission and that the laws afforded a remedy, but that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 granting the Court power to issue such writs to officers was unconstitutional. The Court could not issue the writ. Marshall wrote that “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is,” and that “a law repugnant to the constitution is void.” The National Archives calls the decision the moment the Court first declared an act of Congress unconstitutional.[^ch14-marbury-text][^ch14-marbury-na]

The immediate result was a victory for Jefferson. The commission was never delivered, and the 1801 act was repealed. The lasting result was a victory for the branch Jefferson could not remove. Judicial review gave the Court a power that the Constitution did not describe in so many words. Restraint in the executive coincided with the enlargement of judicial power. It is the chapter’s first example of how a limited government’s conflicts produced lasting state capacity.[^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-fjc-midnight]

### 1.3 The Twelfth Amendment and the rules of party

The tie of 1800 produced a constitutional repair that made partisan succession routine. Congress passed the Twelfth Amendment on December 9, 1803, and the states ratified it on June 15, 1804. Electors thereafter named their choices for president and vice president on distinct ballots, and the House’s contingent election was limited to the top three candidates. Chapter 13 owns the crisis that produced the amendment; this chapter notes the repair.[^ch14-twelfth-amendment][^ch14-check-ch13]

The machinery of the transfer was fixed, but the chapter’s central question was not. How would a limited government expand? The answer came before the amendment was ratified. In April 1803 the United States bought Louisiana, and the purchase raised the constitutional and political questions that would define the rest of the era.[^ch14-twelfth-amendment][^ch14-check-scope]

## 2. Louisiana and the expeditions

### 2.1 The purchase and the constitutional doubt

The Louisiana Purchase doubled the republic’s claimed territory and forced Jefferson to choose between the strict construction he had preached and the expansion he wanted. The treaty with France was signed April 30, 1803. The United States purchased 828,000 square miles west of the Mississippi for $15 million, “for roughly 4 cents an acre.” Robert R. Livingston and James Monroe had been authorized to pay up to $10 million for New Orleans and the Floridas; they were offered the entire territory and agreed to a price beyond their instructions.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-senate-louisiana]

The Senate approved the treaty for ratification by 24 votes to 7 on October 20, 1803. All seven opponents were Federalists who objected that the acquisition lacked specific constitutional authorization. The Senate’s own history records that the revolution in Saint-Domingue (now Haiti) and the prospect of renewed war with Britain had pushed Napoleon to sell.[^ch14-senate-louisiana][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

Jefferson, a strict constructionist, wrote to John Dickinson that “the General Government has no powers but such as the Constitution gives it,” that it had “not given it power of holding foreign territory, and still less of incorporating it into the Union,” and that “an amendment of the Constitution seems necessary for this.” He nevertheless concluded that “we must ratify and pay our money, as we have treated, for a thing beyond the Constitution, and rely on the nation to sanction an act done for its great good, without its previous authority.”[^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

Jefferson drafted an amendment incorporating the province and confirming to the “Indian inhabitants” their rights of occupancy and self-government. His cabinet disagreed about the need for it, and the deadline forced the treaty through without one. The draft is evidence of what Jefferson thought the Constitution required and of what he was willing to do without.[^ch14-jefferson-draft-amendment][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

The purchase passed without an amendment, and the constitutional question was never resolved in court. The episode is the chapter’s clearest case of expansion outrunning the limits of republican doctrine. It is also a case of a president choosing a result he believed good over a rule he believed binding, and trusting the nation to ratify the choice after the fact.[^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-na-louisiana]

### 2.2 The expeditions and the reach of the state

The western expeditions were instruments of commerce, diplomacy, and knowledge, funded quietly and aimed at Native nations as much as at geography. In a confidential message of January 18, 1803, Jefferson asked Congress for $2,500 “for the purpose of extending the external commerce of the United States,” a phrase he expected to “cover the undertaking from notice.” He described a policy of encouraging Native nations to abandon hunting and to multiply trading houses: “to provide an extension of territory which the rapid increase of our numbers will call for, two measures are deemed expedient,” he wrote, the first to encourage agriculture and domestic manufacture, the second to multiply trading houses among them.[^ch14-secret-message]

The message was explicit about the connection between commerce, civilization, and land. Jefferson argued that hunting required extensive forests, that agriculture would teach Native nations to live on less land, and that trading houses would attach them to the United States. The policy was not a secret plan for immediate removal. It was a program for making Native land available by changing how Native people lived. The distinction matters, but it does not make the program benign.[^ch14-secret-message]

Jefferson’s instructions to Meriwether Lewis, dated June 20, 1803, appointed him to carry the objects of the confidential message into execution, provided instruments for celestial observations, and directed the expedition toward the Missouri River and the water communication to the Pacific. The Corps of Discovery departed in 1804 and returned in 1806. The National Park Service notes that it depended on more than fifty Native nations and “set forth untold loss” for the tribes who helped it survive.[^ch14-lewis-instructions][^ch14-nps-lecl]

Map 11 plots the Louisiana Purchase’s claimed boundaries against the routes actually traveled by Meriwether Lewis, William Clark, and Zebulon Pike between 1803 and 1806. It must distinguish the treaty’s claims from the explored and documented routes, and it must state its date. Zebulon Pike’s expedition is part of the map’s scope.[^ch14-check-map][^ch14-check-pike]

The expedition was state policy as well as exploration. The medals, trade goods, and instructions carried west were the instruments of a diplomacy that treated Native nations as sovereigns to be courted and as obstacles to be displaced at once. That double purpose is the subject of the In Focus box.[^ch14-secret-message]

### 2.3 In Focus: a Jefferson peace medal

**In Focus.** The Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History holds a Jefferson peace medal, object 1990.0466.0001, accession 1990.0466. It is silver, hollow, and made of two thin embossed plates joined by a silver ring, about 10.15 centimeters across, because the United States Mint lacked a press strong enough to strike heavy solid medals. One face bears the bust of Thomas Jefferson facing left; the other bears clasped hands beneath a crossed peace pipe and hatchet, with the words “PEACE AND FRIENDSHIP.” The Smithsonian lists Robert Scot as the engraver of both faces. Monticello and Encyclopedia Virginia credit the design to John Reich, a German immigrant who worked at the Mint. The discrepancy matters because the attribution is part of the object’s history. Image 2 reproduces both faces.[^ch14-si-medal][^ch14-monticello-medals][^ch14-encyclopediava-medal][^ch14-check-medal-engraver][^ch14-check-focus]

Jefferson described the practice in 1793 as an “antient Custom,” writing that the medals “confer no power” but serve as “marks of friendship.” The two faces carry the chapter’s contradiction in miniature: the clasped hands of friendship on one side, and the crossed pipe and hatchet — peace and war — on the other, offered by a republic that was also preparing to take the land. Medals of this type traveled with Lewis and Clark, who distributed about 87 of them. The gift did not cause dispossession. It shows how expansion addressed Native sovereignty: with presents, titles, and the language of friendship, while the land policy moved in the other direction.[^ch14-si-medal][^ch14-monticello-medals][^ch14-encyclopediava-medal][^ch14-check-focus]

## 3. Barbary conflict, embargo, and Atlantic war

### 3.1 The Barbary War

The first overseas war under the “frugal” program was a naval war with Tripoli, fought to end tribute and protect Mediterranean commerce. In May 1801 the Pasha of Tripoli, Yusuf Karamanli, declared war on the United States after the consul informed him that the United States would not meet his latest tribute demand. Congress authorized Jefferson to send the Navy in February 1802. On October 31, 1803, the frigate USS *Philadelphia*, commanded by William Bainbridge, ran aground on a reef in Tripoli harbor; Bainbridge and his crew were captured and held about two years.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary]

In February 1804 Lieutenant Stephen Decatur led a party aboard a disguised ketch into Tripoli harbor and burned the *Philadelphia* rather than let Tripoli refit it. A land operation under William Eaton, with a small Marine contingent and the exiled Hamet Karamanli, marched from Alexandria and took Derna. A peace treaty in June 1805 ended the war; it retained Yusuf Karamanli in power and required a ransom for the prisoners, though the Office of the Historian records that it provided for no future tribute.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-state-barbary]

The small-navy policy did not prevent war. The administration fought when it judged the interest great enough. The Barbary War is the chapter’s first instance of the republican government projecting force overseas while promising economy at home. It also showed that a navy built to defend a coast could become an instrument of diplomacy in the Mediterranean.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]

### 3.2 The embargo and the turn to coercion

After the *Chesapeake*–*Leopard* affair, Jefferson chose an economic embargo, and the enforcement of that embargo enlarged the very government it was meant to restrain. On June 22, 1807, the British warship *Leopard* fired on the American frigate *Chesapeake* after its captain refused a search for deserters. The British took four men; three American seamen were killed and others wounded. Early in 1808 Secretary of State James Madison reported that 4,228 American seamen had been impressed into British service since the war’s start, 936 discharged, leaving 3,292 in service.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]

On December 17, 1807, Jefferson recommended “an inhibition of the departure of our vessels from the ports of the United States.” Congress passed the Embargo Act four days later. Supplementary, additional, and enforcement acts followed in 1807 and 1808; the enforcement act allowed officials to seize cargo on suspicion and empowered the President to use the Army or Navy to enforce the embargo.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]

The embargo did not force Britain to relent. It damaged the American economy, and it was repealed in March 1809 and replaced by the Non-Intercourse Act. Jefferson later wrote that “the affair of the Chesapeak put war into my hand. I had only to open it, and let havoc loose.”[^ch14-monticello-embargo]

The embargo is the chapter’s clearest illustration of the central question. A policy chosen to avoid war and to avoid a standing military required domestic coercion — seizure, bonding, and the use of armed force against American merchants — and it failed on its own terms. The enforcement machinery is the bridge between restraint and war.[^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]

### 3.3 The road to war: neutral rights and the West

The declaration of war in 1812 rested on maritime grievances and on the western and southern demand to secure the frontier against Native resistance and to expand. The two leading causes were the British Orders in Council, which restricted American trade with the European continent, and impressment, the Royal Navy’s removal of seamen from American vessels. Congress had already passed non-intercourse measures; the Monroe–Pinkney Treaty of 1806 failed because it did not address impressment, and Jefferson did not send it to the Senate.[^ch14-ussconstitution-1812][^ch14-monticello-embargo]

The “War Hawks,” led by Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun, pressed the case for war as a defense of American honor and as a second war of independence; the Federalists opposed it, and New England and the maritime states feared its effect on trade. Madison asked for a declaration on June 1, 1812; Congress voted on June 18, 1812, and no Federalist voted for war.[^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-na-ghent]

Hickey notes that Britain was preparing to suspend the Orders in Council even as Congress voted, but the news could not cross the Atlantic in time. The declaration was made, in part, against a policy already being abandoned.[^ch14-hickey-loa]

A war fought for neutral rights and against impressment was also a war to secure the western frontier and open Native land to settlement. The western war and the maritime war were one conflict, and §4 turns to its Native center.[^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-check-slavery]

## 4. Tecumseh, Tenskwatawa, and Prophetstown

### 4.1 The confederacy and the Prophet’s town

The Shawnee brothers Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa built a pan-Indigenous movement against unilateral land cessions, and the American response helped drive the country to war. The Treaty of Fort Wayne of 1809, which required Indiana nations to sell about 3 million acres, helped provoke the resistance. Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa founded Prophetstown in 1808 as the center of a new confederacy; Tenskwatawa led a nativist “Purification” movement that rejected Anglo-American ways, and Tecumseh recruited across nations and languages, including among the Muscogee (Creek), whose Red Stick movement he encouraged.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh]

Tecumseh’s reply to the land sales survives only in later renderings. The American Battlefield Trust says he “allegedly” argued that no one could sell a country any more than the air or the great sea. The substance of the claim — that Native nations held land in common and could not alienate it by treaty with the United States — was the confederacy’s central political claim.[^ch14-tecumseh-speech][^ch14-check-tecumseh]

The confederacy disputed the premise of American expansion: that land could be bought and that title rested on the buyer and seller alone. Its rise turned a western land question into a continental conflict. It also gave the British a Native ally in the war that followed, and it gave the United States a reason to see the western war as a war of survival.[^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-check-slavery]

### 4.2 Tippecanoe and the destruction of Prophetstown

William Henry Harrison’s attack at Tippecanoe in November 1811 destroyed Prophetstown and deepened the war’s western dimension. Harrison led about 1,000 soldiers and militia toward Prophetstown. On the evening of November 6, 1811, he was met with a white flag and a request for a parley; he camped about a mile from the town. Before dawn on November 7, Tenskwatawa’s forces attacked the camp. Harrison’s line held, and the Americans then burned Prophetstown. Tecumseh was absent, recruiting in the South.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe]

The American Battlefield Trust estimates American casualties at about 189 and Native casualties at about 120. Estimates vary across sources; the figures here are the Trust’s and are given as approximations.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe][^ch14-check-numbers]

Tippecanoe intensified calls for war and narrowed the confederacy’s options. Tecumseh and the remnant of his forces allied with Britain when the war began, and Native nations became “the greatest losers” of the conflict that followed.[^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-hickey-loa]

## 5. The War of 1812 and peace

### 5.1 The war on the frontiers

The early campaigns failed, and the war exposed the military weakness of a republic that had chosen a small army and navy. The first American invasion of Canada failed. American forces had earlier burned public buildings in Canada, and the British retaliated.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa]

In the western theater, William Henry Harrison’s army defeated the British and their Native allies at the Thames in October 1813. Tecumseh was killed, and the confederacy lost the leader who had held it together. The victory ended the confederacy’s best hope of halting American settlement in the region and left the Native nations without the ally on whom they had counted.[^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-hickey-loa]

On August 24–25, 1814, the British army burned the Capitol, the President’s house, and other public buildings in Washington. In September the British failed to take Baltimore; the defense of Fort McHenry produced Francis Scott Key’s “Defence of Fort M’Henry.” The American victory at Lake Champlain in September, and the failure at Baltimore, strengthened the American position in the negotiations at Ghent.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-nps-mchenry][^ch14-nhhc-champlain]

The burning of Washington showed how quickly a republic that had reduced its army could be forced to mobilize militia, borrow money, and fight on its own soil. The attack was retaliation for American actions in Canada, but it became a symbol of the war’s cost and of the government’s vulnerability.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa]

As the British approached Washington, President Madison asked his wife, Dolley Madison, to stay behind and gather important documents and the state portrait of George Washington. The popular legend has her tearing the portrait from its frame and carrying it out. Historians caution that the enslaved members of the household most likely carried it out under her orders. The documented order is clear; the legend is not, and it erases the labor of the people who did the work.[^ch14-dncr-dolley][^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-check-dolley]

The war demonstrated that the republic’s military weakness had real costs. It also showed that the government’s response to invasion could bypass the restraints the program had promised. The republic called out militia, borrowed money, and fought a war that ended with its capital in ashes.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-slavery]

### 5.2 Ghent, Hartford, and New Orleans

The peace restored the prewar borders, settled none of the war’s declared causes, and arrived after a ruinous final battle. The Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, restored relations to the status quo ante bellum. Article I provided for the restoration of territory and possessions and for the return of “any Slaves or other private property.” Article IX provided that the United States would end hostilities with the tribes and restore to them the “possessions, rights, and privileges” they had enjoyed in 1811. Article X recorded that both parties would use their best endeavors to abolish the slave trade. The treaty said nothing about impressment or neutral rights.[^ch14-na-ghent]

The Senate approved the treaty unanimously on February 16, 1815, the day Madison sent it. News of the treaty took about six weeks to cross the Atlantic, so the final battle was fought in ignorance of it.[^ch14-senate-ghent]

26 Federalists from New England met at Hartford in December 1814 and January 1815 to protest a war they regarded as ruinous to their region. The convention took a moderate, states’-rights course, proposing constitutional amendments rather than voting secession; its report arrived in Washington alongside news of the victory and the peace, and it discredited the Federalist party.[^ch14-battlefields-hartford][^ch14-hickey-loa]

On January 8, 1815, Andrew Jackson’s force, about 5,700 engaged, defeated a numerically superior British army of about 8,000 at the Chalmette plantation below New Orleans. American casualties were about 71; British casualties about 2,034, including the death of Major General Sir Edward Pakenham. Jackson’s army included Tennessee and Kentucky militia, Louisiana militia, Free Men of Color, Choctaw, and Jean Lafitte’s privateers. News of the victory and of the treaty arrived almost together.[^ch14-battlefields-neworleans][^ch14-senate-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa]

The convergence of the victory, the peace, and the Hartford report produced a surge of nationalism and forever discredited the Federalists, even though the treaty conceded none of the war’s stated aims. The war also broke the Native confederacy’s power and ended its ability to count on a European ally. The chapter’s point is that the republic expanded and secured itself by the coercive means it had criticized: war, embargo enforcement, and the dispossession that followed the war, which the treaty’s promises to the tribes did not prevent.[^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-na-ghent]

Casualty figures, the size of the engaged forces, and the death toll including disease differ across sources. Hickey estimates American battle deaths at about 2,300 but total deaths from all causes, including disease, at as many as 20,000.[^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-check-numbers]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Retrenchment was real. Jefferson cut internal taxes, reduced the army and navy, and paid down the debt while in office. The program was a genuine attempt to shrink the federal government’s domestic footprint.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]

*Marbury* established judicial review. The Court for the first time held an act of Congress unconstitutional, and the decision strengthened the judiciary against the administration that had tried to dismantle the 1801 court system.[^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-marbury-text]

Louisiana was acquired on doubtful authority. Jefferson himself believed an amendment was necessary, drafted a proposed one, and then ratified the treaty without it.[^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-jefferson-draft-amendment]

The expeditions were state policy. Congress funded them quietly for commerce and diplomacy, and they carried American power, gifts, and claims into Native homelands.[^ch14-secret-message][^ch14-nps-lecl]

The embargo was coercive and failed. It required seizure, bonding, and armed enforcement, damaged the American economy, and did not move Britain.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]

The war ended in a negotiated status quo, and the Native confederacy lost most. The treaty settled nothing about impressment and left the tribes worse off than in 1811.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the restraint sincere or already compromised? Did Jefferson mean to shrink the state and simply meet emergencies, or was restraint a political language used to legitimate expansion? The evidence supports both a sincere program and a pattern of exceptions that grew into the rule.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

Did expansion strengthen or endanger the republic? Louisiana and the war enlarged the country and deepened the sectional and slavery questions that would produce the Missouri crisis and, eventually, civil war. The chapter holds both outcomes open.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-check-slavery]

What was the War of 1812 about? Maritime rights, national honor, western expansion, or Native dispossession? The chapter weighs the evidence rather than choosing one cause.[^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-hickey-pbs]

How much was contingency? The Orders in Council were being suspended as Congress voted; the peace and the victory arrived together; Napoleon’s defeat changed the war’s conditions. The chapter shows how much depended on timing and accident.[^ch14-hickey-loa]

What do the archives show about the people the expansion displaced and enslaved? Enslaved people and Native nations appear in the treaties and letters mainly as property and as obstacles, and the chapter names that limit rather than letting the documents speak for everyone.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-slavery][^ch14-check-dolley]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which Jeffersonian restraint and continental expansion were not opposites but partners: the frugal republic expanded by borrowing the fiscal, military, and diplomatic instruments it had criticized, and it paid for the expansion in the dispossession and enslavement of others.[^ch14-check-sources]

The sympathetic tradition represented by Dumas Malone and Merrill D. Peterson treats Jefferson’s program as a principled attempt to keep the republic small and to fund expansion without a standing military or heavy taxes. It takes the language of restraint seriously, and it reminds us that Jefferson’s choices were constrained by a small revenue base and a distrust of standing armies.[^ch14-check-sources]

Gordon S. Wood and Peter S. Onuf read the era as the construction of an “empire of liberty,” in which the extension of republicanism across the continent was the central project rather than a departure from it. Onuf’s Miller Center essay offers that reading in brief, and it is an interpretation, not neutral ground.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-check-sources]

Popular memory and much older scholarship framed the War of 1812 as a second war of independence. Hickey, writing in the nationalist tradition, still calls it “probably our most obscure conflict” and questions the concrete gains. The apparent victory, he argues, was assembled from the timing of the news.[^ch14-hickey-pbs][^ch14-hickey-loa]

Histories of the War Hawks and of the West, including work by Reginald Horsman and Alan Taylor, treat the conflict as a war for land and for the destruction of Native power, not only for sailors’ rights. The Federalist critique and Hickey’s verdict note that the treaty conceded nothing and that the military record was largely a story of bungling. Read together, these positions say that a government pledged to restraint bought an empire, fought wars it was unprepared for, and ended with a more capable and more expansive state than it began.[^ch14-check-sources][^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-hickey-pbs]

## Legacies

The era left the republic larger and more capable than it found it. The Louisiana Purchase opened the West and with it the question of slavery’s extension, which returned in the Missouri crisis and would not be settled until the Civil War. *Marbury v. Madison* made judicial review a lasting feature of the government Jefferson had tried to shrink.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-senate-louisiana]

The war’s end broke the Native confederacy’s power and ended the possibility of a European ally for Indigenous resistance in the eastern woodlands, while the Treaty of Ghent’s clauses on Native rights went unenforced. The dispossession that Ch. 17 calls removal was already underway in the treaties and the war.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-check-slavery]

And the chapter’s central irony endured: a government pledged to economy and to peace expanded through a war it could barely fund, an embargo it could barely enforce, and a purchase whose constitutionality its own author doubted. The “empire of liberty” was built with the fiscal and military instruments the Republicans had spent the 1790s denouncing. Chapter 15 takes up the markets and infrastructure that would knit that empire together.[^ch14-check-ch15][^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The Napoleonic Wars, the revolution in Haiti, and the Barbary States shaped American policy at every turn. Napoleon sold Louisiana because Haiti had slipped and Britain threatened; Britain’s Orders in Council drove the embargo; Tripoli’s war made the small navy a fighting force.[^ch14-senate-louisiana][^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **Archives and silences.** Native diplomacy reaches the record mainly through American treaties, instructions, and medals, and the enslaved appear in the expansion’s documents as property — including the Treaty of Ghent’s provision for returning “any Slaves or other private property.” The chapter reads those clauses as evidence of who counted and who did not, and it names the enslaved rescuers behind the Dolley Madison legend.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-dolley][^ch14-check-slavery]
- **Distant connections.** Louisiana opened the slavery question that returned in Missouri (Ch. 16); the confederacy’s defeat prepared removal (Ch. 17); the war’s fiscal and naval legacy fed the Market Revolution (Ch. 15) and later overseas empire (Ch. 27).[^ch14-check-ch15][^ch14-check-slavery]

## Summary

- Jefferson’s program promised a “wise and frugal Government,” cut internal taxes, reduced the army and navy, and paid down the debt.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]
- The conflict with the Federalist judiciary produced *Marbury v. Madison*, which established judicial review even as Jefferson repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801.[^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-fjc-midnight][^ch14-capitol-repeal]
- The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, gave the parties a constitutional rule for succession.[^ch14-twelfth-amendment]
- The Louisiana Purchase doubled the republic’s claimed territory for $15 million, and Jefferson acquired it without the constitutional amendment he thought necessary.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-jefferson-draft-amendment]
- The Lewis and Clark expedition carried American commerce, diplomacy, and claims into Native homelands, and distributed peace medals whose imagery addressed Native leaders as sovereigns.[^ch14-secret-message][^ch14-nps-lecl][^ch14-monticello-medals]
- The Barbary War showed that the frugal republic would still project force overseas to protect commerce.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-state-barbary]
- The *Chesapeake*–*Leopard* affair led to an embargo that required seizure, bonding, and armed enforcement, failed to move Britain, and was repealed in 1809.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa’s confederacy resisted land sales; Harrison’s attack at Tippecanoe in 1811 destroyed Prophetstown and deepened the western war.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh]
- The War of 1812 brought the burning of Washington, the defense of Fort McHenry, and the negotiated peace at Ghent, which restored the prewar borders and said nothing about impressment.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-senate-ghent][^ch14-nps-mchenry]
- Jackson’s victory at New Orleans, fought after the peace was signed, arrived with the treaty and discredited the Federalists, even as the Native confederacy lost its power and its ally.[^ch14-battlefields-neworleans][^ch14-hickey-loa]

> *A closing note.* The era that began with a plainly dressed president walking to the Capitol ended with the capital in ashes and a peace that settled none of the issues that had caused the war. In between, the republic bought an empire, fought a war in the Mediterranean, enforced an embargo with the army and navy it had been reducing, broke an Indigenous confederacy, and doubled its territory. Jefferson had promised “a wise and frugal Government” and warned against entangling alliances; he acquired Louisiana on authority he doubted and met British impressment with a coercive economic policy that failed. The “empire of liberty” was not a departure from republican restraint. It was what restraint became when a republic with continental ambitions met a world at war, and the people who paid most were those the documents counted as property and as obstacles.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-slavery]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on October 26, 1825, when the *Seneca Chief* left Buffalo at the opening of the Erie Canal, carrying a cargo of goods and a barrel of Lake Erie water to New York City. This chapter has followed the republic from Jefferson’s inauguration through the purchase, the embargo, the western war, and the peace of 1815; Chapter 15 takes up the canals, credit, cotton, and labor that would knit the expanded country into a single market and shift its risks onto particular workers and families.[^ch14-check-ch15]
