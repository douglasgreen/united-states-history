# Chapter 6. Conquest, Resistance, and the Imperial Turn (1675–1713)

> “They said thay had bine the first in doing Good to the English, and the English in the first in doing Rong; said when they English first came, their King’s Father was as a great Man, and the English as a littell Child; he constrained other Indians from ronging the English, and gave them Corn and shewed them how to plant, and was free to do them ani Good.”
>
> — John Easton, “True Relation of what I know and of Reports and My Understanding Concerning the Beginning and Progress of the War now Between the English and the Indians,” 1675[^ch06-easton-relation]

> “You say that you are our father and I am your son … We will not be like Father and Son, but like Brothers.”
>
> — Haudenosaunee leaders at the Covenant Chain council, 1692, quoted in Daniel K. Richter, *The Ordeal of the Longhouse* (1992)[^ch06-richter-longhouse]

> “New England colonists waged war to gain Indian lands, to erase Indians from the landscape, and to free themselves of doubts about their own Englishness.”
>
> — Jill Lepore, *The Name of War: King Philip’s War and the Origins of American Identity* (New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 1998), chapter 1[^ch06-lepore-name]

The three quotations disagree about what the war was for. The Wampanoag statement, recorded by a Rhode Island official trying to prevent the fighting, presents the English as the ones who broke faith and as junior partners who had forgotten their debts. The Haudenosaunee speaker rejects the language of father and son and claims the standing of brothers — a claim about sovereignty inside an alliance. Lepore names what the English fought for and what the fighting did to their sense of themselves. Together they frame the chapter’s question: how did colonial wars alter sovereignty and alliance?

In the winter of 1674/75, a Christian Native man named John Sassamon was found dead under the ice of Assowampsett Pond, in present-day Lakeville, Massachusetts. Sassamon had reportedly warned Plymouth’s governor, Josiah Winslow, of a Wampanoag plot. A Native witness named three men as the killers, and a Plymouth court convicted them in June. Two were hanged on June 8, 1675; the third was shot a month later. Metacom, the Pokanoket Wampanoag sachem the English called King Philip, read the trial as an assault on his authority — a claim on jurisdiction over his own people.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-congregational][^ch06-sassamon-csm]

Swansea sat in Plymouth Colony on the border between English settlement and Wampanoag country. In the third week of June 1675, a Native man was shot and wounded there; the records do not agree on who fired or why.[^ch06-easton-relation] On June 20, Wampanoag warriors raided the outlying farms, looting and burning them. Residents fled to the garrison houses and sent for help. Militia from Bridgewater and Taunton arrived on June 21, but the warriors had already gone.[^ch06-swansea-ahc]

The heaviest attacks came on June 24, as Swansea’s people were returning from a day of humiliation and prayer. Wampanoag parties ambushed colonists who had left the garrison houses. Nine were killed and two mortally wounded. Two men sent to Plymouth for help were killed on the road, and the commissioners traveling to treat with Metacom found their bodies and turned back to Boston. Captain Benjamin Church arrived with a small column of Massachusetts militia, and Plymouth and Massachusetts troops gathered at Swansea over the following days.[^ch06-swansea-ahc][^ch06-great-swamp]

No Wampanoag account of those days survives in writing. The events reach us through John Easton’s sympathetic but partial relation, through colonists writing later for English readers, and through the records of the war the English won. Historians doubt that Metacom chose Swansea as the place to begin a war; the early violence may have been set off by young warriors acting without his leave. The outbreak appears to have surprised both sides.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-check-scene]

The question the chapter follows is not who fired first. It is how colonial wars altered sovereignty and alliance.

This chapter follows that question from Swansea in 1675 to Utrecht in 1713. It covers Metacom’s War and the export of Native captives, the Pueblo Revolt and the Spanish reconquest, the Glorious Revolution in America, the Covenant Chain and the imperial wars, and the hardening of coerced labor through the Peace of Utrecht. It draws on colonial correspondence, trial and council records, captivity and shipping records, Spanish and Franciscan documents, Haudenosaunee treaty metaphors and the records of the Covenant Chain, colonial session laws, and the peace instruments of 1713. It treats “sovereignty,” “alliance,” and “captive” as contested terms whose meanings changed over the period, and it distinguishes a war the English won from a settlement that Native nations continued to contest. It uses historical scholarship for what the records cannot establish, names the silences left by the archives of war and enslavement, and does not present the continent’s peoples as either passive victims or a single bloc.[^ch06-fisher][^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-check-names]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines Metacom’s War, its captives, and the export of Native people out of New England. §2 turns to the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 and the Spanish reconquest twelve years later. §3 follows the Glorious Revolution into Boston, New York, and Maryland, where colonists overthrew royal governors. §4 explains the Covenant Chain and the two imperial wars fought on Indigenous ground between 1689 and 1713. §5 traces the hardening of coerced labor and the asiento that made enslaved African labor an instrument of empire. §6 weighs the debate over Richard White’s “middle ground” and its limits. The order is roughly chronological, but each section returns to the same problem: who had the right to rule, and on what terms. The chapter begins at Swansea, where a dispute over jurisdiction became a war that neither side intended on those terms.

## 1. Metacom’s War and captives

### 1.1 The war in New England

The conflict the English called King Philip’s War — and the Wampanoag and their allies fought as a war for sovereignty and survival — was the most destructive in seventeenth-century New England. It began at Swansea and spread across the region. Metacom was killed in August 1676, and the colonial victory that followed did not restore the old alliance.[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-umassd]

Metacom, sachem of the Pokanoket Wampanoag, led a coalition that came to include Nipmuc, Narragansett, and Pocumtuc peoples and, later, Abenaki communities to the north. The English drew on the New England Confederation’s militias and on Indigenous allies, including the Mohegan under Uncas and some Christian “praying Indians.” The war was not a single nation against a single colony. It was a shifting set of alliances and grievances that pulled in most of the region.[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-congregational]

On December 19, 1675, a combined colonial force attacked a Narragansett fort in the Great Swamp of present-day Rhode Island. The Narragansetts had declared neutrality, but the English distrusted them and feared they would join Metacom in the spring. The assault killed hundreds of Narragansett warriors and noncombatants. Historians describe it as one of the most brutal and lopsided engagements in New England’s history. Many of the survivors were later sold into slavery, sentenced to death, or forced to flee.[^ch06-great-swamp]

Metacom was killed on August 12, 1676, by John Alderman, an Indigenous soldier serving in Benjamin Church’s company. The English drew and quartered his body and displayed his head at Plymouth, a punishment reserved for treason. Fighting continued in northern New England into 1678.[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-congregational]

By the war’s end more than 600 colonists had been killed and about a dozen towns destroyed. An estimated 3,000 Native people died at the English colonists’ hands. These are widely repeated estimates, not a census.[^ch06-umassd][^ch06-check-dating]

The war broke the Wampanoag and Narragansett as independent military powers in southern New England and opened their lands to English settlement. It also produced the era’s largest single flow of Native captives into slavery. That flow is the subject of the next three subsections.[^ch06-newell-changing]

### 1.2 The treatment of allied and neutral Native people

The war did not divide cleanly into English and enemy. Christian and neutral Native people suffered internment, dispossession, and sale, and their wartime treatment shows how quickly an alliance could be converted into a claim of conquest.[^ch06-congregational]

The “praying Indians” were Algonquian converts, mostly Nipmuc and Massachusett, who lived in towns associated with John Eliot’s mission. During the war many praying towns were depopulated, and hundreds of Christian Native people were removed to Deer Island in Boston Harbor. There they endured exposure and hunger, and many died.[^ch06-congregational][^ch06-praying-town]

Linford D. Fisher’s study shows that Native people who surrendered to avoid enslavement — individuals, families, and whole communities — were enslaved at nearly the same rate as captured combatants. Each colony had its own policy. English authorities disarmed surrenderers, sold their guns, and then often sold the people themselves, overseas or into local servitude, because the colonies objected to freeing them and found housing and feeding them inconvenient.[^ch06-fisher][^ch06-brown-fisher]

Because New Englanders routinely shipped Native captives to Barbados, Bermuda, Jamaica, the Azores, Spain, and Tangier, surrenderers understood that captivity could mean removal from their homelands forever. That fear drove some to fight on and others to surrender, making enslavement a strategic weapon in the war itself.[^ch06-fisher][^ch06-brown-fisher]

English-allied Native leaders worked to keep surrenderers out of English households and off English ships. Uncas of the Mohegans tried to prevent captives from being carried to the Caribbean, sheltered runaways, and helped them resettle. Native people also bought and held captives as servants, sometimes to keep them from English owners.[^ch06-brown-fisher]

The war’s human cost fell hardest on people the English had promised to protect. The alliance that had sustained Plymouth a half-century earlier ended as a license to enslave.[^ch06-congregational][^ch06-fisher]

### 1.3 In Focus: the *Seaflower* and one shipping record

**In Focus.** In the fall of 1676, as the war in southern New England ended, a ship called the *Seaflower* sailed from Boston Harbor with a cargo of Native captives. The captives, at least 180 by the count in the most detailed accounts, were described in the language of the time as “Heathen Malefactors” being exported as “the reward of war” — a phrase that turned prisoners into property and their sale into the soldiers’ wages. Governor John Leverett defended the shipment in a letter of September 12, 1676, arguing that the captives had rebelled against a king whose authority Metacom had acknowledged. The captives were men, women, and children, and the *Seaflower* carried them toward markets in Barbados and Jamaica that proved hard to sell in. Historians including Jill Lepore have traced the ship in the records, and Margaret Ellen Newell places it within a wider traffic in enslaved Native people from New England. Read closely, the record is not a complete manifest and it is not unique: it is one documentable instance of a pattern — the conversion of a defeated people into an export commodity. The exact count varies among sources, and the surviving record does not yield a complete manifest. The larger scale is reported, not precisely counted: Newell estimates that New England armies, courts, and magistrates enslaved more than 1,200 Native people in the seventeenth century.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-seaflower][^ch06-check-focus]

### 1.4 The scale and meaning of Native enslavement

The enslavement of Native people was not incidental to the war. It was one of its central outcomes and one of the ways the English paid for it. Native slavery and African slavery grew together in the same decades.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-fisher]

Margaret Ellen Newell argues that Native Americans made up the vast majority of people enslaved by European regimes in the Americas before 1700. The *Seaflower* was one vessel among many; hundreds of New England captives were sold locally or overseas. The destinations included Barbados, Bermuda, Jamaica, the Azores, Spain, and Tangier, as well as English households in New England itself.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-fisher]

Local bondage could be as permanent as export. New England colonies bound Native people in English households for terms that often lengthened into lifetime and heritable servitude. A Rhode Island law of 1676 that seemed to forbid Native slavery in practice “disposed of” surrenderers for the colony’s benefit.[^ch06-brown-fisher][^ch06-commonplace-shoemaker]

Newell argues that ambiguity was essential to the system. Colonists held Native people in slavery or under designations that amounted to perpetual, heritable bondage, while the law governing Native slavery remained sparse and contradictory. Small loopholes and dishonest practice turned limited-term service into slavery.[^ch06-commonplace-shoemaker][^ch06-brown-fisher]

The shadow of wartime enslavement reached into the eighteenth century. In 1721 Connecticut debated the status of second-generation Native child servants, the descendants of people first enslaved during the war. Free-descendant petitions continued into the 1740s.[^ch06-brown-fisher]

Metacom’s War both shattered Indigenous power in southern New England and deepened the region’s dependence on unfree labor, Native and African alike. That is the bridge from the war to the imperial slave system of §5.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-brown-fisher]

## 2. The Pueblo Revolt and the reconquest

### 2.1 Eight decades of Spanish rule

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 grew from nearly a century of Spanish demands on Pueblo land, labor, and religion. It was the most successful Indigenous uprising against colonial rule in what became the United States.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman]

Juan de Oñate led Spanish soldiers and Franciscans into the Río Grande valley in 1598. When Acoma Pueblo resisted, Oñate’s forces killed and enslaved hundreds. He ordered a leg chopped from every man over fifteen and the rest of the population enslaved, setting a template of terror that endured for decades. By 1680 some 40,000 Pueblo people lived in the region.[^ch06-pueblo-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]

Franciscan missionaries, backed by armed force, worked to destroy the ancestral Pueblo world. They suppressed kachina worship, banned traditional marriage and religious practice, and imposed Christian observance and forced labor through the encomienda and repartimiento systems, which assigned Pueblo labor to Spanish masters. The Pueblo religious leader Po’pay had himself been arrested for practicing Pueblo rites before he organized the revolt.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-popay-smithsonian]

A severe drought in the 1660s and 1670s worsened hunger and strained the Pueblo–Spanish relationship, while Apache raiding pressured Pueblo communities. The crisis made revolt both more necessary and more difficult to coordinate.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]

By 1680 the Pueblo nations shared a grievance against Spanish rule strong enough to overcome, for a time, their own long-standing divisions.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

### 2.2 The revolt of 1680

On August 10, 1680, most of the Pueblo nations rose together. They killed some 400 Spaniards, drove about 2,000 survivors out of the province, and ended Spanish rule in New Mexico for twelve years.[^ch06-pueblo-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

Po’pay, a Tewa religious leader from San Juan Pueblo (present-day Ohkay Owingeh), organized the revolt across the pueblos. The communities spoke different languages — Keres, Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Piro, and Zuni — so he used runners carrying knotted cords, each knot to be untied one day at a time, to set a common date. When the plan was discovered early, the revolt began before the appointed day.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

The rebels attacked Spanish settlements, killed twenty-one Franciscan priests, sacked mission churches, desecrated Catholic objects, and besieged the governor, Antonio de Otermín, in Santa Fe. Otermín broke out and led a retreat south to El Paso.[^ch06-pueblo-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]

For twelve years the Pueblos lived free of Spanish rule. They rebuilt the kivas and restored the practices the missions had suppressed. The Hopi, the westernmost participants, were never reconquered.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

Unity did not last. Drought and Apache raids continued, villages that had moved for defense did not return, and Po’pay’s insistence on abandoning all Spanish material culture and his heavy-handed leadership cost him support. By the time the Spanish returned, Pueblo unity had eroded.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]

The revolt preserved Pueblo lands, languages, and religion in ways the missions had threatened to erase. It also showed that the Spanish empire could be driven out by the people it had ruled.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

### 2.3 The reconquest, 1692–1696

The Spanish reconquest combined negotiation with the threat of force and exploited Pueblo divisions. Diego de Vargas, a veteran colonial administrator, received the crown’s commission in 1688 and arrived at El Paso in 1691. He organized a return with about 60 soldiers and 100 Indigenous allies. He intended to retake the province without firing a shot, keeping his soldiers’ weapons holstered and inviting each pueblo to return to Spanish rule and the Catholic faith — while keeping two cannons in the rear guard.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]

Vargas’s first foray in the fall of 1692 met little resistance and secured a formal return in Santa Fe, but the peace was fragile. Resistance continued, and by 1696 Spanish and Pueblo forces were again at war. The full consolidation of Spanish rule took years.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]

The reconquest did not restore the old theocracy unchanged. Spanish officials moderated some demands, and Pueblo ceremonies survived in altered form. Recent scholarship stresses that what was lost and what was preserved came from Pueblo choices as much as Spanish policy.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

New Mexico remained a Spanish, then Mexican, then American borderland, but its Pueblo nations survived as distinct political communities. The revolt and the reconquest are both founding events of the Southwest’s later history.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]

## 3. The Glorious Revolution in America

### 3.1 The Dominion of New England

Before 1689 the English crown had tried to centralize its American colonies under a single royal government. The Dominion of New England was that experiment, and it failed.[^ch06-boston-revolt]

After revoking the Massachusetts Bay charter in 1684, James II created the Dominion of New England in 1686 under Governor Sir Edmund Andros. It eventually joined Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, New York, East Jersey, and West Jersey under one administration without a local assembly.[^ch06-boston-revolt][^ch06-mass-glorious]

Andros enforced the Navigation Acts, the laws that channeled colonial trade through England, denied the validity of Massachusetts land titles that rested on the old charter, restricted town meetings, and promoted the Church of England in largely Puritan regions. He appointed regular officers to lead colonial militia. His rule was profoundly unpopular.[^ch06-boston-revolt]

The Dominion concentrated grievances: it threatened land, self-government, and religious identity at once. When news of a change of monarchy arrived, those grievances had an immediate outlet.[^ch06-boston-revolt][^ch06-mass-glorious]

### 3.2 The fall of Andros in Boston, 1689

On April 18, 1689, a Boston uprising against Andros overthrew the Dominion and restored local government. It was the American face of England’s Glorious Revolution.[^ch06-boston-revolt]

In November 1688 William of Orange invaded England and James II fell. When word reached Boston, a well-organized body of militia and citizens formed, arrested Andros and other dominion officials, and took members of the Church of England into custody. Neither side suffered casualties, and the leaders of the former Massachusetts Bay Colony reclaimed power.[^ch06-boston-revolt]

News of the revolution set off parallel uprisings. In New York the militia captain Jacob Leisler seized control in 1689; in Maryland a Protestant association took over the proprietary government; and the former charter colonies restored their governments. The English colonies had conducted their own revolution in the name of the one across the Atlantic.[^ch06-leisler][^ch06-mass-glorious]

The Dominion dissolved, and the colonies regained self-government — but on new terms. Massachusetts received a new royal charter in 1691 that joined it with Plymouth and widened the franchise while keeping a royal governor.[^ch06-mass-glorious][^ch06-boston-revolt]

### 3.3 Leisler’s rebellion and the politics of the revolution

In New York, the revolution became a bitter contest between factions that outlasted it. Leisler’s rebellion shows that “revolution” in America could mean an internal struggle over who should rule as much as resistance to the crown.[^ch06-leisler]

Jacob Leisler, a German-born merchant and militia captain, took control of the southern part of New York in 1689 and governed until 1691, after the Boston revolt had ended the Dominion. His support came largely from merchants, militia, and lesser colonists against the old elite.[^ch06-leisler]

Royal authority was not restored until 1691, when English troops and a new governor arrived. Leisler was arrested, tried, and convicted of treason, and executed. The colony was left split into Leislerian and anti-Leislerian factions that mapped loosely onto English Whig and Tory alignments.[^ch06-leisler]

Boston, New York, and Maryland each turned the fall of James II into a local reckoning. The revolution was celebrated as a defense of English liberties and used to settle scores that had nothing to do with England.[^ch06-leisler][^ch06-mass-glorious]

The 1689 revolutions confirmed that colonial politics now ran through empire: colonists claimed English rights while imperial officials claimed the authority to define them.[^ch06-boston-revolt][^ch06-leisler]

## 4. The Covenant Chain and the imperial wars

### 4.1 The making of the Covenant Chain

In the same years that the New England wars ended, New York and the Haudenosaunee built an alliance — the Covenant Chain — that came to organize English diplomacy across the Northeast. It served both sides and bound neither completely.[^ch06-covenant-wiki]

New York’s governor Sir Edmund Andros asked the Five Nations for help in ending the conflicts of 1675, and worked with the Onondaga leader Daniel Garacontié. The Mohawks attacked Metacom’s winter camp, denying him gunpowder, and their intervention helped end the war in New England.[^ch06-chenussio-covenant][^ch06-covenant-wiki]

The Covenant Chain was imaged as a silver chain binding the English ship to the Haudenosaunee Tree of Peace. A three-link chain symbolized Peace, Friendship, and Respect, and the treaties promised that the chain would last as long as the sun shone, the waters flowed, and the grass grew green. The metaphor was renewed, “polished,” and “brightened” at councils.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-onondaga-covenant]

Later English officials assumed the chain granted them sovereignty over the Haudenosaunee. The Haudenosaunee did not agree. At a covenant council in 1692 their leaders rejected the language of father and son and claimed to be brothers.[^ch06-richter-longhouse][^ch06-covenant-wiki]

The Covenant Chain gave New York a leading role in English diplomacy with Native nations and gave the Haudenosaunee security on their eastern and southern flanks — in exchange for obligations neither party read the same way.[^ch06-chenussio-covenant][^ch06-covenant-wiki]

### 4.2 King William’s War and the northern frontier

European dynastic war became colonial war on Indigenous ground. King William’s War (1689–1697) carried the conflict between England and France into New England, New York, and Canada, where Native nations fought for their own reasons.[^ch06-king-william]

The war in America began with frontier raids by New France and its Abenaki allies against English settlements. The 1689 revolutions had already unsettled colonial governments, leaving them less able to defend their frontiers.[^ch06-king-william][^ch06-boston-revolt]

For the Haudenosaunee and their neighbors the European war was folded into older conflicts over hunting grounds, captives, and trade. The Covenant Chain obliged the Five Nations to fight beside New York, but their aims were their own.[^ch06-chenussio-covenant][^ch06-covenant-wiki]

The war demonstrated that the eastern woodlands’ conflicts could not be separated from the empires’ quarrels, and it exhausted and divided Indigenous communities that had survived King Philip’s War. Map 6 shows the theaters of these wars and the alliances that carried them.[^ch06-king-william][^ch06-check-map]

### 4.3 Queen Anne’s War and the Peace of Utrecht

Queen Anne’s War (1702–1713) transferred the European war to North America again, and the Peace of Utrecht that ended it rearranged imperial claims, gave Britain new territory and the slave-trade contract, and ignored the Indigenous nations whose lands were at stake.[^ch06-qaw][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

On February 29, 1704, a French and Native force attacked Deerfield, Massachusetts, killing about 47 colonists and taking 112 captive on a march to Montreal. Some captives were ransomed, and others were adopted into Mohawk families at Kahnawake. The raid became famous through the published captivity narrative of the town’s minister, John Williams, whose daughter Eunice was adopted and chose to remain among the Mohawks.[^ch06-deerfield]

Queen Anne’s War was fought through frontier raids, ransoms, and captivity on both sides, from New England to the Carolina frontier. The Haudenosaunee pursued a policy of neutrality between the French and English that the Covenant Chain had to accommodate. In 1710 four Haudenosaunee diplomats traveled to London to meet Queen Anne, carrying the alliance into the imperial capital.[^ch06-qaw][^ch06-richter-longhouse][^ch06-onondaga-covenant]

The Peace of Utrecht, signed between 1713 and 1715, ended the War of the Spanish Succession. France ceded claims to Hudson Bay, Acadia, and Newfoundland to Britain. Britain also won the asiento, the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-qaw]

The treaty settlements did not consult the Indigenous nations whose homelands changed hands. The British text described “the five nations or cantons of Indians, subject to the dominion of Great Britain,” a phrase the Haudenosaunee did not accept. They continued to treat the Covenant Chain as an alliance among equals rather than a subjection. The diplomatic formula and the imperial assumption of sovereignty remained at odds.[^ch06-utrecht-doc][^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-richter-longhouse]

By 1713 the English empire in North America had grown and its Native alliances had hardened into a system — but the system rested on agreements the Native parties read differently.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

## 5. Coercion and the imperial slave system through 1713

### 5.1 The imperializing of enslaved labor

In the same decades that empire expanded, coerced labor became more central, more racialized, and more legally complete. The enslavement of Native and African people grew together, and the colonies’ slave codes multiplied.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

Native people continued to be enslaved after the war, from New England to the southern frontier, while the African slave trade expanded. New England courts and households held both Native and African enslaved people.[^ch06-commonplace-shoemaker][^ch06-newell-changing]

“Indian,” “Negro,” and “mulatto” appeared in the same statutes and inventories, and the categories overlapped in people’s lives and in the law. The chapter treats them as one developing labor regime, not two separate stories.[^ch06-brown-fisher][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

The enslavement that began during the wars became a permanent feature of colonial society, and the codes that defined it hardened in the early eighteenth century.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes][^ch06-newell-changing]

### 5.2 The New York slave code and the revolt of 1712

New York’s 1702 slave code and its harsher 1712 code show how colonial law responded to the fear of insurrection by stripping enslaved people of even the few protections they had.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

“An Act for the Regulating of Slaves” barred free people from trading with the enslaved without permission. It allowed enslavers to punish the enslaved as they saw fit short of mutilation or death. It banned gatherings of three or more enslaved people off their owners’ property and barred enslaved testimony except against other enslaved people.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

On the night of April 6, 1712, a group of about twenty-five enslaved people gathered in New York City, set fire to a building, and attacked the White residents who came to put it out. They killed nine and wounded six. Militia captured most of the rebels; six took their own lives. The surviving accounts give about seventy people taken into custody, about forty tried, eighteen acquitted, and roughly twenty-five convicted and sentenced to death by hanging, burning, starvation, or breaking on the wheel — figures that do not fully reconcile.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes][^ch06-1712]

In December 1712 New York passed “An Act for Preventing, Suppressing and Punishing the Conspiracy and Insurrection of Negroes and Other Slaves,” further restricting the enslaved and penalizing manumission, the legal freeing of enslaved people. The rebellion and the code together show that enslaved people resisted and that the law answered resistance with more coercion.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

The number of rebels and the details of the trial vary across accounts, so the figures here are reported, not settled.[^ch06-check-dating][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

### 5.3 The asiento and the peace of 1713

The Peace of Utrecht made enslaved African labor an instrument of European statecraft. Britain won the exclusive right to supply enslaved people to Spanish America, tying the slave trade to the imperial balance of power.[^ch06-utrecht-doc][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

The treaty texts approved “the Assiento adjusted with the company of England, for the importation of Negro Slaves into the Indies, for the term of thirty years.” The asiento gave British traders privileged access to Spanish American markets and became one of the most valuable prizes of the war.[^ch06-utrecht-doc][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

Historians note that the economic value of the asiento lay in its command of the slave trade to Spanish America, a commerce the British would exploit for decades and one that connected the imperial wars to the growth of the Atlantic slave system.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

By 1713 the English empire’s prosperity and its Native alliances and its African slave trade had become one system. The settlement that ended the war over the Spanish throne also fixed the terms of a labor regime that would deepen across the eighteenth century.[^ch06-utrecht-doc][^ch06-newell-changing]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Metacom’s War was the most destructive war in seventeenth-century New England. It killed more than 600 colonists and an estimated 3,000 Native people, destroyed about a dozen towns, and ended Wampanoag and Narragansett military power in the south.[^ch06-umassd][^ch06-great-swamp]

Native enslavement was central, not incidental. New England shipped Native captives to the Caribbean and beyond and bound many more locally; Native people made up the vast majority of those enslaved in the Americas before 1700.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-fisher]

The Pueblo Revolt expelled Spanish rule for twelve years. On August 10, 1680, the Pueblos rose nearly as one and drove the Spanish out until Vargas’s return in 1692. The Hopi were never reconquered.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]

The Glorious Revolution produced colonial uprisings. Boston overthrew Andros on April 18, 1689, New York divided under Leisler, and Maryland’s government changed hands.[^ch06-boston-revolt][^ch06-leisler]

The Covenant Chain was a real and contested alliance. It organized English diplomacy in the Northeast, and the Haudenosaunee claimed equality within it rather than subjection.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-richter-longhouse]

The Peace of Utrecht transferred territory and the asiento. France ceded Hudson Bay, Acadia, and Newfoundland, and Britain won the slave-trade contract.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-doc]

### 6.2 What is open

How to weigh the causes of the wars is open. Was Metacom’s War primarily a contest over sovereignty and land, a religious conflict, a consequence of imperial competition, or a civil war within a divided region? Each framework explains part of the record.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-great-swamp]

Whether the “middle ground” describes this region is open. Richard White’s model of accommodation between Europeans and Native peoples took shape in the Great Lakes; how far it applies to New England, the Chesapeake, or the Southwest is contested.[^ch06-middle-ground-stanford][^ch06-lipman]

How much of Pueblo religion and autonomy survived the reconquest is open. Recent scholarship stresses Pueblo agency in the return, but the balance between accommodation and coercion is debated.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]

The meaning of the 1689 revolutions is open. Were they colonial versions of a metropolitan revolution, local power struggles draped in English rhetoric, or both?[^ch06-leisler][^ch06-mass-glorious]

The scale of Native enslavement is open. The commonly repeated figure of more than 1,200 Native people enslaved in seventeenth-century New England is an estimate; the *Seaflower*’s count varies among sources, and the total traffic is not fully documented.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-seaflower]

What the archives cannot show is part of the problem. The enslaved and the defeated left few records of their own. The 1680 revolt and the 1712 rebellion reach us chiefly through the documents of the people who suppressed them, and the historian must not mistake the victors’ categories for the whole of the past.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which Indigenous nations made their own decisions, empires fought their own wars, and the two became inseparable. Metacom, Po’pay, the Haudenosaunee councilors, and the enslaved rebels of New York were actors, not symbols.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-covenant-wiki]

Richard White’s *The Middle Ground* transformed the field by showing that Europeans and Native peoples constructed common meanings and accommodations rather than simply conquering and being conquered. Its concept, however, was place-specific: it described the *pays d’en haut*, the Great Lakes country, and its admirers extended it to places where the balance of power ran differently.[^ch06-middle-ground-stanford]

Later historians argue that the “middle ground” was an incomplete characterization even on its home turf, and that in many regions Native and European worlds pulled apart rather than merged. In New England, the Chesapeake, and the Missouri Valley, the model explains less than it does in the Great Lakes.[^ch06-lipman]

Lepore reads the war through the language its participants used, showing that the English fought as much over their own identity as over land. Her account of who was permitted to speak, and how, is a model for handling the era’s one-sided archives.[^ch06-lepore-name]

Read together, the positions say that sovereignty and alliance were negotiated across the period, that the negotiations were real, and that they were conducted under conditions of growing coercion. The 1713 settlement did not end the argument; it reset its terms.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

The best history refuses the single-cause story and the single-origin story alike, and it refuses to let either empire or victim stand in for the people who made the choices.[^ch06-check-sources]

## Legacies

The 1713 settlement left an imperial order that was also a set of bargains with Indigenous nations. Britain had gained territory and the slave-trade contract, but its empire in North America rested on the Covenant Chain and on treaties the Haudenosaunee and their neighbors understood as alliances among equals. The empire prospered where the bargains held and faced war where they broke, as they did repeatedly over the next half-century.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

The enslavement that began during the wars left a regime that outlived the empire that made it. New England’s Native captives, sold in the Caribbean or bound in local households, joined the enslaved Africans who would make the eighteenth-century plantation economy. The 1712 New York revolt and the code that answered it were early signs of the coercion that would define the century, and of the resistance that answered coercion.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

The memory of these years became a foundation of American identity. Metacom’s War was retold as the colonists’ heroic ordeal, and Metacom himself as a tragic or villainous foil. The Pueblo Revolt was largely forgotten outside the Southwest until Po’pay’s statue was given a place in the Capitol. The legacies are double: a nation built on conquest also kept alive the memory of those who resisted it.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]

## Optional threads

- **The imperial frame.** Boston, New York, Santa Fe, Albany, and Deerfield all sat inside one European competition for empire, but the colonies and Native nations pursued their own interests within it. The Covenant Chain, the Spanish reconquest, and the Peace of Utrecht were imperial events and Indigenous ones at once.[^ch06-richter-longhouse][^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- **Archives and silences.** The defeated appear in the victors’ records: Wampanoag grievances survive because a Quaker official wrote them down; the enslaved appear in inventories and court minutes; the Pueblos’ revolt is known mainly through Spanish accounts. The chapter names these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 5 supplies the legal making of racial slavery; Chapter 7 owns Stono, Salem, and the Great Awakening and begins at Enfield; Chapter 8 owns the Seven Years’ War and the postwar crisis. Later chapters carry empire, slavery, and Native dispossession into the revolution and the republic.[^ch06-check-ch5][^ch06-check-ch7][^ch06-check-stono]

## Summary

- Metacom’s War (1675–1676) began at Swansea and became the most destructive conflict in seventeenth-century New England, ending with Metacom’s death in August 1676 and fighting in the north until 1678.[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-congregational]
- The Great Swamp Fight of December 19, 1675 killed hundreds of Narragansett warriors and noncombatants and was one of the war’s most brutal engagements.[^ch06-great-swamp]
- New England shipped Native captives to the Caribbean and beyond; the *Seaflower* carried at least 180 out of Boston in 1676, and New England enslaved more than 1,200 Native people in the seventeenth century, by Newell’s estimate.[^ch06-seaflower][^ch06-newell-changing]
- The Pueblos rose on August 10, 1680, killed some 400 Spaniards, and ended Spanish rule in New Mexico for twelve years.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]
- Diego de Vargas reconquered New Mexico beginning in 1692 through negotiation and the exploitation of Pueblo divisions, and Spanish rule took years to consolidate.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- Boston overthrew Governor Andros and the Dominion of New England on April 18, 1689, in the American face of the Glorious Revolution.[^ch06-boston-revolt]
- Leisler’s rebellion divided New York from 1689 until Leisler’s execution in 1691, and the colony stayed split between rival factions.[^ch06-leisler]
- The Covenant Chain allied New York and the Haudenosaunee and gave the Five Nations standing that imperial officials read differently from the Haudenosaunee themselves.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-richter-longhouse]
- King William’s War (1689–1697) and Queen Anne’s War (1702–1713) carried European conflict onto Indigenous ground; the 1704 Deerfield raid became the era’s most famous captivity story.[^ch06-king-william][^ch06-deerfield]
- The Peace of Utrecht transferred Hudson Bay, Acadia, and Newfoundland to Britain and won Britain the asiento, the slave-trade contract to Spanish America.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-doc]
- New York’s slave code of 1702 and the harsher code of 1712 show coercion and resistance advancing together; the 1712 rebellion was answered with more restrictive law.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]
- Historians disagree about whether accommodation, its limits, language, or slavery best explains these decades, but they agree that Indigenous nations and enslaved people made choices that shaped the outcome.[^ch06-middle-ground-stanford][^ch06-lipman]

## Study Questions

1. What does John Easton’s 1675 relation say the Wampanoags wanted? Identify Easton’s audience and purpose, and explain why the fact that an English official recorded the grievances matters. Cite the document.
2. The outbreak at Swansea may have surprised Metacom as much as the English. What does that possibility suggest about how the war began?
3. Compare the treatment of Christian “praying Indians” during King Philip’s War with the treatment of surrenderers in Fisher’s study. What does each reveal about English promises of protection?
4. Use the *Seaflower* record and Newell’s estimate to assess the scale of Native enslavement in New England. What can be counted, and what can only be estimated?
5. Why did the Pueblos rise in 1680, and why were they able to overcome their linguistic and political divisions? Use at least two sources.
6. Compare the Pueblo Revolt with Metacom’s War as responses to colonial expansion. Why did the Pueblo Revolt expel the Spanish for twelve years while Metacom’s War did not dislodge the English?
7. The Spanish “reconquest” of 1692 is described as partly negotiated. What did the Pueblos gain or preserve in the return, and what did they lose?
8. What connected the Glorious Revolution in England to the uprisings in Boston, New York, and Maryland? Was this one revolution or several?
9. The Haudenosaunee told the English in 1692 that they would be brothers, not father and son. What did that claim mean, and how did English officials understand the Covenant Chain differently? Use Map 6.
10. How did the Peace of Utrecht change the map of North America, and whose claims did it ignore? What did the asiento have to do with the balance of power?
11. Read the New York slave code of 1702 and the law of 1712. What did the 1712 rebellion change in the law, and what does the change reveal about colonial fear?
12. Compare two interpretations of these decades — for example, Richard White’s middle ground and Jill Lepore’s language of war. What does each explain, and what does each leave out?

### War and captivity documents

- **John Easton, “True Relation of what I know and of Reports and My Understanding Concerning the Beginning and Progress of the War now Between the English and the Indians” (1675).** A Rhode Island official’s attempt to record the Wampanoag grievances as well as the English case; essential for the opening scene and the first epigraph. Use it to locate primary material and to name the silences.

### The Pueblo Revolt

- **Edward Countryman, “The Pueblo Revolt,” *History Now* (Gilder Lehrman Institute, 2011).** A concise scholarly essay on the revolt’s causes and consequences. Useful for framing; return to the Spanish and Franciscan records for detail.
- **Indian Pueblo Cultural Center, “Pueblo Revolt.”** A Pueblo institution’s account that treats the revolt as an act of resilience and survival. Use it for the Pueblo perspective and for the knotted-cord story.
- **Genealogical Society of Hispanic America, “1692 Reconquest.”** A short account of Diego de Vargas’s return and the erosion of Pueblo unity. A summary; verify its chronology against the documentary record.

### Empire, alliance, and war

- **“Covenant Chain,” Wikipedia.** A usable overview with citations to Richter and Jennings; treat it as a guide to the scholarship, not as the scholarship. The 1692 quotation must be verified in Richter.
- **Onondaga Nation, “Polishing the Silver Covenant Chain.”** The Haudenosaunee account of the chain’s metaphors, including the 1710 visit of four diplomats to Queen Anne. The clearest Indigenous-authored description of the alliance.
- **“Peace of Utrecht,” Wikipedia, and the 1713 treaty text at PrimaryDocuments.ca.** The encyclopedia supplies context; the treaty text supplies the asiento clause and the peace articles. Read the actual instrument before quoting it.

### The debate

- **Richard White, *The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815* (2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2011).** The book that reframed European–Indigenous relations as accommodation. Consulted here through the publisher description; read it before stating its thesis as fact.
- **Jill Lepore, *The Name of War: King Philip’s War and the Origins of American Identity* (New York: Knopf, 1998).** The foundational study of how the war was written and remembered. Consulted through the publisher’s authorized first chapter; read it before quoting beyond that chapter.
- **Margaret Ellen Newell, *Brethren by Nature: New England Indians, Colonists, and the Origins of American Slavery* (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 2015).** The leading study of Native slavery in New England. Consulted through a published review and a university news account; read it before presenting its figures as settled.
- **Linford D. Fisher, “‘Why shall wee have peace to bee made slaves’: Indian Surrenderers during and after King Philip’s War,” *Ethnohistory* 64, no. 1 (2017).** The article that documents the enslavement of surrenderers and the wartime market in Native captives. Consulted through its abstract and opening pages; read it in full before relying on its figures.
- **Andrew Lipman, “No More Middle Grounds?” *Reviews in American History* 44, no. 1 (2016): 24–30.** The review essay that argues the middle ground was place-specific and has been overextended. Consulted through a blog summary; read the review before relying on its argument.

> *A closing note.* The years from 1675 to 1713 made the English empire in North America and unmade the nations that had shaped it. At Swansea a dispute over who had the right to judge and punish became a war; at Santa Fe a coordinated revolt drove out an empire; at Boston, New York, and Albany colonists and Indigenous nations renegotiated who would rule and on what terms; and at Utrecht the empires divided a continent they did not fully control. Through it all, the enslavement of Native and African people deepened, and the people it held resisted. The chapter begins with a shooting in a Plymouth Colony town and ends with a treaty in a Dutch city that never consulted the nations whose homelands it reassigned.[^ch06-seaflower][^ch06-utrecht-doc]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on July 8, 1741, at Enfield, Massachusetts, where Jonathan Edwards preached “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God.” This chapter ends with an imperial order built on codes, treaties, and coerced labor; Chapter 7 takes up the provincial society that this order produced — its migrations, staples, revivals, households, and the enslaved rebellion at Stono.[^ch06-check-ch7]
