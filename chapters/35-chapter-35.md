## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On October 5, 1937, Roosevelt spoke in Chicago at the dedication of a public works project — the transcript calls it “this important project of civic betterment,” and the FDR Library files the address as the Outer Link bridge dedication. He had chosen “this great inland city and this gala occasion to speak to you on a subject of definite national importance.”[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-quarantine-speech-file]
- **What he said.** Roosevelt described a world in which treaties were being violated, civilians bombed, and ships sunk in peacetime, and warned that the disorder would not spare the United States. He said it was “unfortunately true that the epidemic of world lawlessness is spreading,” likened war to a contagion, and observed that “when an epidemic of physical disease starts to spread, the community approves and joins in a quarantine of the patients.”[^ch35-quarantine-speech]
- **The documented close.** “There must be positive endeavors to preserve peace. America hates war. America hopes for peace. Therefore, America actively engages in the search for peace.”[^ch35-quarantine-speech]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the venue, the medium, and the text. It cannot show what every listener thought, and the outline has not yet located a scholarly account of the domestic reaction.[^ch35-check-scene][^ch35-check-scene-reaction]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In October 1937 no one knew that a European war would begin in 1939, that France would fall in 1940, or that the United States would enter the war in 1941. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch35-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A speech opens a question rather than settling it.** The scene’s claim is not that Roosevelt launched a policy but that he named a problem his government could not yet solve. He proposed no sanctions, no alliances, and no rearmament program in the address; he asked his audience to see the world’s disorder as their own.[^ch35-quarantine-speech]
- **The stakes.** The speech marks the moment when the language of “quarantine” entered American politics without a policy to match it, which is why it makes a useful opening. The chapter follows how that gap between diagnosis and action closed over four years.[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-check-scope]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did neutrality give way to intervention, and how much of that outcome was chosen?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from a Chicago bridge dedication to the Neutrality Acts, to Warsaw and Paris, to the America First rallies, and to the fleet at Pearl Harbor.

### What must not be invented

## 1. The neutrality inheritance

### 1.1 The claim: a legislative inheritance that constrained the president

- **The claim.** When war came to Europe in 1939, the United States was bound by a body of neutrality law built in the mid-1930s to keep it out of another European war, and that law constrained what Roosevelt could do even as he tried to help the Allies.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the statutes as a single accumulating inheritance — the arms embargo, the loan ban, the travel restrictions, and cash-and-carry — rather than as four unrelated bills, because their cumulative effect was the real constraint.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, their dates, and their terms. It cannot show that most Americans approved of every provision, and the chapter should separate congressional action from public opinion.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 1.2 The neutrality statutes, 1935–1937, and Figure 8

- **The claim.** Congress passed the first Neutrality Act in 1935, renewed and extended it in 1936, and broadened it in 1937, each time tightening the restrictions on American dealings with belligerents.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **The 1935 act.** On August 31, 1935, Congress prohibited the export of “arms, ammunition, and implements of war” to nations at war and required arms makers to obtain export licenses; Roosevelt opposed the bill but signed it. The 1936 renewal continued the act and barred loans to belligerents; the 1937 act forbade Americans to travel on belligerent ships, banned American ships from carrying arms to belligerents, allowed the president to bar belligerent ships from American waters and extend the embargo, and covered civil wars.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **Figure and data.** The registered visual `ch35-figure-01` (Figure 8, “Neutrality’s statutory architecture, 1935–1941”) belongs to this section. It should show the enactment, amendment, and repeal of each statute as a timeline, mark the 1935–36 acts as background, and carry a 1935–36 background band. Its caption and source line must state that the evidence is the statutes themselves and cite the *Statutes at Large*, volumes 49–55; the production backing is `ch35-figure-data`.[^ch35-check-figure8][^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes and their sequence. It cannot show why each provision commanded its majority, and the chapter should not attribute a single motive to a divided Congress.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-neutrality-acts]

### 1.3 Cash-and-carry and the Neutrality Act of 1939

- **The claim.** The 1937 act contained a concession Roosevelt had engineered — a “cash-and-carry” provision letting belligerents buy non-arms goods if they paid cash and used their own ships — and his aim was to help Britain and France, the only powers with the currency and shipping to use it.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **The revision of 1939.** After Germany occupied Czechoslovakia in March 1939 and then invaded Poland in September, Roosevelt called a special session of Congress on September 21, 1939, to revise the law. After weeks of debate the House repealed the arms embargo on November 2 by a vote of 243 to 181, and Roosevelt signed the measure on November 4. The new law put all trade with belligerents on a cash-and-carry basis, thus reopening arms sales to Britain and France while keeping American ships and loans out of the war.[^ch35-house-neutrality-1939][^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the votes, the dates, and the terms. It cannot show that the revision alone saved the Allies, and the chapter should present the debate as contested.[^ch35-house-neutrality-1939]

### 1.4 The isolationist constituency

- **The claim.** Behind the statutes stood a durable body of opinion, often called isolationist, that drew on the memory of the First World War, the findings of the 1934–36 Senate investigations into the munitions industry, and the belief that bankers and arms makers had drawn the country into that war.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **The term.** The chapter should use “isolationist” as a period label while noting that its targets sometimes preferred “noninterventionist,” and that the movement was never monolithic: it included pacifists, nationalists, German- and Irish-American communities, and conservatives who distrusted Roosevelt for domestic reasons.[^ch35-check-names][^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **What the record can show.** It can show organizations, members, and publications. It cannot easily show the views of the unaffiliated, and the chapter should name that gap.[^ch35-check-silences]

### 1.5 What neutrality law could not control

- **The claim.** The neutrality acts governed the American state’s dealings with belligerents, but they could not govern the actions of Germany, Italy, and Japan, and the chapter should make clear that the initiative in world affairs lay partly abroad.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should end the section by showing that the acts were written for a world of settled great-power war and proved poorly suited to a world of aggression against weak states, civil wars, and undeclared conflict.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **The link to §2.** The outbreak of war in September 1939 tested the inheritance immediately, which is where the next section begins.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

## 2. European war and the fall of France

### 2.1 The claim: the war arrives and the strategic picture breaks

- **The claim.** Between September 1939 and June 1940 the European war moved from a distant conflict to a catastrophe that left Britain alone against Germany, and that change made American aid a live strategic question rather than a debating point.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the military events and the American response together, because the German victories of 1940 did as much as any argument to shift American opinion.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the diplomatic and military chronology and the terms of American aid. It cannot isolate the effect of any single event on the public, and the chapter should treat that as an open question.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 2.2 September 1939: invasion, declarations, and the American response

- **The claim.** Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, Britain and France declared war, and Roosevelt declared American neutrality in law while saying he could not ask that Americans remain neutral in thought.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The quotation.** The Office of the Historian records Roosevelt’s statement that while the United States would remain neutral in law, he could “not ask that every American remain neutral in thought as well.” The chapter should quote it and note the distance between legal neutrality and stated sympathy.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the address and the legal position. It cannot show that the public shared Roosevelt’s sympathy, and the chapter should not assume it did.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 2.3 The fall of France and Britain alone

- **The claim.** The German campaign of May and June 1940 defeated France in six weeks and left Britain as the last major power resisting Germany in Europe, a defeat that reordered American strategic assumptions.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The stakes.** The chapter should present the fall of France as the moment when Roosevelt and his military advisers began to plan for a world in which Britain might fall and the Western Hemisphere would have to be defended alone.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The military view.** The Army’s chief of staff, General George C. Marshall, anticipated that Britain might surrender after France and argued for reserving supplies for hemispheric defense — a position the chapter should state fairly, because it shows that aid to Britain was not the obvious military choice.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

### 2.4 American opinion and the 1940 election

- **The claim.** American opinion in 1940 supported helping Britain more than it supported joining the war, and Roosevelt’s third-term campaign had to hold both positions together.[^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms][^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The evidence gap.** The outline has not yet consulted a polling series for 1939–41, so the chapter cannot yet give numbers for the shift in opinion; this is a research task, and the section should either supply the polls with their question wording and dates or describe the shift without precise figures.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]
- **What the record can show.** It can show election results and official positions. It cannot show what individual voters weighed, and the chapter should avoid attributing a single motive to the electorate.[^ch35-check-public-opinion][^ch35-check-silences]

### 2.5 The strategic alarm and the turn to aid

- **The claim.** The fall of France turned the question from whether to help Britain to how to help it without entering the war, the problem that organizes §4.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should close the section on the strategic problem and hand off to the debate over aid, not repeat the organizational contest that §3 covers.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-check-scope]

## 3. Interventionists and opponents

### 3.1 The claim: a contest of organized opinion

- **The claim.** Between 1940 and 1941 the question of aid to Britain was fought out by two mass organizations, and the outcome of that contest shaped what Roosevelt could do.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies and the America First Committee as rivals with real arguments, not as a patriotic camp and a fringe.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **The chronology note.** This section runs through September 1941 and overlaps in time with §4; the roadmap explains that the sections are thematic.[^ch35-check-scope]

### 3.2 The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies

- **The claim.** The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies (CDAAA), formed in May 1940 and led by the Kansas publisher William Allen White, argued that sending aid to Britain would reduce, not increase, the chance that the United States would be drawn into the war.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **The position.** Its supporters did not necessarily want the United States to enter the war, but they believed that a British defeat would imperil American security, which put them directly against the America First argument.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the committee’s formation, leadership, and public positions. It cannot measure how many Americans it persuaded, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 3.3 The America First Committee

- **The claim.** The America First Committee (AFC), launched on September 4, 1940, became the most powerful isolationist organization in the country, arguing that the war in Europe did not threaten American security and that aid to Britain would lead to war.[^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]
- **The people.** Its chairman was General Robert E. Wood, the head of Sears, Roebuck; its prominent supporters included Lindbergh, Alice Roosevelt Longworth, the University of Chicago president Robert Hutchins, and the actress Lillian Gish. Among younger supporters were Gerald Ford, Potter Stewart, Gore Vidal, and Kingman Brewster.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- **The roster caution.** Naming future public figures shows the movement’s reach, but the chapter must not use their later careers to characterize the 1940–41 organization, which is an appeal to hindsight.[^ch35-check-names][^ch35-check-silences]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the organization, its leaders, and its materials. It cannot show the motives of every member, and the chapter should not treat the whole membership as sharing Lindbergh’s views.[^ch35-check-silences]

### 3.4 The In Focus: Lindbergh at Des Moines

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads Charles Lindbergh’s address “Who Are the War Agitators?,” delivered at an America First rally in the Des Moines Coliseum on September 11, 1941. Lindbergh argued that an “ever-increasing effort” had pushed the country to “the verge of war” and asked who was responsible. His answer named three groups: “The three most important groups who have been pressing this country toward war are the British, the Jewish, and the Roosevelt administration.” The sentence is reproduced here because the chapter’s argument depends on it: it identifies a religious minority as a foreign interest steering the nation toward war, which is the core of the antisemitic conspiracy theory the speech advanced. It is quoted to be analyzed, not endorsed. The box should read the passage closely, set it against the speech’s own claim to be arguing from “facts and issues,” and show how a respected aviator used his credibility to circulate a dangerous lie. It should end on the chapter’s question: how did a public argument about war invite a politics of suspicion?[^ch35-lindbergh-text][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]

- **The registered status.** No image is assigned to this chapter, so the section is planned as a textual close reading. Reproducing a newspaper page or photograph would require a new register entry, with its own caption, credit, and rights, before it could be added.[^ch35-check-focus][^ch35-check-image]
- **The exact text.** The sentence is quoted from the *Des Moines Tribune* of September 12, 1941, page 8, reproduced in the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum’s History Unfolded database. The box should collate it against *The New York Times* text and the full speech, and should present the surrounding argument accurately.[^ch35-lindbergh-text]
- **The surrounding reaction.** The Holocaust Encyclopedia records that after the speech many critics and press outlets accused Lindbergh of antisemitism and some accused him of supporting Nazi Germany, and that Roosevelt’s administration had long disagreed with him. The box should use that reception, not assert the speech’s influence.[^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm][^ch35-lindbergh-cfr]
- **What must not be done.** The box must not treat the sentence as typical of all isolationists, must not reproduce additional antisemitic language that the argument does not need, and must not let the speech stand in for the isolationist case that §3.2 and §3.3 present in its own terms.[^ch35-check-focus][^ch35-check-silences]

### 3.5 The collapse of the opposition

- **The claim.** The attack on Pearl Harbor ended the isolationist contest almost overnight; the House’s declaration of war against Germany drew 393 votes to none, and Frances Bolton, a former opponent of intervention, told colleagues that “all that is passed. We are at war.”[^ch35-house-declarations]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the collapse as sudden and complete, because it shows how much of the opposition rested on the belief that the country could stay out.[^ch35-house-declarations][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the votes and the statements. It cannot recover the private reasoning of every member who changed position, and the chapter should mark that silence.[^ch35-check-silences]

## 4. Destroyers, Lend-Lease, the Atlantic Charter, and undeclared naval war

### 4.1 The claim: aid without war

- **The claim.** Roosevelt advanced American support for Britain through a series of measures that stopped short of war in form but moved steadily toward it in effect, and each measure was justified as defense of the Western Hemisphere rather than as intervention in Europe.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-destroyers-message]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the bases exchange, Lend-Lease, the Atlantic Charter, and the Atlantic naval incidents as one escalating sequence, while noting that the escalation was contested at each step.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the agreements, the statutes, and the incidents. It cannot show what Roosevelt intended in private, and the chapter should separate the documentary record from the question of intent.[^ch35-check-debate-works]

### 4.2 Destroyers for bases, September 1940

- **The claim.** On September 2, 1940, Roosevelt exchanged fifty over-age American destroyers for ninety-nine-year leases on British naval and air bases, an agreement made by executive action rather than treaty.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-destroyers-message]
- **The agreement.** The bases were in Newfoundland, Bermuda, the Bahamas, Jamaica, St. Lucia, Trinidad, and Antigua, and in British Guiana; Roosevelt told Congress that the rights in Newfoundland and Bermuda were “gifts — generously given and gladly received,” while the others were acquired “in exchange for 50 of our over-age destroyers,” the most important reinforcement of national defense since the Louisiana Purchase.[^ch35-destroyers-message]
- **The legal question.** Roosevelt transmitted an opinion of the attorney general on his authority to make the arrangement, and the chapter should present the exchange as a precedent for executive agreements in foreign policy, connecting it to the debate over presidential power.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-beard]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the notes and the message. It cannot show that the destroyers changed the Battle of the Atlantic outcome, and the chapter should treat that claim as open.[^ch35-check-numbers]

### 4.3 Lend-Lease, March 1941

- **The claim.** After Churchill warned in December 1940 that Britain could no longer pay for supplies, Roosevelt proposed on December 17, 1940, that the United States lend or lease war material to nations whose defense it deemed vital, and Congress passed the Lend-Lease Act on March 11, 1941.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-lend-lease-act]
- **The statute.** The act authorized the president to sell, transfer, exchange, lease, or lend “defense articles” to any government “whose defense the President deems vital to the defense of the United States,” and it expressly barred the president from using the act to authorize convoys or to send American vessels into a combat area in violation of the Neutrality Act of 1939.[^ch35-lend-lease-act]
- **The scale.** Over the war the United States contracted Lend-Lease agreements with more than thirty countries and dispensed some $50 billion in assistance; Article VII tied repayment to a liberalized postwar economic order.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, its limits, and the totals. It cannot show that Lend-Lease alone saved Britain, and the chapter should present the aid as necessary but not sufficient.[^ch35-check-numbers]

### 4.4 The Four Freedoms and the Atlantic Charter

- **The claim.** Roosevelt gave the aid program its language of purpose in his annual message of January 6, 1941, and he and Churchill issued the Atlantic Charter on August 14, 1941, as a joint statement of war aims.[^ch35-four-freedoms][^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms][^ch35-atlantic-charter]
- **The Four Freedoms.** Roosevelt called for a world founded on “four essential human freedoms”: freedom of speech and expression, freedom of worship, freedom from want, and freedom from fear. The FDR Library records that the passage appeared only in the fourth of seven drafts and that the president dictated the peroration to his advisers.[^ch35-four-freedoms][^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms]
- **The charter.** Roosevelt and Churchill met aboard naval vessels at Placentia Bay, off Newfoundland, from August 9 to 12, 1941, and issued eight common principles on August 14, including no territorial aggrandizement, the right of peoples to choose their government, equal access to trade and raw materials, and a peace of “freedom from fear and want.” The FDR Library notes that the charter was a joint statement, not a treaty, from one nation technically neutral and one at war.[^ch35-atlantic-charter][^ch35-fdrlibrary-atlantic]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the texts and the conference. It cannot show that the principles constrained later Allied policy, and the chapter should present the charter as a statement of aims whose postwar meaning remained contested.[^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms][^ch35-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The undeclared naval war in the Atlantic

- **The claim.** In the fall of 1941 the United States became a participant in the Battle of the Atlantic without a declaration of war, and German attacks on American warships pushed the two countries toward open conflict.[^ch35-reuben-james][^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- **The incidents.** American destroyers escorting convoys and the German submarines that attacked them produced a series of incidents in 1941 — the USS *Greer* in September, the USS *Kearny* in October, and the USS *Reuben James*, torpedoed on October 31, 1941, while escorting Convoy HX-156, the first American warship lost in the war.[^ch35-reuben-james]
- **The policy shift.** In October and November 1941 Roosevelt sought and won repeal of neutrality provisions: on October 17 the House voted to allow the arming of American merchant ships, and the Senate later repealed the ban on American ships entering belligerent ports and combat zones.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- **The same evening.** On September 11, 1941, Roosevelt delivered a fireside chat on German attacks in the Atlantic, the same evening Lindbergh spoke in Des Moines, a simultaneity the chapter can use to hold §3 and §4 together.[^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm][^ch35-reuben-james]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the incidents and the votes. It cannot reconstruct the private orders or the submarine commanders’ intentions from the evidence consulted, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch35-check-numbers][^ch35-check-silences]

## 5. Japan, sanctions, Pearl Harbor, and declarations

### 5.1 The claim: two crises converge

- **The claim.** The American entry into the war came from the Pacific, but the Pacific crisis was entangled with the Atlantic one: the same American aid that supported Britain also shaped the confrontation with Japan, and the Axis alliance linked the two theaters.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth][^ch35-hitler-declaration]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the Pacific sequence and the Atlantic sequence as converging, not as separate wars, and should use the Tripartite Pact to show the linkage.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]
- **What the record can show.** It can show American and, in part, Japanese policy. It cannot fully reconstruct the Japanese government’s internal debates from the sources consulted, and the chapter should name that gap.[^ch35-check-japan]

### 5.2 Japan’s war in China and the breakdown of the Washington system

- **The claim.** Japan’s expansion in China after 1931 and especially after the Marco Polo Bridge incident of July 7, 1937, destroyed the framework of cooperation the United States had hoped to preserve in the Pacific.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth]
- **The events.** Japan installed a puppet government in Manchuria in 1931, which the United States refused to recognize under the Stimson Doctrine; full-scale war with China began in 1937; Japanese forces bombed the USS *Panay* in December 1937; Japan announced a “Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere” in August 1940; and it signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy on September 27, 1940.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the incidents and the American response. It cannot present Japanese motives as settled, and the chapter should avoid reducing Japan’s decision to a single cause.[^ch35-check-japan][^ch35-barnhart]

### 5.3 Sanctions, the freeze, and the Hull note

- **The claim.** American economic pressure tightened in stages — a partial cutoff in 1940, then a full freeze and effective oil embargo in 1941 — until Japan faced a choice between capitulation and war.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth]
- **The sequence.** In July 1940 Roosevelt cut off shipments of scrap iron, steel, and aviation fuel to Japan while allowing oil to continue; Japan moved into French Indochina; and in July 1941, after Japan’s further moves, Roosevelt froze Japanese assets in the United States, which effectively cut off Japan’s access to American oil. On November 26, 1941, Secretary of State Cordell Hull delivered the American note that restated the American position and became known as the Hull note.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-hull-note]
- **The interpretation.** Michael A. Barnhart’s argument that Japan’s resource dependence and the embargo made war a calculated response to economic strangulation is the strongest statement of the economic-conflict position; the chapter should present it in §6 rather than assert it here.[^ch35-barnhart][^ch35-check-debate-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the measures and the note. It cannot show that the embargo made war inevitable, and the chapter should present the sanctions as a contributing cause among several.[^ch35-check-numbers][^ch35-check-japan]

### 5.4 Pearl Harbor, December 7, 1941

- **The claim.** On December 7, 1941, Japanese naval and air forces attacked the American base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, in a surprise assault that crippled the Pacific Fleet and killed or wounded thousands.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-pearl-harbor-nps]
- **The figures.** The National Park Service records 2,403 service members and civilians killed and 1,178 injured, with the battleships USS *Arizona* and USS *Utah* permanently sunk and 188 aircraft destroyed. The National Archives describes the attack as among the greatest military surprises in the history of warfare, devastating the Pacific Fleet in less than two hours.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-nps][^ch35-day-of-infamy]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the attack, the losses, and the official American response. It cannot answer the counterfactual questions about warning and readiness, which belong to the intelligence debates outside this chapter’s scope.[^ch35-check-numbers][^ch35-check-scope]

### 5.5 Declarations and the end of neutrality

- **The claim.** Congress declared war on Japan on December 8, 1941, and on Germany and Italy on December 11, after Hitler declared war on the United States, ending the neutrality era and the isolationist contest.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-house-declarations][^ch35-hitler-declaration]
- **The address.** Roosevelt told a joint session of Congress that December 7 would be “a date which will live in infamy,” and the Senate voted unanimously for war while Jeanette Rankin of Montana cast the sole House dissent. The National Archives records that the president had considered a longer recitation of Japanese treachery but chose a brief appeal, and that he changed the draft’s first line to “infamy.”[^ch35-day-of-infamy]
- **The Axis declarations.** On December 11, 1941, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States; the House approved a declaration against Germany 393–0 and against Italy 399–0, with Rankin answering “Present.” Hitler’s Reichstag speech invoked the Tripartite Pact and antisemitic conspiracy claims.[^ch35-house-declarations][^ch35-hitler-declaration]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the votes, the speeches, and the dates. It cannot show how quickly ordinary Americans understood the war’s scale, and the chapter should end on the uncertainty of the moment rather than the knowledge of the outcome.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-check-dates-appD]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The neutrality statutes constrained Roosevelt and were progressively loosened.** From the arms embargo of 1935 to the repeal of neutrality provisions in late 1941, the legal framework moved from prohibition to aid to undeclared naval conflict.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-house-neutrality-1939]
- **Aid to Britain was substantial and contested.** The destroyers-for-bases exchange, Lend-Lease, and the Atlantic Charter marked a deliberate American commitment short of war.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-lend-lease-act][^ch35-atlantic-charter]
- **The Pacific crisis contributed directly to entry.** American economic pressure and Japan’s decision for war produced the attack of December 7, 1941.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-hull-note]
- **Germany’s declaration made the European war automatic.** Hitler’s December 11 declaration brought the United States into the European war without a separate debate.[^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-house-declarations]
- **Isolationism collapsed after the attack.** The near-unanimous war votes showed how quickly the opposition dissolved.[^ch35-house-declarations]

### 6.2 What is open

- **How much did Roosevelt intend entry?** One position holds that he maneuvered the country to war behind the appearance of neutrality; another holds that he responded to Axis aggression and moved only as far as politics allowed. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch35-beard][^ch35-langer-gleason][^ch35-divine]
- **How much did public opinion constrain or enable him?** One account treats opinion as a real brake; another treats Roosevelt as a leader who shaped opinion. The outline lacks a polling series, so the chapter should present the evidence it can and mark the gap.[^ch35-check-public-opinion][^ch35-divine][^ch35-cole]
- **Did the oil embargo make war in the Pacific inevitable?** Barnhart’s economic-conflict thesis and the escalation account differ on how decisive American pressure was.[^ch35-barnhart][^ch35-kennedy]
- **Was the outcome a contest of politics or a problem of strategy?** Cole restores the American political contest; Heinrichs presents Roosevelt as a global strategic manager. The chapter should weigh both.[^ch35-cole][^ch35-heinrichs]
- **Where did the initiative lie?** The chapter should ask how much the outcome turned on American choices and how much on German and Japanese decisions that Washington did not control.[^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-barnhart][^ch35-check-japan]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the United States moved from legal neutrality to war through a series of contingent decisions that narrowed options without making entry inevitable from 1937, and in which both American choices and foreign decisions mattered.[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-hitler-declaration]
- **Contingency.** Aid short of war, the Atlantic incidents, the freeze, and the Hull note were choices taken by people who could have chosen otherwise; none of them predetermined December 1941.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-hull-note]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold structural pressures — Britain’s need, Japan’s resource dependence, the Axis alliance — together with the contingent choices made within them.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-barnhart]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that the United States entered the war through a Pacific attack and a German declaration, but that the ground for entry had been prepared by four years of American decisions and arguments that the isolationists lost.[^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-check-table1]

## Legacies

The four years between 1937 and 1941 left a lasting legacy in American government. The destroyers-for-bases exchange and Lend-Lease established that a president could commit the country to far-reaching foreign obligations through executive agreement and statute, a precedent that later administrations used and that Congress would contest for decades.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-lend-lease-act]

The mobilization that began before entry became a permanent national security establishment. The agencies, military programs, and global commitments built to fight the Axis fed directly into the Cold War institutions that Chapter 37 describes, and the argument over how much power a president may wield abroad without a declaration of war has never been settled.[^ch35-lend-lease-act][^ch35-check-ch36]

The isolationist tradition did not vanish with the December votes. It survived in later arguments about alliances, foreign aid, and the terms of American engagement, and the America First name returned to American politics in a different era. The chapter should note the recurrence without claiming that later movements are the same as the 1940–41 organization.[^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-check-silences]

The chapter’s harder legacy is evidentiary. The fight over entry produced a documentary record built by the state and by organized campaigns, which means the arguments of powerful and connected people survived in detail while the reasoning of ordinary Americans left fewer traces. That asymmetry should shape how the chapter uses polling, newspapers, and memoirs.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-check-public-opinion]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The chapter should show how British appeals, Japanese decisions, and German moves set much of the American agenda, so that other nations appear as actors rather than as scenery for American choices.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-hitler-declaration]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, cabinet officers, committee leaders, and major newspapers and under-represents the poor, the unorganized, and dissenting isolationists who left no archive. The Japanese government’s internal reasoning is also under-represented in the sources consulted. The chapter should name both asymmetries.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-check-japan]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 34 owns the New Deal; Chapter 36 owns coalition warfare; Chapter 37 owns postwar containment; Chapter 30 owns the earlier debate over neutrality and intervention of 1914–17. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch35-check-ch34][^ch35-check-ch36][^ch35-check-dates-appD]

## Summary

- Congress passed neutrality acts in 1935, 1936, and 1937 that embargoed arms to belligerents, banned loans, and restricted American travel and shipping; the 1937 act also contained Roosevelt’s cash-and-carry concession.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- After Germany invaded Poland, Roosevelt called a special session on September 21, 1939; the House repealed the arms embargo on November 2 by 243 to 181, and he signed the cash-and-carry revision on November 4.[^ch35-house-neutrality-1939]
- France fell to Germany in June 1940, leaving Britain alone; Roosevelt declared American neutrality in law while saying he could not ask Americans to be neutral in thought.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies, led by William Allen White, and the America First Committee, launched on September 4, 1940 under General Robert E. Wood, fought the aid question in public.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- On September 11, 1941, Lindbergh told a Des Moines rally that “the British, the Jewish, and the Roosevelt administration” were pressing the country toward war, an antisemitic charge that discredited him and his cause.[^ch35-lindbergh-text][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]
- On September 2, 1940, Roosevelt exchanged fifty over-age destroyers for ninety-nine-year leases on British bases by executive agreement, and on March 11, 1941, he signed the Lend-Lease Act, which authorized aid to nations whose defense he deemed vital.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-lend-lease-act]
- Roosevelt’s Four Freedoms message of January 6, 1941, and the Atlantic Charter of August 14, 1941, stated American war aims; the charter was a joint statement, not a treaty.[^ch35-four-freedoms][^ch35-atlantic-charter]
- American destroyers fought an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic in 1941; the USS *Reuben James* was torpedoed on October 31, 1941 while escorting Convoy HX-156, the first American warship lost.[^ch35-reuben-james]
- After Japanese expansion in China and French Indochina, the United States froze Japanese assets in July 1941 and delivered the Hull note on November 26, 1941; Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, killing 2,403 people and wounding 1,178.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-hull-note][^ch35-pearl-harbor-nps]
- Congress declared war on Japan on December 8, 1941, and on Germany and Italy on December 11, after Hitler declared war; the near-unanimous votes ended the isolationist contest.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-house-declarations][^ch35-hitler-declaration]

> *A closing note.* Between 1937 and 1941 the United States moved from a legal neutrality meant to keep it out of Europe’s wars to a set of commitments — bases, Lend-Lease, a charter of war aims, and an undeclared naval war — that made conflict difficult to avoid. The final decisions were not American: Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, and Germany declared war four days later. But the ground had been prepared by four years of American argument and policy, and the isolationists who had fought every step saw their cause collapse in a week. The chapter’s question — how did neutrality give way to intervention, and how much of that outcome was chosen? — is answered by that double record of foreign decision and American preparation.[^ch35-lend-lease-act][^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-house-declarations]
