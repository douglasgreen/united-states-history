# Chapter 48. The Populist Turn and the First Trump Presidency (2015–2021)

> “This American carnage stops right here and stops right now.”[^ch48-trump-inaugural-2017]
>
> — Donald J. Trump, inaugural address, Washington, D.C., January 20, 2017.[^ch48-trump-inaugural-2017]

> “You know, to just be grossly generalistic, you could put half of Trump’s supporters into what I call the basket of deplorables. Right?”[^ch48-clinton-deplorables-2016]
>
> — Hillary Rodham Clinton, remarks at a fundraiser, New York, September 9, 2016.[^ch48-clinton-deplorables-2016]

> “It is as it has always been: the language of people who see ordinary people as a noble group and the elite class as self-serving.”[^ch48-kazin-2010-interview]
>
> — Michael Kazin, interview with NPR, February 5, 2010.[^ch48-kazin-2010-interview]

Three voices, three measurements of the same country. Trump described a nation in decline and promised that the carnage would end at his inauguration. Clinton divided his supporters into two baskets and placed half beyond persuasion. Kazin placed the language of ordinary people against a self-serving elite in a long American tradition. They disagree about whether the turn was a rupture, a dismissed constituency’s revenge, or an old persuasion returning.[^ch48-trump-inaugural-2017][^ch48-clinton-deplorables-2016][^ch48-kazin-2010-interview][^ch48-check-epigraphs]

On the morning of June 16, 2015, Donald J. Trump came down the escalator into the lobby of Trump Tower at 725 Fifth Avenue in Manhattan. His daughter Ivanka Trump introduced him. The crowd was large enough that the campaign and the press would dispute its size; no reliable count is used here. He carried a prepared text. “Our country is in serious trouble,” it began. “We are not respected by anyone. We are a laughing stock all over the world.” The text called politicians “all talk and no action,” said they would never bring the country to “the Promised Land,” and then supplied the sentence the cameras had come for: “Ladies and gentlemen, I am officially running for President of the United States.”[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]

The prepared text was shorter than the speech he delivered. The delivered version expanded the text and added passages about the Iraq War and the candidate’s wealth, and it included a sentence about Mexican migrants that the prepared text did not: “They’re bringing drugs. They’re bringing crime. They’re rapists. And some, I assume, are good people.” The sentence is quoted because it became central to the campaign’s argument about immigration and to the charge that the campaign trafficked in prejudice. It is not evidence that the claim was true. Within hours, the announcement had become a national controversy, and the controversy was part of the campaign’s appeal.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]

Six days later, on June 22, Trump filed a statement of candidacy with the Federal Election Commission, turning the television event into a formal campaign.[^ch48-fec-form2-2015] The record establishes the date and venue of the announcement, the June 22 filing, and the texts. It does not establish the size of the crowd, what each person in the lobby thought, or that the announcement caused what followed. On the morning of the announcement, no one knew that Trump would win the nomination, that he would win the Electoral College while losing the popular vote, that he would sign a large tax cut, that a pandemic would define his final year, or that a mob would attack the Capitol. The scene holds only the moment when a candidate with no prior office promised a comeback and named the country as a scene of decline. The question it left open is the question of this chapter: how did a campaign organized around grievance exercise and contest presidential power, and by what distinct steps?[^ch48-check-scene][^ch48-check-scope]

This chapter covers the years from the 2015 announcement through the first Trump presidency and the immediate aftermath of the January 6, 2021, attack.[^ch48-check-dates-appD] It draws on election returns, executive orders, statutes, court opinions, investigative reports, public-health data, and the secondary literature. It treats “populism,” “the base,” “fake news,” “the deep state,” “the wall,” “the resistance,” and “the establishment” as contested names. Populism here names a language that claims to speak for a virtuous people against corrupt elites; the chapter uses the term as a contested analytical category, not a synonym for demagoguery. The record over-represents presidents, officials, and journalists and under-represents migrants, detained and separated families, the sick and the dead, low-wage workers, and the imprisoned. Where those voices do not survive, the chapter names the silence rather than inventing a witness. Every statistic carries its source, units, denominator, and date.[^ch48-check-names][^ch48-check-silences][^ch48-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 examines the announcement, the primaries, the general-election campaign, and the changing electoral geography. §2 examines the 2016 returns, the split verdict, the competing explanations, and the Russian interference. §3 examines the tax law, deregulation, the judicial appointments, and trade and treaty actions. §4 examines the travel restrictions, border enforcement, asylum, and family separation. §5 examines the Mueller report, the first impeachment, the altered Dorian graphic, and the media environment. §6 examines the pandemic, the 2020 election, the attempt to overturn it, January 6, and the second impeachment. §7 asks what the period settled and what remains open. The chapter begins at Trump Tower, where a candidate who had never held office promised a comeback and a country in trouble.[^ch48-check-scope]

Chapter 47 owns the 2008 election, the recovery, health reform, and the polarization that formed the background to this chapter; this chapter names those developments without re-narrating them. Chapter 49 owns the Biden years and the 2024 election, and this chapter closes by turning to the release of *Dobbs* on June 24, 2022, where Chapter 49 begins.[^ch48-check-ch47][^ch48-check-ch49]

## 1. Announcement and primaries (2015–2016)

### 1.1 The claim: a crowded field and a candidate the party did not choose

**The claim.** Trump entered a Republican field that reached 17 major candidates by the criterion used here, was opposed by much of the party’s national leadership, and won the nomination by winning pluralities in state after state while his rivals divided the rest. On the Democratic side, Hillary Rodham Clinton defeated Bernie Sanders after a longer and more divided contest than the party expected. The nomination fights were organizational and coalitional events, not a single national verdict. A party chooses through a calendar, a set of rules, and a series of state elections, and each shaped the result.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015][^ch48-fec-2016]

The section runs from the announcement and the field through the primaries, the conventions, the general-election campaign, and Map 38. It ends with what the map can and cannot show about the coalition that elected Trump.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015][^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-map38]

### 1.2 The announcement and the field

The announcement was a staged event with a written text, and the two did not match. The prepared remarks identified “politicians” as “the problem,” promised to “Make America Great Again,” and attacked trade with China and Japan and migration across the Mexican border. The delivered speech was longer and more improvisational, adding the sentence about Mexican migrants, a passage about the Iraq War, and an account of the candidate’s wealth. The gap is itself evidence: the campaign was built for television, and the candidate treated the text as a starting point rather than a script.[^ch48-trump-announcement-2015]

The field was large. By the criterion used here — a declared candidacy, a national campaign, and participation in the Republican primary debates — 17 candidates mounted major campaigns. The number depends on the threshold, and the chapter states its criterion rather than presenting a contested count as settled. The size mattered because it divided the opposition: a candidate with a committed plurality could win state after state while the majority that preferred someone else split among several rivals.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]

The party’s national leadership did not coordinate behind Trump. Many elected Republicans endorsed other candidates, and the party’s donors divided. Trump presented himself as an outsider against a party that had failed, and he treated the hostility of party leaders as evidence for his case. The nomination would test whether the party’s rules and informal leadership could stop a candidate who spoke for a faction the leaders did not control.[^ch48-fec-2016]

### 1.3 The primaries and the conventions

The Republican primaries turned pluralities into delegates. Trump lost the Iowa caucuses to Senator Ted Cruz of Texas, won the New Hampshire primary, and then accumulated delegates across the South and the Midwest. He won pluralities rather than majorities in many states, and the rules varied: some states awarded delegates winner-take-all, others proportionally, and several used thresholds that could shut a rival out. The delegate arithmetic, not the national popular vote, decided the nomination.[^ch48-fec-2016]

The Democratic contest was closer and more divided than the party expected. Clinton began with the support of most elected Democrats and a national organization. Sanders, an independent senator from Vermont who caucused with the Democrats, campaigned as a democratic socialist and made inequality, trade, and the power of the wealthy central to his case. Clinton won the delegate count and the nomination. Sanders won states and delegates, and his campaign showed that the party’s left wing could mount a national effort on a program more radical than the leadership’s.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]

Each party’s convention in July 2016 nominated its candidate and adopted a platform. Neither party was fully unified. The Republican convention presented a party whose nominee had attacked its last president and its congressional leadership; the Democratic convention presented a party whose primary had exposed a division between its establishment and its left.[^ch48-fec-2016]

### 1.4 The general-election campaign

The general election matched two candidates with high unfavorable ratings and two coalitions that disliked each other more than they liked their nominees. It ran from the conventions through three debates and a final month consumed by scandal. In October, the release of a 2005 recording in which Trump described grabbing women without their consent dominated the news. Days before the election, the FBI director, James B. Comey, sent Congress a letter about the Clinton email investigation. Each event is documented; the effect of either on the outcome is not directly measurable.[^ch48-2016-campaign-documents][^ch48-check-scope]

Clinton’s September 9, 2016, remark at a New York fundraiser became one of the campaign’s defining moments. She said that “to just be grossly generalistic,” one “could put half of Trump’s supporters into what I call the basket of deplorables,” and she described that half as “the racist, sexist, homophobic, xenophobic, Islamophobic — you name it.” She said the other half were people the government had let down and who deserved empathy. The remark was evidence of how each side described the other’s coalition, and it was a political gift to the Trump campaign, which used it to argue that the Clinton campaign held ordinary Americans in contempt. Clinton later said she regretted saying “half.”[^ch48-clinton-deplorables-2016]

### 1.5 Map 38: the changing electoral geography

Map 38 plots the changing electoral geography from 2000 through 2020. The panel used in this section ends in 2016; the 2020 panel belongs to §6 and is not read back into the 2015–16 narrative. The map uses official returns matched to census geography, states its years and geographic units, and normalizes the vote as a share or a percentage-point change. It shows that the coalition that elected Trump differed from the one that elected Barack Obama in 2008 and 2012, and that the change was concentrated in particular kinds of places. It is not a map of motives. A shift in a county’s margin is a measurement of votes, not evidence that any group changed its mind.[^ch48-check-map38]

The map’s limits are part of its argument. It can show where the vote changed, but not why, and it cannot distinguish a change in turnout from a change in how the same voters voted. Those questions require precinct returns, voter files, and surveys. The map is a description of a changing electorate, not a causal explanation.[^ch48-check-map38][^ch48-fec-2016]

## 2. The 2016 election (2016–2017)

### 2.1 The claim: a split verdict that a system converted into a win

**The claim.** Clinton won the national popular vote by nearly three million votes, but Trump won the Electoral College, 304–227. The chapter holds both results at once rather than treating either as the whole story. The result, the composition of the electorate, the competing explanations, and the foreign interference that accompanied the campaign are separate evidence trails. The system converted a minority of the popular vote into a presidency, and the conversion was constitutional, not accidental.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

The section runs from the returns through the split verdict, the competing explanations, the Russian interference, and the transition. Map 38 is carried forward as a description of the changing geography, not as a verdict on the cause.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-mutz-2018][^ch48-autor-2020]

### 2.2 The returns and the split verdict

The Federal Election Commission records Clinton with 65,853,514 popular votes, or 48.18 percent, and Trump with 62,984,828, or 46.09 percent. The margin was 2,868,686 votes. The Electoral College vote was 304 for Trump to 227 for Clinton after a small number of faithless electors. Other compilations differ slightly because of write-ins, rounding, and the date a canvas was finalized, and the chapter names the series it uses rather than mixing them.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]

The split was the product of state law and state margins. Clinton’s national margin came overwhelmingly from California, New York, and other large states. Trump’s Electoral College margin came from states that had voted Democratic in recent elections, including Pennsylvania, Michigan, and Wisconsin, where his margins were narrow. One count weighs every vote equally; the other weighs states. The chapter does not treat the popular vote as the “real” result or the Electoral College as a formality.[^ch48-fec-2016]

Turnout and the composition of the electorate are separate questions. Exit polls are estimates with sampling error, and they describe self-reported voters rather than a census. The safest statement is that the electorate was closely divided, that Clinton won more votes, and that Trump won the states the system weighted most heavily.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-check-numbers]

### 2.3 Explaining the result: dislocation, status, and backlash

The first account emphasizes economic dislocation. David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson, and Kaveh Majlesi studied local labor markets exposed to the rise in Chinese import competition and found that affected districts shifted toward the Republican Party and toward more extreme candidates. The measure is trade exposure, the period is the decade and a half after China’s entry into the World Trade Organization, and the finding is about districts, not individuals. It explains a real change in the political geography of manufacturing regions, but not every Trump voter, and it cannot establish individual motive.[^ch48-autor-2020][^ch48-check-debate-works]

The second account emphasizes status threat and cultural backlash. Diana C. Mutz used panel data to argue that changes in financial well-being had little effect on individual vote choice in 2016 while measures of status threat did. Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart argued that a cultural backlash among older social conservatives, rather than economic hardship alone, drove support for authoritarian-populist candidates. These accounts work at the level of individual attitudes. Their weakness is that “status threat” is contested: it can name a response to changing hierarchies, a defense of social position, or an anxiety that surveys capture imperfectly.[^ch48-mutz-2018][^ch48-norris-inglehart-2019]

The third account emphasizes place and party. Katherine J. Cramer’s study of rural Wisconsin found a politics of resentment rooted in place and in a perceived urban disregard; her fieldwork preceded 2016, and she described a politics already visible in state elections. Sam Rosenfeld’s history of party polarization traces the parties’ sorting to decades of organizing rather than a single election. Together they suggest that the turn was the latest stage of a longer conflict between places and institutions.[^ch48-cramer-2016][^ch48-rosenfeld-2018]

### 2.4 Russian interference and the investigation

The special counsel’s report describes a Russian social-media and hacking operation that interfered in the 2016 election “in sweeping and systematic fashion.” It identifies “numerous links” between Russians and the Trump campaign. And it states that the investigation “did not establish that members of the Trump Campaign conspired or coordinated with the Russian government in its election interference activities.” Those sentences must be read together. The first establishes interference; the second establishes contacts; the third states what the evidence could not prove under the legal standard the office applied.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

The report also set out an obstruction analysis. It did not conclude that the president committed a crime, and it did not exonerate him. The report is a redacted prosecutorial document, not a neutral history: it records evidence gathered under a specific legal standard and omits material the attorney general withheld. The investigation’s origins and conduct are contested, and the inspector general’s review and the Senate Intelligence Committee’s report must be read beside it.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-check-debate-works]

The interference did not decide the election. The report did not establish that Russian activity changed enough votes in enough states to alter the Electoral College, and it did not claim that it did. What the record establishes is that a foreign government intervened, that Americans associated with the campaign welcomed some of the help, and that the investigation could not prove coordination. The distinction between interference and impact is essential.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

### 2.5 The transition

The transition from the Obama administration to the Trump administration culminated in the inauguration on January 20, 2017. The president-elect named his cabinet, installed a senior staff, and prepared a first set of executive orders. The transition shows what the new administration intended before it had to govern, and how quickly the campaign’s rhetoric became policy.[^ch48-trump-inaugural-2017][^ch48-check-scope]

The section’s question is what the 2016 result did and did not decide. It decided who would hold the presidency and which party would control Congress. It did not decide what the administration would do with that power, whether the coalition would hold, or whether the institutions would constrain it. Those questions belong to the sections that follow.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

## 3. Tax, regulatory, and executive policy (2017–2020)

### 3.1 The claim: a governing program carried through executive power and one-party legislation

**The claim.** With unified Republican control of Congress in 2017–18, the administration and its congressional allies enacted a large tax cut and confirmed a series of judges. After the House flipped in 2018, the administration governed increasingly through executive orders, rule changes, and trade actions. The chapter distinguishes what required legislation from what the executive could do alone. That distinction explains why some of the administration’s most consequential actions were reversible and others were not.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-supreme-court-appointments][^ch48-fec-2018]

The section runs from the tax law through deregulation, the judicial appointments, and trade and treaty actions. The courts are a connecting thread: they reviewed the tax law’s implementation, the deregulatory rules, the travel restrictions, and the administration’s attempts to end programs it disliked.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-supreme-court-appointments][^ch48-paris-withdrawal-2020]

### 3.2 The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act became Public Law 115-97 on December 22, 2017. It was enacted through budget reconciliation, the procedure that allowed the Senate to pass it with a simple majority rather than the 60 votes most legislation requires. The law cut the corporate income-tax rate to 21 percent, changed individual rates and deductions, and altered the international tax treatment of corporations. Many individual provisions were set to expire after 2025, while the corporate rate cut was permanent. That design created a fiscal cliff and made the law’s long-run effect depend on what a later Congress would do.[^ch48-tcja-2017]

Supporters argued that the law would raise investment, wages, and growth. Critics argued that it would increase deficits and concentrate its benefits at the top. The enacted provisions are documented; the revenue and distributional estimates depend on their methods and assumptions. Projections are not outcomes, and a modeled estimate of the law’s effect is not the effect itself. The difference is one of the central arguments about the law.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Deregulation and the executive program

The administration used executive orders and rulemaking to change policy without legislation. Executive Order 13771, “Reducing Regulation and Controlling Regulatory Costs,” signed on January 30, 2017, directed agencies to identify two regulations to repeal for each new one and to keep the incremental cost of new rules at or below zero.[^ch48-eo-13771-2017] The order did not apply to independent agencies and could not override statutes. It was a management tool, not a statute, and its effect depended on how agencies counted costs and which rules they chose to repeal.[^ch48-check-sources]

The administration rolled back or revised numerous environmental, financial, labor, and public-health rules. A count of rules depends on the register used and on each rule’s stage: proposed, final, or rescinded. Claims about the largest deregulation in history depend on how rules, costs, and benefits are counted. A rule that is proposed is not a rule that is final; a rule that is rescinded is not a rule that never existed. The count’s method matters more than the superlative.[^ch48-check-sources][^ch48-check-numbers]

### 3.4 Judicial appointments and the courts

Trump nominated Neil Gorsuch in 2017, Brett Kavanaugh in 2018, and Amy Coney Barrett in 2020. Each was confirmed by the Senate after a contested hearing. The three appointments gave the Court a conservative majority and reshaped its direction on questions that ranged from administrative power to abortion. The Senate record supplies the exact nomination and confirmation dates and votes. The appointments should be described from that record rather than from the later decisions that cited them.[^ch48-supreme-court-appointments]

The administration also confirmed a large number of appeals- and district-court judges. The exact count depends on the period and the court level, and it should come from the official records rather than an unsourced total. The lower courts matter because most federal litigation ends there. The appointments were the administration’s most durable domestic achievement and the part of its program least vulnerable to reversal.[^ch48-supreme-court-appointments][^ch48-check-numbers]

The caution is chronological. A judge confirmed in 2017 may vote in a case decided in 2022, but the president who appointed the judge did not know how the case would come out. The chapter connects the appointments to later decisions without presenting a later ruling as what the appointing president knew at confirmation.[^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Trade, treaty withdrawals, and foreign-policy actions

The administration imposed tariffs on Chinese goods under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, and it renegotiated the North American Free Trade Agreement into the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement, which entered into force in 2020. It announced withdrawal from the Paris Agreement in 2017, gave formal notice in November 2019, and left the agreement in November 2020. It withdrew from the Iran nuclear agreement in 2018. Each action used a different legal instrument, and the chapter states the instrument for each rather than treating them as one category.[^ch48-check-sources][^ch48-paris-withdrawal-2020]

The withdrawals illustrate the difference between an announcement, a formal notice, and an effective withdrawal. The Paris Agreement’s terms set one timetable; the Iran agreement’s terms and the other parties’ response set another. The trade actions illustrate the difference between a tariff imposed by executive authority and an agreement approved by Congress. Foreign policy here was a series of actions, each with its own authority and constraints.[^ch48-paris-withdrawal-2020][^ch48-check-sources]

## 4. Travel restrictions, border enforcement, and family separation (2017–2020)

### 4.1 The claim: immigration policy as the administration’s sharpest exercise of executive power

**The claim.** The travel restrictions, the narrowing of asylum, and the family-separation policy were carried out largely through executive action. They produced intense litigation and public conflict, and they fell hardest on people with the least voice in the record. The chapter traces each from its instrument to its consequences, and it distinguishes what the law required from what the administration chose. Immigration was the policy area in which the administration’s rhetoric and its executive power met most directly.[^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-doj-zero-tolerance-2018][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]

The section runs from the travel ban and its litigation through border enforcement and asylum, the family-separation policy, and the human costs. It ends on the silences in the record, because the people most affected are the least represented in it.[^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]

### 4.2 The travel ban and its litigation

Trump signed Executive Order 13769 on January 27, 2017. The order suspended entry from several countries, paused the refugee program, and took effect immediately. Its rollout produced confusion at airports, where travelers with valid visas were detained, and protests followed. A revised order, Executive Order 13780, followed on March 6, 2017. Proclamation 9645, issued in September 2017, replaced the earlier orders with country-specific restrictions and a process for review.[^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-eo-13780-2017][^ch48-proclamation-9645-2017]

In *Trump v. Hawaii*, decided June 26, 2018, the Supreme Court upheld Proclamation 9645 as a lawful exercise of the president’s authority under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f) and rejected the Establishment Clause challenge. The vote was 5–4. The majority treated the proclamation as a national-security judgment and reviewed the president’s findings deferentially. The dissent argued that the order’s history and its selection of countries showed that it was directed at Muslims, and that the Court should have looked behind the proclamation’s text. The chapter gives the holding and the vote and distinguishes the statutory question from the constitutional one.[^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018]

The administration called the measures national-security tools. Critics called them a “Muslim ban,” pointing to the campaign’s earlier proposal for a “total and complete shutdown of Muslims entering the United States.”[^ch48-trump-muslim-ban-2015] The chapter tests the claims against the orders’ terms and the Court’s reasoning. The first order was broader than the third; the third included countries with Muslim majorities and others with different histories. The label and the record are not the same.[^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Border enforcement and asylum

The administration reduced the refugee-admissions ceiling, terminated or narrowed temporary protected status for several countries, and restricted asylum through rules and metering at the border. Each action had its own instrument and date, and each was challenged in court or contested by advocates. “The border” should not be treated as a single policy.[^ch48-check-sources][^ch48-check-scope]

“The border” is not a single measure. Apprehensions, asylum claims, removals, and the backlog are different series with different definitions, and they can move in different directions at once. A rise in apprehensions may mean more enforcement, more migration, or both. A rise in the backlog may mean more claims, slower processing, or both. Each series requires its definition and source, and none should be used as a proxy for another.[^ch48-check-numbers]

### 4.4 Family separation and zero tolerance

Attorney General Jeff Sessions announced a “zero-tolerance” policy for illegal entry on April 6, 2018. The policy directed federal prosecutors to charge all such offenses criminally under 8 U.S.C. §1325(a). Because children of criminally prosecuted parents could not be held with them in federal custody, the policy separated families. The administration later ended the practice amid an outcry, but the separations had already occurred, and the government did not have a single system for tracking the families it had separated.[^ch48-doj-zero-tolerance-2018]

The Department of Health and Human Services inspector general reported that, as of December 2018, the department had identified 2,737 children who had been separated from their parents or guardians and were required to be reunified under the *Ms. L* court order. The report also stated that the number did not represent the full scope of separations and that thousands more may have been separated before the court-ordered accounting. The figure is a floor, not a total. The government’s lack of a tracking system is why the full number is unknown, and the uncertainty is part of the finding.[^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019][^ch48-check-numbers]

The human cost is the part of the record hardest to document and easiest to forget. The inspector general’s report and the court orders record the separations, the reunifications, and the gaps, but not what each child or parent experienced. The chapter names the children and parents who could not be heard and uses the findings rather than inventing testimony.[^ch48-check-silences][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]

## 5. Courts, investigations, impeachment, and public information (2017–2021)

### 5.1 The claim: a president under investigation, and a fight over what was true

**The claim.** The administration was under investigation from its first months, faced a special counsel and a first impeachment over its dealings with Ukraine, and repeatedly contested the factual record, from the size of its inauguration crowd to the path of a hurricane. The investigations and the information fight were connected exercises in institutional contest. Each asked who had the authority to establish a fact, and each became a test of whether institutions could hold a president to a record he did not accept.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-noaa-statement-2019]

The section runs from the Russia investigation and the Mueller report through the first impeachment and acquittal, the In Focus on the altered Dorian graphic, and the media environment. The sequence is chronological where it can be, but the argument is thematic: the same president who disputed the crowd at his inauguration would later dispute a hurricane forecast, an election result, and the legitimacy of the investigation into his campaign.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-ukraine-call-2019][^ch48-noaa-statement-2019]

### 5.2 The Russia investigation and the Mueller report

The Justice Department appointed Robert S. Mueller III as special counsel in May 2017. The office submitted its report in March 2019, and the department released a redacted version in April 2019. The investigation produced indictments of Russian nationals and entities, Trump campaign associates, and others, and it established a detailed record of the campaign’s contacts with Russians.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

The report describes “sweeping and systematic” Russian interference, identifies “numerous links” between Russians and the campaign, and states that the investigation “did not establish that members of the Trump Campaign conspired or coordinated with the Russian government in its election interference activities.” On obstruction, it set out a series of presidential actions and declined to conclude that they constituted a crime. It also declined to exonerate the president. The two findings are not contradictory: one concerns coordination, the other obstruction, and the report applied a legal standard that was not a historian’s standard of proof.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019]

The report is one evidentiary record among several. The Senate Intelligence Committee’s bipartisan report examined the same interference, and the inspector general reviewed the investigation’s origins and the surveillance applications that preceded it. The chapter presents these records together and does not treat any single document as the final word. The report’s redactions, its legal framework, and its role in a political fight all limit what it can establish.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The first impeachment and acquittal

On July 25, 2019, Trump spoke by telephone with Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelensky. The White House memorandum records Trump asking for a “favor” and for investigations connected to a political rival and to a discredited theory about the 2016 election. The memorandum is a record of the call, not a verbatim recording, and its pages are the primary evidence for the charge that followed.[^ch48-ukraine-call-2019]

The House adopted H. Res. 755 on December 18, 2019, on two articles: abuse of power and obstruction of Congress. The votes were 230–197 and 229–198. The first charged that the president had used his office to solicit foreign interference in the 2020 election and had conditioned a White House meeting and military aid on the investigations he wanted. The second charged that he had directed the defiance of congressional subpoenas. The House did not allege a statutory crime; impeachment is a constitutional remedy, not a criminal indictment.[^ch48-hres-755-2019]

The Senate acquitted Trump on both articles on February 5, 2020, by votes of 48–52 and 47–53, short of the two-thirds needed. Senator Mitt Romney of Utah voted to convict on the abuse-of-power article, the only Republican to do so. The acquittal ended the first impeachment and did not end the argument. The House’s record, the Senate’s trial record, and the whistleblower complaint remained part of the public record, and the dispute over the president’s dealings with Ukraine became one of the grounds on which the 2020 election was fought.[^ch48-senate-acquittal-2020][^ch48-hres-755-2019]

The impeachment record requires care. An accusation is not a finding, and an acquittal is not an exoneration on the facts. The House found that the conduct warranted impeachment; the Senate did not muster the votes to convict. Neither settles the historical question of what the president did or why.[^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2020][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 5.4 The In Focus: the altered Dorian forecast graphic

**In Focus.** On September 1, 2019, President Trump wrote that Alabama would be hit by Hurricane Dorian. The National Weather Service office in Birmingham replied that Alabama would see no impacts. On September 4, in the Oval Office, the president displayed a National Hurricane Center forecast graphic on which a black line appeared to extend the forecast cone into Alabama. On September 6, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration issued an unsigned statement saying that information provided to the president and the public showed that tropical-storm-force winds from Dorian could affect Alabama and that the Birmingham office’s tweet “spoke in absolute terms that were inconsistent with probabilities from the best forecast products available at the time.”[^ch48-noaa-statement-2019]

The Department of Commerce inspector general later examined the statement. The report found that NOAA had issued it in response to a request from the White House acting chief of staff, that the statement rebuked the Birmingham office, and that the episode raised the possibility of political interference. It faulted NOAA’s handling and did not find that the forecast had been altered by NOAA.[^ch48-noaa-oig-2020]

The close reading treats the graphic as evidence of how a forecast was altered and defended, not as evidence about the storm. The September 1 statement, September 4 display, and September 6 statement are distinct events. Image 8 sets the original product beside the image as displayed. It shows how a presidential statement became an institutional problem for the agency that contradicted it.[^ch48-noaa-statement-2019][^ch48-noaa-oig-2020][^ch48-check-focus]

### 5.5 The media and public information environment

The period saw sustained attacks on the press, the spread of the term “fake news,” the rise of social-media misinformation, and a documented asymmetry in the reach of partisan media. “The media” is not one actor: the mainstream press, cable news, and social platforms had different incentives, audiences, and relationships to the truth.[^ch48-benkler-2018][^ch48-check-sources]

Yochai Benkler, Robert Faris, and Hal Roberts argue that a distinct right-wing media ecosystem, rather than a symmetric collapse of the public sphere, produced the period’s asymmetric misinformation. Their study traces how false claims moved from fringe sites to mainstream outlets and how the structure of the ecosystem shaped what its audience saw. The account focuses on one ecosystem and period, and it does not claim that the left had no misinformation; it does claim that the two sides were not symmetrical.[^ch48-benkler-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]

## 6. COVID-19, the 2020 election, January 6, and the second impeachment (2020–2021)

### 6.1 The claim: a pandemic, a contested election, and an attack on the transfer of power

**The claim.** The administration’s final year brought a pandemic that killed hundreds of thousands of Americans, a presidential election held under emergency conditions, a sustained attempt to overturn its result, an attack on the Capitol, and a second impeachment and acquittal. These were connected events with distinct evidence trails. The pandemic changed how Americans voted; the election result changed what the president was willing to do; and the attack tested whether the constitutional system could complete a transfer of power against a sitting president’s refusal to accept it.[^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021][^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-hres-24-2021]

The section runs from the pandemic through the 2020 election, the attempt to overturn it, the January 6 attack, and the second impeachment and trial. The chronological order is also the analytic order: each event created the conditions for the next, and none was inevitable.[^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

### 6.2 The pandemic

The World Health Organization characterized COVID-19 as a pandemic on March 11, 2020. The president declared a national emergency on March 13. The declarations came after weeks of uncertainty, and they did not settle the disputes over testing, masks, school reopening, or supplies. The pandemic was a series of waves, each with its own geography, politics, and toll.[^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-national-emergency-2020]

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated 545,600 to 660,200 excess deaths in the United States from January 26, 2020, through February 27, 2021. The agency also reported that the COVID-19 death toll surpassed 400,000 in January 2021. Excess deaths are not the same as deaths attributed to the virus: the first measures mortality above what would have been expected, and the second counts deaths recorded with a COVID-19 diagnosis. The range is an estimate, not a count, and its method and period belong with the figure.[^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021][^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-check-numbers]

The federal response included travel restrictions, guidance disputes, an effort to accelerate vaccine development, and the authorization of the first vaccines in December 2020. Each action had a date and an instrument, and each was contested. The chapter does not treat the vaccine as a refutation of the criticism, or the criticism as a refutation of the achievement.[^ch48-fda-pfizer-eua-2020][^ch48-national-emergency-2020]

The pandemic’s record over-represents officials and under-represents the dead, the sick, the disabled, and the essential workers who bore the risk. The toll fell unevenly by race, occupation, and age, and the data on who died and who survived is incomplete in ways that reflect who was counted. The silence is part of the history, not a gap to be filled with invention.[^ch48-check-silences][^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021]

### 6.3 The 2020 election

The Federal Election Commission records Joseph R. Biden Jr. with 306 electoral votes and Donald J. Trump with 232. Biden won about 81.3 million popular votes, or about 51.3 percent, to Trump’s about 74.2 million, or about 46.8 percent, on a total of about 158.4 million votes. Some widely cited totals differ slightly by compilation, and the chapter names the series it uses.[^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-check-numbers]

The election was held during the pandemic, with expanded mail voting and altered procedures. States changed their rules for absentee ballots, drop boxes, and early voting, and the changes became part of the political argument before the votes were counted. Turnout reached a record level. The changes and the turnout belong in the record, and a change in procedure is not the same as a change in outcome.[^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-check-numbers]

Biden’s Electoral College margin was comfortable; the state margins that produced it were not. A shift of a few tens of thousands of votes in a handful of states would have produced a different result. That is not a claim that the result was illegitimate. It explains why the losing campaign believed a legal challenge might work. Map 38’s 2020 panel shows the geographic pattern of Biden’s gains and Trump’s losses; like the 2016 panel, it describes the vote, not the reasons for it.[^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-check-map38]

### 6.4 The attempt to overturn the election

The president and his allies filed dozens of lawsuits and pressed state officials and legislatures to overturn the result. Courts repeatedly rejected the claims, some on standing, some on the merits, and some on procedure. A dismissal on standing is not a finding that fraud occurred, and a dismissal on the merits is not proof that the election was flawless. Each ruling decided a legal question, not the whole historical one.[^ch48-post-election-lawsuits][^ch48-check-debate-works]

The pressure campaign extended beyond the courts. The president called state officials and asked them to “find” votes. His allies prepared alternate slates of electors and pressed Congress to reject the certified results. The House select committee’s report documents these efforts and the people who resisted them. The chapter uses the report as retrospective evidence about 2020–21, not as a contemporaneous scene, and reads it beside the court records and state certifications.[^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-post-election-lawsuits]

### 6.5 January 6 and the second impeachment

On January 6, 2021, a mob supporting the president breached the Capitol as Congress met to count the electoral votes. The violence injured officers and others, led to deaths in the immediate aftermath, and delayed but did not prevent the certification of Biden’s victory early on January 7. The House select committee’s report, released in December 2022, reconstructed the attack, the planning that preceded it, and the president’s actions during it. It is retrospective evidence, not a 2017–21 scene.[^ch48-j6-report-2022]

More than 1,500 people were charged. The prosecutions produced convictions and guilty pleas, including seditious-conspiracy convictions for leaders of the Oath Keepers and the Proud Boys. Their legal status changed after 2021, and the chapter notes that later history without importing it into January 6. The prosecutions establish the scale of the attack and the legal response, not a complete account of what happened.[^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-check-sources]

The House adopted H. Res. 24 on January 13, 2021, charging incitement of insurrection, by a vote of 232–197. The Senate acquitted Trump on February 13, 2021, by a vote of 57–43, short of the two-thirds needed, with seven Republicans voting to convict. The second impeachment was the first tried after the president had left office, and the constitutional question of whether the Senate could try a former president was itself contested. The Senate decided that it could, and then declined to convict.[^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2021]

The second acquittal closed the constitutional process and did not close the historical one. The attack, the attempt to overturn the election, and the refusal to accept the result became the terms on which the next administration took office. Chapter 49 takes up those terms; this chapter ends with the certification, the acquittal, and the unsettled aftermath.[^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2021][^ch48-check-ch49]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

The elections are documented. Trump won the Electoral College in 2016, 304–227, while losing the popular vote by 65,853,514 to 62,984,828. Biden won in 2020, 306–232, with about 81.3 million votes to about 74.2 million. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act became Public Law 115-97 on December 22, 2017; the first travel order was signed on January 27, 2017; and the Supreme Court upheld the third version in *Trump v. Hawaii* on June 26, 2018.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-eo-13769-2017][^ch48-trump-v-hawaii-2018]

The investigations and impeachments are documented. The special counsel reported that Russia interfered “in sweeping and systematic fashion” and that the investigation “did not establish” campaign coordination. The House impeached Trump on December 18, 2019, and again on January 13, 2021; the Senate acquitted him on February 5, 2020, and February 13, 2021. The zero-tolerance policy was announced on April 6, 2018, and HHS identified 2,737 separated children as of December 2018 while stating that the number was not the full scope.[^ch48-mueller-report-2019][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2020][^ch48-senate-acquittal-2021][^ch48-doj-zero-tolerance-2018][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]

The pandemic and January 6 are documented. The World Health Organization characterized COVID-19 as a pandemic on March 11, 2020, and the CDC estimated 545,600 to 660,200 excess deaths through February 27, 2021. On January 6, 2021, a mob breached the Capitol and delayed but did not stop the certification of Biden’s victory.[^ch48-cdc-timeline-2020][^ch48-cdc-excess-deaths-2021][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

### 7.2 What is open

Why did Trump win? One account emphasizes economic dislocation and the trade shock; another status threat and cultural backlash; a third place-based resentment and party decline. Each has evidence and limits. The question is which explains more of the result, for which voters, and at what level of analysis.[^ch48-autor-2020][^ch48-mutz-2018][^ch48-cramer-2016]

How much was a party failure and how much a party choice? The Republican leadership largely opposed Trump’s nomination, yet he won it. *The Party Decides* framework, which holds that party leaders shape nominations through endorsements and coordination, would have predicted a different nominee; its critics argue that it understated media, celebrity, and popular mobilization. The 2016 case tests both positions.[^ch48-rosenfeld-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]

Did the media create the misinformation or amplify it? Benkler, Faris, and Roberts argue that a distinct right-wing media ecosystem, rather than a symmetric collapse, produced the period’s asymmetric misinformation. Market and platform explanations stress attention incentives and social-media architecture. The accounts are not mutually exclusive, and the chapter asks what each explains about the claims that spread.[^ch48-benkler-2018][^ch48-check-debate-works]

Was this an episode of democratic backsliding? Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argue that democracies die through the slow erosion of norms; others stress the resilience of courts, elections, and the bureaucracy in 2020–21. Courts rejected the election challenges, state officials certified the results, and Vice President Mike Pence refused to overturn them. A sitting president also encouraged a mob to stop the certification, so resilience and vulnerability are both part of the answer.[^ch48-levitsky-ziblatt-2018][^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-check-debate-works]

Was Trump a populist, and was the American case comparable? Jan-Werner Müller defines populism as a thin-centered opposition of a pure people to corrupt elites; Kazin locates the American version in a long persuasion. The chapter tests the definitions against the record and other cases. The American case shares features with European and Asian populisms and has features specific to the United States: its parties, its media, its racial history, and its Constitution. Comparison is useful only if it preserves those differences.[^ch48-muller-2016][^ch48-kazin-2017][^ch48-check-debate-works]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which a campaign organized around grievance won the presidency while losing the popular vote, and in which the administration used executive power to cut taxes, appoint judges, restrict immigration, and contest investigations, before a pandemic, a contested election, and an attack on the Capitol tested the institutions that had produced the result. The campaign did not cause all of that. Its language and coalition made a certain kind of governing possible, and the governing reshaped the coalition. That coalition formed within a polarization that Kevin M. Kruse and Julian E. Zelizer trace to the 1970s.[^ch48-kruse-zelizer-2019][^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-hres-755-2019][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

The contingencies were specific. The nomination, the general election, the tax law, the travel ban, the two impeachments, and the 2020 result were all contingent. The Republican field could have consolidated around a single rival; the Senate could have convicted on either impeachment; the 2020 election could have been closer or wider; the pandemic could have arrived earlier or later. Each requires evidence rather than hindsight.[^ch48-check-debate-works][^ch48-check-scope]

The synthesis is therefore not a verdict on populism. It is a history of how a campaign organized around grievance exercised and contested presidential power. Between 2015 and 2021 the United States elected a candidate who had never held office, cut taxes and remade the federal judiciary, sharply restricted immigration, impeached and acquitted its president twice, suffered a pandemic that killed hundreds of thousands, and watched a mob attempt to stop the transfer of power. The question is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch48-fec-2016][^ch48-hres-24-2021][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

## Legacies

The tax law and the judicial appointments outlasted the administration that made them. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act cut the corporate rate to 21 percent and set many individual provisions to expire after 2025; the three Supreme Court appointments gave the Court a conservative majority. The appointments were the clearest example of a legacy that no later election could easily undo, because federal judges serve for life. The Court they helped shape later decided *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization*.[^ch48-tcja-2017][^ch48-supreme-court-appointments][^ch48-dobbs-2022]

The immigration policies and the investigations left precedents that later administrations would defend, unwind, or extend. The travel ban’s framework and the family-separation record remained contested, the inspector general’s findings on Dorian remained a case study in political pressure on a scientific agency, and the January 6 prosecutions continued for years. The immigration policies were made through executive action, and their durability depended on the courts and the next administration. That is the characteristic legacy of the period: real, contestable, and reversible.[^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019][^ch48-noaa-oig-2020][^ch48-j6-report-2022]

The contest over the 2020 election became the ground on which the next years were fought. The refusal to accept the result, the attack on the Capitol, and the second acquittal set terms that Chapter 49 takes up as it examines the Biden years and the elections that followed. The chapter does not claim that the contest ended in 2021. It claims that 2021 fixed the terms of the contest that continued.[^ch48-fec-2020][^ch48-j6-report-2022][^ch48-check-ch49]

## Optional threads

The transnational frame. The turn was made in a global context of populist and nationalist movements, from Brexit to elections across Europe and Asia. The American case shared a language of the people against elites, a hostility to immigration, and a suspicion of international institutions. It also had features specific to the United States: its two-party system, federal structure, racial history, and media. The chapter compares rather than treating the case as unique or a mere copy.[^ch48-muller-2016][^ch48-norris-inglehart-2019]

Archives and silences. The record over-represents presidents, candidates, officials, and journalists and under-represents migrants, detained and separated families, the sick and the dead, low-wage workers, and the imprisoned. The chapter names these asymmetries rather than speaking for the silenced. The silences are part of how the period was governed and how it will be remembered.[^ch48-check-silences][^ch48-hhs-oig-separated-2019]

Distant connections. Chapter 47 owns the 2008 election and the Obama-era recovery and polarization; Chapter 49 owns the Biden years and the 2024 election. The chapter cross-references these rather than re-narrating them. The recovery set the conditions for the 2016 campaign, and the 2020 contest set the conditions for the Biden years.[^ch48-check-ch47][^ch48-check-ch49]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 2015 and 2021 the United States nominated and elected a candidate who had never held office, cut taxes and remade the federal judiciary, sharply restricted immigration, impeached and acquitted its president twice, suffered a pandemic that killed hundreds of thousands, and watched a mob attempt to stop the transfer of power.
