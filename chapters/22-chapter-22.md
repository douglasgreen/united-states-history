## Epigraphs

— Abraham Lincoln, “Second Inaugural Address,” Washington, D.C., March 4, 1865.[^ch22-lincoln-second-inaugural]

> “I struck for my country and that alone. A country groaned beneath this tyranny and prayed for this end, and yet now behold the cold hand they extend to me.”[^ch22-nps-booth-diary]

— John Wilkes Booth, diary entry, April 21, 1865.[^ch22-nps-booth-diary]

- **Why these three.** A president urged charity toward the defeated and located the war’s cause in slavery. His assassin insisted that he had struck a blow for a country that demanded it. A historian argued that the war’s deepest meaning was claimed not by its generals but by formerly enslaved people who buried the Union dead and marched in their own cause. The three disagree about whether victory restored the old Union, whether it destroyed a tyranny, or whether it opened a revolution that the victors had not intended.[^ch22-check-epigraphs]

- **The historian’s voice.** Blight supplies the required historian’s voice and frames the chapter’s central disagreement: whether the settlement of 1865 was a restoration of the Union, a revolution in citizenship, or an emancipationist promise that the nation soon suppressed.[^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-check-sources]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and the date.** In the first week of May 1864, the Army of the Potomac crossed the Rapidan River in northern Virginia and met Robert E. Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia in the dense second-growth woods called the Wilderness. Grant crossed on May 4, and Lee attacked the next day along the Orange Turnpike.[^ch22-nps-cold-harbor][^ch22-nps-wilderness]
- **The terrain and the fight.** Fighting on May 5 centered on open ground such as Saunders Field and on the two roads, the Orange Turnpike and the Orange Plank Road, around which the battle turned. The woods were so thick that units lost contact, and the fighting set the undergrowth on fire; the National Park Service records that soldiers from both sides perished in the flames.[^ch22-nps-wilderness][^ch22-nps-cold-harbor]
- **The wounding and the draw.** On the morning of May 6, James Longstreet’s corps helped push back a Union assault, then Longstreet was wounded by his own men’s fire. The battle ended without a clear winner; the American Battlefield Trust calls it a tactical draw and gives about 29,800 total casualties, roughly 17,000 Union and 13,000 Confederate.[^ch22-nps-wilderness][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **The decision that mattered.** In earlier campaigns the Union army had retreated north after defeat. This time Grant ordered the army south, toward Spotsylvania Court House and Richmond. The troops cheered, the National Park Service records, because they understood that they now had a commander who would not turn back.[^ch22-nps-cold-harbor][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **What the scene can show.** The scene can show the woods, the roads, the fires, the casualties, and Grant’s decision to keep moving. It cannot show what Grant or Lee thought in the moment, and it must not stage dialogue the record does not support.[^ch22-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A war of attrition begins, argued from the record.** The scene’s claim is not that Grant was careless with his soldiers’ lives, but that the Union had adopted a strategy of continuous pressure that traded casualties for the destruction of Lee’s army and the Confederacy’s capacity to fight. The human cost of that strategy is the chapter’s first subject.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-nps-cold-harbor]
- **The stakes.** The Overland Campaign put the war’s outcome and the 1864 election on the same calendar. Union victories or the lack of them would decide whether Lincoln or a peace candidate would shape the war’s ending.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: what did victory settle about freedom?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the Wilderness to Atlanta, the ballot box, Appomattox, and the refugee camps of the sea islands.

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The dates, the roads, the fires, Longstreet’s wounding, and Grant’s southward move are documented. The smoke, the noise, and the soldiers’ private thoughts must not be staged beyond what witnesses recorded.[^ch22-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The soldiers and generals of May 1864 did not know that the war would end at Appomattox within a year. The scene must preserve the uncertainty of the campaign.[^ch22-check-scene][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **No exact toll in the scene.** The casualty figures are estimates that vary by source. If the scene uses a number, it must be attributed and reconciled with Appendix B, Table 4.[^ch22-check-numbers][^ch22-battlefields-overland]

### Drafting target

Three hundred to four hundred words. Open on the Rapidan crossing, describe the terrain and the collision on the turnpike, let the woods catch fire, note Longstreet’s wounding, and close on Grant’s decision to march south and the cheer that answered it. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows and supplies the years, scope, and limits.[^ch22-nps-wilderness][^ch22-nps-cold-harbor][^ch22-check-scene]

## 1. Grant and Lee

### 1.1 The Overland Campaign

- **The claim.** Grant’s Overland Campaign kept constant pressure on Lee’s army and made the war a war of attrition, at a cost that reshaped northern politics and the meaning of Union victory.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-nps-cold-harbor]
- **The opening.** In the Wilderness on May 5–7, 1864, the two armies fought to a tactical draw in tangled, burning woods. Longstreet, who had turned the battle on May 6, fell wounded by friendly fire, and the battle ended with no clear result.[^ch22-nps-wilderness][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **The pivot south.** Instead of retreating as earlier Union commanders had, Grant moved the army south toward Spotsylvania Court House. For the next two weeks the armies fought over entrenched lines, including the savage hand-to-hand combat at the “Bloody Angle” on May 12.[^ch22-nps-cold-harbor][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **Cold Harbor.** After flanking movements at the North Anna and Totopotomoy Creek, Grant attacked fortified Confederate lines at Cold Harbor on June 3. The assault failed piecemeal; the American Battlefield Trust estimates about 6,000 Federal casualties, most in less than an hour. Grant later regretted the attack.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-nps-cold-harbor]
- **The consequence.** The campaign cost, in the American Battlefield Trust’s estimate, about 55,000 Union and 33,000 Confederate casualties. It did not destroy Lee’s army, but it pinned it and set up the move to Petersburg.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-check-numbers]

### 1.2 Petersburg and the siege

- **The claim.** By midsummer 1864 the war in Virginia had settled into a siege of Petersburg and Richmond that would last until April 1865, a war of trenches and railroads that most histories of the war compress.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed]
- **The move.** After Cold Harbor, Grant disengaged and moved the Army of the Potomac across the James River toward Petersburg, the railroad junction that supplied Richmond. The failure to take Petersburg by assault in June turned the campaign into a nine-month siege.[^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **Why it mattered.** Holding Petersburg and Richmond pinned Lee’s army and made the 1864 election a referendum on whether the North would keep fighting. The siege is the military background to §3.[^ch22-battlefields-election][^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed]
- **The limits of the claim.** The chapter should not treat the siege as a single event or claim a precise date for its “beginning”; it should present the spring and summer of 1864 as a continuous campaign.[^ch22-check-dates][^ch22-check-scope]

### 1.3 The measure: the cost of the campaign

- **The claim.** The measure of the Overland Campaign is not only ground gained but the human cost and its political consequences.[^ch22-battlefields-overland][^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give the Wilderness casualties (about 29,800 total), the June 3 losses at Cold Harbor (about 6,000 Federal), and the campaign totals (about 55,000 Union and 33,000 Confederate), each with its source and its uncertainty, cross-checked against Appendix B, Table 4.[^ch22-check-numbers][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **The map.** **Map 18 plan.** The registered visual `ch22-map-01` (Map 18, “The final campaigns, 1864–1865”) belongs here and in §2. It should show the Virginia theater from the Rapidan to Petersburg, the Atlanta campaign and the march to the sea, and the interlocking fronts and supply routes, without implying that geography determined the outcome. It needs a caption, source line, credit, alt text, and accessible description, and it must reconcile with the register and Appendix C.[^ch22-check-map][^ch22-nps-wilderness][^ch22-ghs-march]

## 2. Sherman and the interior

### 2.1 The Atlanta campaign

- **The claim.** While Grant pinned Lee in Virginia, William Tecumseh Sherman advanced into Georgia to destroy Joseph E. Johnston’s army and the Confederacy’s interior supply base.[^ch22-nps-atlanta][^ch22-ghs-march]
- **The campaign.** Sherman’s forces began at Rocky Face Ridge on May 8–9, 1864, and fought a series of engagements through Resaca, New Hope Church, Pickett’s Mill, Kennesaw Mountain on June 27, and Atlanta on July 22, reaching Jonesboro on August 31–September 1.[^ch22-nps-atlanta]
- **The fall of Atlanta.** The city fell in early September 1864. Coming on the heels of Mobile Bay, the victory and the near-immobility of Lee’s army at Petersburg strengthened the Union’s political position before the election.[^ch22-battlefields-election][^ch22-nps-atlanta]
- **The consequence.** Atlanta’s capture was both a military and a political event. It converted a season of bloody stalemate into an argument for continuing the war to victory.[^ch22-battlefields-election]

### 2.2 The March to the Sea and the Carolinas

- **The claim.** Sherman’s march from Atlanta to Savannah and then into the Carolinas destroyed war-making capacity and, by freeing and employing thousands of enslaved people, turned the army’s advance into an instrument of emancipation.[^ch22-ghs-march][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- **The march.** On November 15, 1864, Sherman’s roughly 62,500 men left Atlanta and marched more than 250 miles to Savannah, reaching it in mid-December. Abandoning their supply base, they lived off the land and destroyed food and war-related property they could not consume.[^ch22-ghs-march]
- **The correction of a myth.** The Georgia Historical Society’s marker states that, contrary to popular memory, Sherman’s troops primarily destroyed property used for waging war — railroads, depots, factories, cotton gins, and warehouses — and that the campaign also liberated thousands of enslaved people. The chapter should present the destruction and the liberation together rather than repeating the myth of total destruction.[^ch22-ghs-march]
- **The Carolinas.** After Savannah, Sherman turned north. The subsequent campaign into South Carolina and North Carolina set up the largest of the secondary surrenders and the end of the war in the interior.[^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed][^ch22-ghs-march]
- **The consequence.** Sherman’s marches made the war unendurable in the Confederate interior and brought the question of the freedpeople directly under Union control, which leads to §5.[^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order][^ch22-check-race]

### 2.3 The cost and the freedpeople

- **The claim.** The interior campaigns spread destruction and displacement across the South and left thousands of freed people in the army’s path, a circumstance that forced the Union to improvise a refugee policy.[^ch22-ghs-march][^ch22-nps-port-royal]
- **The human cost.** The chapter should connect the marches to the refugee crisis: enslaved people who followed the army, the camps that grew around Union positions, and the pressure on commanders to decide where the freedpeople would live and how they would work.[^ch22-nps-port-royal][^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order]
- **The silence to name.** The record of the marches is fullest for the army’s actions and thinnest for the experiences of the enslaved people who met it. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch22-check-race][^ch22-check-sources]

## 3. The 1864 election

### 3.1 The parties and the candidates

- **The claim.** The election of 1864 was a referendum on continuing the war and on the constitutional amendment to abolish slavery, and it redefined the Republican coalition as a wartime National Union Party.[^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The parties.** Republicans and pro-war Democrats joined as the National Union Party and renominated Lincoln, replacing Hannibal Hamlin with Andrew Johnson of Tennessee to project national unity. Radical Republicans briefly nominated John C. Frémont, who withdrew and endorsed Lincoln. The Democrats nominated General George B. McClellan.[^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The platforms.** The National Union platform called for continuing the war until the Confederacy surrendered and for a constitutional amendment to abolish slavery. The Democratic platform called for peace, and McClellan, though he supported a Union victory, ran on a party whose platform he did not fully accept.[^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The consequence.** The election made the abolition of slavery a partisan and eventually constitutional commitment, connecting the campaign to the Thirteenth Amendment in §5.[^ch22-archives-13th][^ch22-battlefields-election]

### 3.2 The soldier vote

- **The claim.** For the first time in the nation’s history, absentee voting allowed soldiers in the field to vote, and their ballots strengthened Lincoln’s victory.[^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The innovation.** Voting qualifications remained with the states, but northern states arranged absentee ballots or furloughs so that soldiers could vote. The votes were open, not secret, and were cast in camp and at home.[^ch22-battlefields-election]
- **The result.** Of the 40,247 Union soldiers counted, 30,503, or 75.8 percent, voted for Lincoln. The remainder must be reconciled before Table 1 is published.[^ch22-battlefields-election][^ch22-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** McClellan had been a popular general, and the soldier vote was not unanimous. The chapter should not present the army as a single political voice.[^ch22-battlefields-election][^ch22-check-numbers]

### 3.3 The measure: the election of 1864

- **The claim.** The election’s outcome gave Lincoln a mandate to finish the war and to pursue the abolition of slavery, while revealing how close the political contest had been in some states.[^ch22-battlefields-election][^ch22-app-1864]
- **The measure.** Lincoln won 212 electoral votes to McClellan’s 21 and about 55.1 percent of the national popular vote, 2,220,846 to 1,809,445. The popular-vote totals vary slightly among compilations and the shares are rounded; the table must name its source and cross-check Appendix B, Table 2.[^ch22-app-1864][^ch22-check-numbers]

## 4. Surrenders, assassination, and mortality

### 4.1 Richmond, Appomattox, and the other surrenders

- **The claim.** The spring of 1865 brought the fall of Richmond, Lee’s surrender at Appomattox, and a series of further surrenders that ended the fighting over months, not in a single moment.[^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed][^ch22-nps-surrender-meeting]
- **The collapse.** Lee’s final campaign began on March 25, 1865, with the attack on Fort Stedman near Petersburg. Grant counterattacked on April 1 at Five Forks, forcing the abandonment of Richmond and Petersburg on April 2. The Confederate government, including Jefferson Davis, fled.[^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed]
- **Appomattox.** Lee’s army, outnumbered and short of supplies, was trapped near Appomattox Court House. On April 9, 1865, Grant and Lee met in Wilmer McLean’s parlor. Grant’s terms required the army to stack its arms and surrender its flags and public property, allowed officers to keep their side arms and private horses, and paroled officers and men to return home. Grant offered 25,000 rations to the hungry Confederates.[^ch22-nps-surrender-meeting][^ch22-archives-surrender]
- **The turn.** Nobody recorded a word of triumph in the room that day, as far as the evidence shows. The terms were generous by the standards of war, and they made the agreement a model for the surrenders that followed.[^ch22-archives-surrender][^ch22-nps-surrender-meeting]
- **The other surrenders.** Lee surrendered only his army. Other Confederate forces surrendered over the following weeks and months, and President Andrew Johnson did not formally declare the war over until August 20, 1866, sixteen months after Appomattox. The chapter must keep Appomattox and the end of the war distinct.[^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed][^ch22-check-dates]

### 4.2 The assassination

- **The claim.** Lincoln’s assassination on April 14, 1865, five days after Appomattox, turned victory into mourning and made the settlement of freedom a contest over a dead president’s intentions.[^ch22-fords-assassination][^ch22-nps-petersen]
- **The shooting.** On the morning of April 14, the actor John Wilkes Booth learned that Lincoln would attend a performance of the comedy *Our American Cousin* at Ford’s Theatre. That evening Booth shot the president in the head in the presidential box, where the Lincolns sat with Major Henry Rathbone and Clara Harris.[^ch22-fords-assassination]
- **The death.** Lincoln was carried across the street to the Petersen House, where he clung to life for nine hours and died at 7:22 a.m. on April 15, 1865, at age 56. Secretary of War Edwin Stanton, who had set up a temporary office in the house, is traditionally said to have spoken the words “now he belongs to the ages.”[^ch22-nps-petersen]
- **Booth’s purpose.** Booth, on the run for twelve days, wrote in his diary that he had “struck for my country and that alone” and compared himself to Brutus and William Tell. He was killed at the Garrett farm on April 26, 1865; the diary and other evidence from the conspiracy trials are now in Ford’s Theatre’s collection.[^ch22-nps-booth-diary]
- **The consequence.** The assassination removed the president who had framed the war’s meaning in the Second Inaugural and left the terms of Reconstruction to Andrew Johnson, a hand-off whose consequences belong to Chapter 23.[^ch22-lincoln-second-inaugural][^ch22-check-ch23][^ch22-check-race]

### 4.3 The meaning of the death toll

- **The claim.** The scale of the war’s dead shaped how Americans understood the conflict’s meaning, and the number itself is a contested estimate, not a settled count.[^ch22-pnas-mortality][^ch22-faust-republic-suffering]
- **The problem of counting.** For over a century the de facto figure was 618,222, derived by applying Union Army mortality rates to a rough count of Confederate soldiers and widely seen as an undercount because Confederate records are incomplete.[^ch22-pnas-mortality]
- **The revision.** In 2011 J. David Hacker used a census sex-differential method on a 1 percent sample of the 1850–1880 censuses to estimate more than 750,000 deaths. In 2024 Barceló, Jensen, Peisakhin, and Zhai used full-count census records with a migration-adjusted method to estimate about 698,000 deaths, with bounds of roughly 647,000 to 749,000. The chapter should present these as competing methods with different scopes, not choose one as “the” number.[^ch22-pnas-mortality]
- **The unequal burden.** The 2024 study found that core Confederate states suffered an excess mortality rate of about 13 percent among military-age native-born white men, against fewer than 5 percent in the Union — a measure of how unequally the war fell.[^ch22-pnas-mortality]
- **The meaning.** Drew Gilpin Faust argues that the war’s sheer scale of death transformed American understandings of dying, mourning, and the nation’s obligations to its dead; the chapter should treat that as an interpretation to weigh, not a substitute for the counts.[^ch22-faust-republic-suffering]

## 5. Refugees, the Freedmen’s Bureau, and emancipation

### 5.1 The refugee crisis and the Sea Islands

- **The claim.** Even before the war ended, the Union faced a refugee crisis, and the Sea Islands became an early laboratory for the transition from enslavement to free labor.[^ch22-nps-port-royal][^ch22-archives-freedmens-bureau]
- **The Sea Islands.** After Union forces occupied the South Carolina Sea Islands in 1861, most planters fled, leaving behind roughly 10,000 enslaved people. Port Royal and the nearby islands became the site of the Port Royal Experiment, in which the Treasury Department managed abandoned plantations and paid wages.[^ch22-nps-port-royal]
- **The labor reforms.** In February 1862 the government agent Edward Pierce reported that nearly 200 plantations had been abandoned and urged that freedpeople be kept on the land and paid wages. Wages were small, often about $1 per bale of cotton, but the shift from slave labor to wage labor was a large-scale experiment in free labor.[^ch22-nps-port-royal]
- **The freedpeople’s aim.** The chapter should emphasize that freedpeople used their wages to try to secure the land itself, anticipating the land question in §5.2.[^ch22-nps-port-royal][^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order]

### 5.2 Field Order 15 and the land question

- **The claim.** Sherman’s Field Order 15 set aside coastal land for freedpeople in forty-acre plots, making land the central practical question of freedom, and the order was a wartime expedient, not a permanent settlement.[^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order][^ch22-freedmen-umd-sfo15]
- **The meeting.** On January 12, 1865, Sherman and Secretary of War Edwin Stanton met in Savannah with twenty Black leaders, mostly Baptist and Methodist ministers, to discuss emancipation. Four days later, with Lincoln’s approval, Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15.[^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order]
- **The order.** The order reserved the Sea Islands and a coastal strip from Charleston to the St. John’s River for the settlement of freedpeople, directed that each family receive up to forty acres of tillable ground, excluded white residents except military personnel, and encouraged freedmen to enlist in the army.[^ch22-freedmen-umd-sfo15]
- **The scope.** The order covered roughly 400,000 acres, and its language treated the freedpeople’s title as possessory and subject to Congress. It rested on Sherman’s military judgment about how to provide for the thousands of refugees following his army.[^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order][^ch22-freedmen-umd-sfo15]
- **The limits.** The chapter must state that “forty acres and a mule” was never a general federal policy; it was a limited wartime order whose land would later be restored, and the phrase’s later life is a memory as much as a policy.[^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order][^ch22-check-race]

### 5.3 The Freedmen’s Bureau and the Thirteenth Amendment

- **The claim.** In March 1865 Congress created the Freedmen’s Bureau, and in 1865 the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery nationally; together they made freedom a federal responsibility and a constitutional fact, with contested meaning on the ground.[^ch22-senate-freedmens-bureau][^ch22-archives-13th]
- **The Bureau.** On March 3, 1865, Congress passed “An Act to establish a Bureau for the Relief of Freedmen and Refugees,” to provide food, shelter, clothing, medical services, and land, and to supervise contracts and schools. It operated during the war and for one year thereafter, which made its renewal a point of conflict with President Johnson.[^ch22-senate-freedmens-bureau][^ch22-archives-freedmens-bureau]
- **The Bureau’s work.** The Bureau, established in the War Department as Record Group 105, issued rations and clothing, ran hospitals and refugee camps, supervised labor contracts, and helped establish schools; its records now document hundreds of thousands of freedpeople and refugees. Andrew Johnson appointed Oliver Otis Howard commissioner in May 1865.[^ch22-archives-freedmens-bureau]
- **The amendment.** The Thirteenth Amendment passed the Senate in April 1864 and the House on January 31, 1865, by a vote of 119–56; Lincoln approved the joint resolution on February 1, 1865. Georgia’s ratification on December 6, 1865, completed the necessary number of states, and the amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States.[^ch22-archives-13th]
- **The separation of dates.** The chapter must clearly separate the amendment’s congressional passage in January 1865 from the spring military ending and from ratification in December 1865, and it must not fold ratification into the story of Appomattox.[^ch22-check-13th][^ch22-archives-13th]
- **The limits.** The amendment’s enforcement clause and its exception for punishment for crime left future conflicts open; the chapter should note that abolition settled slavery’s legal status without settling the terms of freedom, which belongs to Chapter 23.[^ch22-archives-13th][^ch22-check-ch23]

### 5.4 In Focus: Charleston’s May 1865 commemoration

- **The claim.** A single documented account of a Charleston commemoration on May 1, 1865, shows freedpeople claiming the war’s meaning for themselves, while cautioning the chapter against declaring a uniquely settled “first Memorial Day.”[^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-nyhistory-blight]
- **In Focus: the account.** **In Focus.** After the siege of Charleston, the Twenty-First United States Colored Infantry was among the first troops to enter the ruined city, and its commander accepted the formal surrender. During the war the Confederates had converted the Washington Race Course into an outdoor prison for Union soldiers, at least 257 of whom died and were buried in a mass grave behind the grandstand. On May 1, 1865, some twenty-eight Black workmen reburied the Union dead, built a fence, and inscribed over the entrance “Martyrs of the Race Course.” Then, the historian David W. Blight writes, Black Charlestonians, with white missionaries and teachers, staged a parade of about 10,000 people, led by 3,000 Black schoolchildren carrying flowers and singing “John Brown’s Body,” followed by women with wreaths and by Black men, Union infantry, and Black and white citizens. A *New York Tribune* correspondent described “a procession of friends and mourners as South Carolina and the United States never saw before.”[^ch22-blight-decoration-day]
- **How to read it.** The box should read the event as an emancipationist claim on the war’s memory: the freedpeople buried Union dead on the ground of the planter aristocracy’s race course and announced, in Blight’s phrase, “the triumph of their emancipation over a slaveholders’ republic.” The chapter should present the event as the historian reconstructed it, from a newspaper account and a later reminiscence.[^ch22-blight-decoration-day]
- **The caution.** The chapter must not declare a uniquely settled “first Memorial Day.” The New-York Historical Society’s summary says Blight “believes” the event to have been the earliest Memorial Day, and Blight himself has acknowledged that he has no evidence that the Charleston event led to the national holiday, which John A. Logan’s 1868 order established. Other towns, north and south, have their own claims, most from 1866.[^ch22-nyhistory-blight][^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-check-focus]
- **The evidence and its limits.** The event survives in a *New York Tribune* description quoted by Blight and in a reminiscence written long after the fact; no photograph or program of the ceremony is documented in the consulted account. The chapter should cite the account carefully and avoid inventing the ceremony’s details beyond what it records.[^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-check-focus]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The Confederacy was defeated militarily.** Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia on April 9, 1865, and the remaining Confederate forces surrendered over the following months; President Johnson formally declared the war over on August 20, 1866.[^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed][^ch22-archives-surrender]
- **Slavery was abolished constitutionally.** The Thirteenth Amendment passed Congress on January 31, 1865, and was ratified on December 6, 1865, ending slavery throughout the United States.[^ch22-archives-13th]
- **The war’s cost was enormous.** The dead numbered in the hundreds of thousands, and the best current estimates place the toll between roughly 650,000 and 850,000, far above the conventional 618,000.[^ch22-pnas-mortality]
- **Freedpeople acted to define freedom.** From the Sea Islands to Sherman’s line of march, freedpeople worked for wages, sought land, and claimed the war’s meaning in their own commemorations.[^ch22-nps-port-royal][^ch22-blight-decoration-day]
- **Land was contested.** Field Order 15 and the Port Royal experiment show that the practical terms of freedom — land, labor, and protection — were not settled by military victory or by the amendment.[^ch22-freedmen-umd-sfo15][^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the outcome a revolution, state-building, or a restoration?** Historians disagree whether the war transformed the American nation, built a new central state, or restored the old Union while containing its promises.[^ch22-foner-second-founding][^ch22-bensel-yankee-leviathan]
- **How many died?** The estimates differ by method, sample, and scope, and no single number is settled; the chapter should present the range and the methods.[^ch22-pnas-mortality][^ch22-check-mortality]
- **How should the war’s meaning be measured?** Drawing on Faust, one answer locates the war’s meaning in death and mourning; another locates it in emancipation and citizenship. The chapter should hold the two together rather than choose.[^ch22-faust-republic-suffering][^ch22-blight-decoration-day]
- **What did emancipation settle?** Abolition settled slavery’s legal status but not land, labor, protection, or the vote; the chapter should mark where the settlement of 1865 gave way to the struggles of Reconstruction.[^ch22-archives-13th][^ch22-check-ch23]
- **Whose memory prevailed?** The Charleston commemoration and the later Lost Cause commemorations competed over what the war had been about, and the chapter must not treat one as the inevitable national memory.[^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-nyhistory-blight]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which Union victory destroyed the Confederacy and slavery but did not settle freedom’s practical terms, and in which the war’s meaning was contested at the moment of victory as much as afterward.[^ch22-foner-second-founding][^ch22-blight-decoration-day]
- **The second-revolution position.** McPherson argues that the war remade the nation’s constitutional and social order, with emancipation at its center, and Foner describes the Reconstruction amendments as a “second founding” and, in Carl Schurz’s phrase, a “constitutional revolution.”[^ch22-mcpherson-second-revolution][^ch22-foner-second-founding]
- **The state-building position.** Bensel argues that the war and Reconstruction built new central state authority in national finance, military organization, and administration, a “Yankee Leviathan” whose consolidation was incomplete.[^ch22-bensel-yankee-leviathan]
- **The restoration position.** The older Dunning School treated Reconstruction as an error and its end as the South’s rescue by “Redeemers,” a view built on Lost Cause ideology that later scholarship overturned; a related modern emphasis stresses how quickly the old racial order was reconstructed after 1865. This position must be stated at its strongest and checked against the works.[^ch22-foner-second-founding][^ch22-blight-decoration-day]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Victory was real and emancipation was constitutional, but the terms of freedom were left to be fought over. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not teams.[^ch22-foner-second-founding][^ch22-check-tables]

## Legacies

The Civil War ended with the Union restored and slavery abolished, but it did not settle the meaning of freedom. The final campaigns exhausted the Confederacy, Appomattox paroled its most famous army, and the Thirteenth Amendment made emancipation a constitutional fact; yet the freedpeople who buried the Union dead in Charleston and the families who would seek land in the sea islands understood that legal freedom was not the same as a livelihood, a vote, or protection from violence.[^ch22-archives-13th][^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-freedmen-umd-sfo15]

The cost of the victory was borne unequally. Death fell most heavily on the Confederate states, and the freedpeople who had been the war’s central subject entered freedom with the least. The Thirteenth Amendment’s exception for punishment for crime and its enforcement clause pointed ahead to convict labor and to fights over federal power that would last for a century.[^ch22-pnas-mortality][^ch22-archives-13th]

The chapter’s lasting legacy is a set of questions that outlived the guns: who counts as a citizen, what freedom requires beyond its legal declaration, and whether a nation that had destroyed slavery could protect the people it had freed. Those questions passed from the battlefield to law and labor, and they are the subject of Chapter 23.[^ch22-archives-13th][^ch22-check-ch23]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The war’s final year was watched abroad, and the abolition of slavery in the United States had consequences for antislavery politics in Britain and its empire. The chapter should place the Thirteenth Amendment beside the Atlantic world’s ongoing debates over emancipation without claiming that American events caused foreign reforms.[^ch22-archives-13th][^ch22-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** The surrender documents, the election returns, and the assassin’s diary survive in abundance; the interior lives of the newly freed survive mainly in Bureau records and in freedpeople’s own institutions. The chapter should name that uneven survival without speaking for the silenced.[^ch22-archives-freedmens-bureau][^ch22-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** The Emancipation Proclamation and the Gettysburg Address belong to Chapter 21; the Black Codes and the substantive struggle over Reconstruction belong to Chapter 23; the campaigns named here should not be re-narrated as their own chapters.[^ch22-check-ch21][^ch22-check-ch23]

## Summary

- The Overland Campaign opened in the Wilderness on May 5, 1864, and became a war of attrition; Grant moved south instead of retreating, and the campaign cost tens of thousands of casualties.[^ch22-nps-wilderness][^ch22-battlefields-overland]
- Sherman’s Atlanta campaign and his march from Atlanta to Savannah in late 1864 destroyed war-making capacity in the Confederate interior and liberated thousands of enslaved people.[^ch22-nps-atlanta][^ch22-ghs-march]
- In the election of 1864, Lincoln won 212 electoral votes and about 55 percent of the popular vote, and most of the 40,247 Union soldiers counted voted for him.[^ch22-app-1864][^ch22-battlefields-election]
- Richmond fell on April 2, 1865; Lee surrendered at Appomattox on April 9; and the remaining Confederate forces surrendered over the following months, with the war formally declared over on August 20, 1866.[^ch22-archives-ending-bloodshed][^ch22-nps-surrender-meeting]
- John Wilkes Booth assassinated Lincoln on April 14, 1865, and Lincoln died the next morning; the assassination removed the president who had framed the war’s meaning.[^ch22-fords-assassination][^ch22-nps-petersen]
- The war’s dead numbered in the hundreds of thousands; current estimates range from roughly 650,000 to 850,000 and rest on different census methods.[^ch22-pnas-mortality]
- Congress created the Freedmen’s Bureau on March 3, 1865, and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, passing Congress on January 31, 1865, and being ratified on December 6, 1865.[^ch22-senate-freedmens-bureau][^ch22-archives-13th]
- Sherman’s Field Order 15 set aside coastal land for freedpeople in forty-acre plots, but it was a limited wartime measure, not a general policy of land distribution.[^ch22-freedmen-umd-sfo15][^ch22-georgiaencyclopedia-field-order]
- Freedpeople in Charleston buried Union dead and marched on May 1, 1865, claiming the war’s meaning as their emancipation; the chapter should not declare a uniquely settled “first Memorial Day.”[^ch22-blight-decoration-day][^ch22-nyhistory-blight]
- The debate turns on whether the outcome was a revolution, state-building, or a restoration, and the honest answer holds military victory, constitutional change, and contested freedom together.[^ch22-foner-second-founding][^ch22-bensel-yankee-leviathan]

> *A closing note.* The spring of 1865 gave the United States a victory it had not dared to expect in 1864 and a president it could not replace.
