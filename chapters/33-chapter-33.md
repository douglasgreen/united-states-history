## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On December 11, 1930, the Bank of the United States — a commercial bank despite its name — ceased operations. It was then the fourth-largest bank in New York City, and its roughly $200 million in deposits made it the largest bank failure in United States history to that date.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The run.** The bank had been negotiating a merger with another institution, and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York had helped search for a partner. When the negotiations broke down, depositors rushed to withdraw funds, and New York’s superintendent of banking closed the institution.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics]
- **The bank and its depositors.** A scholarly account reports that the bank had expanded to dozens of branches across the boroughs, that it served immigrant depositors in New York, and that it held more than $268 million in deposits. A rumor of insolvency, spread after a depositor was refused a share buy-back at a Bronx branch, brought depositors to the branches over the following days.[^ch33-gotham-bank]
- **The context of the fall.** The failure came at the end of the first banking crisis of the era, which had begun with the November 1930 collapse of Caldwell and Company and the Bank of Tennessee in Nashville and had spread through correspondent networks across the South and border states. Panic had begun to subside in early December before the New York failure revived it.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the institution, its size, the sequence of the merger talks and the run, the closure by the state banking superintendent, and the disputed question of whether the bank was liquidated by panic or was already insolvent. It cannot show what any individual depositor thought, what was said at the branch counters, or what the weather was on a specific morning in the Bronx, because no such account is cited here.[^ch33-check-scene][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-gotham-bank]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In December 1930 no one knew that the failure would become the emblematic beginning of a decade-long depression, how many more banks would fail, or that deposits would eventually be insured. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch33-check-scene][^ch33-check-dates]

### The scene’s argument

- **A local run becomes a national signal.** The scene’s claim is not that one bank’s failure caused the Depression but that a locally rooted run, in an immigrant bank whose name sounded official, became a signal that the banking system itself was unsafe. Contemporaneous coverage of runs at the bank’s Bronx and East New York branches carried the fear beyond the neighborhood.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-gotham-bank]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question. A run is a social act: it begins with what depositors believe about one another. The failure of a large bank could convert a recession into a panic, and a panic could convert a panic into a depression, but only through institutions — correspondent networks, the gold standard, the discount window — that determined whether fear became a chain reaction.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-scope]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did a financial breakdown become a prolonged social catastrophe?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the Bronx to Wall Street, to the farm counties of the Midwest and South, to the wheat fields of the Plains, and to the breadlines and shantytowns of the cities.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, room, or dialogue.** The date, the institution, its deposits, the merger talks, the run, and the closure are documented. The scene may not stage the weather, the bank’s interior, the queue, or any exchange the record does not contain.[^ch33-check-scene]
- **No interior thought.** The private reflections of depositors, officers, or regulators must not be staged beyond what documents state.[^ch33-check-scene]
- **No degrading language without explanation.** The scholarly account quotes a banker’s contempt for the bank as one that “catered to immigrants.” That phrase may appear only in quotation, only because it explains why other banks withheld help, and with an explanation in the same paragraph of why it is reproduced.[^ch33-gotham-bank][^ch33-check-race-bank]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the Depression’s length, the bank holiday, or the phrase “Great Depression,” which was not yet in use.[^ch33-check-scene][^ch33-check-dates]

## 1. The boom and crash

### 1.1 The claim: a crash that punctuated a contraction it did not cause

- **The claim.** The stock market crash of October 1929 was a symptom and an accelerant of a contraction that had already begun, not the sole cause of the Depression. The crash destroyed wealth and confidence, but the banking panics of 1930–33 did far more to turn a recession into a decade-long catastrophe.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-frh-great-depression]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present three connected stories: the uneven 1920s economy inherited from Chapter 32; the market boom and the crash; and the year of contraction after the crash that still looked like a normal recession. The three belong to one account rather than three separate ones.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-check-ch32]
- **What the record can show.** It can show index levels, margin rates, and production and employment statistics. It cannot show that the crash “caused” the Depression, and the chapter should treat causation as a question for §6, not a premise.[^ch33-check-numbers][^ch33-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 The uneven boom inherited from the 1920s

- **The claim.** The prosperity of the 1920s was real and unequal, and its fragilities — installment debt, a depressed farm sector, and a concentration of income — formed the setting in which the crash became dangerous.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-ch32]
- **The distribution of income.** By 1928 the top 1 percent of families received 23.9 percent of all pretax income while the bottom 90 percent received 50.7 percent. The chapter should give the figure with its year, its source, and its definition of income, and treat the link between inequality and collapse as an argument to test in §6, not an established cause.[^ch33-pew-inequality][^ch33-check-inequality]
- **The farm depression.** Farm mortgage debt rose from about $3.2 billion in 1910 to about $9.6 billion by 1930, and the number of farms fell from about 6.4 million in 1910 to about 6.3 million in 1930. The 1920s were already a depression for much of agricultural America, so rural distress preceded the 1929 crash rather than following it.[^ch33-usda-farm-history][^ch33-check-ch32]
- **Debt and credit.** Installment buying and margin accounts tied household and investor finances to borrowed money. The chapter should present this as the material inheritance of the boom, without asserting that debt alone caused the Depression.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-check-ch32]

### 1.3 The crash of October 1929

- **The claim.** A long bull market ended in a sharp, panicked collapse whose damage to banks was initially contained but whose shock to confidence reduced spending on durable goods, deepening the contraction.[^ch33-frh-crash]
- **The boom.** The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose from 63 in August 1921 to 381 in September 1929. Purchasers of stock on margin typically put down about 10 percent and borrowed the rest, and the borrowed money pushed prices higher.[^ch33-frh-crash]
- **The crash.** On Black Monday, October 28, 1929, the Dow declined nearly 13 percent; on Black Tuesday, October 29, it fell nearly 12 percent. By mid-November 1929 the Dow had lost almost half its value, and it would close at 41.22 in summer 1932, its lowest value of the twentieth century. The chapter should give the index values and dates with their source.[^ch33-frh-crash]
- **The response.** The Federal Reserve Bank of New York bought government securities, lent through the discount window, and lowered its discount rate, actions that later economists judged to have contained the immediate crisis. The chapter should present the New York Fed’s response as effective in the short run and as a contrast to the failures that followed.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-frh-great-depression]
- **What the record can show.** It can show prices, dates, margin rates, and official actions. It cannot show what a given investor or saver felt, and the chapter should mark the gap between the market record and the experience of loss.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-frh-crash]

### 1.4 From crash to contraction

- **The claim.** After the crash the economy contracted, but the contraction initially resembled earlier downturns, and recovery still appeared possible into the fall of 1930; the banking crises changed that trajectory.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics]
- **The recession.** Industrial production and durable-goods output fell, and unemployment rose, as firms slowed production. The chapter should present the 1929–30 downturn as severe but not yet unprecedented.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-check-numbers]
- **The first policy misstep.** The Federal Reserve had raised interest rates in 1928 and 1929 to curb speculation, and because the international gold standard linked monetary policies, its tightening helped push other economies into recession. The chapter should present this as a decision with international consequences that its authors did not foresee.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-state-gold]
- **The turning point.** The banking crises that began in November 1930 turned the contraction toward the Depression. The chapter should mark the collapse of Caldwell and Company as the start of that shift and the Bank of the United States failure as its most visible event.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

## 2. Banking and international contagion

### 2.1 The claim: a credit system and a monetary idea

- **The claim.** The Depression deepened through two connected failures: a domestic banking system that could not stop runs once they began, and an international gold standard that forced governments to defend their currencies by tightening credit and cutting wages in the middle of a slump.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **The structure.** The chapter should present the banking system’s weaknesses as structural — fictitious reserves made of checks in the process of collection, reserve pyramids built on correspondent banks, and a large sector of nonmember banks outside the Federal Reserve’s safety net — rather than as a story of individual recklessness.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics]
- **What the record can show.** It can show bank failures, deposit losses, currency hoarding, and money-supply figures. It cannot show, from the aggregates alone, which failures were caused by panic and which by insolvency, and the chapter should present that dispute as unresolved.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-debate-works]

### 2.2 Figure 7: banking, unemployment, and prices

- **The claim.** Three series together make the collapse legible: the wave of bank suspensions, the climb of unemployment, and the fall of prices. Plotted on one figure they show the mechanism by which a banking crisis became a deflationary spiral.[^ch33-check-figure7][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-stlouisfed-impact]

### 2.3 The banking panics, 1930–1933

- **The claim.** A series of regional panics, each spreading through correspondent networks, progressively disabled the banking system between the fall of 1930 and the winter of 1933.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The first crisis.** The panic began after the November 1930 collapse of Caldwell and Company, the largest financial holding company in the South, and the failure of its Bank of Tennessee affiliate in Nashville. Runs spread through Tennessee, Kentucky, Arkansas, and North Carolina; there were 256 suspensions in November and 352 in December 1930, and about 1,350 across the year.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The New York failure.** The Bank of the United States failed on December 11, 1930, after merger talks broke down. It was the fourth-largest bank in New York City, and its failure revived panic and prompted withdrawals from other banks.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The later crises.** A second, regionally concentrated crisis ran from April to August 1931, with 563 suspensions; a third, nationwide crisis followed Britain’s departure from the gold standard on September 21, 1931, and produced 817 suspensions between September and October. A fourth crisis produced about 4,000 suspensions in 1933, most of them before mid-March.[^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **What the record can show.** It can show suspension counts, dates, and deposit losses. It cannot show the experience of a depositor or a bank officer, and the chapter should name that silence.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

### 2.4 The gold standard and international contagion

- **The claim.** The interwar gold standard transmitted deflation from country to country, and the “gold-standard mentality” of policymakers made them defend their currencies by tightening credit rather than aiding their economies.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-state-gold]
- **The mechanism.** Under the gold standard, countries losing gold had to restrict credit and reduce prices and wages to restore external balance. Because wages had become less flexible in the politicized labor markets of the 1920s and 1930s, the adjustment fell on output and employment instead.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **The international dimension.** The First World War had left the gold standard restored but fragile, and the United States, Britain, France, and others held reserves and made policy in ways that drained gold from weaker economies. Great Britain left the gold standard in September 1931 and the United States in 1933, and the London Economic Conference of 1933 failed to reach collective agreement.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **The domestic link.** The gold standard tied the Federal Reserve’s hands. When Britain left gold in 1931, the Fed raised its discount rate on October 9 to defend the dollar, a move that intensified the domestic banking crisis. The chapter should present this as the clearest case of an international monetary rule producing a domestic disaster.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-check-gold]

### 2.5 Money, credit, and deflation

- **The claim.** The banking collapse shrank the money supply and forced a deflation that raised the real burden of every debt, drove more firms and banks under, and reduced spending.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- **The contraction of money.** From the fall of 1930 through the winter of 1933 the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent; one estimate puts the fall at 30.9 percent from its 1929 level. The chapter should give the figure with its dates, its source, and its definition of money.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-numbers]
- **Deflation.** Consumer prices fell about 25 percent and wholesale prices about 32 percent between 1929 and 1933. Deflation increased debt burdens, distorted decisions, reduced consumption, and increased unemployment, and it forced banks, firms, and individuals into bankruptcy.[^ch33-stlouisfed-impact][^ch33-frh-great-depression]
- **Credit intermediation.** Bank failures also destroyed the local knowledge that banks held about who was creditworthy, so even solvent firms could not obtain credit at any price. The chapter should present this credit channel as distinct from the money-supply channel and as one reason the collapse was so deep.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-debate-works]
- **The output fall.** Real gross national product fell about 30.5 percent and real gross domestic product about 29 percent between 1929 and 1933. The chapter should state the measure and its base year and note that the two series are not the same.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-stlouisfed-impact][^ch33-check-numbers]

## 3. Unemployment, farms, hunger, and early drought

### 3.1 The claim: catastrophe measured and unmeasured

- **The claim.** The Depression’s human cost fell unequally and cannot be fully read from the statistics, because the counts themselves were built with definitions that excluded some of the suffering.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-check-unemployment]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present unemployment, farm loss, hunger and relief, and the first drought as overlapping faces of one catastrophe, not as separate social problems.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-usda-farm-history]
- **What the record can show.** It can show rates, counts, and acreages. It cannot show how families managed, what they ate, or how they understood their losses, and the chapter should turn to letters, testimony, and newspaper records for that and name the silence where the record runs out.[^ch33-check-silences]

### 3.2 Unemployment and its measurement

- **The claim.** Unemployment rose to its highest recorded level in American history, but the exact figure depends on how the statisticians treated work relief and the labor force.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment]
- **The peak.** At the height of the Depression in 1933, 24.9 percent of the total work force — about 12,830,000 people — was unemployed, and farmers were not counted among the unemployed. This is the Lebergott convention.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment]
- **The measurement dispute.** The Lebergott series rises from about 3.2 percent in 1929 to about 25.2 percent in 1933; the Darby series, which counts people on work relief as employed, rises from about 5.9 percent to about 19.2 percent. The chapter should present both and explain the difference, because a single unqualified “25 percent” conceals a definitional choice.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment][^ch33-check-unemployment]
- **The denominator.** The chapter should state the base of each rate — the civilian labor force, with farmers excluded in the Lebergott convention — and note that the figures are annual averages, not a single date.[^ch33-margo-unemployment][^ch33-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Farms and foreclosures

- **The claim.** Farm families had suffered through the 1920s, and the Depression drove a wave of foreclosures that ruined owners and tenants alike.[^ch33-usda-farm-history][^ch33-wheellock-moratoria]
- **The scale.** The farm foreclosure rate was especially high from 1932 through 1934, peaking at nearly 39 foreclosures per 1,000 farms. Family farms were lost by the hundred thousands in the early 1930s, and mortgage distress was concentrated where farm populations and farm debt were largest.[^ch33-wheellock-moratoria][^ch33-check-numbers]
- **The regional pattern.** In Iowa, about 13 percent of farmland was foreclosed on one or more times between 1921 and 1932, a figure that shows how far back the distress ran and how unevenly it fell. The chapter should present the state-level evidence as regional rather than national.[^ch33-alston-foreclosures]
- **The response.** Farmers organized against foreclosure sales, sometimes crowding auctions so that property sold for pennies, and states imposed temporary foreclosure moratoria. The chapter should present the moratoria as a measure that reduced foreclosures in the short run while making credit scarcer and dearer afterward.[^ch33-wheellock-moratoria]

### 3.4 The In Focus: one located foreclosure notice

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads a single newspaper legal notice of a farm mortgage foreclosure sale as an artifact of the collapse. A foreclosure notice is not a narrative; it is a formula — a court, a plaintiff lender, a defendant family, a deed of trust, a date of sale, and a description of the land — and its very ordinariness is the point. Read closely, the notice shows how the law converted a family’s debt into a public auction and how the collapse reached into the columns of the county paper. The box should transcribe the notice’s date, court, parties, and terms exactly and identify the newspaper, issue, and page from the actual scan. It should treat the page as a legal and economic document, cite its source and rights status, and end on the chapter’s question: what did it mean for a community that these notices became routine?[^ch33-check-focus][^ch33-check-foreclosure-notice]

- **The registered status.** No image is assigned to this chapter, so the notice is planned as a textual close reading. Reproducing the page would require a new register entry, with its own caption, credit, and rights, before it could be added.[^ch33-check-image][^ch33-check-focus]
- **The evidence task.** No specific 1929–33 foreclosure notice has yet been pinned for this In Focus. Candidate leads are the digitized newspapers at the Library of Congress’s Chronicling America, including the *Producers News* of Plentywood, Montana; county historical-society collections with abstract or mortgage records; and the farm-mortgage moratoria literature for the surrounding legal context. Each lead is presently `discovered — not yet consulted`, and the In Focus may not be written until one page is identified, read, and recorded with an exact newspaper, issue, and page locator.[^ch33-check-foreclosure-notice][^ch33-wheellock-moratoria]
- **What must not be done.** The box must not reuse a composite or unidentified farm advertisement, must not invent parties or a sale date, and must not present a plausible notice as though it were located. If no notice can be sourced, the chapter should substitute another identified period document rather than fabricate one.[^ch33-check-focus]

### 3.5 Hunger, relief, and the first drought

- **The claim.** With no federal system of direct relief, the hungry depended on local charities and city governments that were themselves collapsing, and the drought of 1930–31 added environmental disaster to economic collapse.[^ch33-erca][^ch33-hoover-archives]
- **The relief gap.** Cities such as Chicago and Philadelphia ran emergency relief organizations that could not meet the demand, and the burden fell on private charity and local government until federal relief loans arrived in 1932. The chapter should present relief as a patchwork that varied by city and state.[^ch33-erca][^ch33-check-relief]
- **The drought.** Dry conditions in 1930 touched 30 states from the Appalachians to the Rockies, with the worst effects in the lower Mississippi and Ohio valleys, and thousands of families abandoned farms. The chapter should keep the early drought distinct from the later Dust Bowl, whose migration and photography belong to Chapter 34.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-check-ch34]
- **What the record can show.** It can show relief appropriations, drought extent, and migration estimates. It cannot show hunger’s distribution with precision, and the chapter should treat hunger and homelessness as documented but imperfectly counted.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-check-numbers]

## 4. Hoover’s responses

### 4.1 The claim: an active president inside a constraining philosophy

- **The claim.** Herbert Hoover was not a do-nothing president; he used federal power more than any predecessor had in a depression, but he refused direct federal relief to individuals and relied on voluntarism, which left the response too small for the crisis.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-oertx-hoover]
- **The philosophy.** Hoover believed the American system rested on “rugged individualism” and feared that federal relief would undermine individual character by making recipients dependent. He preferred “voluntary organization and community service” and asked businesses to maintain wages and dividends.[^ch33-hoover-rugged][^ch33-oertx-hoover]
- **The evidence of activity.** He summoned business leaders to the White House in November 1929 and secured commitments to maintain wages and to spend about $1.8 billion on construction; he ordered federal construction accelerated, asked governors to expand public works, and proposed a $160 million tax cut in 1929.[^ch33-hoover-archives]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the conferences, the public-works orders, the loans, and the tax measures. It cannot show that a larger federal response would have ended the Depression on a given timetable, and the chapter should separate the judgment that Hoover’s response was inadequate from any claim about a precise counterfactual.[^ch33-check-debate-works][^ch33-check-relief]

### 4.2 Voluntarism and the high-wage policy

- **The claim.** Hoover’s attempt to hold wages and dividends steady by agreement kept real wages high as prices fell, which pushed firms to lay off workers rather than cut pay.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-hoover-archives]
- **The mechanism.** In earlier depressions wages had fallen and more workers had kept their jobs; in this contraction, manufacturing firms held wage rates nearly constant into 1931, so real hourly wages rose sharply and firms primarily laid off workers. The chapter should present the high-wage policy as well intended and consequential.[^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- **The tariff.** Hoover proposed a limited tariff revision to help farmers in 1929, lost control of the bill, and signed the Smoot-Hawley tariff on June 17, 1930, after the Senate passed it on June 13; it raised duties on dutiable imports from about 40.1 percent to about 53.21 percent and was meant to protect the firms that held wages steady. The chapter should present the tariff as part of the same strategy as the high-wage policy, and should not claim that it caused the Depression.[^ch33-senate-smoot-hawley][^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-relief]
- **The consequences.** Unemployment soared in durable manufacturing, where production fell about 36 percent between the end of 1929 and the end of 1930 and another 36 percent the following year. The chapter should present the wage policy as a decision that shifted the adjustment onto employment.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-numbers]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the conferences, the pledges, and the wage and employment series. It cannot show that wage cuts would have prevented the Depression, and the chapter should mark the counterfactual as open.[^ch33-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 Federal lending and the instruments of response

- **The claim.** Hoover’s most substantial responses were federal credit institutions that lent to banks, railroads, and farmers rather than to unemployed people.[^ch33-frh-rfc][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The farm and credit measures.** The Agricultural Marketing Act of 1929 created the Federal Farm Board to stabilize farm prices by buying and holding surpluses, and the Federal Home Loan Bank Act of July 1932 created a system to lend to mortgage lenders. The chapter should present these as efforts to support credit rather than to relieve poverty.[^ch33-usda-farm-history][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The RFC.** Hoover signed the Reconstruction Finance Corporation Act on January 22, 1932. The RFC lent to banks and other financial institutions, including those outside the Federal Reserve, and began with $500 million in Treasury stock and $1.5 billion in bonds. The chapter should present the RFC as the largest peacetime federal intervention in the economy to that date.[^ch33-frh-rfc][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The banking and relief laws.** The Banking Act of 1932 widened the collateral the Federal Reserve could accept and, for a time, supported open-market purchases; the Emergency Relief and Construction Act of July 1932 provided about $300 million in relief loans to states and funded public works. The chapter should present the relief loans as the first large-scale federal public welfare program, welcomed by Hoover only as a loan, not a grant.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-erca]

### 4.4 The 1932 tax increase and the limits of the response

- **The claim.** Fearing deficits, Hoover recommended and Congress enacted a large tax increase in 1932 that reduced disposable income and deepened the contraction.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-check-relief]
- **The tax change.** The lowest marginal income-tax rate rose from about 1.125 percent to 4.0 percent, and the top marginal rate rose from 25 percent on taxable income above $100,000 to 63 percent on income above $1 million, with exemptions cut sharply to widen the tax rolls.[^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- **The gold constraint.** Hoover also had to defend the dollar’s gold parity, which limited his room to inflate or spend, and the tax increase was in part a defense of the gold standard and of confidence. The chapter should present the fiscal and monetary constraints as connected.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutory rates, the veto fights, and the loans. It cannot show that Hoover could have overcome congressional and gold-standard constraints, and the chapter should distinguish his choices from the constraints he faced.[^ch33-check-debate-works][^ch33-check-gold]

## 5. The political crisis and the 1932 election

### 5.1 The claim: a crisis of legitimacy

- **The claim.** By 1932 the Depression had become a political crisis in which millions of people no longer believed that the government could or would help them, and the election of 1932 registered that loss of confidence.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-oertx-hoover]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should connect protest, the Bonus Army, the campaign, the results, and the interregnum as one story about legitimacy.[^ch33-nps-bonus-army][^ch33-app-1932]
- **What the record can show.** It can show votes, protest counts, and official statements. It cannot show what individual voters meant by their ballots, and the chapter should not turn an election result into a policy mandate.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-app-1932]

### 5.2 Protest and the Bonus Army

- **The claim.** The Bonus Army’s march and its forcible dispersal became the emblem of the government’s failure to understand the desperation of ordinary people.[^ch33-nps-bonus-army]
- **The march.** After the First World War the government had promised veterans a bonus payable in 1945, and in 1932 jobless veterans marched on Washington to demand earlier payment. About 400 gathered in Portland, Oregon, by May 17, 1932, under the leadership of a fellow veteran, Walter M. Waters, and by June 1 some 1,500 were in Washington, camping at sites including the 30-acre Anacostia Flats.[^ch33-nps-bonus-army]
- **The dispersal.** On July 28, 1932, the administration sent in the army and police to expel the marchers, and troops led by General Douglas MacArthur cleared the camps. Hoover’s public statement described the marchers as including “communists and persons with criminal records,” and the chapter should read the statement alongside the record of the marchers’ actual circumstances.[^ch33-hoover-bonus-statement][^ch33-nps-bonus-army]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the dates, the counts, the camps, and the official statements. It cannot show the marchers’ interior lives, and the chapter should avoid reducing a diverse group to a single motive.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-nps-bonus-army]

### 5.3 The campaign and the election of 1932

- **The claim.** Franklin D. Roosevelt defeated Hoover in a landslide that registered the country’s rejection of the administration’s response, though the campaign itself offered few specifics about what Roosevelt would do.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-frh-great-depression]
- **The result.** Roosevelt won 472 electoral votes and about 57.4 percent of the popular vote, with about 22,818,740 votes; Hoover won 59 electoral votes and about 39.6 percent of the popular vote, with about 15,760,425 votes. The chapter should give the totals with their source and note that compilations differ slightly.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-check-numbers]
- **The campaign.** Roosevelt campaigned on a “New Deal” and on relief of unemployment, but he refused to endorse Hoover’s policies or to commit to a specific program, which increased uncertainty during the interregnum. The chapter should present the campaign as a change of leadership with the policy content still open.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-frh-great-depression]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the returns and the campaign rhetoric. It cannot show how many voters were moved by which issue, and the chapter should not over-read the landslide.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-app-1932]

### 5.4 The interregnum and the banking collapse

- **The claim.** Between the election and the inauguration, the banking system collapsed, and the outgoing and incoming administrations left the response unclear.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The collapse.** In February 1933, Michigan declared a statewide banking holiday after negotiations to rescue Detroit banks broke down, and state holidays spread. By the time of Roosevelt’s inauguration on March 4, 1933, the banking system was in disarray, depositors were hoarding cash, and 48 states had declared a statewide holiday or restricted withdrawals.[^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- **The boundary.** Roosevelt issued the national bank holiday proclamation on March 6, 1933, Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act on March 9, and Roosevelt gave his first banking fireside chat on March 12. Those acts belong to Chapter 34, and this chapter should end on the collapse and the unresolved question, not on the recovery.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-ch34]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the holidays, the hoarding, and the dates. It cannot show what depositors feared specifically, and the chapter should name that silence rather than speak for them.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-fdic-1930s]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The contraction was deep and long.** Real output fell by about 29 to 30 percent between 1929 and 1933, and unemployment reached its highest recorded level, whether measured by the Lebergott or the Darby series.[^ch33-stlouisfed-impact][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment]
- **The banking panics were central.** Bank failures disabled credit, shrank the money supply, and forced deflation that deepened the collapse.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-frh-great-depression]
- **The gold standard transmitted deflation.** Fixed exchange rates and the defense of gold parity linked the world’s economies and constrained policy in the crisis.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-state-gold]
- **Hoover intervened, but not enough.** He used federal credit and public-works powers more than any predecessor, while refusing direct relief to individuals.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-erca]
- **The crash was not the whole cause.** The 1929 market collapse began a contraction, but the banking crises turned a recession into a depression.[^ch33-frh-crash][^ch33-frh-banking-panics]
- **The collapse produced a political reckoning.** The election of 1932 registered a loss of confidence in the administration’s response.[^ch33-app-1932][^ch33-nps-bonus-army]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was money the cause or the consequence?** One position holds that the Federal Reserve’s failure to prevent the collapse of the money stock caused the Depression; another holds that the money stock fell in response to a decline in spending that originated in the real economy. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-check-debate-works][^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- **How central was the gold standard?** One account makes the gold standard the master cause of the worldwide collapse; another treats it as one transmission channel among several, alongside banking structure and demand. The chapter should weigh the evidence for both.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-state-gold]
- **Was Hoover’s failure philosophical or structural?** One account blames an ideology of voluntarism and balanced budgets; another stresses the real constraints of the gold standard, a divided Federal Reserve, and a Congress that would not fund direct relief. The chapter should set the evidence side by side.[^ch33-oertx-hoover][^ch33-check-debate-works]
- **Did inequality make the crash dangerous?** One account ties the concentration of income to underconsumption and fragility; another treats inequality as background rather than a trigger. The chapter should mark the question as open.[^ch33-pew-inequality][^ch33-check-inequality]
- **What did unemployment really reach?** The gap between the Lebergott and Darby series is a definitional dispute about work relief, not merely a data error, and the chapter should report both.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which no single cause — a crash, a monetary rule, an ideology, or an inequality — is sufficient, and in which the Depression deepened at the intersection of a fragile banking system, an international monetary standard, and a set of policy ideas that made leaders slow to act.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **Contingency.** The Depression was the result of decisions taken by people who could have acted differently, not an earthquake. Eichengreen and Temin’s point is precisely that the outcome was contingent on human choices within an inherited framework.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold the structural vulnerabilities — correspondent banking, the gold standard, unequal incomes — together with the contingent choices — the 1928–29 rate rise, the 1931 defense of gold, the 1932 tax increase, the refusal of direct relief.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that a financial breakdown became a social catastrophe because the institutions meant to absorb shocks — the banking system, the lender of last resort, the gold standard, and the relief apparatus — all failed at once, and because the ideas that guided policy made each failure harder to correct.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-check-table1]

## Legacies

The collapse left a double legacy. It discredited the idea that markets would correct themselves and that private charity could meet a national emergency, and it made the federal government responsible, in the public’s expectation, for the health of the economy and the security of the old and unemployed. The New Deal’s programs belong to Chapter 34, but the political demand for them was created here.[^ch33-smiley-econlib][^ch33-oertx-hoover]

The banking collapse left a durable institutional legacy. The failure of thousands of banks and the loss of depositors’ savings produced the demand for federal deposit insurance and securities regulation, which reshaped American finance for generations. The transition from the RFC and the 1932 banking act to the FDIC and the Securities and Exchange Commission belongs to the New Deal chapters, but the argument for it was written by the failures of 1930–33.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-frh-great-depression]

The international legacy was ambivalent. The Depression’s transmission under the gold standard taught policymakers to prefer domestic recovery to exchange-rate orthodoxy, while the failure at London in 1933 seemed to prove that cooperation was impossible; the United States would later build international institutions on the premise that the 1930s must not be repeated, a story that belongs to the Cold War chapters.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-check-dates-appD]

The decade’s measurement legacy persists. The unemployment and foreclosure series created to describe the Depression still shape how Americans argue about hard times, and the gap between the Lebergott and Darby measures is a reminder that the way a society counts unemployment is itself a political choice.[^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment][^ch33-margo-unemployment]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The gold standard, the 1931 central-bank rate increases, the collapse of the German and Austrian banks, and the world depression connect this chapter to Europe, Latin America, and Asia. The chapter should place these connections in the argument rather than treat other countries as scenery for American decisions.[^ch33-state-gold][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents the counted — the unemployed who registered, the farms that entered legal foreclosure, the banks that filed receivership papers — and under-represents the uncounted: tenant farmers, migrant workers, women without independent claims to work, and the Black southerners whom relief systems served unevenly. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch33-check-silences][^ch33-check-unemployment]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 32 owns the 1920s boom; Chapter 34 owns the New Deal and the bank holiday. Chapter 26 owns the southern racial order, and Chapter 40 owns the Great Society’s later extension of social provision. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch33-check-ch32][^ch33-check-ch34][^ch33-check-dates-appD]

## Summary

- The contraction began in the summer of 1929, and the stock market crash of October 1929 accelerated it; the Dow fell nearly 13 percent on Black Monday and nearly 12 percent on Black Tuesday, and lost almost half its value by mid-November.[^ch33-frh-crash]
- The banking panics that began in November 1930 turned the recession into the Depression; about 1,350 banks suspended in 1930, about 2,300 in 1931, about 1,450 in 1932, and about 4,000 in 1933.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-frh-banking-panics]
- The Bank of the United States failed on December 11, 1930, then the largest bank failure in American history, after a merger attempt collapsed and depositors ran.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-fdic-1930s]
- The money supply fell nearly 30 percent from the fall of 1930 to the winter of 1933; consumer prices fell about 25 percent and wholesale prices about 32 percent between 1929 and 1933.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-stlouisfed-impact]
- The interwar gold standard transmitted deflation, and the Federal Reserve raised its discount rate in October 1931 to defend the dollar after Britain left gold, which deepened the domestic banking crisis.[^ch33-eichengreen-temin][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-state-gold]
- Unemployment reached about 24.9 percent, or about 12,830,000 people, in 1933 on the Lebergott measure, and about 19.2 percent on the Darby measure that counts work relief as employment.[^ch33-fdr-library][^ch33-richmondfed-unemployment]
- Farm foreclosures peaked at nearly 39 per 1,000 farms from 1932 to 1934, and states imposed mortgage moratoria that reduced foreclosures while making credit harder to get.[^ch33-wheellock-moratoria]
- Herbert Hoover relied on voluntarism, secured business pledges to hold wages, created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation in January 1932, signed the Emergency Relief and Construction Act in July 1932, and then raised taxes in 1932.[^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-frh-rfc][^ch33-erca][^ch33-smiley-econlib]
- The Bonus Army marched on Washington in 1932 and was dispersed by the army on July 28.[^ch33-nps-bonus-army]
- Franklin D. Roosevelt won the election of 1932 with 472 electoral votes and about 57.4 percent of the popular vote; Hoover won 59 electoral votes.[^ch33-app-1932]
- In February and March 1933 the banking system collapsed, and Roosevelt’s national bank holiday, the Emergency Banking Act, and his first banking fireside chat, on March 12, 1933, begin Chapter 34.[^ch33-fdic-1930s]

### The debate

- **Monetary and financial explanations.** Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz’s *The Great Contraction*; Ben Bernanke’s “Nonmonetary Effects of the Financial Crisis…”; and Charles Calomiris and Joseph Mason’s “Fundamentals, Panics, and Bank Distress” are recommended for the monetary and financial-frictions positions.[^ch33-frh-banking-panics][^ch33-frh-great-depression]

- **The Hoover record.** Hoover’s “rugged individualism” speech and his Bonus Army statement are the primary documents; the Hoover library exhibit and an open United States history text supply the policy context.[^ch33-hoover-rugged][^ch33-hoover-bonus-statement][^ch33-hoover-archives][^ch33-oertx-hoover]

> *A closing note.* Between 1929 and 1933 the United States suffered the deepest contraction in its history. A stock market crash was followed by a series of banking panics; a gold standard meant to guarantee stability transmitted deflation around the world; a banking system without deposit insurance turned local runs into a national collapse; and a president who believed in voluntarism used federal credit but refused direct relief. By early 1933 real output had fallen by nearly a third, millions were out of work, farm families were losing land in waves, and the banking system stood on the edge of shutdown. The chapter’s question — how did a financial breakdown become a prolonged social catastrophe? — is answered by a record in which a fragile system, an international rule, and a set of guiding ideas failed together.[^ch33-frh-great-depression][^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-eichengreen-temin]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on March 12, 1933, when President Franklin D. Roosevelt spoke to the nation by radio in his first fireside chat and explained, in plain language, what his administration had done to reopen the banks. Chapter 34 takes up the New Deal, and asks what kind of security it created. The bank holiday’s proclamation of March 6 and the fireside chat are separate acts, and this bridge keeps them so.[^ch33-fdic-1930s][^ch33-check-ch34][^ch33-check-bridge]
