# Chapter 42. The Unraveling (1968–1974)

> “And so tonight — to you, the great silent majority of my fellow Americans — I ask for your support.”[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote]
>
> — Richard M. Nixon, address to the nation on the war in Vietnam, November 3, 1969.[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote]

> “It was a rebellion, it was an uprising, it was a civil rights disobedience — it wasn’t no damn riot.”[^ch42-stonewall-quote]
>
> — Stormé DeLarverie, recollection of the Stonewall uprising, quoted in the Library of Congress LGBTQIA+ Studies resource guide.[^ch42-stonewall-quote]

> “But there was nothing natural or inevitable about how he did it — nothing inevitable in the idea that a president could come to power by using the angers, anxieties, and resentments produced by the cultural chaos of the 1960s.”[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland]
>
> — Rick Perlstein, *Nixonland: The Rise of a President and the Fracturing of America* (2008).[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland]

Three voices, three verdicts. Nixon claimed that the nation’s center was silent, patient, and on his side. DeLarverie claimed that the people the state policed were citizens demanding rights, not rioters. Perlstein argues that the coalition Nixon won was assembled, not simply given by events. They disagree about who the nation was, what its conflicts were about, and whether the outcome was inevitable. Their disagreement frames the chapter’s question: how did demands for liberation coexist with a politics of order?[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote][^ch42-stonewall-quote][^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-check-epigraphs]

Chicago, August 1968. The Democratic National Convention opened at the International Amphitheatre on August 26. Outside, in Grant Park and Lincoln Park, thousands of demonstrators gathered to protest the Vietnam War and the party’s nominating rules. The National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam and the Youth International Party helped organize them, and many others came on their own. On the night of August 28, as the convention completed its roll call and nominated Vice President Hubert H. Humphrey, police and demonstrators fought in the streets and parks.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-dnc-primary]

The Chicago Study Team, chaired by Daniel Walker, reported to the National Commission on the Causes and Prevention of Violence on December 1, 1968. It found that “the Chicago police were the targets of mounting provocation by both word and act,” including “obscene epithets, and of rocks, sticks, bathroom tiles, and even human feces.” It also found that “the nature of the response was unrestrained and indiscriminate police violence on many occasions, particularly at night,” often “inflicted upon persons who had broken no law, disobeyed no order, made no threat,” including “peaceful demonstrators, onlookers, and large numbers of residents who were simply passing through.” The violence, the report said, “reached its culmination on Wednesday night.”[^ch42-walker-summary]

The report rested on more than 20,000 pages of statements from 3,437 eyewitnesses, 180 hours of film, and more than 12,000 still photographs. Its findings were widely summarized as the judgment that Chicago had seen a “police riot,” but the report’s own language was more careful: provocation had occurred, and the response had been indiscriminate. Eight protest leaders were later indicted for conspiring to violate the Anti-Riot Act of 1968, and their convictions were reversed on appeal.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-dnc-primary]

The record can show the setting, the organizers, the provocations, the police violence, and the later prosecutions. It cannot show what every participant intended, whether the violence was centrally ordered, or what the crowd felt. In August 1968, no one in the streets or the hall knew that Humphrey would lose narrowly in November, that Nixon would resign six years later, or that the convention’s violence would become an emblem of the decade. None of it was yet an explanation.[^ch42-check-scene][^ch42-check-silences]

A party that had carried an unpopular war met a movement it could not absorb, and the image of disorder became an argument for order. How did demands for liberation coexist with a politics of order?

This chapter covers the years from 1968 to 1974 and asks how demands for liberation coexisted with a politics of order. It treats the period as one connected story rather than as a president’s biography with social movements attached. It draws on presidential speeches and recordings, the *Public Papers of the Presidents*, the *Foreign Relations of the United States* series, congressional and court records, official election returns, agency and commission reports, contemporaneous journalism, movement records, and the secondary literature.[^ch42-check-scope][^ch42-check-dates-appD]

The chapter uses several contested names. “Law and order” was a political appeal that named disorder without defining it. “The silent majority” was a claim about who legitimately belonged to the nation, not a survey finding. “Realignment” describes a durable change in party coalitions; “backlash” describes a reaction to a social movement; neither is self-evident from an election map. “New Federalism” named a shift toward states and localities that often increased federal spending and regulation. “Vietnamization” was a policy of transferring combat responsibility while expanding American air power. “Détente” described a managed rivalry, not friendship. “Liberation” was the movements’ own word for demands that others called disorder. “Watergate” began as a building and became a shorthand for a constitutional crisis.[^ch42-check-names]

The available record over-represents presidents, officials, prosecutors, and journalists and under-represents the poor, the young, people of color, and the organizers who built the movements. The Walker Report rests on the eyewitnesses who came forward, and the White House taping system preserved the president’s voice more fully than the voices of those who opposed him. Election returns, poll figures, casualty counts, and price indexes measure different things; they are given with their definitions, dates, and denominators. Where the archive is thin, the chapter names the silence rather than filling it with a composite witness.[^ch42-check-silences][^ch42-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements, organized thematically around questions because the domestic, diplomatic, and rights stories overlap in time. §1 examines the Chicago convention and the election of 1968, with Map 33. §2 examines New Federalism, the environmental and labor regulation of 1970–72, the 26th Amendment, and the busing fight. §3 examines Vietnamization, the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the opening to China, SALT I, and the Paris settlement. §4 examines women’s and gay liberation, Native and Chicano activism, and the rights litigation of *Furman* and *Roe*. §5 examines Watergate, the tapes and the In Focus, the resignation and pardon, and the oil shock. §6 asks how historians have weighed realignment, liberal continuity, grassroots rights, and backlash. The chapter begins in Chicago in August 1968.[^ch42-check-scope]

## 1. Chicago and the 1968 election (1968)

### 1.1 The claim: a politics of order meets a politics of liberation

**The claim.** The election of 1968 was not a single verdict on the war or on the movements. It was a narrow, three-way contest in which Richard M. Nixon assembled a coalition out of the politics of order and the resentments of the late 1960s. The result ended the Democratic coalition’s dominance without producing a stable new majority, and the meaning of the “realignment” is still disputed.[^ch42-electoral-1968][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-perlstein-nixonland]

The section runs from the Chicago convention through the election, the Wallace vote, and the first year of the Nixon presidency.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

### 1.2 Convention and conflict

The Democratic Party nominated Humphrey in Chicago in late August 1968 amid protests and police violence. Humphrey had not competed in the primaries, and his support for Johnson’s war made him the target of the antiwar movement inside and outside the hall. The convention’s rules, the war, and the nomination were all contested on the floor and in the streets.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-dnc-primary]

The Walker Report documents both provocation and an “unrestrained and indiscriminate” police response. The week was a conflict with responsibility on more than one side; the phrase “police riot” should not stand in for a full account. The report’s evidence base was enormous, and its conclusion was not that the demonstrators were innocent of provocation; it was that the police response was indiscriminate and often fell on people who had done nothing.[^ch42-walker-summary]

The political effect was not automatic. The violence gave Nixon’s campaign an image it could use, but the election was decided by the war, the economy, the Wallace candidacy, and the Electoral College. The convention made the politics of order visible; it did not create the result.[^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-perlstein-nixonland]

### 1.3 The election of 1968: three coalitions: Map 33

Nixon won 301 electoral votes to Humphrey’s 191; George C. Wallace of the American Independent Party won 46. In the popular vote Nixon received 31,785,480 votes (43.4 percent), Humphrey 31,275,166 (42.7 percent), and Wallace 9,906,473 (13.5 percent), out of 73,211,875 total votes cast. The popular margin between Nixon and Humphrey was 510,314 votes, or seven-tenths of a percentage point. Wallace carried five states in the Deep South and took 13.5 percent of the national vote.[^ch42-electoral-1968][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular]

Map 33, “Elections of 1968 and 1972,” is the chapter’s registered visual. It will show the 1968 three-way race and the 1972 landslide side by side, so that the reader can compare the coalitions rather than assume that one produced the other. The map’s source is the official returns tabulated in App. B, Table 2, and its caption will state the years, the units, and the difference between popular and electoral votes.[^ch42-check-map33]

The map has limits. It cannot prove that the voters who chose Wallace in 1968 became Nixon voters in 1972, nor that any single group’s motives explain the national result. Elections are aggregates of individual choices taken for many reasons. The map describes outcomes; it does not report motives. It is a comparison of results, not a reading of a coalition off the returns.[^ch42-check-map33][^ch42-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 Law and order, the “silent majority,” and the Moratorium

In a televised address on November 3, 1969, Nixon asked for the support of “the great silent majority of my fellow Americans,” presenting the war and the protests as matters of order and patience. He described his policy as “Vietnamizing the search for peace” and warned that a “vocal minority” must not be allowed to dictate national policy. The speech was a claim about who the nation was and which Americans spoke for it.[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote]

The claim was answered in the streets. The Moratorium demonstrations of October 15, 1969, and the Mobilization march of November 15 brought hundreds of thousands of people into the streets of Washington and other cities. The “silent majority” and the mass movements were rival claims about the same nation, each advanced by people who believed they spoke for its center. Neither claim was a survey finding.[^ch42-miller-vietnamization][^ch42-check-debate-works]

Neither the returns nor the “silent majority” language proves what most Americans thought. The returns measured votes for candidates, not approval of a program; the phrase did political work because it could not be tested. Polls and returns each measure something narrower than a national mood.[^ch42-check-numbers][^ch42-check-names]

## 2. New Federalism, regulation, and the vote (1969–1972)

### 2.1 The claim: a conservative president expanded the state

**The claim.** Nixon governed as a conservative in rhetoric and an expansionist in administration. He proposed to shift power to states and localities through revenue sharing while signing a burst of environmental, safety, and welfare legislation. The contradiction is central to the debate over whether the New Deal order survived him or began to come apart.[^ch42-dol-nixonford][^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-hoff-nixon]

The section runs from the New Federalism and welfare proposals through the environmental legislation, OSHA, the 26th Amendment, and the busing conflict.[^ch42-dol-nixonford][^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-26th-nara]

### 2.2 New Federalism and the welfare state

The Nixon and Ford administrations pursued what they called a “new working partnership between the Federal Government and the States and localities.” The unifying theme was revenue sharing, and the preferred instrument was the block grant rather than the categorical program. The principle was that “decisions are better made when they are made by those directly concerned.” General revenue sharing sent federal money to states and localities with fewer strings than the Great Society’s categorical grants, though Washington continued to set conditions and pay for programs it favored.[^ch42-dol-nixonford]

Nixon proposed a Family Assistance Plan built around a negative income tax, a guaranteed minimum income for poor families with children. The House passed a version in 1970 and again in 1971, but the Senate rejected it in 1972. Part of his welfare reform did pass, in the form of Supplemental Security Income, which provided a guaranteed income for elderly, blind, and disabled citizens, while Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid benefits increased. The proposal and the outcome were different things: Nixon proposed a major expansion and Congress refused the part he called the centerpiece.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-nara-homefront]

Liberals thought the plan too stingy and conservatives thought it too generous. Nixon presented it as welfare reform, not a new entitlement, and its defeat did not end the federal role in income support. The episode shows a president willing to propose a guaranteed income and a Congress unwilling to enact one, which complicates both the “conservative Nixon” and the “liberal continuity” stories.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-hoff-nixon]

### 2.3 The environmental and labor-regulatory burst

Nixon signed the National Environmental Policy Act on January 1, 1970, which required environmental impact statements for major federal actions. He proposed and then established the Environmental Protection Agency, signed the Clean Air Act at the end of 1970, and signed the Occupational Safety and Health Act on December 29, 1970. The Marine Mammal Protection Act followed in 1972 and the Endangered Species Act in 1973. The Miller Center concludes that “probably more new regulation was imposed on the economy” during the Nixon years “than in any other presidency since the New Deal.”[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-epa-caa][^ch42-nara-homefront]

The record is not one of uniform enthusiasm. Nixon also impounded funds Congress had authorized for clean-air enforcement, and Congress restricted impoundment in 1974. He supported some environmental measures and resisted others, and his administration’s environmental record was shaped by Congress, the courts, and a mass movement as much as by the president. The first Earth Day, on April 22, 1970, showed the political force behind the legislation; the chapter names that movement without re-narrating it.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-nara-homefront][^ch42-check-dates-appD]

The Occupational Safety and Health Act created an agency empowered to set and enforce workplace standards, a significant expansion of federal authority over employers. Nixon signed it while preaching decentralization: the administrative state grew under a president who said he wanted to shrink it.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-dol-nixonford]

### 2.4 The 26th Amendment and the changing electorate

The 26th Amendment, lowering the voting age to 18, was passed by Congress on March 23, 1971, and ratified on July 1, 1971. It followed the Supreme Court’s decision in *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970), which held that Congress could lower the voting age for federal elections but not for state and local elections. The amendment resolved the inconsistency by making 18 the voting age everywhere, and it was the fastest ratification of any constitutional amendment.[^ch42-26th-nara][^ch42-26th-reagan]

The amendment tied the chapter’s threads together. The draft that sent 18-year-olds to fight had made “old enough to fight, old enough to vote” a national demand. The new voters entered the electorate in time for the 1972 election, and both parties tried to win them.[^ch42-26th-reagan][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

### 2.5 The Southern strategy and the busing fight

The administration’s electoral strategy and its opposition to court-ordered busing shaped the coalition Nixon assembled. In *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education* (1971), the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the use of busing as one tool for dismantling state-imposed school segregation, while cautioning that no fixed racial balance was constitutionally required. Nixon opposed court-ordered busing and proposed limits on it, while his administration continued to enforce desegregation orders in districts that had segregated by law.[^ch42-swann][^ch42-miller-domestic]

The “Southern strategy” is a contested term and a contested causal claim. The administration courted white southern voters, and race, rights, and taxes became central to its coalition. But the strategy cannot by itself explain the 1968 or 1972 results, and it cannot justify attributing a single motive to all White voters. Politicians’ intentions and voters’ decisions are different questions.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-check-map33][^ch42-check-debate-works]

## 3. Vietnamization, Cambodia, and the opening to China (1969–1973)

### 3.1 The claim: a war ended on terms the settlement could not hold

**The claim.** Nixon and Henry Kissinger ended direct American combat in Vietnam through a combination of troop withdrawals, expanded bombing, an opening to China, and détente with the Soviet Union. They signed a settlement in January 1973 that neither Vietnamese party honored. The war’s ending became a source of the era’s distrust of government.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-state-salt][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

The section runs from the secret bombing of Cambodia and the first withdrawals through the Cambodian incursion and Kent State, the China opening and SALT I, the Easter Offensive, and the Paris settlement.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-kent-state][^ch42-state-salt]

### 3.2 Vietnamization and the widening war

Nixon began withdrawing forces in June 1969 while building South Vietnam’s capacity in a program Secretary of Defense Melvin Laird called “Vietnamization.” The administration simultaneously expanded the air war, including secret B-52 bombings of North Vietnamese base camps in Cambodia. The policy was designed to shift the fighting to South Vietnamese forces while keeping American air power and advisers in place.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

In the November 3, 1969, address Nixon said, “In the previous administration, we Americanized the war in Vietnam. In this administration, we are Vietnamizing the search for peace.” He asked the “great silent majority” for support and warned that a “vocal minority” must not be allowed to dictate policy. The speech joined a promise of withdrawal to a claim about order at home.[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

### 3.3 Cambodia, Kent State, and the campus strike

After the fall of Prince Norodom Sihanouk in March 1970, Nixon announced on April 30 that United States and South Vietnamese forces had entered Cambodia. The operation was limited to a 30-kilometer strip along the border and to the end of June. It was intended to destroy North Vietnamese bases and to buy time for Vietnamization, and it widened a war the administration had promised to narrow.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

On May 4, 1970, members of the Ohio National Guard fired into a crowd of demonstrators at Kent State University, killing four students and wounding nine. Twenty-eight of more than 70 Guardsmen fired, discharging between 61 and 67 shots in about 13 seconds. The Scranton Commission concluded that “the indiscriminate firing of rifles into a crowd of students and the deaths that followed were unnecessary, unwarranted, and inexcusable.”[^ch42-kent-state]

The shootings triggered a nationwide student strike that closed hundreds of campuses. Ten days later, on the night of May 14–15, 1970, state police killed two students at Jackson State College in Mississippi. The two events had different actors and records; together they showed how quickly the war’s violence could reach American campuses.[^ch42-kent-state][^ch42-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 China, Moscow, and SALT I

Kissinger’s secret trip to Beijing in July 1971 was followed by Nixon’s visit to the People’s Republic of China in February 1972, the first by an American president. A summit in Moscow followed in May 1972. The strategy, as the Miller Center describes it, was to “play China against the Soviet Union, the Soviet Union against China, and both against North Vietnam.” The opening did not end the war, but it changed the war’s diplomatic context and gave Nixon a claim to statesmanship.[^ch42-miller-china][^ch42-state-ending-vietnam]

After formal talks opened in Helsinki on November 17, 1969, Nixon and Soviet General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev signed the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty and the interim SALT agreement in Moscow on May 26, 1972. The ABM Treaty limited strategic missile defenses to 200 interceptors each and two sites. SALT I was the first Cold War agreement to limit strategic nuclear arsenals, and it became the centerpiece of the case for détente.[^ch42-state-salt]

### 3.5 Paris and after

The North Vietnamese Easter Offensive opened in late March 1972. Nixon mined North Vietnam’s harbors in May and began sustained air operations known as Linebacker. After talks stalled, he ordered the December “Christmas bombing” of the North Vietnamese heartland. It helped produce an agreement, at a cost that deepened the war’s bitterness at home.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam]

The United States and North Vietnam initialed an agreement on January 23, 1973, and signed it on January 27. President Nguyen Van Thieu of South Vietnam accepted it reluctantly after American pressure. Neither Vietnamese party abided by the settlement, and the war continued until the North Vietnamese offensive of 1975 and the fall of Saigon on April 30, 1975. Those later events belong to the chapter’s retrospective frame, not to what the signers knew in January 1973.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-check-dates-appD]

The legal and constitutional accompaniments of the war’s ending belonged to the same years. In *New York Times Co. v. United States*, 403 U.S. 713 (1971), the Supreme Court refused to enjoin publication of the classified Pentagon Papers, holding that the government had not met the heavy burden required for a prior restraint. Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon’s veto in November 1973, requiring the president to report the introduction of forces into hostilities and to terminate hostilities absent congressional authorization. The resolution tried to write the era’s distrust into law.[^ch42-nyt-v-us][^ch42-warpowers]

## 4. Liberation movements and rights litigation (1969–1973)

### 4.1 The claim: rights expanded while the coalition that would define them narrowed

**The claim.** The same years that produced the politics of order produced a widening of rights — for women, gay people, Native nations, and Chicanos — through organizing, litigation, and legislation. These movements did not fit neatly into either party, and their gains and the backlash they provoked are two parts of one story.[^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-justia-roe][^ch42-skrentny-rights]

The section runs from the Women’s Strike and Stonewall through Title IX and the ERA, Alcatraz and Wounded Knee, the Chicano Moratorium, and *Furman* and *Roe*. It treats the movements as distinct, with different constituencies, tactics, and demands.[^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-usmarshals-wounded-knee][^ch42-loc-chicano][^ch42-justia-furman]

### 4.2 Women’s liberation, Title IX, and the ERA

On August 26, 1970, the fiftieth anniversary of the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment, the National Organization for Women organized a nationwide Women’s Strike for Equality. The largest event was a march down Fifth Avenue in New York City that witnesses estimated at more than 50,000 women. NOW’s membership increased by about half within a few months. The strike joined liberal feminists’ demand for legal equality to radical feminists’ critique of the family, work, and sexuality, and it made women’s liberation a visible national presence.[^ch42-nyhistory-strike]

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, signed June 23, 1972, provided that “no person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Its later application to athletics and its enforcement are matters for later chapters. The statute’s immediate effect was to make sex discrimination in federally funded education a legal claim.[^ch42-justice-titleix][^ch42-check-ch43]

Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment in March 1972 and sent it to the states. Ratification was not completed within the deadline then set; the later campaign belongs to Chapter 43.[^ch42-check-ch43][^ch42-check-dates-appD]

### 4.3 Gay liberation after Stonewall

On June 28, 1969, police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York City’s Greenwich Village, and patrons and community members fought back. The protests stretched over several days. Veterans of the events prefer “uprising” or “rebellion” to “riot,” a preference the Library of Congress records and explains: the word “riot” had been used by police to justify force. The events were not the first act of LGBTQ+ resistance, but they became the symbolic beginning of gay liberation and the annual Pride traditions.[^ch42-loc-stonewall]

The first Pride march was held on June 28, 1970, the first anniversary of the uprising. The record of the uprising is largely retrospective and contested. Statements must be attributed to the witnesses who made them, recollections dated, and the roles of people of color and gender-nonconforming people acknowledged, since early accounts often minimized them.[^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-check-silences]

### 4.4 Native and Chicano activism

In November 1969, Native activists began the occupation of Alcatraz Island, which lasted until June 1971. The occupiers called themselves Indians of All Tribes, asserted Native claims to the island, and brought the slogan of reclaimed land and self-determination to national attention. The occupation became a founding episode of the Red Power movement.[^ch42-nps-alcatraz]

On February 27, 1973, followers of the American Indian Movement seized the town of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, in a 71-day occupation that ended on May 8, 1973. Two Native people were killed and a United States marshal was seriously wounded. The occupation drew attention to treaty rights and conditions on Pine Ridge Reservation and ended in a standoff with federal agents.[^ch42-usmarshals-wounded-knee]

On August 29, 1970, between 20,000 and 30,000 demonstrators marched through East Los Angeles in the National Chicano Moratorium against the Vietnam War. The march ended in violence after the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department arrived. Three people were killed, including the journalist Rubén Salazar, and the park where the rally was held was later renamed for him. The organizers argued that Mexican Americans were dying in Vietnam in disproportionate numbers, and the moratorium joined the antiwar movement to the struggle for Chicano rights.[^ch42-loc-chicano]

Native and Chicano activism belonged to the broader rights revolution, but distinct nations, organizations, and demands must not be merged. Alcatraz, Wounded Knee, and the Chicano Moratorium were different kinds of events, and their participants did not agree on goals or tactics. Their common ground was a claim to belonging that the state had long denied.[^ch42-skrentny-rights][^ch42-check-silences]

### 4.5 Rights litigation: *Furman* and *Roe*

In *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, decided June 29, 1972, the Supreme Court held, per curiam, that the imposition and carrying out of the death penalty in the cases before it constituted cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments. The decision invalidated the death penalty laws then in force as applied, but it did not hold that capital punishment was always unconstitutional. It was followed by a wave of new state statutes that the Court addressed in *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976), which belongs to Chapter 43.[^ch42-justia-furman][^ch42-check-roe-furman]

In *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U.S. 113, decided January 22, 1973, the Court held, in an opinion by Justice Harry A. Blackmun, that the constitutional right of privacy protected a woman’s decision to have an abortion, subject to the state’s interests in maternal health and fetal life as pregnancy advanced. The opinion divided pregnancy into trimesters and set different limits for each. It was a legal event with a contested aftermath, not a national consensus.[^ch42-justia-roe]

## 5. Watergate, resignation, pardon, and the oil shock (1972–1974)

### 5.1 The claim: the politics of order consumed the president who had promised it

**The claim.** Watergate began as a burglary and became a constitutional crisis because the president and his aides used the powers of the office to conceal it. The tapes made the concealment provable, the courts and Congress forced disclosure, and Nixon resigned in August 1974. His successor pardoned him, and the country absorbed the oil shock that struck the same months.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-smoking-gun][^ch42-proclamation-4311][^ch42-frbsf-oil]

The section runs from the break-in through the cover-up, the Senate hearings, the Saturday Night Massacre, the tapes, the impeachment articles, the resignation, the pardon, and the oil embargo.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-frbsf-oil]

### 5.2 The break-in and the cover-up

On June 17, 1972, five men were arrested at the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate building. The bugging equipment had been installed on May 28. The burglars were connected to the White House through E. Howard Hunt and G. Gordon Liddy, who had helped organize the earlier break-in at the office of Daniel Ellsberg’s psychiatrist as part of the “Plumbers” operation. The break-in was not a random crime; it was part of a covert campaign against the president’s enemies.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-domestic]

The administration denied White House involvement and sought to contain the investigation. On August 30, Nixon announced that John Dean had completed an investigation and that no one from the White House was involved. On June 23, 1972, Nixon and H. R. Haldeman had discussed having the CIA tell the FBI to “stay the hell out of this,” a conversation that became the “smoking gun” when it was released in 1974.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-miller-smoking-gun]

### 5.3 The investigations and the tapes

The Senate created the Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities on February 7, 1973, and began public hearings on May 17. On July 16, Alexander Butterfield revealed the existence of a White House taping system. Archibald Cox was sworn in as special prosecutor on May 25, 1973. The tapes turned the investigation into a struggle over evidence and executive privilege.[^ch42-nara-watergate]

On October 20, 1973, after Cox refused a compromise on the tapes, Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire him. Richardson resigned, Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus refused and resigned, and Acting Attorney General Robert Bork fired Cox. On November 21, 1973, the Senate committee announced an 18½-minute gap in a tape of a Nixon–Haldeman conversation of June 20, 1972. The gap was never fully explained.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-domestic]

The House authorized an impeachment inquiry on February 6, 1974. The Supreme Court unanimously upheld the special prosecutor’s subpoena for the tapes on July 24, 1974. The House Judiciary Committee adopted three articles of impeachment on July 27–30, 1974, charging obstruction of the investigation, misuse of powers, and failure to comply with House subpoenas. The endgame was no longer a political scandal; it was a constitutional process.[^ch42-nara-watergate]

### 5.4 In Focus: the June 23, 1972, Oval Office recording

**In Focus.** The recording captures a meeting in the Oval Office between Nixon and Haldeman on June 23, 1972, from 10:04 to 11:39 a.m.; the Miller Center identifies it as the “smoking gun” and gives it the tape number 741-002. Haldeman proposes that the CIA’s Vernon Walters call Acting FBI Director L. Patrick Gray and say, “Stay the hell out of this…this is ah, business here we don’t want you to go any further on it.” Nixon agrees: “All right. Fine.” Later he tells Haldeman, “You call them in. Good. Good deal! Play it tough,” and suggests saying that the investigation “will open the whole, the whole Bay of Pigs thing,” a phrase that referred to the 1961 invasion and, in this context, to secrets the administration did not want exposed. The recording mattered because it contradicted the president’s public claim that he had not interfered with the investigation. It did not prove the break-in itself, and it was not the whole case; it was one conversation, six days after the burglary, in which the president approved a plan to use the CIA to limit an FBI investigation. The transcript was released in August 1974, after the Supreme Court ordered the tapes turned over. The National Archives holds the tapes; no image is registered for this chapter, so the box is a textual and audio close reading.[^ch42-miller-smoking-gun][^ch42-pbs-smoking-gun][^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-check-focus][^ch42-check-rights]

### 5.5 Resignation, succession, and the pardon

On August 8, 1974, Nixon addressed the nation: “Therefore, I shall resign the Presidency effective at noon tomorrow.” He resigned on August 9, 1974, and Gerald R. Ford was sworn in as president. The resignation was the first by an American president, and it ended a crisis that had begun with a burglary and become a test of whether the president was subject to the law.[^ch42-miller-resignation][^ch42-nara-watergate]

On September 8, 1974, Ford issued Proclamation 4311, granting “a full, free, and absolute pardon unto Richard Nixon for all offenses against the United States which he, Richard Nixon, has committed or may have committed or taken part in during the period from January 20, 1969 through August 9, 1974.” Ford said he acted to spare the country a prolonged trial and to begin healing. The pardon became a defining issue of his presidency; it was not an established legal conclusion about Nixon’s guilt.[^ch42-proclamation-4311][^ch42-check-debate-works]

### 5.6 The oil shock

On October 19, 1973, immediately after Nixon asked Congress for $2.2 billion in emergency aid to Israel during the Yom Kippur War, the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries imposed an oil embargo on the United States, ceased imports from participating states, and began production cuts. The embargo was a political act, and it struck an economy already under strain.[^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-state-oil]

The production cuts nearly quadrupled the price of oil, from $2.90 a barrel before the embargo to $11.65 a barrel in January 1974. The embargo was lifted in March 1974, but the higher prices remained. The Nixon administration announced “Project Independence” on November 7, 1973, and the crisis prompted the creation of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, a national 55-mile-per-hour speed limit, and, later, fuel-economy standards.[^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-state-oil]

The embargo was not the sole cause of the inflation that followed. It coincided with the collapse of Bretton Woods, the dollar’s devaluation, and fiscal and monetary strains that predated the war. This chapter owns the embargo’s onset and immediate politics; the longer stagflation and energy policy belong to Chapter 43.[^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-check-ch43]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The returns establish that Nixon won narrowly in 1968 and overwhelmingly in 1972. In 1968 the three-way race gave Nixon 301 electoral votes, Humphrey 191, and Wallace 46, with a popular margin of 510,314 votes. In 1972 Nixon won 520 electoral votes to George McGovern’s 17; John Hospers received one from a faithless elector. Nixon carried 49 states.[^ch42-electoral-1968][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-electoral-1972]

The Chicago convention week produced documented police violence. The Walker Report documents provocation and an “unrestrained and indiscriminate” police response. Its findings do not establish that the demonstrators were peaceful at all times; they establish that the response was often indiscriminate and fell on people who had done nothing.[^ch42-walker-summary]

The Nixon administration expanded federal regulation and the welfare state even as it preached restraint. The environmental legislation, OSHA, and revenue sharing are documented. The administration also impounded funds and fought court-ordered busing, so expansion and retrenchment happened together.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-dol-nixonford]

The United States ended direct combat in Vietnam in January 1973. The Paris settlement was signed on January 27, 1973, after the Christmas bombing and American pressure on Thieu. Neither Vietnamese party honored it, and the war continued until 1975.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam]

Watergate was a documented abuse of presidential power. The break-in, the cover-up, the taping system, the Saturday Night Massacre, the Supreme Court decision, and the impeachment articles are documented, and Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974. The oil embargo struck in October 1973 and was lifted in March 1974; the price increase is documented by the Federal Reserve’s history and the State Department’s account.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-state-oil]

### 6.2 What is open

Was 1968 a realignment or a dealignment? Perlstein and the backlash school argue that Nixon assembled a durable new majority out of the politics of order. Dealignment accounts emphasize falling turnout, unstable institutions, and a party system that was coming apart rather than re-forming. Tested against the returns, the evidence supports neither a clean realignment nor a simple dealignment but a period of coalition change and instability.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-burnham-dealignment][^ch42-check-debate-works]

Did Nixon expand the liberal state or begin to dismantle it? Hoff argues that he preserved and even extended the New Deal order. The “end of the New Deal order” school emphasizes that its economic and political foundations were already cracking. The administrative record and the long-term fiscal story must be kept separate: Nixon could sign the Clean Air Act and impound the money to enforce it.[^ch42-hoff-nixon][^ch42-fraser-gerstle]

Were the movements’ gains a cause of the backlash or independent of it? The grassroots-rights school treats the “rights revolution” as a real expansion; the backlash school treats the same expansion as the trigger for a conservative realignment. Both are partly right. The movements changed the country, and the reaction to them changed the party system.[^ch42-skrentny-rights][^ch42-edsall-chain]

What is the right unit for the politics of the “silent majority”? Lassiter and Kruse locate the era’s decisive politics in the suburbs and metropolitan growth, where race, taxation, and neighborhood were fused. National accounts locate it in presidential coalitions. The two levels answer different questions: a metropolitan study can explain how neighborhoods changed without explaining a landslide, and a national study can explain the landslide without explaining the neighborhood.[^ch42-lassiter-silent][^ch42-kruse-whiteflight]

How much did Watergate change policy? One position treats it as the decisive wound that paralyzed the Nixon presidency and shaped the Ford and Carter years. Another treats it as a scandal that confirmed existing distrust without redirecting policy on its own. The resignation changed the presidency; the policy consequences are harder to isolate.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-check-debate-works]

What does an election map measure? Electoral geography describes aggregated outcomes, not motives. Map 33 cannot prove that Wallace voters became Nixon voters, and the chapter does not read a coalition off the returns.[^ch42-check-map33][^ch42-check-numbers]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the politics of liberation and the politics of order grew from the same soil: a war, a rights revolution, and an economic order under strain. The era’s outcome was decided by contingent choices — a narrow election, a set of administrative decisions, an opening to China, a break-in, and an embargo that no one controlled.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-skrentny-rights][^ch42-miller-domestic]

The 1968 election, the Cambodian incursion, the China opening, the Watergate cover-up, and the oil embargo could have gone otherwise. The alternatives actually considered, and why they were rejected, are part of the record; structural pressures and choices within them must be distinguished.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-frbsf-oil]

Between 1968 and 1974 the United States ended one war and unmade one presidency, widened rights for many Americans while assembling a conservative coalition against the movements that had won them, and discovered that neither the politics of order nor the promise of liberation could by itself hold the center together. That synthesis is an argument about how the pieces fit, not a verdict on who was right.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nara-watergate]

## Legacies

The years from 1968 to 1974 left a politics of distrust that outlasted the individuals. The Pentagon Papers and Watergate taught Americans to expect their government to conceal what it knew. The resignation and the pardon attached that lesson to the highest office, and the War Powers Resolution tried to write it into law. Distrust can be a civic guard and a corrosive habit at the same time.[^ch42-nyt-v-us][^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-warpowers][^ch42-proclamation-4311]

The era also left a changed party system. The coalitions that contested 1968 and 1972 are the ancestors of the coalitions that contest the present. Map 33 places the two elections side by side, letting the reader see how the same electorate produced a narrow three-way result and a landslide in four years, without pretending that the map explains why.[^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-electoral-1972][^ch42-check-map33]

And it left the movements that had demanded recognition. Women, gay people, Native nations, and Chicanos won rights and visibility in these years that reshaped American life, and the backlash those gains provoked became a lasting feature of the politics that followed. The gains and the backlash are the same history, and the questions they raised about who belongs to the nation are the question the book keeps asking.[^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-usmarshals-wounded-knee][^ch42-justia-roe]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1968 and 1974 the United States ended one war and unmade one presidency, widened rights for many of its people while assembling a coalition against the movements that had won them, and discovered that neither the politics of order nor the promise of liberation could hold the center together. The chapter’s question — how did demands for liberation coexist with a politics of order? — is answered by a decade in which both were real, both were contested, and neither could claim the whole nation.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nara-watergate]
