## Epigraphs

— *Charleston Mercury*, November 3, 1860, quoted by the National Park Service, “South Carolina Secession.”[^ch20-nps-sc-secession]

> “In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war. The Government will not assail you. You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors.”[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

— Abraham Lincoln, First Inaugural Address, Washington, D.C., March 4, 1861, quoted in the National Park Service’s Fort Sumter historical handbook.[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-check-epigraphs]

A historian said the South did not rush headlong out of the Union at all. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The convention and the place.** The Democratic National Convention opened at Charleston, South Carolina, on April 23, 1860, in the South Carolina Institute Hall. The incumbent president, James Buchanan, was a Democrat who had no interest in a second term, and Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, the doctrine of popular sovereignty’s leading advocate, was the favorite.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]
- **The demand.** Deep South delegates arrived determined to have the party endorse a federal slave code for the territories. Alabama’s party had instructed its delegates in January 1860 to “insist” on a plank protecting slavery in the territories and to withdraw if it were rejected. In February, Mississippi senator Jefferson Davis introduced resolutions calling for a federal code, which the Senate Democratic caucus passed.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]
- **The platform fight.** The platform committee presented two reports: a majority report protecting slavery in the territories and a minority report endorsing popular sovereignty. The secessionist William Lowndes Yancey of Alabama spoke for the Southern position and invoked John Brown’s raid as evidence of the North’s intention to dominate the South. Douglas’s supporters had nearly passed their platform by the third day.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-essential-cwc-election]
- **The walkout.** On April 30, 1860, 50 Southern delegates left the convention in protest. The convention could not agree on a nominee and adjourned without one.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]
- **The sequel.** Delegates who had walked out met at Richmond on June 11 in an unsuccessful attempt to nominate a candidate. The Democratic convention reconvened at Baltimore on June 18 and nominated Douglas after anti-Douglas delegations withdrew; a separate convention of disgruntled delegates nominated John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky, a supporter of the federal slave code, for president.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]

### The scene’s argument

- **A party breaks, not a nation yet.** The Democratic Party had managed sectional conflict for a generation by postponing it. At Charleston it broke on a single question — whether the federal government should protect slavery in the territories — and the break produced two Democratic tickets. The scene should open on the platform fight and narrow to the walkout on April 30.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The stakes.** A divided Democracy made a Republican victory possible, and a Republican victory was the trigger the Lower South had promised to answer with disunion. The scene lets the collision stand: a party of Union trying to hold itself together by deciding how far to nationalize slavery.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did an election become a war?
- **The turn.** The walkout did not itself cause secession, but it made the Democratic Party incapable of offering a single national candidate who could defeat Lincoln and reassure the South at once.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-check-scope]
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from Charleston to Chicago, Baltimore, Montgomery, and Charleston Harbor.[^ch20-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The convention dates, the two platform reports, the ~50-delegate walkout on April 30, and the Baltimore nominations are documented. The hall’s appearance, the delegates’ private thoughts, and what any speaker said in the moment beyond the cited texts are not attested in the cited sources; do not stage them.[^ch20-check-scene][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **Do not relocate the scene to Chicago’s “Wigwam.”** The README corrects the draft’s apparent borrowing of the Chicago convention’s “Wigwam” setting for this opening. The Republican convention met at Chicago’s Wigwam on May 16–18, 1860; the Democratic convention met at Charleston’s Institute Hall in April. The two events and places must not be merged.[^ch20-check-scene][^ch20-aha-chronology]

## 1. Four tickets

### 1.1 The Democratic split: Charleston and Baltimore

- **The claim.** The Democratic Party broke in two over a federal slave code for the territories, and the split produced two sectional tickets that together could not stop Lincoln.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The demand and the walkout.** Deep South delegates demanded a platform plank protecting slavery in the territories; Alabama’s party had instructed its delegates to withdraw if it were rejected. Douglas’s supporters defeated the plank, and 50 Southern delegates walked out on April 30, 1860.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]
- **The two conventions.** Delegates who had walked out met at Richmond on June 11 without success. The main convention reconvened at Baltimore on June 18 and nominated Douglas with Herschel V. Johnson of Georgia; a rival Baltimore convention nominated Breckinridge with Joseph Lane of Oregon.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]
- **The consequence.** The Democrats entered the fall with two candidates who each claimed the party’s name. For the first time, the country’s oldest party could not present a national choice; the rupture made a Republican plurality in the electoral college more likely.[^ch20-harpweek-1860][^ch20-aha-chronology]

### 1.2 The Republicans at Chicago and the Constitutional Unionists at Baltimore

- **The claim.** The Republicans nominated a moderate who could hold the free states together, and a new Constitutional Union Party nominated a border-state conservative who refused to take a position on slavery in the territories.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]
- **Chicago.** The Republican convention opened at Chicago on May 16, 1860, with William H. Seward the early favorite. Lincoln, seen as a moderate with fewer enemies, was nominated on the third ballot. The platform opposed slavery’s extension into the territories and called for a higher tariff, federal internal improvements, and a homestead act.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]
- **Baltimore.** The Constitutional Union Party, made up mainly of conservative former Whigs and Know-Nothings, met at Baltimore on May 9, 1860, and nominated John Bell of Tennessee with Edward Everett. The delegates refused to adopt a platform and pledged themselves only to “the Union and the Constitution.”[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The consequence.** The four tickets mapped the sections: Lincoln for the free states, Douglas for northern Democrats, Breckinridge for the Lower South, and Bell for the border and Upper South. No ticket except Douglas’s even tried to compete nationally.[^ch20-harpweek-1860][^ch20-essential-cwc-election]

### 1.3 Table 1: the election of 1860

- **The claim.** Lincoln won a decisive electoral majority while receiving well under half the popular vote, because his support was concentrated in the free states and split among three opponents.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]
- **The result.** Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and 1,867,198 reported popular votes, 39.8 percent; Douglas won 12 electoral votes and 1,379,434 votes, 29.4 percent; Breckinridge won 72 electoral votes and 854,248 votes, 18.2 percent; and Bell won 39 electoral votes and 591,658 votes, 12.6 percent. The electoral college total was 303, with a majority of 152.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]
- **The geography.** Lincoln carried 17 of the 33 states and not one slave state. Douglas carried Missouri and part of New Jersey’s electors; Breckinridge carried most of the Lower South plus Delaware and Maryland; Bell carried the border and Upper South states of Virginia, Kentucky, and Tennessee.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860][^ch20-encva-election]

The American Presidency Project series gives 1,867,198 for Lincoln, while the AHA chronology gives 1,866,452; the chapter must state its source, note the variation, and not present a single figure as settled. The split electors of New Jersey and South Carolina’s legislative choice of electors must be shown in the table’s notes rather than hidden in a national total. ### 1.4 The meaning of a sectional victory

- **The claim.** Lincoln’s victory was a majority of the electoral college and a minority of the popular vote, won entirely in the free states — and the South read it as the capture of the federal government by an antislavery party.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-encva-election]
- **The campaign.** The Republicans staged theatrical “Wide-Awake” torchlight parades, most spectacularly in New York City on October 5, 1860. Douglas campaigned in person, telling a Norfolk crowd in August that Lincoln’s election would not justify secession and that the federal government had the right to use force to preserve the Union.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The warnings.** Southern leaders had said openly that a Republican victory would mean disunion. The election did not cause secession by itself; it supplied the event the Lower South’s secessionists had said they would treat as the point of no return.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-constitution-center-secession]
- **The consequence.** On November 6, 1860, the republic elected a president who had received no votes in the states where four million enslaved people lived. The question of whether those states would remain became the question of whether the Union could be held without war.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-check-race]

## 2. Lower South secession

### 2.1 South Carolina’s ordinance and declaration

- **The claim.** South Carolina seceded first, on December 20, 1860, and published a declaration of causes that named the North’s hostility to slavery as the reason.[^ch20-battlefields-secession-acts][^ch20-avalon-sc-declaration][^ch20-nps-sc-secession]
- **The convention and the vote.** The state’s secession convention opened at Columbia on December 17, 1860, and voted 169 to 0 on December 20 to dissolve the union between South Carolina and the other states.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-battlefields-secession-acts]
- **The declaration.** The convention adopted a “Declaration of the Immediate Causes which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina” on December 24, 1860, arguing that the free states had elected a president “hostile to slavery” and had nullified the Fugitive Slave Law.[^ch20-avalon-sc-declaration][^ch20-constitution-center-secession]
- **The consequence.** South Carolina’s action made disunion a fact and emboldened secessionists in the other Lower South states. Federal property in Charleston Harbor, including Fort Sumter, became the immediate point of confrontation.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sc-secession]

### 2.2 In Focus: Mississippi’s declaration of causes

- **The claim.** Mississippi’s declaration of causes, adopted January 9, 1861, states in plain language that secession was undertaken to protect slavery, and it must be read as the secessionists’ own public argument rather than through the later apologetics of “states’ rights.”[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-constitution-center-secession]
- **In Focus: the text.** **In Focus.** The box reads one document: “A Declaration of the Immediate Causes which Induce and Justify the Secession of the State of Mississippi from the Federal Union,” adopted January 9, 1861. It declares, “Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery,” which it calls “the greatest material interest of the world,” and warns that “a blow at slavery is a blow at commerce and civilization.” It describes the danger in property terms: Mississippians must choose between “submit[ting] to degradation” and “the loss of property worth four billions of money” or secession.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration]
- **How to read it.** The declaration is a catalog of grievances — the Ordinance of 1787, the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the resistance to slavery’s extension, the nullification of the Fugitive Slave Law, and the election of a president “hostile to slavery.” Its analytical power lies in what it does not say: it does not rest on a general theory of state sovereignty as an end in itself, but on the security of slavery as the state’s “greatest material interest.” The box should let the document’s own words, including its racial language, speak.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-constitution-center-secession]
- **The related declarations.** South Carolina, Georgia, and Texas also published declarations of causes; all four name slavery and the election of Lincoln. The box should read Mississippi as one example in a shared secessionist argument, not as a uniquely frank outlier that the others avoided.[^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-avalon-sc-declaration]
- **The text and its limits.** The quotations are transcribed from the Avalon Project text and must be collated against the printed convention journal. The transcript renders some dashes as double hyphens; house style normalizes them to em dashes, and the normalization must be noted. The declaration is a convention’s public argument, not a record of every secessionist’s private motive, and it cannot establish that all white Mississippians supported secession.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-check-focus]

### 2.3 The Lower South secedes

- **The claim.** Between January 9 and February 1, 1861, six more Lower South states followed South Carolina out of the Union, each through a convention and an ordinance of secession.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The sequence.** Mississippi seceded on January 9, Florida on January 10, Alabama on January 11, Georgia on January 19, Louisiana on January 26, and Texas on February 1, 1861. Georgia published a declaration of causes that accused the “non-slave-holding confederate States” of hostility to “African slavery.”[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-battlefields-declarations]
- **The pattern.** Secession proceeded through state conventions and votes, not a single plebiscite. The conventions’ declarations and the secession commissioners’ speeches (discussed in §2.5) made the protection of slavery and racial subordination the leading public argument.[^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-nytimes-dew-review]
- **The consequence.** By February 1, seven states had left the Union and controlled most federal property within their borders. The remaining slave states of the Upper South and border had not yet decided, and their decision would depend on events in Charleston Harbor.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]

### 2.4 The Confederate States of America at Montgomery

- **The claim.** The seceded states built a new nation at Montgomery, Alabama, with a constitution that protected slavery more explicitly than the United States Constitution had, and elected Jefferson Davis and Alexander H. Stephens to lead it.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-constitution-center-confederate-constitution]
- **The founding.** Delegates from the seven states opened their convention at Montgomery on February 4, 1861, adopted a provisional constitution on February 8, elected Davis provisional president and Stephens vice-president on February 9, and inaugurated Davis on February 18. A permanent constitution followed on March 11.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-davis-first-inaugural][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution]
- **The slavery clauses.** The Confederate Constitution required that no law deny or impair “the right of property in negro slaves,” barred any state from abolishing slavery, and guaranteed slaveholders the right to travel with enslaved people through the Confederacy. It retained the Three-Fifths Clause for representation.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-constitution-center-confederate-constitution]
- **The cornerstone.** In his Savannah speech of March 21, 1861, Stephens declared that the new government’s “corner-stone rests upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery — subordination to the superior race — is his natural and normal condition.” Davis’s inaugural the month before had cast secession as a defense of the consent of the governed and the states’ sovereignty.[^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-davis-first-inaugural]
- **The consequence.** The Confederate founding is the chapter’s clearest evidence for “slaveholding state-building”: the new nation wrote slavery’s protection into its fundamental law and described racial subordination as its foundation.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-check-race]

### 2.5 Federal property, the *Star of the West*, and the commissioners

- **The claim.** Secession immediately became a contest over federal property and a diplomatic offensive aimed at the still-undecided Upper South.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nytimes-dew-review]
- **The forts.** Major Robert Anderson moved his small garrison from Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter on December 26, 1860, which South Carolinians treated as a violation of the understanding with President Buchanan. On January 9, 1861, Southern guns fired on the *Star of the West* as it tried to resupply the fort; the ship withdrew.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- **The takeovers.** State forces seized federal forts, arsenals, and property across the Lower South. By the end of December 1860, Fort Sumter was the last federal property in Charleston Harbor still held by the United States.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The commissioners.** The seceded states sent commissioners into the Upper South and border states to argue for disunion. Their message, as Charles B. Dew reconstructed it, warned of “servile insurrection” and defended slavery as “ordained by God and sanctioned by humanity,” and the Confederacy as a republic whose “chief cornerstone” was “African inequality and subordination, and the equality of white men.”[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review]
- **The consequence.** The commissioners’ words and the state declarations supply the evidence for the chapter’s debate: the secessionists argued their cause in terms of slavery and race, not in the abstract language their descendants would later prefer.[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-check-race]

## 3. Failed compromise

### 3.1 Buchanan’s paralysis and the secession of the government

- **The claim.** President Buchanan condemned secession but denied that he had the power to coerce a state, so the federal government drifted while its own officials resigned and the seceded states took its property.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The message.** In his State of the Union message of December 4, 1860, Buchanan blamed the crisis on “the intemperate interference of the Northern people with the question of slavery,” condemned secession, and signaled that he would defend federal forts if attacked, pleasing neither side.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The resignations.** Secretary of the Treasury Howell Cobb of Georgia resigned on December 8, Secretary of State Lewis Cass of Michigan on December 12 over Buchanan’s refusal to reinforce Charleston’s forts, and Secretary of War John B. Floyd of Virginia on December 29 over Anderson’s move to Fort Sumter.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The consequence.** With the executive unwilling to act, the winter’s decisions fell to Congress — which also could not agree — and to the seceded states, which acted.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-senate-crittenden]

### 3.2 The Crittenden Compromise

- **The claim.** Senator John J. Crittenden of Kentucky proposed constitutional amendments restoring the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific, and the proposal failed because the Republicans would not extend slavery or abandon the election’s verdict.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-battlefields-crittenden]
- **The proposal.** Crittenden submitted his plan on December 18, 1860. Its central article would have prohibited slavery north of 36° 30′ and recognized and protected it south of that line; other articles barred Congress from abolishing slavery in places under its exclusive jurisdiction and in the District of Columbia.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-battlefields-crittenden]
- **The failure.** Referred to a special committee, the plan won support from some powerful senators and from members of the public who petitioned Congress, but Republicans such as Senator James Grimes of Iowa rejected it. It died in committee, and the Senate defeated it on January 16, 1861.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The consequence.** Crittenden’s plan would have reopened to slavery the territories the Republicans had just won an election to keep free. Its failure showed that the election’s result, not Senate maneuvering, was the boundary of what the North would concede.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-check-ch19]

### 3.3 The Washington Peace Conference and the Corwin amendment

- **The claim.** The last winter compromise efforts, a national peace conference and a proposed constitutional amendment protecting slavery in the states where it existed, failed to halt secession.[^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-capital-corwin][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The conference.** On Virginia’s call, delegations from 21 states gathered in Washington beginning February 4, 1861, with former president John Tyler among them. The conference proposed a set of constitutional amendments on February 27, including the 36° 30′ line and a bar on acquiring territory without slave-state consent; Congress took no action.[^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The amendment.** In a separate effort, Representative Thomas Corwin of Ohio proposed an amendment barring Congress from interfering with slavery in any state. The House passed it on February 28, 1861, with President-elect Lincoln’s support; it was ratified by only a few states and never took effect.[^ch20-capital-corwin][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The consequence.** Even the one antislavery-party concession that could pass, a guarantee of slavery where it already existed, addressed the wrong grievance. The secessionists sought new guarantees for slavery’s expansion and protection, which the North would not give.[^ch20-capital-corwin][^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-check-scope]

### 3.4 Why compromise failed

- **The claim.** The winter compromises failed because they tried to settle by amendment what the election had settled by votes: whether slavery could expand into the territories. The Republicans would not extend slavery; the secessionists would not accept its containment.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-check-ch19]
- **The strongest case for compromise.** A union saved on Crittenden’s or the peace conference’s terms might have preserved the peace for a time, as the settlements of 1820 and 1850 had; their advocates argued that war would destroy the institution they meant to protect and the Union they valued.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-avalon-peace-conference]
- **The strongest case against.** The Republicans had just won an election on the promise to keep slavery out of the territories, and to concede the point would have been to surrender the election’s meaning and to admit that the secessionists could veto a lawful result by threatening to leave. Lincoln made his position clear in private and, later, at Sumter: he would not trade away the election’s outcome.[^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **The consequence.** By the time Lincoln was inaugurated on March 4, 1861, seven states had seceded and armed themselves, and the only remaining question was what would happen at the two federal forts still in Southern waters: Sumter and Pickens.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

## 4. Sumter and the Upper South

### 4.1 Lincoln’s inauguration and the Sumter decision

- **The claim.** Lincoln’s inaugural offered the South no new provocation but refused to recognize secession or surrender federal property, and his decision to resupply Fort Sumter rather than abandon it forced the Confederacy to choose between accepting a federal presence and firing first.[^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- **The inaugural.** On March 4, 1861, Lincoln said he had “no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists,” but he declared secession illegal and the Union perpetual. He promised to “hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government,” and he told the seceded states, “In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war.”[^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- **The decision.** On March 5, 1861, Lincoln learned from Major Anderson that Fort Sumter must be re-supplied or abandoned within weeks. General Winfield Scott and most of the cabinet counseled evacuation, but Lincoln concluded that surrendering the fort would mean conceding federal sovereignty. On March 29 he ordered a relief expedition; on April 4 he sent word to Anderson, and on April 6 he informed South Carolina’s governor, Francis Pickens, that the government would send provisions and no troops if the ships were allowed to land.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- **The consequence.** Lincoln’s choice shifted the decision to Jefferson Davis and the Confederate government at Montgomery, who could allow the resupply and accept a continuing federal presence or fire on the fort and begin the war.[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

### 4.2 The bombardment of Fort Sumter

- **The claim.** On April 12–13, 1861, Confederate batteries under General P. G. T. Beauregard bombarded Fort Sumter, and Major Anderson surrendered after 34 hours of fighting.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- **The opening.** On April 10, Confederate Secretary of War LeRoy Walker authorized Beauregard to use force if the federal government tried to resupply the fort. On April 11 Anderson refused a demand to surrender; a final demand came in the early morning hours of April 12, and the bombardment began at 4:30 a.m.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]
- **The surrender.** The fort was unable to reply effectively. Anderson surrendered at 2:30 p.m. on April 13 and evacuated the garrison the next day.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]
- **The consequence.** The bombardment was the opening engagement of the Civil War. It gave Lincoln the provocation he needed to treat the secessionists as the aggressors, and it forced the Upper South and border states to choose.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-app-proclamation-80]

### 4.3 The call for volunteers and the Upper South

- **The claim.** Lincoln’s call for 75,000 militia on April 15, 1861, turned the secession crisis into a war effort and drove four more Upper South states out of the Union.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]
- **The proclamation.** Lincoln called forth “the militia of the several States of the Union to the aggregate number of 75,000 in order to suppress said combinations and to cause the laws to be duly executed,” named the seven seceded states, and said the first service of the forces would probably be to “repossess the forts, places, and property which have been seized from the Union.” He also summoned Congress to meet on July 4, 1861.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80]
- **The Upper South responds.** Virginia’s convention voted 88 to 55 for secession on April 17, 1861, and a popular referendum ratified it on May 23 by 132,201 to 37,451. Arkansas seceded on May 6, North Carolina on May 20, and Tennessee on June 8. The four western counties of Virginia began a separate movement that would become West Virginia.[^ch20-encva-virginia-ordinance][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]
- **The consequence.** Lincoln’s call forced the Upper South to choose between furnishing troops against the Lower South and joining it, and most of the Upper South chose secession. The Confederate capital moved to Richmond, and the war’s geography took shape.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-check-ch21]

### 4.4 The border states and the shape of the coming war

- **The claim.** Four slave states — Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri — remained in the Union, and holding them was central to any Union strategy.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-check-ch21]
- **The border.** Maryland’s position around Washington, D.C., made it decisive; Kentucky and Missouri controlled the Ohio and Mississippi valleys. Their retention was never guaranteed and required both political and military effort.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]
- **The ownership.** This chapter stops at the first mobilization; the campaigns, the border-state fighting, and the transformation of the war’s aims belong to Chapter 21. The chapter should state the boundary explicitly rather than beginning the military narrative.[^ch20-check-ch21][^ch20-check-scope]

### 5.2 The first war aims

- **The claim.** Lincoln’s first war aim was to suppress a rebellion and restore the Union, not to end slavery; the Confederacy’s aim was independence and the protection of slavery.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]
- **The Union.** Lincoln’s April 15 proclamation framed the conflict as the suppression of unlawful “combinations” obstructing federal law and the repossession of seized property; it promised to “avoid any devastation” and interfere as little as possible with peaceful citizens. Preserving the Union, not emancipation, was its stated object.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80]
- **The Confederacy.** The Confederate Constitution protected slavery, and Stephens’s Cornerstone Speech made the new nation’s foundation the inequality of the races. For the Confederacy, the war was for independence and the security of the slaveholders’ republic.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]
- **The coming change.** The war’s aims were not fixed. Enslaved people began fleeing to Union lines, and the question of emancipation would transform the conflict within two years. That transformation belongs to Chapter 21, but this chapter should note its opening conditions.[^ch20-check-ch21][^ch20-upenn-mccurry]

### 5.3 The measure: asymmetry and its limits

- **The claim.** The Union’s advantages in population, industry, and railroads did not determine the war’s length, because the Confederacy fought on the defensive, expected European help from cotton, and began with better military leadership.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy]
- **The Union’s advantage.** The North could field and supply far larger armies and replace losses more readily; its factories, banks, and rail network gave it a margin that grew with the war.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy]
- **The Confederacy’s hopes.** On the eve of the war, cotton prices were at an all-time high, and Confederate leaders were confident that Britain and France, dependent on Southern cotton, would intervene or at least recognize the new nation.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy]
- **The hidden weakness.** The Confederacy’s foundation rested on an enslaved population that it excluded from its politics and depended on for its economy. McCurry argues that the slaveholders built “a modern proslavery and antidemocratic state dedicated to the proposition that all men were not created equal,” and that the enslaved majority and white women would become political actors the Confederacy had not reckoned with.[^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-mccurry-book]
- **The consequence.** Table 2 measures power at the outset, not the outcome. The chapter should present the numbers as conditions, not as a prediction, and keep the enslaved people whose labor the economy rested on at the center of the accounting.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Secession was undertaken to protect slavery.** South Carolina, Mississippi, Georgia, and Texas published declarations of causes that named slavery and the election of Lincoln, and the secession commissioners preached the same message.[^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-nytimes-dew-review]
- **The election was sectional.** Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and no slave state; the three other tickets divided the vote in ways that made a Republican electoral majority possible.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]
- **The Confederate founding protected slavery.** The Confederate Constitution barred any state from abolishing slavery and barred laws impairing property in enslaved people, and Stephens called racial subordination its “cornerstone.”[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]
- **The sequence is documented.** Seven states seceded before Sumter and four after Lincoln’s call; the bombardment of April 12–13, 1861, was the first engagement.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]
- **The costs fell unequally.** The enslaved people whose labor produced the wealth of the slaveholding republic had no voice in the decision, and they and ordinary soldiers would bear the war’s heaviest costs. The chapter must keep that asymmetry central.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-check-race]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was secession a revolution, a counterrevolution, or slaveholding state-building?** The secessionists called it a revolution; it also functioned as a conservative counterrevolution to protect an unequal order; and it built a new slaveholders’ state. The chapter should weigh all three without collapsing them.[^ch20-nps-sc-secession][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution]
- **Was secession popular?** Lower South conventions moved quickly, but Freehling argues that disunion was contested and that the Upper South left only after Sumter. The chapter should distinguish the secessionists’ confident words from the evidence about support.[^ch20-nytimes-freehling-review][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]
- **What started the war: the election, the failed compromise, or Sumter?** Lincoln’s relief decision and Davis’s order to fire each shaped the timing. The chapter should present the choices of both governments, not only one.[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-aha-chronology]
- **Who acted?** Enslaved people, free Black Americans, and Unionists in the Upper South shaped events alongside the commissioners and cabinet officers. A story that reports only the politicians will misstate who decided what.[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-check-race]
- **Whose voices survive?** Conventions, ordinances, platforms, and speeches document the politicians; the enslaved majority left few direct records, and no enslaved person voted or sat in the conventions. The chapter must name that silence.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which secession and the coming of the war were contingent in timing but structured by slavery: the secessionists’ own words name slavery as the cause, while the contest over disunion in the Upper South and the sequence of choices at Sumter show that the outcome was not automatic.
- **The secessionists’ own argument.** The declarations of causes and the commissioners’ speeches make the slaveholders’ purpose explicit; the postwar “states’ rights” and “constitutional government” explanations were, as Dew writes, a way not “to come to grips with our own history at a time when honesty might have carried the day.”[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-battlefields-declarations]
- **The contingency and division position.** Freehling’s account of the aftermath of Lincoln’s election “overturns the conventional picture of a state rushing headlong into disunion,” stressing the Lower South’s lead and the Upper South’s hesitation. This restores contingency and internal division without denying slavery’s centrality.[^ch20-nytimes-freehling-review]
- **The state-building position.** McCurry treats the Confederacy as a proslavery, antidemocratic project whose exclusion of the enslaved majority and white women produced a wartime reckoning. It connects secession to the Confederate state and to the enslaved as political actors.[^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-mccurry-book]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The modern consensus holds slavery central while debating popular support and contingency. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not teams, and test each against the declarations, the ordinances, the votes, and the Sumter correspondence.[^ch20-jhu-foner-forum][^ch20-check-sources]

## Legacies

The election of 1860 did not make a war, but it made secession possible and secession made a war likely. A four-way race produced a president who received no votes in the slave states, and the Lower South answered with ordinances that cited slavery and the election as their cause. The winter’s compromises failed because they offered the South either less than it demanded or more than the North would give, and the Confederate attack on Fort Sumter turned a constitutional crisis into a shooting war.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

The Confederate republic wrote slavery into its founding law and called racial inequality its cornerstone. The war it began would destroy that republic, and the enslaved people whose labor it relied on would help decide the outcome. What began in 1860 as a question about the territories became, within two years, a question about whether the nation could endure half slave and half free at all.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-check-ch21]

The chapter’s lasting legacy is a question about legitimacy. A party of Union broke rather than accept a platform; a section left the Union rather than accept an election; a president-elect told the seceded states that the choice of war was theirs. The republic had decided that majorities governed, and a minority had decided it would not be governed against its central interest. The war settled the immediate question by force; the argument over the terms of the settlement is the subject of the chapters that follow.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-check-race]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The crisis was entangled with the wider Atlantic: the Confederacy expected British and French dependence on cotton to bring recognition, while the Union counted on Europe’s reluctance to back a slaveholders’ republic. The chapter should place the cotton economy’s diplomacy beside the domestic conflict without settling the outcome in advance.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy][^ch20-upenn-mccurry]
- **Archives and silences.** Platforms, conventions, ordinances, speeches, and correspondence document the politicians; the enslaved majority left few direct records, held no seats in the conventions, and cast no ballots. The chapter should name that silence without speaking for the silenced.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-check-race]

## Summary

- The Democratic National Convention at Charleston split in April 1860 over a federal slave code for the territories; 50 Southern delegates walked out, and the party ended with two tickets, Douglas’s and Breckinridge’s.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]
- The Republicans nominated Lincoln at Chicago in May, and the Constitutional Union Party nominated Bell at Baltimore in May; the four tickets mapped the sections.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- On November 6, 1860, Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and 1,867,198 reported popular votes, about 39.8 percent, with no slave state; Douglas won 12 electoral votes, Breckinridge 72, and Bell 39.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]
- South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, and six more Lower South states followed by February 1, 1861; their declarations of causes named slavery and the election of Lincoln.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-battlefields-declarations]
- Mississippi’s declaration of causes of January 9, 1861, stated that the state’s “position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery,” “the greatest material interest of the world.”[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration]
- The seceded states formed the Confederate States of America at Montgomery in February 1861, with a constitution that protected slavery and a leadership of Jefferson Davis and Alexander H. Stephens.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution]
- The Crittenden Compromise, the Washington Peace Conference, and the Corwin amendment all failed to stop secession; the Republicans would not extend slavery past the election’s verdict.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-capital-corwin]
- Lincoln’s inaugural refused to recognize secession; on March 29, 1861, he ordered a relief expedition to Fort Sumter, shifting the choice of war to the Confederacy.[^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- Confederate forces bombarded Fort Sumter on April 12–13, 1861; Major Anderson surrendered after 34 hours, and the Civil War had begun.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]
- Lincoln’s call for 75,000 militia on April 15, 1861, drove Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee out of the Union, while Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri stayed.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]
- The debate turns on whether secession was a revolution, a counterrevolution, slaveholding state-building, or a contingent political failure, and the honest answer holds slavery’s centrality together with the contingency of the choices at Charleston, Montgomery, and Sumter.[^ch20-check-tables][^ch20-jhu-foner-forum]

### Primary documents and contemporary accounts

> *A closing note.* The year between Charleston and Sumter is the record of a republic discovering that it could not decide by election what it had avoided deciding for a generation. A party broke rather than agree on whether to nationalize slavery’s protection; a section left rather than accept a lawful result; a president-elect refused to surrender a fort and thereby forced his opponents to choose between peace and the first shot. The secessionists said plainly what they were doing, and their own declarations are the chapter’s central evidence. The war that followed would settle the immediate question by force and leave the meaning of the settlement to be fought over again.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-app-proclamation-80][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at First Bull Run on July 21, 1861, where spectators rode out from Washington expecting a short and decided battle and a Confederate victory scattered the Union army back toward the capital. The chapter takes up the war’s campaigns, the enslaved people who fled to Union lines, and the transformation of a war for Union into a war against slavery.
