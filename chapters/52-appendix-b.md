## 1. How Appendix B Works

### 1.1 Purpose, numbering, and limits

- Appendix B supplies eight numbered reference tables. Each table may have lettered panels, but a panel is not a new table number. The stable display numbers are App. B, Table 1 through App. B, Table 8.
- The tables support comparisons that chapters make in passing: population scale, election returns, territorial cessions, war mobilization, immigration, slavery, economic benchmarks, and the New Deal landscape. They do not replace the direct sources cited in the chapters.
- Appendix B does not publish a ninth “Tables in the Chapters” crosswalk. That crosswalk remains a README production record, as the plan requires. The crosswalk is summarized in §10.1 for the editor, not for the reader.
- A table is finished only when it has a caption, a source line, definitions for every column, notes on breaks and missing data, and a textual reference from the appendix prose. A list of sources is not a table.
- The appendix is planned at 20 text pages. The eight tables and their roughly 254–260 data rows, together with the captions, column definitions, source lines, and break notes required by this section, are estimated at 18–21 pages at a compact reference setting; 20 pages is the working allowance, not a measured typeset result. The row caps below are chosen to fit that setting. If a table cannot be set legibly within the cap, tighten columns or cut the agreed fallback rows before cutting definitions or source lines.

### 1.2 Common conventions

- **Time.** Label every date as a census date, calendar year, fiscal year, or observation year. Colonial figures are annual estimates, not censuses. Election returns are election-year returns. Immigration admissions are federal fiscal years. War costs are for the spending period stated in the source.
- **Geography.** Use present-day U.S. boundaries unless a row says otherwise. For historical rows, give the boundaries and jurisdictions in force at the time. Do not compare a colonial “population of the colonies” with the 2020 resident population without saying that the geographic base changed.
- **Population.** Use resident population. Census counts are not adjusted for underenumeration unless the row says “adjusted.” Do not mix de jure and de facto populations, or state and national totals from different vintages.
- **Money.** Use nominal dollars of the stated year. Add constant-dollar figures only with the named index and base year. Do not combine war appropriations, outlays, veterans’ costs, and interest in one “cost” column.
- **Rates.** Give the numerator and denominator. Percentages are rounded to one decimal unless the source reports more precision. A change in percentage points is not a percent change.
- **Race and status.** Keep the historical categories and explain them. “White,” “Black,” “Negro,” “colored,” “free,” and “enslaved” do not mean the same thing in every source or decade. Do not use “Black” and “enslaved” interchangeably.
- **Missing values.** Use “n.a.” for not available, “—” for not applicable, and “0” for a true zero. Do not leave a data cell blank; a blank data cell invites a reader to infer zero.
- **Provisional values.** Mark a value the source labels preliminary or estimated with “(p)” and define the flag in the table note. Do not drop the flag until the source issues a final value.
- **Rounding and arithmetic.** Follow the source’s rounding. If a total is calculated, say so in the source line or a note. Do not force a column to add when the source’s components are independently rounded.
- **Source lines.** Give the responsible institution or author, the exact title, the series or table number, the observation years, the URL or DOI, and the access date. “Compiled for this volume” is acceptable only when the transformation is documented in a production note.
- **Rights.** Official statistical tables and treaty texts are generally public domain in the United States. Quoted captions, modern maps, and third-party photographs are not automatically public domain. Appendix B uses no images; if a table reproduces a third-party chart or facsimile, the register and rights review must be updated first.

### 1.3 Source hierarchy and verification status

- Official statistical series come first: the Census Bureau, the Office of Management and Budget, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of Defense, the Bureau of Land Management, and the National Archives.
- The core historical compendium for several tables is the Census Bureau’s 1975 Bicentennial edition of *Historical Statistics of the United States, Colonial Times to 1970*. It supplies the colonial population series, the long-run election returns, the slavery and finance series, and several communications series. Its chapter definitions are part of the evidence, not background.[^app-b-hsus-1975]
- Scholarly compilations and peer-reviewed articles come second. They are used for definitions, corrections, and contested estimates, not as substitutes for the official series when an official series exists.
- Secondary summaries and reference entries are leads. They may establish a date or a name, but they do not support a numerical claim in the finished table.
- “Consulted” means the source was opened and read for this outline. “Discovered” means the item was located but not read. This outline marks unread items and does not present their contents as verified.
- Citations in this outline are source targets as well as evidence records. A note may identify a table that was consulted for its definitions and series while leaving row-level verification for drafting. The limitations field distinguishes the two; no table value should be treated as checked merely because it appears in this outline.
- The values printed in this outline are benchmarks for drafting. They are not the final table values and must be rechecked against the cited locator before the table is called drafted.

The 2010 and 2020 urban shares are not directly comparable. The published 2010 share is 80.7 percent; the published 2020 share is 80.0 percent. Applying the 2020 minimum thresholds to 2010 data puts the 2010 share at 79.6 percent, so the apparent decline is a definitional artifact, not evidence of de-urbanization. The 2020 criteria raised the minimum threshold from 2,500 to 5,000 people or 2,000 housing units, shifted to housing-unit density, and stopped distinguishing urbanized areas from urban clusters. The urban counts are rounded to thousands; shares are calculated from the underlying counts.[^app-b-census-2022-urban-redefinition][^app-b-census-2022-urban-release][^app-b-census-2020-urban]

The 2020 national total is the 50 states and D.C.; Puerto Rico (3,285,874) and the four Island Areas (338,021) are outside it and are not added to the regional totals. Philadelphia consolidated with Philadelphia County in 1854. Brooklyn was a separate city ranked third in 1860 and was consolidated into New York City in 1898. The 2020 rows use incorporated-place boundaries; they are not urban areas, metropolitan areas, or census designated places. ### 8.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Public finance.** Use fiscal years, not calendar years. OMB Table 1.1 begins in 1789, Table 1.2 in 1930, and Table 7.1 in 1940. For 1789–1939, use the *Historical Statistics* series and note the administrative-budget basis. The percentage columns are n.a. before 1930; do not compute a pre-1930 ratio from incomplete GDP. Do not splice the series without a note.
- **Deficits and debt.** The deficit is receipts minus outlays. The gross federal debt is not the same as the deficit; it includes debt held by the public and by government accounts. Do not add the deficit to the debt.
- **Railroad mileage.** Miles operated is a stock at a point in time. Miles built is a flow. Do not sum annual mileage to get a total. The column ends in 1970; do not fill the 2000 or 2024 cells from a different series.
- **Steel.** Steel ingots and castings are a primary product, not finished steel. Do not compare with a finished-steel series without a note. The column begins in 1887 and ends in 1960; the 1830, 1860, 1970, 2000, and 2024 cells are n.a.
- **Unemployment.** The cells use the Darby–Weir civilian rate (Ba475) for 1900, 1930, and 1940 and BLS LNS14000000 for 1950–2024. The Lebergott alternative is in the Panel B note. ### 8.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Early public finance is not modern accounting.** The 1789–1915 figures are based on warrants issued; trust funds and the unified budget change the totals after 1934. A long series that ignores these breaks will mislead.[^app-b-hsus-y335-342]
- **Railroad mileage peaks and declines.** The series is useful for the 19th-century build-out, not as a measure of transport output. Traffic, ton-miles, and revenue are different series. The series ends in 1970; the 2000 and 2024 cells are n.a.
- **Steel is cyclical.** A single benchmark year can misstate capacity or decline. Use several years or label the cycle. The series ends in 1960; the 1970, 2000, and 2024 cells are n.a.
- **Unemployment before 1940 is reconstructed.** The 1930s rate is one of the most contested numbers in U.S. economic history. The table uses Darby–Weir in the cells and prints Lebergott in the note; it does not average. The choice changes the 1940 level by 5.1 points and moves the peak from 1933 (Lebergott, about 25 percent) to 1932 (Darby–Weir, 22.9 percent).
- **CPI is not a universal deflator.** Deflating war costs, slave prices, or GDP with the CPI is approximate. Use the deflator that matches the series where possible. E 135 and CPI-U use different baskets and bases; they cannot share a column.
- **GDP per capita before 1929 is a scholarly reconstruction.** Maddison is in 2011 international dollars and ends in 2022. BEA is in chained 2017 dollars. The 1900/1930 break is a change of basis, not a growth spurt.
- **Inequality measures are not interchangeable.** Top income shares, the Gini index, wealth shares, and consumption inequality can move in different directions. The table uses pre-tax national income and household Gini, not post-tax income or wealth. WID values are model-based and revised. Auten and Splinter produce lower top-1 percent levels and a flatter post-tax trend; the table does not use their series, but the chapters that discuss inequality must engage the disagreement.[^app-b-auten-splinter-2024]
- **Union density is not union power.** Density measures membership, not bargaining coverage, strikes, or political influence.
- **Newspaper and telephone data have different denominators and endpoints.** Circulation is copies; telephone is lines or households. Panel D ends at 2000 because the post-2000 newspaper and telephone measures are not comparable.
- **The table is not a regional employment series.** Cutting Panel E removed the only regional manufacturing comparison. Chapters 24–26 must source state-level manufacturing employment directly; the appendix does not support a 1860–2020 regional series.
- **The 2024 endpoint mixes vintages.** OMB’s FY 2024 value is an actual, WID is a model vintage, BEA is subject to revision, and the household Gini is a survey estimate. Do not treat the 2024 column as one final-vintage year.

### 9.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **TVA dates.** Construction start and completion are not the same as first power generation. Use the dates on the dam pages and label them. All nine dams in the 1936 plan were structurally complete by 1944; Kentucky Reservoir filled in 1945. Wilson Dam predates the plan and is not one of the nine.
- **TVA capacity.** Capacity is modern summer net dependable capacity after turbine upgrades, not 1940s nameplate. TVA’s own pages sometimes disagree: Watts Bar is 182 megawatts in the page prose and 196 in its Facts & Figures; Chickamauga is 119 and 142. Use the Facts & Figures value, label it modern, and put the discrepancy in the note.
- **CCC camp counts.** The 1,330 figure is June 29, 1933; the 2,650 figure is cumulative camps established by 1935; the 6,036 figure is cumulative recorded camp sites across the program. These are not the same measure. Panel B does not print a camp-count series.
- **CCC enrollees.** The official monthly series peaks at 505,782 in August 1935. The NPS “1936” height reads as fiscal year 1936, not an August 1936 count. The three million figure is cumulative enrollment; do not compare it with a monthly strength.
- **Land in farms.** Land in farms includes cropland, pasture, woodland, and wasteland. Cropland harvested is a subset. The 1940 and 1930 farm and land figures are April 1 inventory dates; 1935 is January 1. Cropland harvested refers to the prior crop year: 1939, 1934, and 1929. Do not treat the year labels as one observation date.
- **Dust Bowl counties.** The 120,859 to 97,606 figure is for the 20 Soil Conservation Service counties in Colorado, Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas, not all Great Plains counties. The decline is a population change, not a net migration count; births and deaths are part of the change.
- **Migration.** The 1935–1940 census asks where people lived five years earlier. It measures gross moves, not net migration. A state can gain migrants and still lose population if deaths and out-migration exceed births and in-migration. The 876,829 in-migrants and 211,963 out-migrants are gross interstate moves; their difference is +664,866 net interstate migration.
- **California.** The state’s 1930–1940 growth was +1,230,136. Net interstate migration was +664,866, or 9.6 percent of the 1940 population. The arithmetic residual, +565,270, is not net migration; it includes natural increase, net international migration, and coverage differences. Do not attribute all of California’s growth to the Dust Bowl.
- **Arithmetic check.** The count of Wilson, Norris, and the nine plan dams is 11; Table 8 prints seven and notes the other four. The CCC panel has five rows and no state rows. Land use has three national and three state rows; population has five. The total is 23 rows. California: 876,829 − 211,963 = 664,866; 1,230,136 − 664,866 = 565,270.

### 9.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **TVA is an agency history.** The TVA pages are useful for dates and capacity, but they are institutional accounts. They do not measure the distribution of benefits, the displacement of valley residents, or the environmental costs. The capacity figures are modern, not 1940s, and the wartime additions sit outside the 1936 nine.
- **CCC counts measure different things.** The NPS, NARA, PBS, and CCC Legacy figures use different dates and definitions. The NPS “1936” height reads as fiscal year 1936; the official monthly peak was August 1935. NARA’s 2,650 is cumulative, CCC Legacy’s 6,036 is cumulative sites, and the PBS state counts are a secondary reproduction.
- **CCC camps were segregated.** The program included Black and Native American enrollees, but most camps were segregated, and the Indian Division operated differently. A camp or enrollment count does not show who was served or under what conditions.[^app-b-nps-ccc]
- **Census of Agriculture data are farm-based.** They cover land in farms, not all land. They miss non-farm land, and they classify fallow and pasture differently across censuses. The 1940 and 1930 enumeration dates are April 1; 1935 is January 1; cropland harvested is a prior-year figure.
- **Dust Bowl migration is contested.** Long and Siu find that the popular “Okie” exodus is overstated: out-migration was only slightly higher than in the 1920s, farmers were less likely to move, and California was not a disproportionate destination. Their 20-county definition is not the whole Plains, and the 2018 article confirms the working paper’s totals.[^app-b-long-siu-2016]
- **The Dust Bowl was not only drought.** Mechanization, wheat prices, credit, and federal policy all mattered. A table of population and land use cannot establish causation.
- **The 1940 totals differ by universe.** Table 8 uses the continental total of 131,669,275 for 1940 and 122,775,046 for 1930. Do not substitute a 50-state retrospective total that adds Alaska and Hawaii, and do not mix it with Table 1’s 1950-and-later 50-state series.[^app-b-census-1940-pop]
- **TVA and CCC counts do not measure outcomes.** They measure projects and enrollees, not income, soil conservation, electrification, or long-run economic change.
