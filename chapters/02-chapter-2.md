## Epigraphs

> “There I found very many islands, filled with innumerable people, and I have taken possession of them all for their Highnesses, done by proclamation and with the royal standard unfurled, and no opposition was offered to me.”
>
> — Christopher Columbus, letter to Luis de Santángel, 1493, as printed in *Epistola Christofori Colom* (Rome, after April 29, 1493)[^ch02-columbus-letter-loc]

> “Into and among these gentle sheep … did creep the Spaniards, who no sooner had knowledge of these people than they became like fierce wolves and tigers and lions who have gone many days without food or nourishment.”
>
> — Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies*, written 1542, published 1552[^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]

> “Virgin soil epidemics are those in which the populations at risk have had no previous contact with the diseases that strike them and are therefore immunologically almost defenseless.”
>
> — Alfred W. Crosby, “Virgin Soil Epidemics as a Factor in the Aboriginal Depopulation in America,” 1976, p. 289[^ch02-crosby-virginsoil]

- **Why these three.** Columbus supplies the language of possession: islands full of people, claimed with a standard and a proclamation. Las Casas supplies the language of predation: the same encounter seen as a moral catastrophe. Crosby supplies the language of epidemiology: a biological condition that appears to explain the catastrophe without naming a conqueror. The epigraphs disagree about what kind of event contact was, and that disagreement is the chapter’s problem.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Columbus’s letter is a printed, promotional account, not a neutral report. Las Casas’s tract is a polemic written to move a king. Crosby’s definition is an analytical tool, not a verdict.
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that Columbus’s claim was lawful, that Las Casas’s numbers are accurate, or that “virgin soil” explains the mortality. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **Place.** L’Anse aux Meadows sits at the tip of Newfoundland’s Great Northern Peninsula, beside the shallow waters of Islands Bay, formerly Epaves Bay. The site is an open plain bordered by a high rocky ridge, with a narrow terrace, a bog, and a small brook. The sea and the Strait of Belle Isle lie beyond.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The buildings.** Excavation found eight timber-framed turf structures in four complexes. Two complexes contained a large hall flanked by a small hut; a third contained a large hall, a hut, and a small house; the fourth was an iron-smelting hut built into the brook bank. The halls were built in a distinctly Icelandic style of the late tenth and early eleventh centuries.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The people.** The total number of sleeping places suggests that the buildings could house between 70 and 90 people. The largest hall appears to have been the leader’s quarters; a small hut, possibly for enslaved or low-status workers, suggests social hierarchy. Spindle whorls and a bone pin indicate that women were present.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The date.** High-precision radiocarbon dating of three wooden items, all modified with metal tools, converges on the year 1021 for the felling of the trees. The date is the only secure calendar date for a European presence across the Atlantic before Columbus.[^ch02-lam-1021]
- **The economy.** The site was a base for exploration and resource extraction, not a colonizing settlement. There are no barns or livestock structures and no evidence of grazing. Butternuts and butternut wood, whose northern limit lies in eastern New Brunswick, show that the Norse traveled into the Gulf of St. Lawrence. The site’s occupants may have named the wider region Vinland for its grapes.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The duration.** The cultural deposits, the size of the middens, and the lack of rebuilding indicate a short occupation, about a decade. The Greenland colony at the time comprised only about 400 people; the 70–90 occupants of L’Anse aux Meadows formed a larger workforce than Greenland could spare for any other such enterprise.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The Indigenous context.** The site was occupied intermittently by five Indigenous groups before the Norse and one after them. It appears that no group was living at the site during the Norse occupation, but hearths and tent rings would have been visible, and people of the Cow Head tradition lived in the larger vicinity. The Norse built among the traces of older Indigenous camps.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The absence.** No written record from the Norse occupants survives at the site. The Vinland sagas describe voyages and encounters with Indigenous peoples, but they were written down centuries later, they conflict with one another, and their scenes have no clear connection to the excavated buildings. The site does not name a single person, record a single conversation, or preserve a single weather observation.[^ch02-lam-1021]

### The scene’s argument

- **A base, not a colony.** The eight buildings, the iron furnace, the boat repairs, and the butternuts describe a small, purposeful camp. They do not describe a permanent settlement, a farming community, or a colonial capital. The Norse landing at L’Anse aux Meadows was a working base that lasted about a decade.
- **The date is precise; the meaning is not.** The tree rings fix the year 1021. They do not explain why the Norse came, how they understood the people whose old camps they could see, or why they left. The scene should hold the precision and the silence together.
- **The land was already inhabited.** The Norse did not arrive on an empty shore. They arrived in a landscape that had been used for thousands of years. The scene should make that clear without inventing a meeting.
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: Why did one Atlantic encounter remain limited while another reorganized the hemisphere?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that the Norse were peaceful and the Spanish were cruel. The answer lies in scale, duration, purpose, follow-up, and the political and biological conditions of the two encounters. The chapter moves from a small excavated place to a hemispheric system.

### What must not be invented

- No imagined Norse dialogue, thought, prayer, clothing, weather, ceremony, or reaction. The scene may describe the excavated buildings, the bog, the brook, the sea, and the reconstructed buildings at a distance, but not an invented day.
- No claim that the Norse met Indigenous people at the site. The record shows that earlier camps were visible and that people lived nearby; it does not record an encounter at L’Anse aux Meadows.
- No claim that the Norse “discovered” an empty continent. The site’s earlier occupations are part of the evidence.
- No claim that the sagas are a transcript. They are later, conflicting, and shaped by oral tradition and literary convention.
- No claim that the site was a permanent village, a farming colony, or a failed kingdom. The archaeological evidence points to a short-lived base.
- No claim that disease spread from this site. The site has not yielded evidence of a sustained biological exchange, and the absence of evidence is not proof that no encounter occurred.
- No claim that the butternuts prove a specific route or a specific meeting. They prove travel to the south, not where or with whom.
- No claim that Indigenous resistance drove the Norse away. The evidence does not identify the reason for the site’s abandonment.[^ch02-check-scene]

Then turn: the people who built the camp left no written record, and the sagas that mention such voyages were written centuries later. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. ## 1. Norse and Iberian landfalls

### 1.1 The Norse base camp at L’Anse aux Meadows

- **The claim.** Norse voyagers reached Newfoundland by 1021 and built a small base camp. They did not establish a lasting colony, and their landing did not reorganize the hemisphere.
- **The site.** Eight turf buildings in four complexes, a forge, workshops, boat repair, and about 800 artifacts place the site within the Norse world of Greenland and Iceland. The buildings could house 70–90 people, and the occupation lasted about a decade.[^ch02-lam-unesco][^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The date.** The 1021 date comes from tree-ring matching of the cosmic-ray event of 993 CE in three wooden items. The wood was modified with metal tools, which Indigenous people of the region did not make at the time. The date is exact for the felling; it is not a date for the first or only Norse landing.[^ch02-lam-1021]
- **The purpose.** The site was a base for exploration and extraction: lumber, furs, and luxury food such as walnuts and grapes. The butternuts and butternut wood point to travel into the Gulf of St. Lawrence. There are no barns, no livestock structures, and no evidence of grazing. The location is exposed, and more sheltered coves are nearby.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The sagas.** The Vinland sagas describe Leif Eriksson, Þorfinnr Karlsefni, and encounters with Indigenous peoples. They were written down centuries after the events, they disagree about the number and duration of voyages, and their scenes have been conflated. They are evidence for how later Icelanders remembered the voyages, not a transcript of what happened at the site.[^ch02-lam-1021][^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The Indigenous context.** The site was used by five Indigenous groups before the Norse and one after them. Hearths and tent rings from earlier occupations would have been visible to the Norse. The record does not show a meeting at the site, and it does not show that the Norse and Indigenous people avoided one another.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- **The consequence.** The Norse landing was real, dated, and brief. It was a base camp within an Indigenous landscape, not a founding moment. The chapter should not call it a “discovery,” and it should not treat it as a prelude that makes Columbus inevitable.

### 1.2 Columbus and the Caribbean landfall

- **The claim.** Columbus’s 1492 landing began a sustained transatlantic system. It was not the first European presence in the Americas, but it was the first to produce permanent settlement, coerced labor, and a continuing exchange of people, plants, animals, and pathogens.
- **The landfall.** Columbus reached an island the Lucayan people called Guanahani and renamed San Salvador. The exact island remains uncertain. His letter describes “very many islands, filled with innumerable people,” and claims possession for the Spanish crown, “done by proclamation and with the royal standard unfurled, and no opposition was offered to me.”[^ch02-columbus-letter-loc][^ch02-columbus-settlement]
- **The Taíno world.** The Taíno were not a single unified people or polity. They were diverse Arawak-speaking communities with hierarchical religious, political, and social systems, skilled farmers and navigators, living across the Greater Antilles. The Spanish chronicles and the archaeological record show dense settlements, caciques, and regional networks.[^ch02-columbus-taino-loc][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]
- **The second voyage.** In 1493 Columbus returned with seventeen ships and about 1,200 men. He found the fort of La Navidad destroyed and its occupants dead. Near its ruins he founded La Isabela, a short-lived town on the north coast of Hispaniola. The second voyage turned a landing into a settlement project.[^ch02-columbus-settlement]
- **The labor order.** The settlement project demanded labor. The encomienda granted Spaniards the tribute and forced labor of Indigenous people in a defined territory. It became the legal and economic foundation of the early Caribbean colony.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]
- **The consequence.** The Spanish landing mattered because it was followed by ships, soldiers, settlers, priests, laws, and mines. The chapter should distinguish the event from the system that followed it.

### 1.3 What the first encounters were and were not

- **The claim.** “First contact” is a misleading phrase. There were many encounters, in different places, with different scales, purposes, and consequences.
- **The comparison.** The Norse built a small base, stayed about a decade, extracted resources, and left. The Spanish built towns, granted encomiendas, mined gold, converted souls, and stayed. The difference is not a difference in virtue; it is a difference in scale, duration, purpose, and follow-up.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia][^ch02-columbus-settlement]
- **Indigenous agency.** Indigenous people interpreted the newcomers, traded with them, allied with them, resisted them, and used them. They were not passive recipients of a European story. The Taíno caciques negotiated and fought; the Lucayans were enslaved and deported; some Nahua polities allied with Cortés while others fought him.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount][^ch02-dumbarton-fall]
- **The evidence limits.** The Norse record is archaeological and the sagas are late. The Spanish record is written by the conquerors and their critics. The Taíno left no written account. The chapter should name these asymmetries rather than pretend to a neutral view from nowhere.
- **The transition.** Once the Spanish established a settlement system, the Atlantic became a zone of competing voyages and claims. §2 turns to the treaties and routes that tried to divide it.

## 2. Voyages and the division of the Atlantic

### 2.1 Tordesillas and the papal grant

- **The claim.** The Treaty of Tordesillas divided the Atlantic on paper. It did not describe the Atlantic as it was, and it could not bind the people who already lived there.
- **The terms.** On June 7, 1494, Spain and Portugal agreed to a line running from pole to pole, 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. Lands east of the line would belong to Portugal; lands west to Spain. The line shifted the boundary established by the papal bull *Inter caetera* in 1493 and allowed Portugal to claim the route to India and the land that became Brazil.[^ch02-tordesillas-unesco][^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The purpose.** The treaty was a bilateral diplomatic agreement about discovery, navigation, and trade. It did not settle evangelization. It treated the Atlantic as a space to be allocated between two crowns.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The challenge.** France, England, and later the Dutch Republic rejected the papal grant and the treaty as a bilateral arrangement. They argued that the pope’s decisions were not binding on them and that the sea and the new lands were open to other crowns. The line became a source of dispute, not a settled border.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The consequence.** The treaty created a legal fiction of two monopolies. In practice, the Atlantic became a zone of smuggling, fishing, privateering, and competing settlements. The map of 1494 did not match the map of 1551.

### 2.2 Cabot, Cabral, and the routes

- **The claim.** The Atlantic crossing was not a single Spanish achievement. English and Portuguese voyages turned the ocean into a zone of competing claims.
- **Cabot.** John Cabot sailed from Bristol in May 1497 in the *Matthew*. He coasted Newfoundland and Labrador, possibly sighted Beothuk or Innu people, and reported that the waters teemed with cod. The voyage became the basis of England’s claim to North America, although the surviving accounts are second-hand and the landfall is debated.[^ch02-cabot]
- **Cabral.** Pedro Álvares Cabral left Lisbon in March 1500 with thirteen vessels and about 1,200 men, bound for India. He made landfall on the coast of present-day Bahia, erected a cross, and claimed the land for Portugal. Whether the landfall was accidental or informed by earlier knowledge is debated; the Portuguese crown soon treated Brazil as part of its domain.[^ch02-cabral][^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The fishery.** Cabot’s report of cod was as important as his claim. The Grand Banks fishery became a major attraction for European ships and laid the foundation for the northern Atlantic fishing economy.[^ch02-cabot]
- **The consequence.** By 1500, at least three crowns had claims in the Atlantic, and the Portuguese had a route around Africa and a landfall in South America. The Spanish Caribbean was one part of a wider competition.

### 2.3 Magellan and the global frame

- **The claim.** Magellan’s expedition turned the Atlantic division into a global problem. The first circumnavigation showed that a western route to Asia lay across a vast ocean, that the anti-meridian could not be fixed by the Tordesillas line alone, and that the Americas were not the edge of Asia.
- **The voyage.** Five ships and about 270 men left Sanlúcar de Barrameda in September 1519. Magellan passed through the strait that bears his name, crossed the Pacific, reached the Philippines, and was killed at Mactan in April 1521. Juan Sebastián Elcano brought the *Victoria* back to Spain in September 1522 with eighteen survivors.[^ch02-magellan]
- **The political consequence.** Spain and Portugal disputed the Moluccas because the anti-meridian of Tordesillas could not be determined. The Treaty of Saragossa in 1529 set a new line and recognized the Moluccas as Portuguese. The global division was an ongoing negotiation, not a single settlement.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The consequence for the chapter.** The Atlantic was not a closed Spanish lake. It was one theater in a global system of trade, conquest, and competing legal claims. The chapter should keep the global frame visible without losing the Caribbean center.

### 2.4 The limits of the line

- **The claim.** The Tordesillas line was a European claim, not a description of political reality. It could not be fixed precisely, it ignored Indigenous sovereignty, and it was rejected by other European powers.
- **The technical problem.** The treaty specified a distance west of Cape Verde but did not settle how to measure it or where the corresponding anti-meridian fell. The dispute over the Moluccas exposed the problem. The line was a map convention, not a surveyed boundary.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The political problem.** Indigenous polities did not recognize the line, and their lands, alliances, and trade routes crossed it. The Spanish and Portuguese crowns could claim a monopoly, but they could not enforce one against Indigenous people or other Europeans without war, missionization, and settlement.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- **The consequence.** The division of the Atlantic was a project, not a fact. The chapter should use Map 2 to show the claims and the routes, and it should distinguish documented voyages from uncertain reconstructions.

## 3. Biological and material exchange

### 3.1 The exchange framework

- **The claim.** The Columbian exchange was a package of pathogens, plants, animals, and people. It moved in both directions, and it transformed both sides of the Atlantic.
- **The term.** Alfred W. Crosby’s *The Columbian Exchange* (1972) gave the process its name. Crosby divided the exchange into diseases, animals, and plants. The term is useful as a shorthand, but it can obscure timing, region, and human agency.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- **The crops.** New World crops included maize, white potatoes, sweet potatoes, manioc, peanuts, tomatoes, squash, pineapple, papaya, and avocado. Old World crops included rice, wheat, barley, oats, rye, turnips, onions, cabbage, lettuce, peaches, pears, and sugar. The lists were sharply different, with cotton as a possible exception.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- **The animals.** The Americas had dogs, llamas, guinea pigs, and a few species of fowl. The Old World brought horses, donkeys, pigs, cattle, goats, sheep, and barnyard fowl. The difference in domesticated animals was even sharper than the difference in crops.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- **The consequence.** The exchange reshaped diets, landscapes, labor, and disease environments. It was not a one-way transfer of European superiority; it was a biological and political reorganization of two hemispheres.

### 3.2 Pathogens and the first epidemics

- **The claim.** Old World pathogens caused catastrophic mortality among Indigenous peoples, but the timing, scale, and causes varied by region. “Virgin soil” names a biological condition, not a complete explanation.
- **The diseases.** Smallpox, measles, influenza, malaria, yellow fever, cholera, typhoid, and bubonic plague were among the infections brought from the Old World. Indigenous communities had no prior exposure to many of them. In populations not previously exposed, smallpox could infect almost everyone and kill 30 to 100 percent of the infected, depending on the strain and the conditions.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox]
- **The Caribbean.** By 1518–19, smallpox was spreading through the Caribbean and toward Yucatan. The precise route and the first carrier are not documented. The disease reached Tenochtitlan in 1520, during the Spanish war against the Mexica, and contributed to the city’s fall in 1521.[^ch02-nlm-1518][^ch02-dumbarton-fall][^ch02-check-1518]
- **The scale.** Current estimates suggest a population loss of five to eight million people in Mesoamerica during the 1520 outbreak. Some historians estimate that the Indigenous population of the Americas fell by as much as 90 percent by the end of the sixteenth century; others argue for a figure as high as 95 percent in the first century. These are estimates, not counts, and they do not agree.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox][^ch02-jones-virginsoils]
- **The social conditions.** Mortality was worsened by warfare, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and the collapse of care. People too sick to move could not feed themselves or care for others; famine followed epidemic. The outcome varied with the conditions the colonizers imposed.[^ch02-dumbarton-century][^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The recovery.** Some populations declined for a century and then began to recover. Some, such as the Lucayans of the Bahamas, disappeared as distinct communities. Others, such as the Navajo, grew after contact. The variation is the evidence that a single “virgin soil” story cannot explain the whole hemisphere.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils][^ch02-newson-demographic]

**In Focus.** The *Florentine Codex* is a detailed Nahua account of the 1520 epidemic. Book 12, folio 53r, remembers a sickness of pustules that “began in Tepeilhuitl,” covered people with agonizing sores from head to foot, and left the sick unable to move. “A great many died from this plague, and many others died of hunger. They could not get up to search for food, and everyone else was too sick to care for them, so they starved to death in their beds.” The codex was not written in 1520. It was compiled in the 1570s, under the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún, with Nahua elders and students at the College of Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco; production ended in 1577. It is a collaboration shaped by a missionary project, written in Nahuatl and translated into Spanish, and illustrated by Nahua artists. It establishes that Nahua people remembered the epidemic as a social catastrophe as well as a bodily one: the failure of care, the hunger, and the isolation. It does not establish a clinical diagnosis, an exact death count, or the experience of every community. The modern identification of the disease as smallpox is a retrospective judgment, built from the Nahua terms *hueyzahuatl* and *totomonaliztli* and from the later history of the pandemic. No reproduction of the folio is planned; the box reads the text, not an image.[^ch02-dumbarton-florentine][^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox]

### 3.3 Plants, animals, and labor

- **The claim.** The biological exchange was inseparable from labor and land. The movement of crops and animals was accompanied by the movement of people, the seizure of land, and the coercion of workers.
- **The animals.** Horses, cattle, pigs, and sheep transformed Indigenous and colonial economies. Horses changed travel, hunting, and warfare; cattle and pigs became new sources of food and property. The animals did not arrive as neutral tools; they arrived within a regime of settlement and property.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- **The plants.** Wheat, sugar, and rice became staples of colonial agriculture; maize, potatoes, and manioc became important foods in European, African, and Asian diets. The exchange changed the food base of the world, but the benefits were distributed unequally.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- **The labor.** Sugar, mining, and livestock required labor. The encomienda and the enslavement of Africans and Indigenous people supplied it. The biological exchange and the coercive labor regime were part of the same history.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery]
- **The consequence.** The “exchange” was not a fair trade. It was a reorganization of land, labor, and life, and its costs fell heavily on Indigenous and enslaved people.

### 3.4 What exchange explains and what it does not

- **The claim.** The exchange explains vulnerability and speed. It does not explain why some communities recovered, why others disappeared, or why mortality varied so widely.
- **What it explains.** Old World pathogens could spread quickly through populations with no prior exposure, and they could kill large numbers before any immune response developed. That is a real and important part of the story.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils]
- **What it does not explain.** The timing and scale of mortality depended on social conditions: war, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and the destruction of care. The same pathogen produced different outcomes in different places. A biological condition cannot by itself explain a political catastrophe.[^ch02-newson-demographic][^ch02-jones-virginsoils]
- **The transition.** The Caribbean is the first place where the exchange, the labor regime, and the conquest came together. §4 turns to Hispaniola and the moral debate it provoked.

## 4. The Caribbean and the Valladolid debate

### 4.1 Hispaniola: encomienda, gold, and death

- **The claim.** Hispaniola became the laboratory of Spanish conquest. The encomienda, gold mining, forced labor, African slavery, and epidemic disease produced a catastrophic decline in the Indigenous population and a durable colonial order.
- **The settlement.** Columbus founded La Isabela in 1493–94. The town was short-lived, but the Spanish presence on Hispaniola became permanent. The crown granted encomiendas: a Spaniard received the tribute and labor of the Indigenous people in a defined territory.[^ch02-columbus-settlement][^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]
- **The labor.** Encomenderos demanded tribute in crops or money and labor in mines, forts, towns, and plantations. The power was practically absolute; the records describe abuse and death. The crown issued regulations, but it did not abolish the system.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The Requerimiento.** In 1513 the crown issued the *Requerimiento*, a document to be read to Indigenous people before conquest. It demanded submission to the pope and the king; if they refused, the Spaniards would “make war against” them, enslave them, and seize their goods. The document was often read in Spanish to people who did not understand it.[^ch02-requerimiento-nlm]
- **The population.** One colonial estimate counted 60,000 Indigenous people on Hispaniola in 1508 and fewer than 20,000 ten years later. The numbers are not a census, but they show the direction and speed of the collapse. The causes included disease, forced labor, flight, and violence.[^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery][^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The African labor.** As the Indigenous population fell, the Spanish imported enslaved Africans. From 1501 to 1518 the trade came largely through Iberia; in 1518 Charles V authorized a charter for 4,000 Africans from the Cape Verde Islands. The racial labor system that would define the Atlantic world was already taking shape.[^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery]
- **The consequence.** Hispaniola was not a single tragedy of disease. It was a political and economic order that made disease, hunger, and overwork lethal.

### 4.2 The Taíno collapse and its sources

- **The claim.** The sources do not support a single Taíno population figure or a single story of extinction. “Taíno” is a broad name for diverse communities, and the collapse must be described with ranges, regional variation, and named silences.
- **The estimates.** Las Casas claimed that Hispaniola had held “three millions souls” and that only two hundred remained. Modern estimates vary widely: a survey table lists Caribbean estimates from 225,000 to more than six million in 1492, while other scholars have argued for several million on Hispaniola alone. The differences reflect different assumptions about household size, agriculture, and the reliability of colonial counts.[^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount][^ch02-newson-demographic][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]
- **The category.** “Taíno” is a convenient umbrella for Arawak-speaking communities of the Greater Antilles, not a single nation or a single language. The communities had caciques, regional alliances, and different histories. The chapter should use the name while acknowledging its limits.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-check-names]
- **The extinction claim.** By 1550 the Taíno were close to extinction in the colonial record, and many had died from disease and violence. But Taíno descendants and cultural traditions survived, and modern genetic studies show Indigenous ancestry in the Caribbean. “Extinct” is a colonial classification, not a biological verdict.[^ch02-columbus-taino-loc][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]
- **The silence.** No Taíno written account survives. The evidence comes from Spanish chronicles, encomienda records, archaeology, and descendant communities. The chapter should name the silence rather than ventriloquize a Taíno witness.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino]
- **The consequence.** The Taíno collapse was real, catastrophic, and uneven. It was not a single event, and it did not end the people.

### 4.3 Las Casas and the moral critique

- **The claim.** Bartolomé de las Casas made the most influential critique of Spanish conquest in the sixteenth century. His critique was powerful, eyewitness, and limited by his own history and by the politics of reform.
- **The biography.** Las Casas arrived in Hispaniola in 1502, became a land and slave owner, and was ordained a priest in 1510. After participating in the conquest of Cuba in 1513, he turned against the conquest. By 1515 he had renounced his encomienda, returned to Spain, and begun to petition the crown; he spent the rest of his life arguing for Indigenous rights.[^ch02-lascasas-origins]
- **The remedy.** In 1516 Las Casas proposed importing African slaves to relieve the Indigenous population. He later repudiated the proposal and condemned the African slave trade. The episode matters: it shows that a critic of one form of coercion could be entangled in another.[^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery][^ch02-lascasas-origins]
- **The text.** *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* was written in 1542 and published in 1552. The printed tract’s title page frames the question as a dispute over whether the conquests were “lawful” or “tyrannies, unjust and iniquitous.” The book describes the Spaniards as “fierce wolves and tigers and lions” and claims that Hispaniola’s three million people had been reduced to two hundred. The numbers are polemical, but the account is an eyewitness indictment of a system he had once served.[^ch02-gilder-valladolid][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]
- **The reform.** Las Casas’s lobbying contributed to the New Laws of 1542, which banned Indian slavery and regulated tribute and labor. The laws were widely resisted and unevenly enforced, but they marked a change in imperial policy.[^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The consequence.** Las Casas did not end conquest. He made its moral cost visible, and he helped create the language of Indigenous rights that later critics would use. The chapter should present him as a complicated reformer, not a saint.

### 4.4 Valladolid, 1550–51

- **The claim.** The Valladolid debate was a public argument about the legitimacy of conquest. It did not produce a verdict, and it did not stop the conquest, but it exposed the contradiction between evangelization and coercion.
- **The setting.** In April 1550 Charles V halted Spanish conquests in the Americas and convened a panel of theologians, officials, and administrators at the Colegio de San Gregorio in Valladolid. The debate opened on August 15, 1550.[^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]
- **Sepúlveda.** Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda argued from Aristotle that Indigenous peoples were “natural slaves,” incapable of self-government, and that war against them was a just means of Christianization. He claimed that they were as inferior to Spaniards as children to adults.[^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]
- **Las Casas.** Las Casas replied for five days. He argued that Indigenous peoples were not natural slaves and that the conquest was unjust. He went so far as to defend human sacrifice as a form of religious devotion, a move that shows how far his argument was from modern human rights language.[^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]
- **The outcome.** A second session followed in 1551. Both sides claimed victory, but the junta never issued a formal decision, and at least one judge did not deliver his opinion until 1557. Conquest and cruelty continued. In 1573 Philip II issued an ordinance that banned the word “conquest” and recommended “pacification,” a change in language rather than in power.[^ch02-kci-valladolid][^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]
- **The consequence.** Valladolid is important because it made the moral question public. It is not important because it settled the question. The chapter should resist the popular story that Las Casas won and that the empire reformed.

## 5. De Soto, Coronado, Cabeza de Vaca, and Cartier

### 5.1 Cabeza de Vaca and the survivor’s account

- **The claim.** Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca’s account of the Narváez expedition is a primary source, a literary construction, and a document that helped shape later conquest.
- **The expedition.** Cabeza de Vaca was treasurer of a 300-man royal expedition led by Pánfilo de Narváez. The expedition sailed into Tampa Bay in April 1528, marched overland, and attempted to reach Mexico in makeshift boats. Cabeza de Vaca and three others survived: Dorantes, Castillo, and Esteban, an enslaved North African. They reached a Spanish settlement on the Sinaloa River in 1536.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate]
- **The narrative.** The *Relación* describes his years as a captive, a trader, and a healer among Indigenous peoples of the Southwest. It is the first European account of many of those communities, and it is shaped by his own survival, his Christian frame, and his later political purposes.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate]
- **The route.** The route of the journey is disputed. Scholars have proposed at least five different reconstructions. The chapter should say “probably” and should not turn a reconstructed line into a fact.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate]
- **The consequence.** Cabeza de Vaca’s stories of wealthy cities to the north fed the expeditions of De Soto and Coronado. The survivor’s account became a guide for conquest, even as it described Indigenous worlds that the conquerors would destroy.

### 5.2 De Soto in the Southeast

- **The claim.** Hernando de Soto’s expedition was a mobile campaign of violence and extraction, not a settlement. It left no colony, but it destabilized Indigenous societies across the Southeast.
- **The expedition.** De Soto landed in Florida in 1539 with about 700 participants. He marched through the Southeast, fought at Mabila, and reached the Mississippi River in 1541. He died of fever in 1542, probably in Arkansas or Louisiana. About 311 of the original 700 survived.[^ch02-desoto-nps]
- **The violence.** At Mabila the Spaniards burned a fortified town and killed an estimated 2,000 to 6,000 Indigenous warriors; they lost about 200 dead and 150 wounded. The expedition took hostages and burned Mabila. The numbers come from the chronicles and are uncertain, but the pattern is clear.[^ch02-desoto-nps]
- **The route.** The route through Alabama and Mississippi is disputed. The chapter should present the campaign as a set of documented episodes and contested reconstructions, not a single line on a map.[^ch02-desoto-nps]
- **The consequence.** De Soto’s expedition did not find the wealth it sought. It did spread violence and disruption, and it may have spread disease. Its failure did not make it harmless.

### 5.3 Coronado in the Southwest

- **The claim.** Francisco Vázquez de Coronado’s expedition searched for wealth and converts. It found neither, but it transformed the Pueblo world through violence, demands, and the opening of a route.
- **The expedition.** Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza commissioned Coronado in January 1540. The expedition left Compostela in February with more than 300 Spanish soldiers, more than 1,200 Indigenous allies, and 1,500 stock animals. It reached Háwikuh, the first of the fabled Cities of Cibola, on July 7, 1540.[^ch02-coronado-nps]
- **The violence.** The Spaniards attacked Háwikuh when negotiations failed. They wintered at Tiguex, where violations of hospitality turned the Pueblo people hostile; the Spaniards killed the occupants of one pueblo and forced the abandonment of several others. They tortured and killed “The Turk,” the Plains guide who led them toward Quivira.[^ch02-coronado-nps]
- **The failure.** Coronado reached Quivira, in present-day Kansas, in 1541 and found no gold. He returned to Mexico City in the spring of 1542, discredited but not powerless.[^ch02-coronado-nps]
- **The consequence.** The expedition did not establish a colony. It did open a route for later Spanish missions and settlement, and it left a trail of violence and disruption. The Pueblo world would remember it.

### 5.4 Cartier in the St. Lawrence

- **The claim.** Jacques Cartier’s voyages were commercial and exploratory probes, not a conquest. They still used possession claims, kidnapping, and coercion, and they destabilized the St. Lawrence world.
- **The first voyage.** Cartier left Saint-Malo in April 1534 with two ships and 61 men. He reached Newfoundland, explored the Gulf of St. Lawrence, met Micmac people at Chaleur Bay, and erected a thirty-foot cross at Gaspé on July 24. Donnacona, the leader of Stadacona, objected to the cross; Cartier lied and called it an insignificant landmark. He took Donnacona’s sons, Domagaya and Taignoagny, to France.[^ch02-cartier-museum]
- **The second voyage.** Cartier returned in 1535 with three ships and a larger crew. He sailed up the St. Lawrence, established headquarters at Stadacona, and traveled to Hochelaga, the fortified village on the island of Montreal, where he arrived on October 2 and visited the village on October 3. Hochelaga had about fifty houses, a palisade, and cornfields.[^ch02-cartier-museum][^ch02-hochelaga]
- **The kidnapping.** In May 1536 Cartier seized about ten St. Lawrence Iroquoians, including Donnacona, and took them to France. Most of them died there. Cartier admitted Donnacona’s death but claimed the others had remained in France “where they were living as great lords.” The claim was a lie.[^ch02-cartier-museum]
- **The third voyage.** In 1541 Cartier returned with five ships and about 1,500 people, part of a colonization scheme under Jean-François de La Roque de Roberval. The colonists settled at Charlesbourg Royal, near present-day Quebec, but the venture failed. Cartier left the St. Lawrence in June 1542 and returned to Saint-Malo.[^ch02-cartier-museum]
- **The disappearance.** When Samuel de Champlain traveled to the region in 1603, the village of Hochelaga and its inhabitants had disappeared. The causes are uncertain: disease, wars with other Indigenous groups, and control of trade routes are all possible. The chapter should state the uncertainty, not choose a single cause.[^ch02-hochelaga]
- **The consequence.** France did not establish a permanent colony in the St. Lawrence in this period. Cartier’s voyages nevertheless opened a route, named a river, and left a record of Indigenous societies that would be transformed by the next century of contact.

### 5.5 The balance by 1551

- **The claim.** By 1551 the Spanish had built a durable imperial system in the Caribbean, Mexico, and Peru. The Norse, English, and French had not. Indigenous worlds had been devastated, but they had not disappeared.
- **The Spanish system.** Hispaniola, Cuba, Mexico, and Peru were governed through towns, encomiendas, mines, and imperial administration. Silver, gold, and forced labor connected the Americas to Europe. The system was contested and violent, but it endured.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The northern claims.** Cabot’s 1497 voyage gave England a claim, but no permanent English colony followed in this period. Cartier’s voyages gave France a claim and a route, but the 1541–42 settlement failed. The Norse base had been abandoned five centuries earlier.[^ch02-cabot][^ch02-cartier-museum]
- **The Indigenous world.** The Taíno of Hispaniola had been reduced to a remnant in the colonial records, but descendants survived. The St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages had disappeared from the valley by 1603. The Pueblo, Maya, Nahua, and many other peoples endured conquest, epidemic, and forced labor. The balance was not a simple story of European victory.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-hochelaga]
- **The transition.** By 1551 the moral and political questions raised by conquest had become public. Map 2, panel B, places these expeditions in the wider Atlantic competition. §6 turns to the historical debate over how to explain the demographic catastrophe.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The Norse reached Newfoundland.** The date 1021 is secure, and the site is a Norse base camp, not an Indigenous settlement or a later European village.[^ch02-lam-1021]
- **Columbus began a durable system.** The 1492 landing was not the first European presence, but it was followed by settlement, encomienda, mining, and a continuing transatlantic exchange.[^ch02-columbus-settlement]
- **Old World pathogens caused catastrophic mortality.** Smallpox, measles, influenza, and other diseases killed large numbers of Indigenous people who had no prior exposure.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox]
- **The mortality was regional and uneven.** Some communities disappeared; some declined and recovered; some grew. The variation is documented across the hemisphere.[^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **Coercion was central.** Violence, forced labor, hunger, and displacement shaped the catastrophe. Disease and conquest were not separate stories.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]
- **Valladolid did not settle the question.** The debate ended without a formal verdict, and conquest continued.[^ch02-kci-valladolid]

### 6.2 What is open

- **The first landfall.** The exact island of Columbus’s first landing remains uncertain. The sources name Guanahani and San Salvador, but the identification is disputed.[^ch02-columbus-settlement][^ch02-check-first-landfall]
- **The 1518 epidemic.** The precise route and first carrier of the smallpox that reached the Caribbean and Mexico are not documented. The chapter should not invent a single infected person.[^ch02-nlm-1518]
- **Pre-contact populations.** Estimates for Hispaniola and the hemisphere vary enormously. The differences reflect assumptions as much as evidence.[^ch02-newson-demographic][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]
- **The relative roles.** Historians disagree about how much of the mortality should be attributed to pathogen exposure, and how much to violence, hunger, forced labor, and social disruption. The evidence supports a combined explanation, not a single cause.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils]
- **Recovery.** Why some communities recovered and others did not is still debated. The answers vary by region, ecology, and colonial policy.[^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The St. Lawrence.** The disappearance of the St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages is unresolved. Disease, war, and trade are all plausible; none is proved.[^ch02-hochelaga]
- **The expeditions.** The routes of Cabeza de Vaca, De Soto, and Coronado are disputed. The numbers in the chronicles are estimates.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate][^ch02-desoto-nps][^ch02-coronado-nps][^ch02-check-routes]
- **The category.** “Taíno” is a broad label, and its use is contested. The chapter should explain the term rather than treat it as a nation.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The choice is not between a biological explanation and a political one. The evidence shows an interaction between novel pathogens and the conditions of conquest.
- **Crosby’s correction.** Crosby’s 1976 definition of virgin soil epidemics is a useful starting point, but Crosby himself emphasized malnutrition and the social chaos of colonization, not a simple genetic weakness. Later writers often reduced his argument to “no immunity,” which is a slogan, not an explanation.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils]
- **The critics.** David S. Jones argues that the specific contribution of genetic or developmental factors is probably unknowable, and that the fates of populations depended on their physical, economic, social, and political environments. Suzanne Alchon argues that Indigenous mortality rates were comparable to those of other populations exposed to new infections, and that colonialism, warfare, slavery, and forced migration explain why Indigenous populations failed to recover. Paul Kelton shows how the Indigenous slave trade spread disease in the Southeast. Andrés Reséndez recenters Indigenous enslavement in the colonial catastrophe.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils][^ch02-alchon-pest][^ch02-kelton-enslavement][^ch02-resendez-other]
- **The disease-centered synthesis.** Noble David Cook’s *Born to Die* remains a necessary counterweight to accounts that make pathogens incidental. Cook argues that epidemic disease was the principal agent of continental depopulation, while attending to regional variation. The chapter should present that position fairly and then explain why later historians found it insufficient by itself.[^ch02-cook-born]
- **The regional evidence.** Linda Newson’s survey shows considerable variation in depopulation and recovery. The Caribbean lost many communities entirely; Mexico and Central America recovered slowly in the seventeenth century; the Andes continued to decline. A single continental percentage cannot capture that pattern.[^ch02-newson-demographic]
- **The lesson.** The best history states its position, gives the strongest version of the rival, and shows what the evidence can bear. The chapter should not replace one myth with another. It should say that pathogens were necessary but not sufficient, that coercion was central, and that the dead were not merely biological victims.

## Legacies

The contact era is not a prologue that ends with the nation. It is the moment when the Atlantic world became a single system of ships, pathogens, crops, animals, and coerced labor. The chapter’s evidence matters to later chapters because it establishes that the land was already inhabited, that the labor regimes of the Americas were built in the sixteenth century, and that the catastrophe was both biological and political.

The “virgin soil” idea had consequences. If disease did the work, the conquerors can appear to be bystanders. But the evidence shows that mortality was worsened by violence, forced labor, hunger, and the destruction of care. The chapter should state that connection without turning the past into a modern morality play. It should also avoid the opposite error: treating disease as a minor factor because it complicates the story of conquest. Pathogens were real, and they killed.

The Taíno did not disappear. Their descendants live in the Caribbean, and their words, foods, and religious practices survive. The St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages did disappear from the valley, and the causes remain uncertain. These are not decorative details. They show that the outcomes of contact were different in different places, and that “extinction” is often a colonial classification rather than a biological fact.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-hochelaga]

The chapter’s legacies are therefore double. The Atlantic world was made by exchange, and that exchange created new possibilities. It was also made by conquest, and that conquest created durable inequalities. The chapter ends with the debate over how to explain the dead, not with a verdict about who deserved what.

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The Norse voyages crossed the North Atlantic; the Spanish crossed the Atlantic and the Pacific; Cartier crossed the Atlantic and entered the St. Lawrence. The book’s fifty-state frame is a political geography, not a cultural one. The chapter should say so once and then return to the Caribbean center.
- **Archives and silences.** The Taíno left no written account. The Norse sagas were written centuries later. The *Florentine Codex* was compiled decades after the epidemic it describes. The De Soto and Coronado chronicles were written by participants and partisans. The chapter should name these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch02-dumbarton-florentine][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 3 takes up Indigenous political worlds and the Two Row Wampum. Chapter 5 takes up the legal consolidation of racial slavery. Chapter 25 returns to conquest and the West; Chapter 49 returns to the recent reckoning; Chapter 50 weighs the book’s through-lines. This chapter’s job is to establish the Atlantic frame those later chapters presuppose.

- The Norse reached Newfoundland by 1021 and built a small base camp, but they did not establish a lasting colony.[^ch02-lam-1021][^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- Columbus’s 1492 landing began a durable transatlantic system of settlement, encomienda, mining, and exchange.[^ch02-columbus-settlement][^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]
- The Treaty of Tordesillas divided the Atlantic on paper, but other European powers and Indigenous polities did not accept the line.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- The Columbian exchange moved pathogens, plants, animals, and people in both directions and reshaped diets, landscapes, and labor.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- Old World pathogens caused catastrophic mortality, but the timing and scale varied by region and depended on social conditions.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox][^ch02-newson-demographic]
- Hispaniola became the laboratory of Spanish conquest: encomienda, gold, forced labor, African slavery, and epidemic disease.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery]
- Las Casas made a powerful critique of conquest, but he was a complicated reformer whose early proposal to import African slaves exposes the limits of his sympathy.[^ch02-lascasas-origins][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]
- The Valladolid debate of 1550–51 made the moral question public but produced no formal verdict, and conquest continued.[^ch02-kci-valladolid]
- De Soto, Coronado, Cabeza de Vaca, and Cartier carried Spanish and French power, trade, and captivity into the interior; the expeditions’ routes are disputed, and the Spanish expeditions’ violence is documented.[^ch02-desoto-nps][^ch02-coronado-nps][^ch02-cartier-museum]
- The debate over “virgin soil” is a debate about evidence, scale, and causation, not about whether Indigenous peoples were “close to nature.”[^ch02-jones-virginsoils][^ch02-alchon-pest]

## Further Reading

This is a selective guide, not a citation ledger. The groups move from primary voices and archaeological evidence to syntheses and specialized studies, then to the historiographical debate. Unless the annotation says otherwise, the works below were consulted for this outline and are cited in the combined notes. Alchon, Cook, Kelton, and Reséndez were consulted through abstracts or reviews; their entries say so.

### Primary voices and archaeological evidence

- **Christopher Columbus, *Epistola Christofori Colom* (1493).** The promotional letter that announced the first voyage and claimed possession of “very many islands.” Read it as a claim-making document, not a neutral report.
- **Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (1552).** The most influential indictment of Spanish conquest. Its numbers are polemical, but its eyewitness detail and moral force remain essential.
- **The *Florentine Codex*, Book 12 (1575–77).** The Nahua account of the conquest and the 1520 epidemic, compiled with Sahagún and Nahua scholars. Read it with attention to its later composition and missionary context.
- **Birgitta Wallace, “L’Anse aux Meadows,” *The Canadian Encyclopedia* (2006; updated 2025).** The clearest short synthesis of the excavation, the buildings, the butternuts, and the site’s brief occupation. It is an encyclopedia entry by a leading archaeologist, not a full site report.
- **National Park Service, “De Soto Expedition” and “Coronado National Memorial: Stories.”** Accessible summaries of the expeditions, their routes, and their violence. They are institutional syntheses, not primary chronicles.

### Surveys and syntheses

- **Alfred W. Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange* (1972; 30th anniversary ed. 2003).** The book that named the exchange and put diseases, plants, and animals at the center of Atlantic history. Read it with the later critiques of “virgin soil” in mind.
- **Linda A. Newson, “The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas, 1492–1650” (1993).** A concise, comparative survey of regional variation and recovery. It is the best short correction to a single hemispheric percentage.
- **David S. Jones, “Virgin Soils Revisited” (2003).** The strongest critique of the “no immunity” slogan and a careful review of the evidence for social and environmental causes. It is a synthetic article, not a new archival study.
- **Noble David Cook, *Born to Die* (1998).** A disease-centered account of the conquest that nonetheless attends to regional variation. It was consulted through abstracts and reviews for this outline, so its page locators must be checked before quotation.

### Specialized studies

- **Suzanne Austin Alchon, *A Pest in the Land* (2003).** Argues that Indigenous mortality rates were comparable to those of other populations exposed to new infections, and that colonialism explains the failure to recover. It was consulted through the publisher’s abstract for this outline.
- **Paul Kelton, *Epidemics and Enslavement* (2007).** Shows how the Indigenous slave trade spread disease in the Native Southeast. It is a regional study, not a hemispheric synthesis.
- **Andrés Reséndez, *The Other Slavery* (2016).** Recenters Indigenous enslavement in the colonial catastrophe. It was consulted through reviews for this outline; its argument should be read in full before the chapter is checked.
- **Michel Gagné, “Hochelaga,” *The Canadian Encyclopedia* (2013; updated 2026).** A careful account of Cartier’s visit, the village, and the disappearance of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians. It states the uncertainty rather than solving it.

### The debate

- **Alfred W. Crosby, “Virgin Soil Epidemics as a Factor in the Aboriginal Depopulation in America” (1976).** The article that defined the term. Read it alongside Jones’s critique and Crosby’s own later emphasis on malnutrition and social chaos.
- **David S. Jones, “Virgin Soils Revisited” (2003).** The best single introduction to the critique of immunological determinism. It is also a useful guide to the scientific literature.
- **Linda A. Newson, “The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas” (1993).** The best short demonstration that the catastrophe was regional, uneven, and sometimes followed by recovery.

> *A closing note.* The two records that anchor the chapter do not speak in the same voice. At L’Anse aux Meadows, the past is a set of low walls, a bog, a brook, and a date fixed by tree rings; the people who built the camp left no written account. In the *Florentine Codex*, the past is a Nahua memory of pustules, hunger, and the failure of care, compiled decades later under a Franciscan’s direction. One record is silent; the other is mediated. Together they show that contact was not a single event and that the evidence for it is never neutral. The chapter ends with the dead and with the debate over how to explain them, not with a verdict.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia][^ch02-dumbarton-florentine]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with the Two Row Wampum, a living diplomatic tradition whose association with the Dutch in 1613 must be assessed rather than assumed. The tradition belongs to Chapter 3, and its dating and documentary associations must be examined there rather than settled here. This chapter ends with the Atlantic world in 1551; Chapter 3 turns to the Indigenous political worlds that the newcomers did not erase.[^ch02-check-ch3]
