## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On August 15, 1914, the American steamship *Ancon* made the first official transit of the Panama Canal. The *Ancon* was a cargo and passenger vessel that had been acquired by the Panama Railroad Company to haul freight.[^ch30-smithsonian-ancon]
- **The scale of the work.** The canal ran roughly 50 miles across the isthmus and cut nearly 8,000 miles off the Cape Horn route between the Atlantic and Pacific. The United States had taken over the French excavation in 1904 and completed the canal ten years later.[^ch30-smithsonian-ancon][^ch30-state-panama-canal]
- **The larger context of the day.** Europe had gone to war two weeks earlier, and the canal opened into a world whose shipping and politics were being remade. The canal was announced as an American achievement, and it was reported as an immediate success with shippers.[^ch30-smithsonian-ancon][^ch30-check-dates]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the ship, the route, the engineering milestone, and the completion of a project the United States had acquired by treaty in 1903. It cannot show what the crew or the assembled spectators said, how the deck looked, or what any Panamanian on the shore thought, because no such account is cited here.[^ch30-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In August 1914 no one knew that the canal would become an irritant in United States–Panamanian relations, that the United States would cede the Canal Zone in the 1970s, or that the year’s European war would draw the United States in within three years. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch30-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **Rule without annexation.** The scene’s claim is that the canal was the clearest example of the chapter’s subject: a structure the United States controlled through a treaty that granted it sovereign rights over a strip of another country’s territory, without annexing that country. The legal name — a convention, a grant, a zone — did not conceal the effect.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla][^ch30-check-focus]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question. The same decade that produced the canal produced a Cuban protectorate, customs receiverships in the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua, and marine occupations in Haiti and the Dominican Republic. The canal was the most visible of a set of instruments by which the United States ruled without owning.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: what was empire without formal annexation?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the Canal Zone to Cuba, Santo Domingo, Port-au-Prince, Managua, Beijing, Veracruz, and the Atlantic sea lanes.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, deck, or dialogue.** The date, the ship, and the fact of the transit are documented. The scene may not stage the crowd, the ceremony, the weather, or any exchange the record does not contain.[^ch30-check-scene]
- **No interior thought.** The private reflections of the engineers, the crew, or Panamanian officials must not be staged beyond what documents state.[^ch30-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the coming American entry into the war, of the Canal Zone’s later political history, or of the phrase “informal empire.”[^ch30-check-scene][^ch30-check-dates]

## 1. Caribbean instruments of control

### 1.1 The problem: empire without annexation

- **The claim.** After 1898 the United States built power in the Caribbean and Central America through treaties, debts, customs houses, bases, and short occupations rather than through territorial annexation, so that the legal form of each arrangement — protectorate, receivership, lease, intervention — must be read for what it actually transferred.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-roosevelt-monroe][^ch30-state-dr]
- **The pattern.** The instruments differed by country but shared one feature: they gave the United States a durable right or presence without the responsibilities of sovereignty over the whole nation. Cuba became a protectorate; the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua accepted American control of customs; Haiti and the Dominican Republic were placed under military rule; the United States leased coaling and naval stations at Guantánamo and elsewhere.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the treaties, the funds, the officers, and the dates. It cannot show that the people of these countries consented or that the instruments produced the stability their authors promised.[^ch30-check-silences]

### 1.2 The Platt Amendment and the Cuban protectorate

- **The claim.** The Platt Amendment made Cuba independent in name and a protectorate in law, because it gave the United States a treaty right to intervene in Cuban affairs and a lease for a naval base.[^ch30-platt-amendment]
- **The instrument.** The amendment, drafted by Secretary of War Elihu Root and named for Senator Orville Platt, was attached to the Army Appropriations Act of March 2, 1901, and incorporated into the Cuban constitution and then into a permanent treaty approved May 22, 1903. Article III recorded Cuba’s consent that the United States “may exercise the right to intervene for the preservation of Cuban independence, the maintenance of a government adequate for the protection of life, property, and individual liberty.” Article VII provided for lands for coaling or naval stations, which became Guantánamo Bay.[^ch30-platt-amendment]
- **The use.** The Platt Amendment supplied the terms for American intervention in Cuba in 1906, 1912, 1917, and 1920, and was repealed in 1934 under the Good Neighbor policy, though the Guantánamo lease remained.[^ch30-platt-amendment]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the text and the interventions. It cannot establish that Cuban governments welcomed the intervention right, and the chapter should present the amendment as a limitation on Cuban sovereignty, not a grant of independence.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-check-silences]

### 1.3 The Roosevelt Corollary and the customs receivership

- **The claim.** Roosevelt’s 1904 corollary converted the Monroe Doctrine from a warning against European colonization into a claim of a United States “international police power,” which the administration then exercised through control of customs revenues.[^ch30-roosevelt-corollary][^ch30-state-roosevelt-monroe]
- **The doctrine.** In his annual message of December 6, 1904, Roosevelt argued that “chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society” might require “intervention by some civilized nation,” and that the United States might be forced “to the exercise of an international police power” in the hemisphere.[^ch30-roosevelt-corollary]
- **The practice.** Citing the corollary, the United States took over the administration of Dominican customs in 1905 to pay foreign creditors, a step formalized by treaty in 1907. After the assassination of President Ramón Cáceres in 1912, the United States sent 750 marines, cut off customs funds, and installed a president; in 1916 it established direct military rule, which lasted until 1924.[^ch30-state-dr]
- **The consequence.** The receivership made debt collection an instrument of governance, and it became a template for American financial control in Nicaragua and Haiti. The chapter should present the corollary as the legal frame and the receivership as the mechanism.[^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti]

### 1.4 Table 1 and Map 23: the instruments, 1901–1917

- **The claim.** Set side by side, the instruments reveal a graduated system of control in which annexation was only one form and usually not the one chosen.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti][^ch30-check-table1]
- **Cuba.** The Platt Amendment created a protectorate with a right of intervention and a leased naval base at Guantánamo Bay.[^ch30-platt-amendment]
- **The Dominican Republic.** A customs receivership from 1905, formalized by treaty in 1907, gave way to a military government from 1916 to 1924.[^ch30-state-dr]
- **Nicaragua.** Marines intervened in 1912 and kept a legation guard in Managua; the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty, signed August 5, 1914, gave the United States an option to build an interoceanic canal, a 99-year lease on the Corn Islands, and the right to a naval base on the Gulf of Fonseca.[^ch30-tah-nicaragua][^ch30-frus-bryan-chamorro]
- **Haiti.** Following the assassination of President Jean Vilbrun Guillaume Sam in July 1915, Wilson sent the Marines; the Haitian-American Treaty of 1915 created a gendarmerie controlled by American officers, gave the United States control of Haitian finances, and granted a right to intervene.[^ch30-state-haiti]
- **Panama.** The 1903 treaty granted a canal zone and, within it, the rights of a sovereign, and guaranteed Panamanian independence.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **The table.** The registered table `ch30-table-01` (Ch. 30, Table 1) belongs here. It gives each territory, its instrument and date, what the United States gained, and the legal form of the arrangement, with the observation period stated.[^ch30-check-table1]

## 2. Panama

### 2.1 The road to the 1903 treaty

- **The claim.** The Panamanian route and the 1903 treaty were products of a contested choice, a rejected Colombian treaty, and a revolution that the United States supported with warships — not of a settled plan.[^ch30-state-panama-canal]
- **The route question.** After the Senate voted on June 19, 1902, to build through Panama rather than Nicaragua, Secretary of State John Hay signed a treaty with Colombian Foreign Minister Tomás Herrán, but the financial terms were unacceptable to Colombia’s congress, which rejected the offer.[^ch30-state-panama-canal]
- **The revolution.** Roosevelt dispatched American warships to Panama City and Colón, Colombian troops could not cross the Darién Gap, and Panama declared independence on November 3, 1903. The new republic named the French engineer Philippe Bunau-Varilla as its envoy, and he negotiated the canal treaty.[^ch30-state-panama-canal]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the rejected treaty, the warships, the declaration, and the negotiation. It cannot show that the United States refrained from encouraging the revolution, and the chapter should present the events as an intervention that created the counterparty the treaty required.[^ch30-state-panama-canal][^ch30-check-silences]

### 2.2 What the treaty granted

- **The claim.** The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty combined a guarantee of Panamanian independence, a perpetual grant of the canal zone, and, within that zone, the rights of a sovereign — the clearest legal expression of the chapter’s subject.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **Article I.** “The United States guarantees and will maintain the independence of the Republic of Panama.”[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **Article II.** Panama granted the United States “in perpetuity the use, occupation and control” of a zone ten miles wide, five miles on each side of the canal route, excluding the cities of Panama and Colón, plus any lands needed for the canal.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **Article III.** Panama granted “all the rights, power and authority within the zone … which the United States would possess and exercise if it were the sovereign of the territory … to the entire exclusion of the exercise by the Republic of Panama of any such sovereign rights, power or authority.”[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **Other grants.** Later articles provided for sanitary control of Panama and Colón, a monopoly on interoceanic communication, naval and coaling stations, and the compensation of $10 million plus an annual $250,000 annuity beginning nine years after ratification.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the exact language and the payments. It cannot show that the Panamanian signatory represented a fully sovereign negotiation, and Bunau-Varilla signed before Panamanian commissioners reached Washington.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla][^ch30-check-silences]

### 2.3 The In Focus

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads Articles II and III of the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty as the legal centerpiece of empire without annexation. Article II granted the United States the “use, occupation and control” of a ten-mile-wide zone “in perpetuity,” and Article III granted within that zone “all the rights, power and authority … which the United States would possess and exercise if it were the sovereign … to the entire exclusion of the exercise by the Republic of Panama of any such sovereign rights, power or authority.” Read together, the two articles do what no annexation did: they transfer the substance of sovereignty over a defined territory while leaving the surrounding nation formally independent and under American guarantee. The box should quote the articles at their exact locators in the Avalon text and, if possible, collate them against the *Statutes at Large* or the treaty as printed by the Department of State. It should treat the articles as a legal instrument whose terms can be compared with the Platt Amendment’s intervention right and the customs receiverships, not as a stage for reconstructed action. The box should end on the question the chapter asks of every instrument: if a power holds the rights of a sovereign without the name, what does independence mean for the nation that surrounds it?[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla][^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-check-focus]

### 2.4 Building, opening, and the 1914 distinction

- **The claim.** The canal’s completion in 1914 must be kept distinct from its acquisition in 1903: the treaty is one event, the opening another, and the chapter should not let the engineering achievement obscure the terms of the grant.[^ch30-state-panama-canal][^ch30-check-dates]
- **The opening.** The *Ancon* made the first official transit on August 15, 1914, cutting nearly 8,000 miles off the Cape Horn route.[^ch30-smithsonian-ancon]
- **The cost in labor and lives.** The canal was built after the French attempt failed with the loss of roughly 20,000 lives; the American construction drew on a vast workforce whose experiences the chapter should name without inventing them.[^ch30-state-panama-canal][^ch30-check-silences]
- **The distinction.** The seed’s `plan-panama` correction stands: distinguish the 1903 treaty and intervention from the canal’s 1914 opening, and do not read later Panamanian grievance back into the opening scene.[^ch30-state-panama-canal][^ch30-check-scene][^ch30-check-dates]

## 3. The Open Door, Japan, and Pacific strategy

### 3.1 The Open Door notes

- **The claim.** The Open Door policy was a claim of equal commercial access to China that the United States asserted without the power to enforce it, which is why the chapter should present it as a diplomatic posture and not a settled arrangement.[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- **The notes.** Secretary of State John Hay sent the first Open Door note on September 6, 1899, to Britain, France, Russia, Germany, and Japan, proposing equal trading opportunity and that Chinese tariffs be collected by China. None of the powers openly opposed the note, and Hay declared their responses “final and definitive.”[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- **The limit.** The notes were non-binding and did not prevent any power from seeking Chinese territory; Hay himself briefly considered seizing Chinese territory before rejecting the idea. The policy expressed American interest in East Asia more than it constrained the other powers.[^ch30-state-hay-china]

### 3.2 The Boxer Rebellion and the second note

- **The claim.** The Boxer crisis of 1900 tested the Open Door, and Hay’s second circular sought to preserve China’s territorial and administrative integrity while foreign armies fought their way to Beijing.[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- **The crisis.** An anti-foreign movement, backed at points by the imperial court, attacked missionaries and Chinese Christians and besieged the foreign legations in Beijing. Foreign armies marched from the coast to rescue their nationals.[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- **The second note.** On July 3, 1900, Hay circulated a message urging respect for China’s “territorial and administrative integrity,” but the circular requested no formal agreement from the other powers.[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the notes and the crisis. It cannot show that the Open Door protected China, and the chapter should note the irony the record itself supplies: the United States proclaimed an open door in China while barring Chinese immigration at home.[^ch30-state-hay-china][^ch30-check-race]

### 3.3 Managing Japan: agreements and the fleet

- **The claim.** The United States managed rivalry with Japan through limited agreements and a display of naval power, because it could not defend its Pacific interests by force and would not concede them by treaty.[^ch30-state-japan]
- **The agreements.** Roosevelt mediated the Russo-Japanese War at Portsmouth in 1905; the secret Taft–Katsura Agreement of that year had the United States acknowledge Japanese rule over Korea and Japan recognize American control of the Philippines. After San Francisco segregated Japanese schoolchildren in 1906, the Gentlemen’s Agreement of 1907–08 had Japan restrict labor emigration while the United States pressed the city to withdraw the measure. The Root–Takahira Agreement of 1908 had Japan respect American possessions and the Open Door, and the United States recognize Japanese interests in Manchuria.[^ch30-state-japan]
- **The fleet.** On December 16, 1907, Roosevelt sent sixteen battleships of the Atlantic Fleet from Hampton Roads on a 43,000-mile, 14-month circumnavigation that included 20 port calls on six continents and returned on February 22, 1909.[^ch30-loc-gwf]
- **The later strain.** The California Alien Land Act of 1913 barred Japanese residents from owning or leasing land, and Japan’s Twenty-One Demands of 1915 and the Ishii–Lansing Agreement of 1917 continued the contest over China.[^ch30-state-japan]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the agreements, the fleet’s itinerary, and the laws. It cannot show that the agreements resolved the underlying racial and strategic conflict, which the chapter should present as managed rather than settled.[^ch30-state-japan][^ch30-check-race]

## 4. Mexico and Pershing

### 4.1 Revolution, Huerta, and non-recognition

- **The claim.** The Mexican Revolution created the conditions for the decade’s most direct American interventions, and Wilson’s refusal to recognize the government of Victoriano Huerta made the United States a party to a civil war it claimed only to observe.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **The revolution.** A rebel movement led by Francisco I. Madero overthrew Porfirio Díaz in 1911; Huerta arrested Madero in February 1913, forced his resignation, and was widely believed to have ordered his killing. Civil war followed between Huerta’s forces and supporters of Madero led by Venustiano Carranza and Francisco “Pancho” Villa.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **Non-recognition.** Wilson refused to recognize Huerta and imposed an arms embargo, then lifted it to help Carranza as Huerta gained, a decision with volatile consequences.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the coup, the civil war, and the American refusal. It cannot establish that Wilson’s preference among Mexican factions served Mexico’s self-determination, and the chapter should name that contradiction.[^ch30-nara-punitive][^ch30-check-silences]

### 4.2 Tampico and Veracruz, 1914

- **The claim.** The Tampico incident turned a small arrest into a military occupation of Veracruz, showing how quickly the search for “dignity” could become intervention.[^ch30-nara-punitive][^ch30-app-tampico]
- **The incident.** On April 9, 1914, sailors from the USS *Dolphin* were arrested at Tampico while loading supplies. They were quickly released, and Huerta apologized, but Admiral Henry T. Mayo demanded a twenty-one-gun salute that Huerta refused.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **The address.** Wilson asked Congress to approve the use of armed force, arguing that the incident was not isolated and that only the “fullest recognition of the rights and dignity of the United States” would satisfy him.[^ch30-app-tampico]
- **The occupation.** Learning that a German ship was bringing arms to Huerta, Wilson ordered the seizure of the Veracruz customhouse on April 21, 1914. American forces occupied the town by April 22 after street fighting; American losses were 17 killed and 61 wounded, and reported Mexican losses were roughly 152 to 172 killed and 195 to 250 wounded.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **The outcome.** Huerta resigned on July 15, 1914, and the Americans left Veracruz in November, but the occupation had driven a wedge between the United States and Mexican nationalists.[^ch30-nara-punitive]

### 4.3 Villa, Columbus, and the punitive expedition

- **The claim.** The punitive expedition of 1916–17 pursued Villa into Mexico, failed to capture him, and strained relations with the government the United States had recognized.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **The betrayal and the raid.** The United States recognized Carranza’s government on October 19, 1915, which Villa’s followers read as a betrayal. After Villa’s men killed seventeen Americans on a train at Santa Isabel, Villa attacked Columbus, New Mexico, on March 9, 1916, with about 485 men; eighteen Americans were reported killed.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **The expedition.** From March 16, 1916, to February 14, 1917, more than 14,000 regular troops under Brigadier General John J. Pershing operated in northern Mexico to capture Villa, while another 140,000 regular and National Guard troops patrolled the border. The force advanced some 400 miles into Mexico, experimented with motor transport and aircraft reconnaissance, and never caught Villa.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **The consequence.** The expedition was withdrawn in early 1917 as the United States turned toward the European war. The chapter should present it as a demonstration of the limits of American power on land in the hemisphere.[^ch30-nara-punitive][^ch30-check-dates]

## 5. Neutrality, submarines, and entry into the war

### 5.1 Proclaiming neutrality

- **The claim.** Wilson proclaimed neutrality at the outset of the European war and asked Americans to be impartial “in thought as well as in action,” a standard that proved impossible to sustain.[^ch30-miller-neutrality]
- **The message.** In a message of August 19, 1914, Wilson warned against “passionately taking sides,” said the United States must be neutral “in fact as well as in name,” and asked citizens to be “impartial in thought as well as in action.”[^ch30-miller-neutrality]
- **The difficulty.** Most Americans had European roots; the majority leaned toward the Allies, while German Americans and Irish Americans held other sympathies. Wilson’s own neutrality policy would be tested by the conduct of both sides.[^ch30-miller-neutrality][^ch30-ww1c-neutrality]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the proclamation and the sentiment. It cannot show that a genuinely even-handed neutrality was possible once the war’s economic and naval geography favored one side.[^ch30-ww1c-neutrality][^ch30-check-neutrality-case]

### 5.2 The economic tilt and the blockade

- **The claim.** Neutrality in law did not mean neutrality in trade, because the British blockade let the Allies buy far more American goods and borrow far more American money than the Central Powers could.[^ch30-ww1c-neutrality]
- **The tilt.** Because of the blockade, the Allies purchased far more American goods than the Central Powers, often with loans from American financial institutions, which gave American businesses and banks a stake in an Allied victory. The chapter should present this as a structural tilt, not evidence of a secret decision.[^ch30-ww1c-neutrality][^ch30-check-neutrality-case]
- **The strongest neutrality case.** The chapter must state the strongest case for staying out before explaining the decision for war. Senator Robert M. La Follette argued that Wilson’s policy had effectively favored Britain and France, which had access to the seas and American ports, while the United States overlooked British infringements — including the prevention of American trade with Germany — and highlighted German ones. He warned that entering the war would destroy democracy at home and asked whether the country should be hurled into “the bottomless pit of the European horror” for “commercial profits.”[^ch30-cfr-lafollette]
- **The filibuster.** In March 1917 La Follette and a small group of senators blocked the armed-ship bill in a filibuster, a maneuver Wilson denounced as the work of “a little group of willful men,” and the episode shows that the decision for war was contested to the end.[^ch30-cfr-lafollette]

### 5.3 From the *Lusitania* to the *Sussex* pledge

- **The claim.** German submarine warfare produced the recurring crisis of the neutrality years, and the deaths of Americans at sea changed the political climate without producing an immediate decision for war.[^ch30-ww1c-neutrality][^ch30-check-numbers]
- **The *Lusitania*.** A German submarine sank the passenger liner *Lusitania* on May 7, 1915. Accounts differ on the totals, which the chapter should give as a documented range: the Library of Congress states that 1,195 of 1,959 people aboard perished, including 123 Americans, while other standard references state 1,198 dead including 128 Americans. The discrepancy itself should be shown, not smoothed over.[^ch30-check-numbers]
- **The *Sussex* pledge.** After the sinking of the unarmed French steamer *Sussex* in March 1916, Wilson threatened to sever relations unless Germany refrained from attacking passenger ships and allowed crews to abandon enemy merchant vessels. Germany accepted these terms on May 4, 1916, in the “Sussex pledge.”[^ch30-state-wwi]
- **The 1916 election.** Wilson won reelection in November 1916 with the slogan “He kept us out of war,” which the chapter should cite as evidence of the public mood, not as a promise about the future.[^ch30-archives-zimmermann]

### 5.4 Unrestricted warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, and the declaration of war

- **The claim.** Germany’s decision to resume unrestricted submarine warfare, and its offer of an alliance with Mexico, gave Wilson the proof he said he needed and led to the declaration of war in April 1917.[^ch30-state-wwi][^ch30-archives-zimmermann]
- **The German decision.** German leaders concluded that unrestricted submarine warfare could defeat Britain within months and that the United States could no longer be considered neutral after supplying the Allies. On January 31, 1917, Germany notified the United States that it would resume unrestricted attacks the next day, and on February 3 Wilson severed diplomatic relations.[^ch30-state-wwi]
- **The telegram.** British naval intelligence intercepted and decrypted a telegram of January 19, 1917, from Foreign Minister Arthur Zimmermann to the German minister in Mexico, promising German help to recover the territory Mexico had ceded after the Mexican-American War in return for Mexican support. The British forwarded it to Wilson on February 24, and the American press published the story on March 1.[^ch30-state-wwi][^ch30-archives-zimmermann]
- **The declaration.** Wilson went before Congress on April 2, 1917, and asked for a declaration of war, citing the submarine attacks and the attempt to entice Mexico into an alliance. The Senate voted in support on April 4 and the House two days later; the United States declared war on Austria-Hungary on December 7, 1917, a labeled later event.[^ch30-state-wwi]
- **The war message.** In the address Wilson said, “The world must be made safe for democracy. Its peace must be planted upon the tested foundations of political liberty. We have no selfish ends to serve.” The chapter must quote this from the consulted edition and set it beside La Follette’s objection as rival accounts of the same decision.[^ch30-yawp-wilson-war][^ch30-cfr-lafollette]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the sequence, the documents, and the votes. It cannot prove that the submarine attacks alone caused the decision, and the chapter should present the interplay of German actions, Allied ties, and Wilson’s own reasoning, marking causation as contested.[^ch30-state-wwi][^ch30-check-neutrality-case]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Annexation was not the main instrument.** After 1898 the United States ruled in the Caribbean mostly through treaties, customs receiverships, leases, and occupations, not territorial annexation.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti]
- **The canal’s terms were exceptional.** The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty granted the United States the rights of a sovereign within the Canal Zone in perpetuity, a grant distinct from the 1898 acquisitions.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **The Open Door was non-binding.** The Open Door notes expressed American policy toward China without restraining the other powers, and they coexisted with the exclusion of Chinese immigrants from the United States.[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- **Japan was managed, not settled.** The agreements of 1905–08 and the Great White Fleet reduced the danger of war without resolving the racial and strategic conflict.[^ch30-state-japan][^ch30-loc-gwf]
- **Mexico exposed the limits of intervention.** The punitive expedition failed to capture Villa, and the occupation of Veracruz drove Mexican nationalists away without securing a durable settlement.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- **Neutrality was contested to the end.** The armed-ship filibuster and La Follette’s warning show that the decision for war in 1917 was not unanimous and had a serious argument against it.[^ch30-cfr-lafollette]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was empire economic, strategic, ideological, or all three?** One position holds that the search for markets drove expansion; another stresses strategic geography and the naval base system; a third stresses racial and gendered ideology. The chapter should state each at its strongest before judging particular episodes.[^ch30-williams-tragedy][^ch30-lafeber-new-empire][^ch30-hunt-ideology]
- **Was intervention in the Caribbean a departure or a continuity?** Historians have long argued over whether overseas empire was an aberration from the continental republic or a continuation of its expansion. The chapter should present the disagreement as a real one and not assume continuity in advance.[^ch30-shibusawa]
- **Why did the United States enter the European war?** One account stresses German violations of neutral rights, especially unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram; a revisionist account stresses the economic tilt toward the Allies. The chapter should set the evidence for both side by side and mark the causal question as open.[^ch30-state-wwi][^ch30-cfr-lafollette]
- **How should the legal names be read?** The chapter’s whole argument depends on reading “protectorate,” “receivership,” and “zone” for what they transferred. Whether those forms constituted empire is a matter of definition as well as fact.[^ch30-shibusawa][^ch30-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the United States built a differentiated system of control abroad and asserted a neutrality it could not maintain, so that the same years produced both global reach and a war the nation had said it would avoid. The chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch30-shibusawa][^ch30-check-debate-works]
- **Names and substance.** A treaty right in Cuba, a customs house in Santo Domingo, a military government in Haiti, and a canal zone in Panama were different instruments with different effects, and the chapter’s synthesis should keep them distinct rather than reduce them to a single “empire.”[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-haiti][^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter’s synthesis should say that the legal form of American power abroad — protectorate, lease, occupation — was the point, because it delivered control while denying the responsibilities of sovereignty, and that the same habits of reasoning shaped the neutrality crisis at home.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla][^ch30-cfr-lafollette][^ch30-check-table2]

## Legacies

The instruments of control outlasted their makers. The Platt Amendment was repealed in 1934, but the Guantánamo lease remained, and the Dominican and Haitian occupations left constabularies and financial arrangements that shaped those countries for decades. The canal the United States governed as a sovereign would become a central grievance in Panamanian politics long after 1917.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti][^ch30-check-dates]

The habit of intervention outlasted the circumstances. The corollary’s claim of a police power, and the practice of training and directing local forces, supplied a template that later administrations in the Caribbean and Central America would reuse, which belongs to later chapters.[^ch30-state-roosevelt-monroe][^ch30-state-haiti]

The war decision also left a double legacy: a conviction that American power could be used to make the world “safe for democracy,” and a durable suspicion, voiced by La Follette and others, that the nation had gone to war to protect loans and trade. The chapter should present both as claims that the record can test but not finally settle.[^ch30-yawp-wilson-war][^ch30-cfr-lafollette]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The canal was a world project; the British blockade and Allied finance tied the American economy to the war before 1917; and the Open Door was a diplomacy aimed at rival European empires. The chapter should place these connections in notes rather than claim that Europe caused American decisions.[^ch30-state-hay-china][^ch30-ww1c-neutrality]
- **Archives and silences.** The American record — treaties, presidential messages, and departmental reports — is rich, while the testimony of Cubans, Dominicans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, Panamanians, and Mexicans survives unevenly in United States archives. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch30-state-haiti][^ch30-check-silences]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 27 owns the 1898 war and the acquisitions; Chapter 29 owns domestic reform; Chapter 31 owns mobilization and the aftermath; Chapter 32 owns the 1920s quotas. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch30-check-ch27][^ch30-check-ch29][^ch30-check-ch31]

## Summary

- After 1898 the United States ruled in the Caribbean mostly through treaties, customs receiverships, leases, and occupations rather than annexation.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-state-dr][^ch30-state-haiti]
- The Platt Amendment of 1901 gave the United States a treaty right to intervene in Cuba and a naval base at Guantánamo Bay, and it supplied the terms for interventions in 1906, 1912, 1917, and 1920.[^ch30-platt-amendment]
- Roosevelt’s 1904 corollary claimed an “international police power,” and the Dominican customs receivership of 1905 became a template for later financial control.[^ch30-roosevelt-corollary][^ch30-state-dr]
- The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty of 1903 guaranteed Panamanian independence and granted the United States, within the Canal Zone, “all the rights, power and authority” of a sovereign in perpetuity.[^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla]
- The *Ancon* made the first official transit of the Panama Canal on August 15, 1914, two weeks into the European war.[^ch30-smithsonian-ancon]
- The Open Door notes of 1899–1900 asserted equal commercial access to China without binding the other powers, and they coexisted with Chinese exclusion at home.[^ch30-state-hay-china]
- The United States managed rivalry with Japan through the Taft–Katsura, Gentlemen’s, and Root–Takahira agreements and the Great White Fleet’s 1907–09 world cruise.[^ch30-state-japan][^ch30-loc-gwf]
- Wilson’s non-recognition of Huerta, the Tampico incident, and the occupation of Veracruz in 1914 made the United States a party to the Mexican Revolution.[^ch30-nara-punitive][^ch30-app-tampico]
- After Villa’s raid on Columbus in March 1916, more than 14,000 troops under Pershing pursued him into Mexico and failed to capture him.[^ch30-nara-punitive]
- Wilson proclaimed neutrality in August 1914, but the British blockade tilted American trade and credit toward the Allies, and La Follette argued that the tilt made true neutrality impossible.[^ch30-miller-neutrality][^ch30-ww1c-neutrality][^ch30-cfr-lafollette]
- Germany’s resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, and the loss of American ships led Wilson to ask for war on April 2, 1917; Congress declared war on April 6, and Wilson said “the world must be made safe for democracy.”[^ch30-state-wwi][^ch30-yawp-wilson-war]

> *A closing note.* Between 1900 and 1917 the United States ruled abroad without annexing. It held the rights of a sovereign in a ten-mile strip of Panama, intervened at will in Cuba, collected customs in Santo Domingo and Managua, and governed Haiti through a gendarmerie and a treaty. It asserted an open door in China while closing one to Chinese immigrants, sent a fleet around the world to impress Japan, and twice occupied Mexican ports and territory. It proclaimed neutrality in 1914 and declared war in 1917 after German submarines and a German telegram destroyed the neutrality it had promised. The chapter’s question — what was empire without formal annexation? — is answered by a record in which control was delivered through legal names that were meant to conceal its extent.[^ch30-platt-amendment][^ch30-hay-bunau-varilla][^ch30-state-haiti][^ch30-state-hay-china][^ch30-state-wwi]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on July 20, 1917, when the United States held a lottery to call the first draftees of the war it had entered in April. The power that had been exercised abroad through treaties and occupations would now be turned on the nation’s own citizens, who would be registered, selected, trained, censored, and sent to France. Chapter 31 takes up the First World War and its aftermath, and asks what total mobilization did to a democracy.[^ch30-check-ch31][^ch30-check-bridge]
