## 1. How Appendix B Works

### 1.1 Purpose, numbering, and limits

- Appendix B supplies eight numbered reference tables. Each table may have lettered panels, but a panel is not a new table number. The stable display numbers are App. B, Table 1 through App. B, Table 8.
- The tables support comparisons that chapters make in passing: population scale, election returns, territorial cessions, war mobilization, immigration, slavery, economic benchmarks, and the New Deal landscape. They do not replace the direct sources cited in the chapters.
- Appendix B does not publish a ninth “Tables in the Chapters” crosswalk. That crosswalk remains a README production record, as the plan requires. The crosswalk is summarized in §10.1 for the editor, not for the reader.
- A table is finished only when it has a caption, a source line, definitions for every column, notes on breaks and missing data, and a textual reference from the appendix prose. A list of sources is not a table.
- The appendix is planned at 14 text pages. That allowance accommodates the eight tables at a compact reference setting. The row caps below are chosen to fit that setting; if a table cannot be set legibly within the cap, request a further reallocation rather than cutting definitions or source lines.

### 1.2 Common conventions

- **Time.** Label every date as a census date, calendar year, fiscal year, or observation year. Colonial figures are annual estimates, not censuses. Election returns are election-year returns. Immigration admissions are federal fiscal years. War costs are for the spending period stated in the source.
- **Geography.** Use present-day U.S. boundaries unless a row says otherwise. For historical rows, give the boundaries and jurisdictions in force at the time. Do not compare a colonial “population of the colonies” with the 2020 resident population without saying that the geographic base changed.
- **Population.** Use resident population. Census counts are not adjusted for underenumeration unless the row says “adjusted.” Do not mix de jure and de facto populations, or state and national totals from different vintages.
- **Money.** Use nominal dollars of the stated year. Add constant-dollar figures only with the named index and base year. Do not combine war appropriations, outlays, veterans’ costs, and interest in one “cost” column.
- **Rates.** Give the numerator and denominator. Percentages are rounded to one decimal unless the source reports more precision. A change in percentage points is not a percent change.
- **Race and status.** Keep the historical categories and explain them. “White,” “Black,” “Negro,” “colored,” “free,” and “enslaved” do not mean the same thing in every source or decade. Do not use “Black” and “enslaved” interchangeably.
- **Missing values.** Use “n.a.” for not available, “—” for not applicable, and “0” for a true zero. Do not leave a data cell blank; a blank data cell invites a reader to infer zero.
- **Provisional values.** Mark a value the source labels preliminary or estimated with “(p)” and define the flag in the table note. Do not drop the flag until the source issues a final value.
- **Rounding and arithmetic.** Follow the source’s rounding. If a total is calculated, say so in the source line or a note. Do not force a column to add when the source’s components are independently rounded.
- **Source lines.** Give the responsible institution or author, the exact title, the series or table number, the observation years, the URL or DOI, and the access date. “Compiled for this volume” is acceptable only when the transformation is documented in a production note.
- **Rights.** Official statistical tables and treaty texts are generally public domain in the United States. Quoted captions, modern maps, and third-party photographs are not automatically public domain. Appendix B uses no images; if a table reproduces a third-party chart or facsimile, the register and rights review must be updated first.

### 1.3 Source hierarchy and verification status

- Official statistical series come first: the Census Bureau, the Office of Management and Budget, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of Defense, the Bureau of Land Management, and the National Archives.
- The core historical compendium for several tables is the Census Bureau’s 1975 Bicentennial edition of *Historical Statistics of the United States, Colonial Times to 1970*. It supplies the colonial population series, the long-run election returns, the slavery and finance series, and several communications series. Its chapter definitions are part of the evidence, not background.[^app-b-hsus-1975]
- Scholarly compilations and peer-reviewed articles come second. They are used for definitions, corrections, and contested estimates, not as substitutes for the official series when an official series exists.
- Secondary summaries and reference entries are leads. They may establish a date or a name, but they do not support a numerical claim in the finished table.
- “Consulted” means the source was opened and read for this outline. “Discovered” means the item was located but not read. This outline marks unread items and does not present their contents as verified.
- Citations in this outline are source targets as well as evidence records. A note may identify a table that was consulted for its definitions and series while leaving row-level verification for drafting. The limitations field distinguishes the two; no table value should be treated as checked merely because it appears in this outline.
- The values printed in this outline are benchmarks for drafting. They are not the final table values and must be rechecked against the cited locator before the table is called drafted.

## 2. Table 1. Population and Urbanization, 1630–2020

### 2.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 1 shows the scale of population growth, the changing share of the population classified as urban, the regional redistribution of the population, and the growth of the largest cities.
- The table supports a claim the chapters make repeatedly: the United States did not grow by adding equal increments to a fixed society. It grew by incorporating new regions, reclassifying settlement, and moving people across a continent. The urban share crossed 50 percent between 1910 and 1920, and the South and West gained share after 1950.
- The table must not imply that colonial estimates are censuses, that “urban” means the same thing in 1790 and 2020, or that city boundaries are stable.

### 2.2 Table design

- **Panel A. National population and urban share.** Benchmark years: 1630, 1700, 1750, 1780, 1790, 1820, 1860, 1900, 1920, 1950, 1970, 2000, 2010, and 2020. Columns: year; total population; White population; Black population; enslaved population where the source separates it; urban population; urban share. **Universe.** The national row is the conterminous United States (the area enumerated) through 1940 and the 50 states and the District of Columbia from 1950, on the Census Bureau’s retrospective 50-state geography; Alaska and Hawaii were territories in 1950. The table’s 1950 and 1970 rows are 50 states and D.C.; the caption must mark the 1950 geographic break. Do not add Puerto Rico or the Island Areas to the national row.
- **Panel B. Regions.** Years: 1790, 1860, 1900, 1950, 2000, and 2020. Columns: Northeast, Midwest, South, and West, in counts and shares of the national total. Use the modern Census regions consistently and say that they are applied retrospectively. The 1860 and 1900 rows must sum to the conterminous total; the 1950, 2000, and 2020 rows must sum to the 50-state and D.C. total. The 1790 row is the exception: the Midwest region cannot be complete because the Northwest Territory was not enumerated, and the West region lay outside the enumerated area; print “n.a.” for both and note that the shares describe only the enumerated area, not the present-day United States. The regions exclude Puerto Rico and the Island Areas.
- **Panel C. Largest cities and municipal units.** Years: 1790, 1860, 1920, and 2020. Columns: rank, city, population, and city share of the national population. Display label: “Largest Cities (Legal City-Proper / Municipal Boundaries).” For 2020 use incorporated-place (city-proper) boundaries. For 1790, 1860, and 1920 use Gibson’s legal-designation urban places, which include New England towns and the municipal districts around Philadelphia. Do not substitute urban areas, metropolitan areas, metropolitan districts, or census designated places. Add three boundary notes: Boston and Baltimore were towns in 1790, while New York, Philadelphia, and Charleston were cities; Philadelphia consolidated with Philadelphia County in 1854; Brooklyn was a separate city ranked third in 1860 and was consolidated into New York City in 1898.
- **Row cap.** About 14 national rows, six regional rows, and 20 city rows, for roughly 40 data rows. Target: 1.5 to 2 pages.
- **Captions and notes.** The caption must state that colonial figures are estimates; that the national universe is conterminous through 1940 and 50 states and D.C. from 1950 on the retrospective 50-state geography; that urban definitions changed in 1950 and 2020; and that city boundaries are legal or municipal. A table note must give the 2020 populations of Puerto Rico and the Island Areas, the 2010 and 2020 urban shares with the 2010 figure under 2020 criteria, and the 1970 allocation caveat.

### 2.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Urban share.** Divide the urban population by the total resident population of the same geographic universe and multiply by 100. For 2010 use 249,253,271 / 308,745,538 = 80.7 percent. For 2020 use 265,149,027 / 331,449,281 = 80.0 percent. For 1950 use the 50-state retrospective counts: 96,846,817 urban / 151,325,798 = 64.0 percent. Do not use the conterminous 1950 counts (96,467,686 urban and 54,229,675 rural), which sum to 150,697,361 and belong to a different geographic base. For 2020, use the 2020 urban-area criteria; for 1950–2010, use the published urban population under the definition in force. Do not use the 80.3 percent figure that appears in some tables; it is not the 2010 decennial urban-area share.
- **Regional shares.** Divide each regional total by the national total. The regional groups are the four modern Census regions. A state that moved from one region to another in the census’s classification must be noted; do not silently redraw the regions. The four regional totals must sum to the national total for the same row, or the note must explain the difference. In 1790 the Midwest region cannot be complete because the Northwest Territory was not enumerated, and the West region lay outside the enumerated area; print “n.a.” for both and note that the shares describe only the enumerated area, not the present-day United States.
- **City share.** Divide city population by the national resident population for the same geographic universe. City boundaries are those used at the census date. Do not substitute metropolitan-area or urban-area population for a city row without labeling the change.
- **Race columns.** For the colonial period, the source’s “White and Negro” total is not the same as the later “White” and “Black” categories. For 1790–1860, “Black” includes free and enslaved Black people; the enslaved column is a subset. Do not add the enslaved column to the Black column. For 1790, use 697,681 enslaved people after the Vermont correction; the 1790 enslaved count in Table 6 inherits this correction. Panel A has columns only for Total, White, and Black; it omits the earlier “all other free persons” category and the later “Other races” category, so White plus Black will not equal Total in every row. In 1970, the Census Bureau estimated that the “other races” population was overstated by about 327,000.
- **Rounding.** The colonial figures are rounded to the nearest person in the source but rest on estimates. Do not print them to a precision the source does not support. For the 2020 figures, print the census counts as published.
- **Arithmetic check: 1970.** The official 1970 total in Series A 1–5 is 203,235,298. The race and urban tables exclude 23,372 persons and sum to 203,211,926. Do not add a “not allocated” row; put the 23,372 difference in a table note. ### 2.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Colonial estimates are reconstructions.** The Z 1–19 series was compiled from colonial officials’ reports, tax lists, militia rolls, and estimates. The 17th-century enslaved figures are especially uncertain; the compilers themselves called them “doubtlessly too low.”[^app-b-hsus-z1-19]
- **Census undercounts are not uniform.** The 1870 census is notoriously incomplete in the South, and undercounts vary by race, region, and age. A growth rate calculated across 1860–1870 without a note will overstate or understate change depending on the group.
- **“Urban” is an administrative category.** The threshold was 8,000 in 1874, 4,000 in 1880, and 2,500 in 1910; the 1950 census added urbanized areas; the 2020 census uses housing-unit density and a 5,000-person or 2,000-housing-unit threshold. A series that treats “urban” as a natural category will mislead.[^app-b-census-2020-urban]
- **Regional totals hide state and local variation.** The South’s share can rise while particular states lose population. The West’s share can rise while rural counties decline. The table is a national and regional summary, not a settlement map.
- **City populations hide metropolitan growth.** New York City’s 2020 population is smaller than its metropolitan area; Houston’s city boundaries include vast annexed territory. City-proper comparisons are useful only if the boundary rule is stated.
- **The 1940 totals differ by universe.** Table 1 and Table 8 both use the conterminous United States through 1940. The 1940 total is 131,669,275 and the 1930 total is 122,775,046. Do not substitute a 50-state retrospective total that adds Alaska and Hawaii; if a source gives one, label the universe and do not mix it with the conterminous series. The 1950 break to 50 states and D.C. remains as stated in §2.6.[^app-b-hsus-a1-5][^app-b-census-1940-pop]
- **The 1950 urban series has two geographic bases.** The 1950 Census reports 96,467,686 urban and 54,229,675 rural for the conterminous United States (150,697,361 total). The 1960 Census retrospectively reports 96,846,817 urban and 54,478,981 rural for the 50 states (151,325,798 total). Panel A uses the 50-state figures; the conterminous figures are the alternative and are not comparable without a note.[^app-b-census-1950-urban][^app-b-hsus-a57-72]
- **The 2010 and 2020 urban shares are not directly comparable.** The published 2010 share is 80.7 percent; the published 2020 share is 80.0 percent. Applying the 2020 minimum thresholds to 2010 data puts the 2010 share at 79.6 percent (245,658,565 urban / 308,745,538 total). The apparent decline from 80.7 to 80.0 is a definitional artifact, not evidence of de-urbanization. Do not use the 80.3 percent figure; it is not the 2010 decennial urban-area share.[^app-b-census-2022-urban-redefinition][^app-b-census-2022-urban-release]
- **The 1970 total has three vintages.** The 1970 census tables show 203,211,926; the official count in *Historical Statistics* is 203,235,298; the latest revision is 203,302,031. The table uses the official count and puts the other two in a note. Do not average them or treat the revised total as a demographically allocated count.[^app-b-statab-1987-1970]
- **Territories are outside the national series.** Puerto Rico and the Island Areas have separate urban-area delineations and enumeration instruments. Their 2020 urban shares (91.9 and 87.1 percent) are not comparable to the 80.0 percent U.S. share, and their populations are not part of the 331,449,281 total.[^app-b-census-2020-urban]

## 3. Table 2. Presidential Elections, 1789–2024

### 3.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 2 gives the electoral vote, the reported popular vote where a comparable national record exists, and a turnout estimate for each presidential election.
- The table supports three claims: the Electoral College and state election rules shaped outcomes; the popular vote is not a complete national series before the 1820s; and turnout is a constructed rate, not a raw count.
- The table must not invent a national popular vote for elections in which many states chose electors by legislature or did not report a popular vote.

### 3.2 Table design

- **Rows.** One row for each presidential election from 1789 through 2024, 60 elections. The earlier count of 59 omitted 2024, the 60th election.
- **Panels and headers.** Keep one table, App. B, Table 2, with three panels and two header sets. Panel A (1789–1824) uses the short header: year; winner and party; winner’s electoral votes; runner-up and party; runner-up’s electoral votes; other electoral votes; method of choosing electors; turnout rate; notes. Panels B (1828–1900) and C (1904–2024) use the full header, adding the winner’s and runner-up’s reported popular vote and share and the turnout denominator series. Do not split the table or add a second election table.
- **Panel A. 1789–1824.** Electoral votes, the method of choosing electors, and turnout. No popular-vote columns: before 1828 the states used a mix of legislative selection and popular election, and the 1824 returns cover only part of the country. Put the 1824 partial totals in the table note, not in a cell.
- **Panel B. 1828–1900.** Reported popular vote with coverage notes. Some states still had incomplete returns or legislature-chosen electors.
- **Panel C. 1904–2024.** Modern official returns. Use the House Clerk’s series from 1920 and the FEC’s official 2024 results.
- **Row cap.** 60 rows. Target: 2.5 pages in a compact two-column setting.
- **Notes.** Flag the 1800 tie and House election, the 1824 House election, and the 1876, 1888, 2000, and 2016 electoral-vote/popular-vote splits; record faithless electors in the Notes column, not in a separate column; flag the 1824 partial returns and the 1872 count. Party labels before 1828 are retrospective; print them as the source assigns them. Leave a notes cell blank where no flag applies; the blank is not a zero.

### 3.3 Source series and exact locators

- **1789–1968.** *Historical Statistics*, series Y 79–83, pp. 1073–81. The table gives electoral and popular vote by party. Its general note states that “the record of popular votes is inadequate to explain the elections until after 1824.”[^app-b-hsus-y79-83]
- **Pre-1824 selection.** *Historical Statistics*, series Y 1–26, “Methods of Electing Presidential Electors, 1788–1836,” pp. 1071–72, for the state-by-state switch from legislative selection to popular election. The series is a necessary check on any claim that a national popular vote existed in 1789.[^app-b-hsus-y1-26]
- **1824 state returns.** The American Presidency Project, “1824 Presidential Election.” The page tabulates returns from 18 states and gives Jackson 151,271 of 365,833 recorded votes (41.3 percent), Adams 113,122 (30.9 percent), Clay 47,531 (13.0 percent), and Crawford 40,856 (11.2 percent). It identifies Delaware, Georgia, Louisiana, New York, South Carolina, and Vermont as states whose electors were chosen by the legislature.[^app-b-app-1824]
- **1824 compilation.** Donald J. Ratcliffe, “Was Andrew Jackson Really the People’s Choice in 1824?” Ratcliffe follows Edward Stanwood’s totals from the 14 states where, in his account, there was a popular ballot for president: Jackson 153,544, Adams 108,740, Clay 47,136, and Crawford 46,618. Ratcliffe’s notional reconstruction of the legislative states and New York puts Adams ahead nationwide, about 195,000 for Jackson and at least 224,000 for Adams.[^app-b-ratcliffe-1824]
- **1872 count.** National Archives and Records Administration, “1872 Electoral College Results.” The participating states cast 352 electoral votes; 349 were counted: Grant 286 and other candidates 63, with Greeley at 0 after three votes cast for him were not counted. Arkansas and Louisiana submitted no electoral votes.[^app-b-nara-1872]
- **1876 count.** NARA, “1876 Electoral College Results.” Hayes 185, Tilden 184, total 369, majority 185. The votes of Florida, Louisiana, Oregon, and South Carolina were disputed and referred to the Electoral Commission.[^app-b-nara-1876]
- **1877 count in Congress.** *Congressional Record*, 44th Cong., 2d Sess., vol. 5, pp. 1992–96. ### 3.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Popular vote.** Sum the state returns only where the source reports them. Do not estimate missing states from neighboring states or from the Electoral College. If a state chose electors by legislature, its popular vote is not zero; it is “n.a.”
- **1824.** Print no popular-vote total and no share. Six states chose electors by legislature; the American Presidency Project’s 18-state total and Stanwood’s 14-state total differ by thousands of votes, and neither is a national count. If the table note gives figures, give both series, label them “partial,” and say that the shares rest on different denominators.[^app-b-app-1824][^app-b-ratcliffe-1824]
- **Popular-vote share.** Divide the candidate’s reported votes by the total reported presidential votes in the states with returns. If the total is incomplete, label the share “partial” and do not compare it with a complete national share. Do not compute an 1824 share at all; the returns do not cover the electorate.
- **Electoral vote.** Use the official count of votes cast, not the number of electors appointed. Note faithless electors and any vote that Congress rejected or that a state did not submit. For 1789–1800, each elector cast two presidential votes, so the candidate totals sum to twice the number of electors; do not treat them as a partition of the elector count. In 1872, 352 votes were cast and 349 counted: Arkansas and Louisiana submitted none, and three Georgia votes for Greeley were not counted after his death.[^app-b-nara-1872] In 1876, 185–184 was the final count after 20 disputed votes went to Hayes.[^app-b-nara-1876] In 2020, 306–232 was the count after the Arizona and Pennsylvania objections failed.[^app-b-nara-2020]
- **Turnout.** Divide ballots counted by the voting-eligible population and multiply by 100. Do not use the voting-age population, registered voters, or the adult citizen population as if they were the same denominator. The Election Lab’s VEP subtracts noncitizens and disenfranchised felons and adds eligible overseas voters at the national level.[^app-b-uf-election-lab]
- **2024 turnout.** Divide 156,766,239 ballots counted by 243,803,423 and multiply by 100: 64.30 percent, printed as 64.3. Do not compute the rate from the FEC’s 155,238,302 presidential votes; that gives 63.7 percent and understates turnout because about 1.53 million ballots had no presidential vote or were counted only down-ballot. Do not recompute the Lab’s VEP from the rounded noncitizen and felon cells; the components do not reproduce the published denominator exactly.[^app-b-uf-2024][^app-b-fec-2024]
- **Turnout series break.** Pre-1948 turnout is Burnham’s reconstruction; 1948 to the present is McDonald’s. The two series are not a single homogeneous series. Label the break in the table note.
- **Panel A turnout.** The pre-1828 rates divide partial presidential votes by the VEP; print them as “partial” and do not compare them with 1828 or later. Where the Election Lab gives no value, print “n.a.”[^app-b-uf-election-lab]
- **Third parties.** Include third-party candidates only when the source reports them. Do not lump all “other” votes into a major-party total. A note should give the largest third-party vote where it matters.
- **Faithless electors.** Count only counted presidential-ballot votes cast for a candidate other than the one to whom the elector was pledged. Report an abstention as an abstention. Note an absence or vacancy in the Notes column without counting it. Do not count vice-presidential-only defections or unpledged electors. Put the count and the names in the Notes column, not in a separate column.[^app-b-crs-faithless]
- **2024.** Use the FEC’s official state returns for the popular vote and the NARA certificate count for the electoral vote. Do not use media calls or network projections.

### 3.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **The electorate was restricted.** Before the Fifteenth Amendment, Black men were enslaved or disenfranchised in much of the country; before the Nineteenth, women could not vote; property, tax, and citizenship qualifications excluded many White men. A turnout rate is a rate among the eligible, not among the adult population.
- **Popular-vote coverage is incomplete.** In 1824, six states chose electors by legislature, and the states that held popular elections did not all report a usable presidential vote. The American Presidency Project tabulates 18 states; Stanwood’s totals rest on 14. A national popular-vote total before 1828 is a construct, not a contemporaneous count.[^app-b-app-1824][^app-b-ratcliffe-1824] In 1789, New York did not cast an electoral vote because the legislature failed to agree.[^app-b-hsus-y79-83]
- **The 1876 election was contested.** NARA records 185–184, with 20 votes from Florida, Louisiana, Oregon, and South Carolina disputed and awarded to Hayes by the Electoral Commission. The table should flag the commission and the disputed votes, not print a smooth result.[^app-b-nara-1876][^app-b-congressional-record-1877]
- **The 2020 election was contested.** NARA records 306–232; objections to Arizona and Pennsylvania were filed and not sustained, and the result was certified after the Capitol attack. Use the certified result and note the contest, not a news summary.[^app-b-nara-2020]
- **Turnout denominators are estimates.** The VEP depends on estimates of noncitizens, felons, and overseas voters. A one-point difference between sources may be a denominator difference, not a mobilization difference.
- **The 2024 turnout figure is provisional.** The Election Lab’s estimates may be revised as states report final ballots. Do not freeze a number that the source labels an estimate without saying so.
- **Faithless counts are not one series.** Aggregate totals mix presidential votes, vice-presidential votes, abstentions, and invalidated or replaced votes. The table reports only counted presidential deviations and says so in the note.[^app-b-crs-faithless]

## 4. Table 3. Territory, Treaties, and Jurisdiction, 1763–1917

### 4.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 3 lists the instruments by which the United States acquired, claimed, annexed, leased, or occupied territory between 1763 and 1917.
- The table supports a claim about legal form: continental expansion included cessions, purchases, annexations, and boundary settlements, not only purchases. Overseas expansion included a treaty cession, an annexation by joint resolution, a lease-like canal zone, and a purchase.
- The table must distinguish sovereignty from control, and it must not treat Indigenous lands as empty or unowned before a European or American instrument.
- It must also distinguish a treaty from a domestic statute: the 1845 Texas and 1898 Hawaii annexations were joint resolutions, and the Canal Zone was a grant of control without a transfer of sovereignty.

### 4.2 Table design

- **Rows.** 1763 Treaty of Paris (imperial baseline); 1783 Treaty of Paris; 1803 Louisiana Purchase; 1819 Florida cession; 1845 Texas annexation (with the 1850 settlement in the cost and notes); 1846 Oregon Treaty; 1848 Mexican Cession; 1853 Gadsden Purchase; 1867 Alaska Purchase; 1898 Hawaii annexation; 1898 Spanish treaty (Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines); 1903 Panama Canal Zone; 1917 Danish West Indies purchase.
- **Columns.** Instrument and date; counterparty; legal form; area (gross square miles, with land and water cells only where the source provides components that sum to the gross total); nominal cost and what it covered; sovereignty or control status; termination or current status; notes.
- **Panel A. Continental acquisitions, 1763–1867.** Include the 1763 baseline in a shaded row labeled “Imperial baseline, not a U.S. acquisition.” The 1845 Texas row carries the 1850 settlement in its cost and notes; do not add a separate 1850 row.
- **Panel B. Overseas and insular acquisitions, 1898–1917.** Include Hawaii, one Spanish treaty row with sub-entries for Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, the Canal Zone, and the Danish West Indies. Cuba was relinquished but not ceded; it is not a U.S. acquisition row.
- **Row cap.** 13 rows: nine continental and four overseas. The Spanish treaty’s three sub-entries and the Texas 1850 settlement do not create additional rows. If the 1763 row is dropped for layout, retitle the table 1783–1917 rather than keeping a 1763 title over a later series.
- **Notes.** State the area basis for each row group: BLM acres for the 1803, 1819, 1846, 1848, 1853, and 1867 continental rows, the 1947 Census gross area for the 1783 row, Texas, and the overseas possessions, and the 1940 Census for the Canal Zone. Use “n.a.” where the source does not split land and water; do not fill those cells with 2010 figures that sum to a different total. Identify which costs are cash, assumed claims, stock, or a debt and boundary settlement.

### 4.3 Source series and exact locators

- **Area series.** U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Statistical Abstract of the United States: 1947*, Table 1, “Territorial Expansion of Continental United States and Acquisitions of Territories and Possessions,” p. 3. The table reports gross area (land and water) in square miles on the 1940 remeasurement: Territory in 1790, 888,811; Texas, 1845, 390,144; Oregon, 1846, 285,580; Mexican Cession, 1848, 529,017; Gadsden, 1853, 29,640; Alaska, 1867, 586,400; Hawaii, 1898, 6,433; Puerto Rico, 1899, 3,435; Guam, 1899, 206; Panama Canal Zone, 1904, 553; Virgin Islands, 1917, 133. The Philippines (115,600) appears in footnote 2 and is excluded from the table’s territorial total. Table 3 uses the BLM acreage for Oregon, the Mexican Cession, Gadsden, and Alaska; the 1947 figures for those rows are comparison values, not the printed cells. Use this series for every other row whose area is not taken from the BLM public-domain table, and state the basis in the table note. Do not mix this gross-area series silently with 2010 totals that include territorial waters.[^app-b-statab-1947-area]
- **Continental area and cost.** Bureau of Land Management, *Public Land Statistics 2020*, Table 1-1, “Acquisition of the Public Domain, 1781–1867,” p. 3. The table gives acres of land, water, and total area, the percentage of total U.S. area, and cost. Key rows: Louisiana Purchase 523,446,400 land, 6,465,280 water, and 529,911,680 total acres, at $23,213,568; Florida cession 43,342,720 land, 2,801,920 water, and 46,144,640 total acres, at $6,674,057; Oregon Compromise 180,644,480 land, 2,741,760 water, and 183,386,240 total acres, at no cost; Mexican Cession 334,479,360 land, 4,201,600 water, and 338,680,960 total acres, at $16,295,149; purchase from Texas 78,842,880 land, 83,840 water, and 78,926,720 total acres, at $15,496,448; Gadsden Purchase 18,961,920 land, 26,880 water, and 18,988,800 total acres, at $10,000,000; Alaska Purchase 365,333,120 land, 12,909,440 water, and 378,242,560 total acres, at $7,200,000. - **1763 baseline.** Definitive Treaty of Peace, February 10, 1763, Avalon Project transcription. France ceded Canada and its claims east of the Mississippi except New Orleans; Spain ceded Florida. The treaty is an imperial baseline, not a U.S. acquisition, and Indigenous nations were not parties to it.[^app-b-treaty-paris-1763]
- **1783 boundaries.** Treaty of Paris, September 3, 1783, Avalon Project transcription. The treaty recognizes the United States and defines its boundaries; it is the legal baseline for the public-domain cessions that follow. The 1947 Census gives 888,811 square miles for the “Territory in 1790”; use that figure for the 1783 row, with a note that it is a 1790 measurement of boundaries recognized in 1783, not a survey at the treaty date.[^app-b-treaty-paris-1783][^app-b-statab-1947-area]
- **1819 Florida.** Adams-Onís Treaty, February 22, 1819, Avalon Project transcription. Spain cedes East and West Florida; the United States assumes up to $5 million in claims.[^app-b-treaty-florida-1819]
- **1846 Oregon.** Oregon Treaty, June 15, 1846, Avalon Project transcription. The boundary is the 49th parallel; the treaty ends joint occupancy but does not involve a purchase price.[^app-b-treaty-oregon-1846]
- **1848 Mexican Cession.** Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, February 2, 1848, National Archives milestone page. The United States pays $15 million and assumes up to $3.25 million in claims; the cession is more than 525,000 square miles.[^app-b-treaty-guadalupe-1848]
- **1853 Gadsden.** Gadsden Purchase Treaty, December 30, 1853, Avalon Project transcription. The United States pays $10 million for about 30,000 square miles; the Senate amended the treaty.[^app-b-treaty-gadsden-1853]
- **1867 Alaska.** National Archives, “Check for the Purchase of Alaska.” The United States agrees to pay Russia $7.2 million for 586,412 square miles; the check is dated 1868.[^app-b-alaska-1867]

Section 2 provides that Texas “shall retain all the public funds, debts, taxes, and dues of every kind …” and “shall also retain all the vacant and unappropriated lands lying within its limits, to be applied to the payment of the debts and liabilities of said Republic of Texas …” and that “in no event are said debts and liabilities to become a charge upon the Government of the United States.” The resolution did not assume the debt. The 1845 claim area is 390,144 gross square miles. The 1850 Texas and New Mexico Act (9 Stat. 446, September 9, 1850) offered Texas $10 million in 5 percent stock for accepting the boundary, ceding its claims outside it, and relinquishing its claims against the United States; BLM records the resulting public-domain acquisition as 78,842,880 land acres, 83,840 water acres, and 78,926,720 total acres (123,323 square miles), at a cost of $15,496,448. Label the legal form “joint resolution (domestic statute; not a treaty)” and the status “U.S. control and statehood; Native Hawaiian sovereignty contested.”[^app-b-hawaii-1898][^app-b-statab-1947-area][^app-b-silva-1997-petitions][^app-b-apology-1993][^app-b-hawaii-oha-2009]

Record the legal form as a treaty grant of exclusive control, not a cession; record titular sovereignty as remaining with Panama.[^app-b-panama-1903][^app-b-census-1940-canal-zone][^app-b-panama-treaties-1977]

### 4.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Area.** Use the source named for each row, not a single conversion rule. The continental acquisition rows other than Texas (1803, 1819, 1846, 1848, 1853, and 1867) use BLM acres divided by 640; the quotient is the total, and the land and water columns are the BLM components converted the same way. The 1763 row has no U.S. area, and the 1783 row uses the 1947 “Territory in 1790” figure (888,811), not a BLM row. Texas, Hawaii, the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Virgin Islands use the 1947 Census gross area; print “n.a.” in the land and water cells because the 1947 table does not split them. The Canal Zone uses the 1940 Census: 362 land, 191 water, 553 total. Do not fill a land or water cell with a 2010 figure that sums to a different total. Alaska is the clearest continental discrepancy: the common 586,412-square-mile figure, the 1947 gross area of 586,400, and the BLM total of 378,242,560 acres, or 591,004 square miles, differ because they use different area definitions and boundary surveys; print the BLM figure in the row and put the variants in a note.[^app-b-blm-pls-2020][^app-b-statab-1947-area]
- **Area accuracy.** The 1947 table’s headnote states that the boundaries were “indefinite” and that its figures rest on 1912 interpretations of the treaties. Treat every area in Table 3 as approximate, round to whole square miles, and do not derive a precise boundary from a subtracted area.[^app-b-statab-1947-area]
- **Cost.** Use nominal dollars of the instrument date. Do not inflation-adjust in this table. If a constant-dollar column is added, name the index and base year.
- **What the cost covers.** The Louisiana figure of $23,213,568 includes $15 million principal plus assumed claims; do not print $15 million as the total. The Florida figure of $6,674,057 is assumed claims, not cash paid to Spain; it is higher than the treaty’s $5 million claims cap, and the BLM table does not itemize the difference. The Texas row is $0 cash in 1845 and $15,496,448 in the 1850 settlement; the 1850 act authorized $10 million in 5 percent stock, and BLM’s compiled cost is higher than that stock principal. The table does not itemize the difference. Do not print $10 million as the total settlement cost or attribute it to 1845. The $20 million for the Spanish treaty is one lump sum and was not allocated among Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. The Canal Zone cost is $10 million plus a $250,000 annual annuity, not a purchase of sovereignty.[^app-b-blm-pls-2020][^app-b-texas-1850][^app-b-treaty-spain-1898][^app-b-panama-1903]
- **Legal form.** Define “cession,” “purchase,” “annexation,” “lease,” and “occupation.” The 1845 Texas joint resolution is an annexation, not a treaty. The 1898 Hawaii joint resolution is an annexation by domestic statute, not a treaty. The Canal Zone is a grant of control, not a transfer of sovereignty. The 1763 Treaty of Paris is an imperial baseline, not a U.S. instrument.
- **Sovereignty and control.** A row may record control without sovereignty: the Canal Zone, Guantánamo, and the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands are examples. Hawaii is a case of U.S. domestic sovereignty and statehood with Native Hawaiian sovereignty contested; do not reduce it to “annexed” without the qualification. The Philippines was under U.S. sovereignty from 1898 to 1946 and then became independent. Do not put these rows in a simple “U.S. territory” column without qualification.
- **Termination.** The Philippines became independent on July 4, 1946. The Canal Zone ceased to exist on October 1, 1979, and canal operations were transferred to Panama on December 31, 1999. Hawaii and Alaska became states in 1959. Texas became a state in 1845. The Danish West Indies became the U.S. Virgin Islands, a U.S. territory. The table should give the current status, not only the acquisition date.
- **Arithmetic check.** Do not sum the area column. The rows rest on three bases: BLM public-domain acres for the 1803, 1819, 1846, 1848, 1853, and 1867 rows, the 1947 Census gross-area series for the 1783 row, Texas, and the overseas possessions, and the 1940 Canal Zone census for the zone. The 13 rows are nine continental and four overseas; the Spanish treaty’s three sub-entries and the Texas 1850 settlement do not create additional rows. If the table prints a grand total, label it a calculation and explain the bases.[^app-b-blm-pls-2020][^app-b-statab-1947-area][^app-b-census-1940-canal-zone]

### 4.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Area figures are not neutral.** They depend on water and land definitions, boundary surveys, and the treatment of disputed claims. The same acquisition can appear as 827,192 or 827,987 square miles, or as 529,911,680 acres; Hawaii can appear as 6,433 or 10,932 square miles, depending on the source, unit, and water definition.
- **The 1947 and 2010 series are not interchangeable.** The 1947 gross-area series excludes territorial waters; the 2010 State Area Measurements include them. The difference is largest for Hawaii and the Virgin Islands. A table that puts a 2010 total beside a 1947 total without a note will imply a change in the land that did not occur.[^app-b-statab-1947-area][^app-b-census-area]
- **Cost figures are not prices for land.** They include claims, debt assumption, and diplomatic settlements. They do not measure the value of the land, resources, or labor taken.
- **Indigenous title is prior.** The treaties and statutes record the claims of European and American states. They do not establish that Indigenous nations had no title. The table must not present a cession as a transfer from an empty landscape.
- **Some acquisitions were not purchases.** Oregon was a boundary settlement; Texas was an annexation, and Texas retained its public lands and debt in 1845; the 1850 settlement paid $10 million in stock and resolved the boundary and claims; Hawaii was a joint resolution, not a treaty; the Canal Zone was a control arrangement. A “purchase price” column will be mostly “—” for these rows.
- **Hawaii is not a settled cession.** The 1898 joint resolution is a domestic statute, not a treaty. The 1993 Apology Resolution acknowledges that the land cession occurred without Native Hawaiian consent or compensation and that Native Hawaiians never directly relinquished their sovereignty claims. Native Hawaiian scholars and sovereignty advocates reject the premise that annexation extinguished those claims. The table records the U.S. legal form and the contest; it does not declare the contest resolved.[^app-b-hawaii-1898][^app-b-apology-1993][^app-b-silva-1997-petitions][^app-b-hawaii-oha-2009]
- **The 1763 baseline is not U.S. territory.** The Treaty of Paris of 1763 ended the Seven Years’ War and transferred Canada and Florida to Britain. It is included only as a shaded imperial baseline before the United States existed. It is not counted in U.S. area or cost totals.[^app-b-treaty-paris-1763]
- **The table cannot measure the consequences.** It does not show how much land remained in Indigenous hands, how much was sold or granted to settlers, or how federal land policy worked. Those are chapter subjects, not appendix data.

## 5. Table 4. Wars, Mobilization, and Cost

### 5.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 4 gives service totals, death and wound totals, and cost estimates for selected U.S. wars from the Revolution through 2021.

### 5.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Early war records are incomplete.** The Revolutionary War and War of 1812 totals are estimates compiled from pension records and other sources, not a complete roster. The CRS table itself warns that data before World War I are based on incomplete records.[^app-b-crs-rl32492]
- **Civil War mortality is contested.** The conventional 618,222, Hacker’s 752,000 (upper bound 851,000), and Barceló’s 698,000 (647,439–748,561) use different methods and populations. The rows print roster counts; a table that prints one excess-mortality number without the range will overstate certainty.
- **Cost definitions are not comparable.** CRS military operations, OMB outlays, and Costs of War budgetary costs answer different questions. The table can show them side by side only with a definition column and a note. The $8 trillion total is a budgetary and future-obligations estimate, not a war cost comparable to the CRS rows.[^app-b-costs-of-war-2021]
- **Allied contributions matter.** The Persian Gulf War’s net cost to U.S. taxpayers was much lower than the gross cost because allies reimbursed much of it. The table prints the gross $61 billion and puts the $4.7 billion net cost in the notes; a gross-cost column without that note will mislead.[^app-b-crs-rs22926]
- **Civilian deaths are excluded.** The table’s mortality columns are military deaths by side unless a note says otherwise. It cannot support a claim about total war deaths, enemy deaths, or civilian casualties.
- **DoD civilians and contractors are outside the cells.** The Panel C rows are uniformed military only. DoD civilians (6 in Afghanistan, 13 in Iraq, and 2 in OIR) and contractors (8,189 across the post-9/11 war zones, by Crawford and Lutz’s estimate) are reported in the note, not added to the rows. ### 6.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Admissions.** “Persons obtaining lawful permanent resident status” is a legal admission category. It is not net migration, and it includes both new arrivals and adjustments of status (57 percent in FY 2024). The historical series is not a single measure: 1820–1867 counts foreign passenger arrivals at seaports, including temporary visitors (about 1.5 percent in 1856–1867) and possibly passengers who died at sea; 1868–1891 and 1895–1897 count immigrant arrivals; 1892–1894 and 1898 onward count immigrants admitted for permanent residence. Cabin-class noncitizens were excluded from 1892 to 1903; land arrivals were not completely enumerated until 1908. The 1906 break is geographic: country of origin before 1906, country of last residence from 1906; the 1906 total includes 32,897 returning U.S. residents. Use “recorded arrivals” for 1820–1891 and 1895–1897, “immigrants admitted for permanent residence” for 1892–1894 and 1898 onward, and “LPR admissions” only for the modern administrative series. Do not call the early series a count of all entrants.
- **Fiscal years.** The annual figures are federal fiscal years, and the lengths are not uniform: FY 1843 covers nine months; FY 1832 and FY 1850 cover 15 months; FY 1868 covers six months; FY 1976 covers 15 months. Do not compute annual rates across these rows without a note.
- **Net migration.** Net migration = immigration − emigration, with adjustments for temporary and unauthorized flows. Table 5 does not compute net migration. Do not subtract admissions from foreign-born stock and call the result net migration.
- **Foreign-born stock.** The foreign-born population includes naturalized citizens, LPRs, temporary migrants, refugees, asylees, and unauthorized residents. It is a stock, not a flow. A stock can rise even if admissions fall, because of births, deaths, emigration, and status changes. The 2010, 2020, and 2024 stock figures come from ACS estimates, not decennial counts; the 2024 figure is 50,234,858 ±162,664, or 14.8 percent of 340,110,990.
- **Unauthorized population.** The DHS estimate is a residual: total foreign-born minus legally resident foreign-born. It includes people with temporary protected status, DACA, and pending removal proceedings. It is a model, not a census. Panel E uses only the DHS series, is dated January 1, and is a subset of Panel D; do not add it to the stock or present it as a count of all unauthorized entries or all foreign-born noncitizens.
- **Quota totals.** The 1921 act sets a percentage formula; the official administrative allotments are 356,995 for FY 1922 and 357,803 for FY 1923–1924, before the 1924 act took effect. The 1924 interim quotas total 164,667 and took effect in FY 1925; the 1929 national-origins schedule took effect July 1, 1929. The 164,667 and 153,714 figures are sums of the proclaimed national and area quotas, not statutory totals. State “calculated from the schedule.” Print the 1921 totals with the formula, not as a figure stated in the statute or as the number admitted (357,642 in FY 1924).
- **Regions.** DHS “North America” includes Mexico, Canada, the Caribbean, and Central America. The 2022 historical table reports a combined “America” row, while the 2024 table reports North America and South America separately; Panel A keeps one “Americas” column, using the 2022 “America” figure for 1820s–2010s and the sum of the two 2024 rows for 2020–2024. Before 1906, the geographic assignment is country of origin; from 1906, country of last residence. Table 3 is region of birth. Do not compare a “region of last residence” row with a “region of birth” row without a note.
- **Decades.** The 1820s begin in 1820, the first year of the series. The 2020–2024 row is a five-year partial period, not a decade. DHS does not publish a 2020s multi-year total; calculate it from annual Table 1 totals. The 2022 Yearbook Table 2 has separate 2020, 2021, and 2022 columns; the 2024 Yearbook Table 2 is a 2015–2024 window. Do not add the 2022 columns and call the result a decade.
- **Rounding.** The 2024 Yearbook rounds cells to the nearest ten. Do not sum rounded cells and expect the published total to match exactly.
- **Arithmetic check: 2020–2024.** Summing the rounded Table 1 annual totals gives 707,360 + 740,000 + 1,018,350 + 1,172,910 + 1,364,090 = 5,002,710. Summing the rounded Table 2 regional cells gives 5,002,750, 40 more; the difference is rounding. For FY 2024 alone, the regional cells sum to 1,364,100, 10 more than the 1,364,090 total. For 2020–2022, the 2024 North America plus South America sums (275,790; 300,810; 369,570) match the 2022 “America” cells (275,790; 300,808; 369,564) within rounding, so the splice is consistent. “Unknown” is 688,230 for the five years, including 440,430 in FY 2024. Do not treat the regional shares as complete.

### 6.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Admissions are not migration.** A person can be admitted as a temporary worker, student, refugee, or asylee without becoming an LPR. A person can be an LPR and later emigrate. The table measures one legal flow.
- **Early data are arrivals or admissions, not LPR grants.** The 1820–1867 figures are passenger arrivals; 1868–1891 and 1895–1897 are immigrant arrivals; 1892–1894 and 1898 onward are immigrants admitted for permanent residence. A table that labels the whole 1820–1905 span “green cards” is wrong. The 1906 change is geographic, not a change from arrivals to LPR grants.
- **Quotas were discriminatory.** The 1921 and 1924 acts used census nationality and national-origins data to favor Northern and Western Europe and to exclude most Asians. The 1929 national-origins schedule embedded those preferences. The table should show the formula and the exclusions, not only the totals.
- **The Western Hemisphere was treated differently.** The 1924 act did not impose the same national-origins quotas on Western Hemisphere countries; later statutes changed the system. A quota table that covers only the Eastern Hemisphere must say so.
- **Foreign-born stock includes the unauthorized.** The Census Bureau’s foreign-born total and the DHS unauthorized estimate overlap. Panel D and Panel E overlap; do not add them, and do not subtract Panel E from Panel D to derive a legal foreign-born population, because the reference dates and methods differ.
- **Unauthorized estimates differ.** DHS, Pew, MPI, and CIS use different methods and produce different totals. Use one series, name it, and do not average them. DHS has not published an estimate after January 2022; do not fill the gap with another model.
- **Recent data are affected by policy and pandemic.** 2020 admissions fell sharply; 2021–2024 rebounded, with 2024 at 1,364,090. A five-year partial total is not a decade total and should not be compared with a full decade.
- **The 2024 last-residence data are incomplete.** Of 1,364,090 FY 2024 LPRs, 440,430 (32.3 percent) have an unknown region of last residence, compared with 2,430 unknown by birth. The 2020–2024 unknown total is 688,230. Do not read the 2024 last-residence regional declines, including Asia (392,140 to 326,470) and Europe (81,690 to 47,150), as real declines; by birth, Asia rose from 418,930 to 452,100 and Europe fell from 80,280 to 73,660.
- **The 1890s America row is incomplete.** The 2022 table notes that no data are available for Canada or Mexico from 1886 to 1893, which makes the 1890s “America” figure anomalously low and the 1890s total correspondingly incomplete; the 1890s decline is therefore partly an artifact of the missing data, not a complete measure of hemispheric migration.[^app-b-dhs-table2-2022]
- **ACS is not a census.** The 2010, 2020, and 2024 foreign-born stock figures are ACS estimates, not decennial counts. The 2024 estimate has a ±162,664 margin; do not treat it as exact.

### 7.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Enslaved population.** Panel B uses the unadjusted census counts. For 1790, print 697,681 enslaved within 757,208 Black; for 1860, print 3,953,760 within 4,441,830 Black. Do not mix the 1909/1918 revised 697,624 and 757,181 with the 1975 series, and do not substitute Hacker’s 706,514 modeled count for a census count. If a variant is printed, put it in the note with its vintage.
- **Enslaved share.** Divide the enslaved population by the total population and by the Black population. The 1790 share of total population is 17.8 percent (697,681 / 3,929,214) and the 1860 share is 12.6 percent (3,953,760 / 31,443,321). The 1790 share of Black population is 92.1 percent (697,681 / 757,208); the 1860 share is 89.0 percent (3,953,760 / 4,441,830). The second share is not 100 percent before 1865 because free Black people existed in every census. Do not label the enslaved share “Black share.”
- **Natural increase.** Natural increase = births − deaths. It is not the same as the change in the enslaved population, which also includes imports, sales, and escapes. Hacker’s estimates of births and imports are models; use them only with the method note.
- **Transatlantic trade.** Embarked and disembarked are different counts. The difference is mortality during the Middle Passage. Do not use “12.5 million arrived” or “10.7 million shipped.”
- **Domestic trade.** The 835,000 and 1,110,000 figures are net-migration estimates, not counts of sales. They are alternative estimates of the same flow, not a range or two components; do not add them. The Millennial Edition’s chapter Bb essay summarizes Pritchett (2001) as finding the relocation about equally divided between market transactions and planter migration; use that as the qualitative note, not as a third estimate. Do not label either figure “number sold.”
- **Caribbean benchmarks.** Panel A has three British Caribbean benchmarks. Barbados 1643 is a colonial report; Barbados 1679/80 is a property-and-enslaver census with no island-wide White count; Jamaica 1673 is a governor’s return that excludes privateers and Maroons. Do not interpolate a smooth series, do not fill the Barbados White cell with a nearby year, and do not add French, Dutch, Danish, or Spanish islands.
- **Prices.** Use the published Engerman series, Bb210, not a raw median from ICPSR 7423. The series is a standardized sample of guaranteed, unskilled males aged 18–30 in New Orleans notarized sales; it is not an average price for all enslaved people. Print nominal dollars and real 1860 dollars: real price = nominal price × 100 / Cc2 index (1860 = 100). Record the eight benchmark values and the deflator in the production file. Bb211 is the alternative for all men aged 21–38, but its published table begins in 1820.
- **Arithmetic check: 1790 and 1860.** 697,681 + 59,527 = 757,208; 3,953,760 + 488,070 = 4,441,830. The 1860 census introduction prints 487,970 free and 4,441,730 total, 100 fewer; do not mix the two vintages. The 1860 Recapitulation prints 3,542,147 “black” + 411,613 “mulatto” = 3,953,760.

Round to whole dollars; the source’s nominal values are whole dollars, and the published index is rounded, so the real values are approximate.

### 7.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **SlaveVoyages is a voyage database.** It records ships and captives where records survive. It does not record every voyage, every overland coffle, or every sale. Its own methodology essay warns that the voyage model cannot capture most overland domestic trafficking.[^app-b-slavevoyages-intra]
- **The transatlantic total is an estimate.** The 12.5 million embarked and 10.7 million disembarked figures are model-based estimates, not a census. Regional totals have margins of error.
- **Domestic-trade estimates disagree.** The 835,000 and 1,110,000 figures use different assumptions about children and census survival. They are alternative estimates of the same net flow, not a range; the gap is a substantive methodological disagreement, not a rounding error.
- **Caribbean data are sparse.** The 1643 Barbados figure comes from a colonial report; the 1679/80 census is a property-and-enslaver census with no island-wide White count; the 1673 Jamaica return excludes privateers and Maroons. None is representative of all British, French, Dutch, or Danish islands, which is why Panel A is British Caribbean only.
- **Prices are urban and selected.** New Orleans was a major market, but the Bb210 sample is restricted to guaranteed, unskilled young men and may not represent Upper South or rural sales. The sample overrepresents sales with notarized bills, and the real series depends on the Cc2 deflator.
- **Census slave schedules are not a full count of individuals before 1850.** The 1790–1840 census aggregates enslaved people by household; the 1850 and 1860 slave schedules list individuals but are still subject to undercount and transcription error.
- **The 1790 count is unstable.** The *Historical Statistics* footnote prints 697,681; the 1909/1918 revisions print 697,624; Hacker models 706,514. The table uses 697,681 with 757,208 and puts the variants in the note; it does not average them.
- **The 1860 free Black count has two vintages.** The census introduction prints 487,970 free “colored” and a “colored” total of 4,441,730; *Historical Statistics* A 91–104 prints 4,441,830 Black, implying 488,070 free. The 100-person difference is a vintage difference; Panel B uses the *Historical Statistics* figure and the note gives the census variant.

- **Early public finance is not modern accounting.** The 1789–1915 figures are based on warrants issued; trust funds and the unified budget change the totals after 1934. A long series that ignores these breaks will mislead.[^app-b-hsus-y335-342]
- **Railroad mileage peaks and declines.** The series is useful for the 19th-century build-out, not as a measure of transport output. Traffic, ton-miles, and revenue are different series. The series ends in 1970; the 2000 and 2024 cells are n.a.
- **Steel is cyclical.** A single benchmark year can misstate capacity or decline. Use several years or label the cycle. The series ends in 1960; the 1970, 2000, and 2024 cells are n.a.
- **Unemployment before 1940 is reconstructed.** The 1930s rate is one of the most contested numbers in U.S. economic history. The table uses Darby–Weir in the cells and prints Lebergott in the note; it does not average. The choice changes the 1940 level by 5.1 points and moves the peak from 1933 (Lebergott, about 25 percent) to 1932 (Darby–Weir, 22.9 percent).

- **GDP per capita before 1929 is a scholarly reconstruction.** Maddison is in 2011 international dollars and ends in 2022. BEA is in chained 2017 dollars. The 1900/1930 break is a change of basis, not a growth spurt.
- **Inequality measures are not interchangeable.** Top income shares, the Gini index, wealth shares, and consumption inequality can move in different directions. - **Union density is not union power.** Density measures membership, not bargaining coverage, strikes, or political influence.
- **Newspaper and telephone data have different denominators and endpoints.** Circulation is copies; telephone is lines or households. Panel D ends at 2000 because the post-2000 newspaper and telephone measures are not comparable.

### 9.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **TVA dates.** Construction start and completion are not the same as first power generation. Use the dates on the dam pages and label them. All nine dams in the 1936 plan were structurally complete by 1944; Kentucky Reservoir filled in 1945. Wilson Dam predates the plan and is not one of the nine.
- **TVA capacity.** Capacity is modern summer net dependable capacity after turbine upgrades, not 1940s nameplate. TVA’s own pages sometimes disagree: Watts Bar is 182 megawatts in the page prose and 196 in its Facts & Figures; Chickamauga is 119 and 142. Use the Facts & Figures value, label it modern, and put the discrepancy in the note.
- **CCC camp counts.** The 1,330 figure is June 29, 1933; the 2,650 figure is cumulative camps established by 1935; the 6,036 figure is cumulative recorded camp sites across the program. These are not the same measure. Panel B does not print a camp-count series.
- **CCC enrollees.** The official monthly series peaks at 505,782 in August 1935. The NPS “1936” height reads as fiscal year 1936, not an August 1936 count. The three million figure is cumulative enrollment; do not compare it with a monthly strength.
- **Land in farms.** Land in farms includes cropland, pasture, woodland, and wasteland. Cropland harvested is a subset. The 1940 and 1930 farm and land figures are April 1 inventory dates; 1935 is January 1. Cropland harvested refers to the prior crop year: 1939, 1934, and 1929. Do not treat the year labels as one observation date.
- **Dust Bowl counties.** The 120,859 to 97,606 figure is for the 20 Soil Conservation Service counties in Colorado, Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas, not all Great Plains counties. The decline is a population change, not a net migration count; births and deaths are part of the change.
- **Migration.** The 1935–1940 census asks where people lived five years earlier. It measures gross moves, not net migration. A state can gain migrants and still lose population if deaths and out-migration exceed births and in-migration. The 876,829 in-migrants and 211,963 out-migrants are gross interstate moves; their difference is +664,866 net interstate migration.
- **California.** The state’s 1930–1940 growth was +1,230,136. Net interstate migration was +664,866, or 9.6 percent of the 1940 population. The arithmetic residual, +565,270, is not net migration; it includes natural increase, net international migration, and coverage differences. Do not attribute all of California’s growth to the Dust Bowl.
- **Arithmetic check.** The count of Wilson, Norris, and the nine plan dams is 11; Table 8 prints seven and notes the other four. The CCC panel has five rows and no state rows. Land use has three national and three state rows; population has five. The total is 23 rows. California: 876,829 − 211,963 = 664,866; 1,230,136 − 664,866 = 565,270.

### 9.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **TVA is an agency history.** The TVA pages are useful for dates and capacity, but they are institutional accounts. They do not measure the distribution of benefits, the displacement of valley residents, or the environmental costs. The capacity figures are modern, not 1940s, and the wartime additions sit outside the 1936 nine.
- **CCC counts measure different things.** The NPS, NARA, PBS, and CCC Legacy figures use different dates and definitions. The NPS “1936” height reads as fiscal year 1936; the official monthly peak was August 1935. NARA’s 2,650 is cumulative, CCC Legacy’s 6,036 is cumulative sites, and the PBS state counts are a secondary reproduction.
- **CCC camps were segregated.** The program included Black and Native American enrollees, but most camps were segregated, and the Indian Division operated differently. A camp or enrollment count does not show who was served or under what conditions.[^app-b-nps-ccc]
- **Census of Agriculture data are farm-based.** They cover land in farms, not all land. They miss non-farm land, and they classify fallow and pasture differently across censuses. The 1940 and 1930 enumeration dates are April 1; 1935 is January 1; cropland harvested is a prior-year figure.
- **Dust Bowl migration is contested.** Long and Siu find that the popular “Okie” exodus is overstated: out-migration was only slightly higher than in the 1920s, farmers were less likely to move, and California was not a disproportionate destination. Their 20-county definition is not the whole Plains, and the 2018 article confirms the working paper’s totals.[^app-b-long-siu-2016]
- **The Dust Bowl was not only drought.** Mechanization, wheat prices, credit, and federal policy all mattered. A table of population and land use cannot establish causation.
- **The 1940 totals differ by universe.** Table 8 uses the continental total of 131,669,275 for 1940 and 122,775,046 for 1930. Do not substitute a 50-state retrospective total that adds Alaska and Hawaii, and do not mix it with Table 1’s 1950-and-later 50-state series.[^app-b-census-1940-pop]
- **TVA and CCC counts do not measure outcomes.** They measure projects and enrollees, not income, soil conservation, electrification, or long-run economic change.

- Do not add Civil War roster deaths to excess-mortality estimates, or present Barceló’s or Hacker’s war total as a Union or Confederate row total.
- Do not add DoD civilians or contractors to the uniformed-military casualty cells in Table 4.
- Do not compare CRS military-only costs with Costs of War budgetary costs, or print the $8 trillion total as a war cost.
- Do not print the Persian Gulf net cost in the gross-cost column or compute a constant-dollar net figure.
- Do not treat the 4,234 Philippine-American War enlisted deaths as total deaths; the War Department total is 4,374.
- Do not compare immigration admissions with foreign-born stock as if they were the same measure.
- Do not call pre-1892 entries “LPR admissions” or “green cards”; label the series by period, and do not treat the 1895–1897 return to arrival counts as a continuous admissions series.
- Do not compare 2024 region-of-last-residence shares with earlier years without the 440,430 “Unknown” note; the FY 2024 last-residence distribution is incomplete.
- Do not treat the 2010, 2020, or 2024 ACS foreign-born figures as decennial census counts, and do not add Panel E’s unauthorized estimate to Panel D’s stock or subtract it to derive a legal foreign-born population.
- Do not treat SlaveVoyages as a domestic-trade census or as a count of all enslaved people.
- Do not present Goldin’s 835,000 and Tadman’s 1,110,000 as a range or add them; they are alternative estimates of net forced migrants, not sales or components.
- Do not compute a raw median from ICPSR 7423 or label it a prime-field-hand price; use the published Bb210 series and the Cc2 deflator.
- Do not add French, Dutch, Danish, or Spanish Caribbean benchmarks to Table 6, Panel A; it is British Caribbean only.
- Do not treat the Barbados 1679/80 White population as zero or estimate it from another year; print “n.a.” and give Dunn’s adjacent benchmarks in the note.
- Do not mix the 1860 census free Black count (487,970; total 4,441,730) with the *Historical Statistics* count (488,070 implied; total 4,441,830).
- Do not compute turnout from the voting-age population and compare it with a voting-eligible-population rate.
- Do not print a single 1824 national popular-vote total or share, and do not compare the 18-state American Presidency Project total with the 14-state Stanwood total as if they were the same series.
- Do not count faithless electors by mixing presidential votes, vice-presidential votes, abstentions, and invalidated or replaced votes, and do not treat unpledged electors as faithless.
- Do not use “urban” across censuses without the definition in force.
- Do not use “Black” and “enslaved” interchangeably. Not all Black people were enslaved, and not all enslaved people were Black.
- Do not present CCC camp counts from different dates as one series.
- Do not describe the 1936 plan’s nine dams as the whole TVA system, count Wilson Dam among the nine, or add the wartime dams to the nine.
- Do not present modern TVA summer net dependable capacity as 1940s installed capacity, and do not average the prose and Facts & Figures values when TVA’s own pages disagree.

- Do not use the PBS August 1933 state camp counts as an official series; they are a secondary reproduction for Map 26 verification only.
- Do not treat “Hispanic” as a race across all censuses; the categories change.
- Do not add the enslaved population to the Black population; the first is a subset of the second before 1865.
- Do not compare a city-proper population with a metropolitan-area population.
- Do not splice the 2022 PSZ appendix tables (through 2019/2021) onto RealTimeInequality or a later WID release; Table 7’s top-share column uses one WID 2025/2026 vintage through 2024.
