# Chapter 29. The Progressive Movement (1900–1916)

> “I speak for neither the operators nor the miners but for the general public.”[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
>
> — President Theodore Roosevelt, opening address to the anthracite coal conference, Washington, D.C., October 3, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt Papers, Library of Congress, as quoted in Jonathan Grossman, *The Coal Strike of 1902: Turning Point in U.S. Policy*, 1974.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

> “It is time to set the public welfare in the first place.”[^ch29-progressive-platform]
>
> — Progressive Party Platform, adopted at Chicago, August 1912.[^ch29-progressive-platform]

> “The Progressive Era is more than a matter of nostalgia. It is the argument of this book that progressivism created much of our contemporary predicament.”[^ch29-mcgerr]
>
> — Michael McGerr, *A Fierce Discontent: The Rise and Fall of the Progressive Movement in America, 1870–1920*, 2003, Preface.[^ch29-mcgerr]

A president claimed to speak for a public that stood above capital and labor. A new party claimed that the public welfare outranked private interest. A historian claimed that the reform impulse left a difficult inheritance. The three agree that the era enlarged public authority. They disagree about whom that authority served. This chapter asks who benefited when reformers enlarged public authority.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-mcgerr][^ch29-check-epigraphs]

On Friday, October 3, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt received the owners and the miners’ representatives in the temporary White House at 22 Lafayette Place in Washington, D.C. A strike in the anthracite coalfields of Pennsylvania had begun on May 12 and had run for 163 days. It threatened a coal famine in the eastern cities. Roosevelt had no clear legal authority to intervene, but he sent telegrams summoning both sides.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

Roosevelt was still unable to leave his wheelchair after a carriage accident a month earlier. He disclaimed any right to intervene. He asked instead for an immediate resumption of mining to meet “the crying needs of the people,” and he appealed to the contestants’ patriotism and to “individual sacrifices for the general good.”[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike] John Mitchell, president of the United Mine Workers of America, treated the summons itself as recognition and was conciliatory. The operators, whose spokesman was George F. Baer, were, in Roosevelt’s word, “insolent.” They berated Mitchell and refused to deal with the union.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

Roosevelt wrote to Senator Marcus Hanna that evening that he had tried and failed. Secretary of War Elihu Root and the banker J. P. Morgan then brokered an arbitration formula. The strike ended on October 23, 1902, with an Anthracite Coal Strike Commission. The commission awarded most miners a 10 percent increase and a nine-hour day, and it refused to recognize the union. Of roughly 147,000 strikers, about 30,000 soon left the region; the commissioner of labor reported that 8,000 to 10,000 of them returned to Europe.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

The record establishes the date, the place, the participants, the deadlock, and the award. It does not record the room’s atmosphere, the exact words of unrecorded exchanges, or the private calculations of Mitchell, Baer, Root, or Morgan. In October 1902 no one knew what the commission would award, whether the federal government would return as an arbiter in later disputes, or that the episode would be read as the birth of a reform presidency. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch29-check-scene]

Who benefited when reformers enlarged public authority?

This chapter covers the years from 1900 to 1916. It follows the reformers and their state and municipal experiments; the muckraking magazines, the social settlements, and the campaign for workplace safety; Roosevelt’s Square Deal and conservation; the Taft years, the Republican split, the election of 1912, and Wilson’s New Freedom; and the suffrage campaign, federal segregation, immigration enforcement at Angel Island, and the industrial violence that marked reform’s limits.[^ch29-check-scope]

It draws on statutes and court opinions, the reports of investigating commissions, the magazines and books of the muckrakers, the records of settlements and unions, presidential papers, and the campaign data of 1912. It treats “Progressive,” “reform,” “muckraker,” “conservation,” “reclamation,” the “square deal,” the “New Freedom,” and “the public interest” as contested period terms that require definition rather than neutral descriptions. “Progressive” here names an overlapping coalition, not a single party or creed. “Muckraker” began as a period insult. “Conservation” meant managed use, not untouched nature. “Reclamation” meant irrigation. The “square deal” was Roosevelt’s phrase for evenhanded treatment of capital, labor, and the public. The “New Freedom” was Wilson’s name for his program. “The public interest” was a claim that each side in a dispute defined for itself.[^ch29-check-scope][^ch29-check-names]

The archive over-represents officials, reformers, editors, and experts and under-represents the workers, tenants, immigrants, and disfranchised people whose lives reform changed. The commission reports show what inspectors recorded. The magazines show what editors printed. Neither shows what every worker or family believed. The imbalance is named here rather than filled with invented voices.[^ch29-check-silences]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the reformers and their state and local experiments. §2 follows the muckraking magazines, the social settlements, and the campaign for workplace safety. §3 asks what Roosevelt’s Square Deal and conservation accomplished and cost. §4 turns to Taft, the election of 1912, and Wilson’s New Freedom. §5 examines woman suffrage, federal segregation, immigration enforcement, and the limits of reform. §6 asks how historians have explained the Progressive movement and whom it served. The chapter begins in October 1902 with a president who summoned the owners and the miners to his own house and claimed to speak for the public.[^ch29-check-scope]

## 1. Reformers and state and local experiments

The Progressive movement was a coalition before it was a program. Its members agreed that government should address the dislocations of industrial society. They disagreed about which dislocations mattered, who should pay for the remedy, and who counted as the public.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-yawp-progressive]

### 1.1 Who were the Progressives?

The most visible Progressives were educated professionals: lawyers, journalists, physicians, social workers, and academics. They were tied to the emerging national economy and drawn to expert administration. Following Robert Wiebe, historians have described this group as a “new middle class” seeking to stabilize a disordered society by building national institutions.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe]

Other historians have located much of the reform energy in working-class and immigrant communities and in urban political machines. Members of those communities pressed for workmen’s compensation, wage-and-hour laws, and public services. The two readings disagree less about the facts than about who drove reform.[^ch29-glencoe-historians]

Black reformers and organized women pressed their own agendas. Black club women, the Niagara Movement, and later the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People demanded civil rights and an end to racial violence, and they often found the White-dominated reform organizations indifferent or hostile. Women’s clubs and settlement workers built a public role for women while usually accepting the era’s gender order. The coalition was real, and it was unequal.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-naacp-history][^ch29-check-race]

The record can show who joined which organization and what each demanded. It cannot reduce the coalition to a single motive. The constituencies overlapped; they did not form one movement with one interest.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-check-silences]

### 1.2 The Wisconsin Idea and the machinery of direct democracy

The most durable early Progressive achievement was procedural. Reformers rewrote the rules of representation to weaken party machines and give voters more direct control. In doing so, they redefined who counted as a competent citizen.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots][^ch29-progressive-platform]

Robert M. La Follette, elected governor of Wisconsin in 1900 and later a United States senator, built a program of state regulation staffed by university experts. The arrangement became known as the Wisconsin Idea. He pressed for direct primaries, campaign-spending limits, and a state railroad commission, and he treated the university as an instrument of public administration.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots]

The instruments spread. The direct primary let party members choose candidates rather than conventions; South Carolina adopted it for statewide elections in 1896, and Florida for presidential nominations in 1901. The initiative let voters propose laws; South Dakota adopted it in 1898, and 20 states had it by 1920. The referendum let voters approve or reject laws. The recall let them remove officials; Oregon adopted it in 1910, and 12 states had it by 1920.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots]

The Seventeenth Amendment completed the campaign at the federal level. The 1912 Progressive platform called for the direct election of United States senators. Congress sent the amendment to the states in 1912, and the states ratified it on April 8, 1913. The first regular popular Senate elections followed in 1914.[^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-archives-17th]

The same years that widened the franchise through these devices also saw the completion of Black disfranchisement in the South. They also saw the adoption of at-large and commission systems that diluted the votes of immigrant and working-class city dwellers. The democratizing and the narrowing belonged together; the machinery was not simply inclusive.[^ch29-check-race][^ch29-check-ch26][^ch29-municipal-reform]

### 1.3 Municipal reform, efficiency, and the managed city

City government was a principal Progressive laboratory. Its reforms substituted trained administration for elected patronage. That change promised efficiency, and it often reduced the influence of the very neighborhoods it served.[^ch29-check-race][^ch29-check-silences]

After a hurricane destroyed much of Galveston, Texas, in September 1900, the city replaced its aldermanic government with a commission. The new government, adopted in 1901, concentrated legislative and executive power in a small board elected at large. Each commissioner ran a department. The plan spread, and by 1913 about 300 cities had adopted some version of it.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots][^ch29-municipal-reform]

Beginning with Staunton, Virginia, in 1908, cities appointed a professional city manager to run daily administration under an elected council. Staunton’s council chose Charles E. Ashburner as its first manager on April 2, 1908. The manager would “devote his entire time to the duties of his office” and have “entire charge and control of all the Executive work of the City.” The form separated administration from electoral politics, at least in theory.[^ch29-staunton][^ch29-openstax-grassroots]

Reformers argued that skilled administrators, not politicians, should make policy, and they borrowed the language of business management for city government. The ideal was a claim about expertise, not a neutral description. At-large elections and nonpartisan ballots, adopted with the manager and commission plans, weakened ward representation. In the South they worked alongside disfranchisement. The ward politicians and the immigrant and working-class voters they represented often lost influence as administration was insulated from politics.[^ch29-municipal-reform][^ch29-check-race]

### 1.4 The courts and the boundaries of the police power

State and federal courts set the outer limit of reform. Their reasoning sorted workers by gender and by the kind of labor the law could touch.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-lochner]

In *Lochner v. New York* (1905), the Supreme Court struck down a New York law limiting bakery workers to a ten-hour day and a sixty-hour week. The Court held that the law interfered with the liberty of contract protected by the Fourteenth Amendment and that the state had not shown a sufficient health justification. Justice Rufus Peckham wrote for a 5–4 majority that the trade of a baker was not unhealthy enough to authorize the legislature’s interference. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. dissented, writing that “the Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer’s Social Statics.”[^ch29-lochner][^ch29-check-lochner]

In *Muller v. Oregon* (1908), the Court upheld Oregon’s ten-hour law for women in laundries and factories. Justice David Brewer reasoned that women’s “physical structure and the performance of maternal functions” placed them at a disadvantage and justified a difference in legislation. The decision protected some women workers by treating them as a class apart, a logic later used against sex-based equality.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-brandeis-brief]

The Oregon case was argued partly through social and medical evidence compiled by Florence Kelley and Josephine Goldmark for the National Consumers League and filed by Louis Brandeis. The “Brandeis brief” presented the facts of women’s working lives rather than only legal precedent. It filled 113 pages, 98 of them with data drawn from factory inspectors, physicians, unions, economists, and social workers. The Court cited “a very copious collection” of material, and the brief became a model for sociological jurisprudence.[^ch29-brandeis-brief][^ch29-check-brandeis]

The courts permitted protective laws for women and children while invalidating general hours laws for men. Reform therefore advanced along the lines the judiciary allowed, and the law hardened a gendered divide between workers who could claim protection and workers who could not.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-lochner][^ch29-check-gender]

## 2. Muckraking, settlements, and workplace safety

The magazines, the settlements, and the factory investigations made reform a national subject. They turned corruption, corporate power, and industrial danger into stories that readers could follow. They also showed the limits of exposure: a scandal could move a legislature, but it could not by itself give workers a share of authority over the workplace.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-dol-fic]

### 2.1 The magazines and the exposure of corruption

The mass magazines made reform a national subject by treating corruption and corporate power as scandals. Their exposés moved readers and legislatures even when the reporting overstated its case.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-loc-muckrakers]

In October 1902 *McClure’s Magazine* began Lincoln Steffens’s series on municipal corruption. In January 1903 it carried Steffens’s Minneapolis installment alongside the opening of Ida Tarbell’s series on Standard Oil. Circulation rose, and other publishers commissioned their own exposés. The magazine became a national institution for investigation.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]

The roster was broad. Tarbell’s *History of the Standard Oil Company* (1902–04) examined the monopoly. Steffens’s *The Shame of the Cities* (1902–03) dissected urban machines. Ray Stannard Baker’s *Following the Color Line* (1907–08) documented segregation. David Graham Phillips’s “The Treason of the Senate” (1906) attacked senators’ ties to corporations. Samuel Hopkins Adams’s “The Great American Fraud” (1905–06) exposed patent medicines.[^ch29-loc-muckrakers]

Roosevelt coined “muckraker” in a speech on April 14, 1906, drawing on John Bunyan’s man with the muck-rake. He meant it as a rebuke to those who saw only filth. Many journalists adopted it as a badge of honor.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-loc-muckrakers]

The magazines can show what editors chose to print and what readers bought. They cannot show that a given exposé caused a given law. They set an agenda; they did not manufacture reform by themselves.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-check-silences]

### 2.2 *The Jungle*, pure food, and the meat inspection acts

Upton Sinclair wrote *The Jungle* to build support for socialism. He instead produced federal regulation of food. The episode shows how the era’s reform could serve ends its authors did not intend.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]

Sinclair, a socialist, investigated Chicago’s Packingtown for the *Appeal to Reason* in 1904. He published the novel on January 25, 1906. It followed a Lithuanian immigrant family through the stockyards and described unsanitary meat and exploited labor in detail. The book was a novel, not an inspection report, and its scenes compressed details from many workers’ lives. Its power came from the story it told about the people who made the meat.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-yawp-progressive]

Roosevelt was at first annoyed by the book. He ordered an investigation that confirmed unclean methods. After the Senate passed a pure-food bill, the House wrangled until Roosevelt released the report. He signed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act on June 30, 1906. The laws regulated the food supply and the labels on medicines. They did not regulate the workers’ hours or wages.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]

Sinclair later remarked that he had aimed at the public’s heart and by accident hit it in the stomach. The sentence captures how reform reached consumers more readily than it reached workers.[^ch29-sinclair-quote][^ch29-check-quotations]

The episode shows that public authority could be enlisted against corporate abuse when voters were aroused as consumers. It also shows that the same authority was slower to protect the workers who produced the goods. Both results belonged to the same reform impulse.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-check-race]

### 2.3 Settlements, Hull-House, and social work

The social settlements put reformers into the neighborhoods that industry had built. The work they did — kindergartens, clinics, classes, and investigations — became the evidence base for later law.[^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-check-gender]

In 1889 Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr moved into a residence on Chicago’s Near West Side and opened Hull-House. It grew into the country’s most influential settlement. Its residents lived “with” rather than “for” their immigrant neighbors, and they built an employment bureau, an art gallery, libraries, English and citizenship classes, and a theater.[^ch29-hullhouse]

Hull-House residents such as Florence Kelley carried what they found into law through organizations such as the National Consumers League and the factory committees of several states. Frances Perkins, who worked with the factory investigations in New York, later applied their methods as Secretary of Labor.[^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-dol-fic]

The settlements can show the conditions investigators documented and the programs they built. They cannot show that their neighbors shared the reformers’ goals. The settlement was an encounter between unequal parties, not a one-way gift.[^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-check-silences]

### 2.4 The Triangle fire and the fire escape

The Triangle Shirtwaist fire of March 25, 1911, killed workers whose strike for safety had failed the year before. The disaster turned a documented workplace danger into law.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-cornell-triangle]

Fire broke out late on a Saturday afternoon in the workrooms of the Triangle Waist Company, which occupied the eighth, ninth, and tenth floors of the Asch Building near Washington Square. The exits were inadequate. The stairways and the fire escape failed to hold the crowd. The fire department’s ladders reached only the seventh floor, and workers at the windows jumped.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-dol-fic]

The Factory Investigating Commission’s preliminary report states that 145 employees, mainly women and girls, lost their lives. Other accounts give 146. The owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, were tried on manslaughter charges and acquitted.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-yawp-progressive]

**In Focus.** The fire escape on the Asch Building was a legal device that was supposed to do one thing: let people leave a burning building alive. The Factory Investigating Commission’s *Preliminary Report* found “inadequate fire-escape facilities” and “conditions in factories and manufacturing establishments that constituted a daily menace to the lives of the thousands of working men, women and children.” The report did not describe the escape as a symbol. It described a mechanism that had been inspected, found wanting, and written into a legislative record. At the commission’s first public hearing, on October 10, 1911, Fire Chief Edward F. Croker, Labor Department inspector G. I. Harmon, and Fire Marshal William L. Beers testified about the Asch Building and the escape. Read together, the report and the testimony show what a fire escape was supposed to do, how this one failed, and how the failure became evidence. They do not show what each worker saw or did. The investigation documented the danger. Who gained the power to prevent the next one?[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-triangle-testimony][^ch29-cornell-triangle][^ch29-check-focus][^ch29-check-numbers]

### 2.5 The Factory Investigating Commission and the safety laws

The fire produced one of the most thorough state investigations of the era and a body of safety and health law that rebuilt the state’s labor code.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-fic-preliminary]

The New York legislature created the Factory Investigating Commission by Chapter 561 of the Laws of 1911, signed on June 30, 1911. The commission included Senator Robert F. Wagner, Assemblyman Alfred E. Smith, and Samuel Gompers. It was charged to investigate factory conditions and recommend remedial law. Beginning in October 1911, it took testimony from fire officials, labor inspectors, manufacturers, and workers about the Asch Building and other workplaces.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-triangle-testimony]

The commission’s work led the legislature to adopt workmen’s compensation and to revise much of the state’s occupational safety and health law. It recommended more stairwells and exits, fire walls, fireproof construction, sprinklers, alarms, and better sanitary facilities. Its methods became a model for other states.[^ch29-dol-fic]

The reforms that followed regulated the conditions investigators could document. They did not give the workers a share of authority over the workplace, and the owners were acquitted. The section closes on that gap.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-check-silences]

## 3. Roosevelt’s Square Deal and conservation

Roosevelt made the presidency the center of reform. He used the antitrust law, the railroad acts, and the public lands to assert federal supervision over a national economy. He also made the federal government a party to a private industrial conflict. The results were real, and they were limited by what a president could persuade or order without a statute.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

### 3.1 The Square Deal and the coal strike

Roosevelt’s handling of the 1902 coal strike made the federal government a broker in industrial disputes. The “Square Deal” named his promise to treat capital, labor, and the public evenhandedly. The promise enlarged presidential power.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

After the October 3 conference failed, Root and Morgan arranged arbitration. The strike ended on October 23 with a commission that awarded most miners a 10 percent increase and a nine-hour day while refusing to recognize the union. Mitchell claimed de facto recognition through the arbitration board the award created.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

The episode marked the federal government’s turn from strikebreaker to peacemaker. Roosevelt argued that “the public” had an interest in the dispute. That interest was a claim the president asserted, not a settled fact. The commission’s award showed that the claim could produce a settlement. It did not show that the public and the miners wanted the same thing.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

The operators kept their property and refused recognition. The government arbitrated terms but did not take the mines. Much of the Square Deal consisted of presidential persuasion rather than statutory power. That was its strength and its limit.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

### 3.2 Trusts, railroads, and the reach of federal regulation

Roosevelt used the antitrust law and railroad regulation to assert federal supervision over corporations that the states could not control alone.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

In 1902 the Justice Department sued the Northern Securities Company, a railroad combination assembled by J. P. Morgan and others, under the Sherman Antitrust Act. In 1904 the Supreme Court ordered it dissolved. The case signaled that a Republican administration would regulate corporate combinations.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

The Elkins Act of 1903 ended the rebate system by which large shippers won lower rates. The Hepburn Act of 1906 strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission’s power to set rates. Together they gave the federal government a lasting instrument over interstate carriers.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-check-dates]

Roosevelt believed big business was a permanent feature of the economy. He sought to regulate it rather than break it up, and he said he acted to forestall socialism. Regulation was both a curb on corporate power and a stabilization of it.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

### 3.3 Conservation and the public domain

Roosevelt’s conservation program withdrew vast lands from private disposal and put them under federal management for sustained use. It was an enlargement of federal authority over the West and its resources.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-usbr-reclamation]

The Reclamation Act of June 17, 1902, funded irrigation works from the sale of federal lands and required water users to repay construction costs. The Reclamation Service was created within the Geological Survey in July 1902. Its advocates argued that irrigation would make homes on family farms in the arid West.[^ch29-usbr-reclamation]

Roosevelt created the present United States Forest Service. He established 150 national forests, 51 federal bird reserves, four national game preserves, five national parks, and 18 national monuments. The Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906, let him designate monuments without Congress, which is how he protected the Grand Canyon. In all he set aside roughly 230 million acres.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation]

Conservation meant managing resources for continued use, not leaving nature untouched. Roosevelt reserved the forests for use under federal supervision. The program was an exercise of state power over land and water.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation]

### 3.4 The cost of conservation

The same program that preserved forests and canyons also displaced the people who lived on the reserved lands. It reorganized the West around federal authority. Conservation must therefore be judged by who gained and who lost access.[^ch29-check-conservation][^ch29-check-race]

Federal forest and park reservations often overlapped Native homelands and the grazing and hunting grounds of rural communities. The new rules restricted their use. In Yosemite, the park created in 1890 overlapped the homeland of the Ahwahneechee, who had been attacked and dispersed in 1851, and whose descendants were later restricted and finally evicted. The dates after 1916 are consequences, not facts the actors knew.[^ch29-yosemite][^ch29-spence]

Preservationists who wanted nature protected from use, such as John Muir, and conservationists who wanted it managed for use, such as Gifford Pinchot, disagreed about the purpose of the public lands. Their dispute would divide the movement in the years after Roosevelt left office.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-check-dates]

The record can show the acreage, the proclamations, and the rules. It cannot show that the people removed from the reserves consented or that the program’s benefits reached them. That asymmetry is part of the record.[^ch29-check-conservation][^ch29-check-silences]

## 4. Taft, 1912, and Wilson’s New Freedom

Roosevelt’s successor inherited a reforming coalition and split it. The Republican rupture of 1909–12 opened the way for a Democratic president who extended the regulatory state while narrowing its promises. The years from 1909 to 1916 are a study in how reform advanced through division and how it stopped at the color line and the workplace door.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-check-dates]

### 4.1 Taft and the rupture over conservation

William Howard Taft, Roosevelt’s chosen successor, continued antitrust suits but alienated the conservationists. The resulting split opened a path between the Republican factions.[^ch29-taft-domestic]

In 1909 Interior Secretary Richard Ballinger was accused by a subordinate in the General Land Office, Louis R. Glavis, of opening Alaskan coal lands to private claimants. Gifford Pinchot, the chief forester, pressed the charge. Taft sided with Ballinger and dismissed Pinchot in January 1910. The affair convinced Roosevelt’s allies that Taft had abandoned conservation.[^ch29-taft-domestic][^ch29-ballinger]

Taft signed the Payne–Aldrich tariff of 1909 after promising revision. The high rates disappointed reformers in his own party. He later called it the best tariff bill ever passed. The tariff and the conservation fight together became the sources of the Republican split.[^ch29-taft-domestic]

The record can show the charges, the dismissal, and the tariff. The dispute over Ballinger’s intent remains contested. The accusation was a charge, not a settled fact.[^ch29-check-sources]

### 4.2 The Republican split and the Progressive Party

Roosevelt returned to politics with a program of national regulation stronger than his earlier one. When the Republicans renominated Taft in 1912, Roosevelt left to lead a new party.[^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-check-dates]

In a speech at Osawatomie, Kansas, on August 31, 1910, Roosevelt called for federal regulation of corporations and a stronger national government. He named the doctrine the New Nationalism. It extended his earlier trust regulation and separated him from Taft.[^ch29-osawatomie]

In August 1912 the new Progressive Party — the “Bull Moose” party — nominated Roosevelt and adopted a platform calling to “set the public welfare in the first place.” The platform pledged direct primaries, direct election of senators, the initiative, referendum, and recall, and equal suffrage. It also promised social and industrial justice: safety standards, child-labor prohibition, minimum wages for women, and social insurance.[^ch29-progressive-platform]

The Republican split divided the party’s reform wing from its conservative leadership. It shaped the outcome of the election. The split was a contingent choice, not an inevitability.[^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-app-1912]

### 4.3 The election of 1912

The election gave the Democrats the White House with less than half the popular vote because the Republicans were divided. It measured how far reform sentiment had reshaped both parties.[^ch29-archives-1912][^ch29-app-1912]

Woodrow Wilson won 435 of 531 electoral votes with about 42 percent of the popular vote. Roosevelt took about 27 percent and 88 electoral votes. Taft took about 23 percent and 8. Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist, took about 6 percent and no electoral votes. California split its electoral votes between Wilson and Roosevelt.[^ch29-archives-1912][^ch29-tah-1912][^ch29-app-1912]

### 4.4 Wilson’s New Freedom

Wilson’s program lowered the tariff, created a central bank, and put new federal agencies over business. It extended the regulatory state his predecessor had begun.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

The Underwood–Simmons Act of 1913 achieved the largest tariff reductions since the Civil War. Average rates fell from about 40 percent to about 27 percent. The act offset lost revenue with a graduated income tax authorized by the Sixteenth Amendment, which was ratified on February 3, 1913, and proclaimed on February 25. Wilson broke precedent by addressing Congress in person to present the program.[^ch29-underwood][^ch29-archives-16th][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 created twelve regional reserve banks under a presidentially appointed board. The system could adjust interest rates and the money supply, and it became the most important domestic achievement of the administration. It was signed on December 23, 1913.[^ch29-federal-reserve][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

The Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914 created a board to investigate unfair practices. The Clayton Antitrust Act, signed on October 15, 1914, prohibited price discrimination and interlocking directorates and declared that labor and farm organizations were not illegal combinations in restraint of trade, though the courts would narrow that protection. Wilson also signed the Adamson Act of 1916, granting railroad workers an eight-hour day.[^ch29-ftc-clayton][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-check-dates]

Wilson framed his program as the “New Freedom.” It promised to remove special privilege and restore competition rather than to supervise monopoly through a strong state, as Roosevelt proposed. The New Freedom and the New Nationalism were rivals with different theories of government.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

### 4.5 The limits of the New Freedom

Wilson’s reforms stopped at several boundaries. The two most consequential were child labor and race.[^ch29-archives-keating-owen][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

Congress passed the Keating–Owen Act in 1916. It barred from interstate commerce goods produced by children under 14 or by children of 14 to 16 working more than eight hours a day or six days a week. In *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918), the Supreme Court held the act outside the commerce power, and the law fell. The date is a consequence beyond this chapter’s range.[^ch29-archives-keating-owen][^ch29-oyez-hammer][^ch29-check-dates]

Wilson allowed his cabinet to introduce segregation into departments such as the Post Office and the Treasury. Many Black federal employees were downgraded or discharged. The administration separated workstations, lunchrooms, and restrooms, and from 1914 it required photographs with civil-service applications. Wilson defended the separation as a way to reduce friction.[^ch29-postal-segregation][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

In 1914 William Monroe Trotter of the National Independent Political League and the Boston *Guardian* confronted Wilson at the White House over the segregation of Black federal employees. Wilson declared that segregation would continue and ordered Trotter from the office. Trotter took the case to the press. Wilson had courted Black voters in 1912 with promises of fair dealing and then disappointed them.[^ch29-whitehouse-trotter][^ch29-postal-segregation]

The pattern shows that the New Freedom’s universal language did not include racial equality. Wilson’s reform achievements and his segregation belonged to the same administration.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-check-race]

## 5. Suffrage, segregation, immigration, and reform’s limits

The reforming state expanded its capacity and its exclusions at the same time. The suffrage campaign widened the public, and the federal government segregated its own offices. The immigration station at Angel Island enforced exclusion, and the Colorado coalfields showed what happened when the state chose not to arbitrate. These developments belonged to the same years and the same government.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-nps-ludlow]

### 5.1 The suffrage movement and the winning plan

Woman suffrage gained ground in the states before the national amendment. Its organizations regrouped in 1916 around a two-track strategy. The amendment itself belongs to Chapter 31.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-check-ch31]

By 1912 nine western states had adopted woman suffrage, and more followed. By 1916 eleven states — Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Nevada, Oregon, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming — had granted women full suffrage. All were west of the Mississippi. Illinois had granted women the vote for presidential electors and for many local offices in 1913, and several other states allowed women to vote in school or bond elections. Most of the South and much of the East denied women the vote for president and for state offices, even where school or bond suffrage existed.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-suffrage-yearbook]

At the National American Woman Suffrage Association convention at Atlantic City in September 1916, Carrie Chapman Catt presented a “winning plan.” It combined a federal amendment campaign with state-by-state work in promising states. Catt urged the organization to avoid “detours” into states that could not win, especially in the South, and to build support for future ratification. The plan treated the federal amendment and the state campaigns as parts of one strategy.[^ch29-catt-plan][^ch29-check-suffrage-plan]

Alice Paul’s more confrontational National Woman’s Party pursued the federal amendment and picketing. The two organizations disagreed about tactics and about how much to compromise with the political parties. Their competition was part of the movement’s strength and part of its difficulty.[^ch29-catt-plan][^ch29-check-suffrage-plan]

By 1916 almost all major suffrage organizations backed a constitutional amendment. The vote remained partial and uneven by state and by race. Black women in the South faced disfranchisement along with Black men. The Nineteenth Amendment is completed in Chapter 31, and 1920 must not be read back into 1916.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-check-race][^ch29-check-ch31]

A map of suffrage by state in 1916 shows how patchwork the American franchise was. It also shows how much of the country still denied women any vote.[^ch29-gilder-suffrage-map][^ch29-archives-19th]

Map 22, “Woman suffrage by state, 1916,” distinguishes states with full suffrage, states with partial or presidential suffrage, and states with none. It states its date and the state laws behind each category. The map is an argument about the franchise, not a neutral background. Its categories decide what counts as a gain.[^ch29-check-map]

Its categories rest on the state suffrage laws and the NAWSA record. The Gilder Lehrman interactive map is a ca. 1919 suffrage map and shows how suffragists used such maps. It is not a 1916 source and cannot be reproduced as the 1916 map.[^ch29-gilder-suffrage-map][^ch29-check-map]

### 5.3 Federal segregation and the Black response

The same federal authority that Wilson used for reform he used for segregation. Black reformers answered with organization and protest.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-naacp-history]

The Niagara Movement, founded in 1905 and led by W. E. B. Du Bois, demanded full civil rights. After a deadly race riot in Springfield, Illinois, in 1908, a group of Black and White reformers issued a call on February 12, 1909, that founded the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. Du Bois began *The Crisis* in 1910.[^ch29-naacp-history]

In 1914 William Monroe Trotter of the National Independent Political League and the Boston *Guardian* confronted Wilson at the White House over the segregation of Black federal employees. He was removed after the president declared himself offended. Trotter then took the case to the press.[^ch29-whitehouse-trotter]

The NAACP won an early victory in *Guinn v. United States* (1915) against the Oklahoma grandfather clause. The federal government’s own segregation continued. The Black response was part of the era’s reform and its sharpest indictment.[^ch29-naacp-history][^ch29-check-race]

### 5.4 Angel Island and the enforcement of exclusion

On the Pacific coast, federal reform of immigration meant exclusion. The Angel Island station, opened in 1910, was its instrument.[^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-plan-angel-island]

The Angel Island Immigration Station opened on January 21, 1910, and operated until 1940. The Bureau of Immigration chose the island for its isolation and designed the station to detain rather than welcome newcomers. It separated arrivals by race, gender, and class.[^ch29-aiisf-history]

Under the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the laws that followed, only a few classes of Chinese could enter legally. After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake destroyed records, the “paper son” system of purchased identities supplied false claims of dependent children. Detainees faced invasive medical inspections and long interrogations before the Board of Special Inquiry.[^ch29-aiisf-history]

The station’s files — the interrogations, the barracks poems discovered in 1970, and the reports — give the immigration-exclusion side of the same years in which the federal government expanded its reach. Angel Island belongs beside the arbitrated coal field and the segregated office as faces of the same enlarged authority.[^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-plan-angel-island][^ch29-check-race]

### 5.5 Industrial violence and the limits of reform

Where arbitration failed and where the federal government chose not to act, the era’s labor conflict produced open warfare. Ludlow exposed both the reach and the limits of reform.[^ch29-nps-ludlow]

In September 1913 coal miners in southern Colorado struck for enforcement of the eight-hour day and the right to choose their own housing and doctors. The Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, controlled by the Rockefeller interests, rejected the demands and evicted the strikers from company housing. The miners built a tent colony at Ludlow.[^ch29-nps-ludlow]

On the morning of April 20, 1914, National Guardsmen aligned with the company attacked the Ludlow tent colony. They killed 21 people, including 11 children. The attack ignited ten days of fighting until Wilson sent federal troops to disarm both sides.[^ch29-nps-ludlow]

Ludlow shows that the same federal authority that arbitrated at the White House could also stand aside while a state militia fought a private company’s war. Reform advanced mainly where the public was aroused. The section closes on that uneven record.[^ch29-nps-ludlow][^ch29-check-silences]

## 6. The Debate

The argument over the Progressive movement began while it was being made. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open. The debate is not a contest between reformers and their enemies. It is a disagreement about which mechanisms mattered most and whom the reforms served.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-mcgerr]

### 6.1 What is settled

Public authority grew. Between 1900 and 1916 the federal and state governments regulated railroads, food, drugs, and banks; created new agencies; and asserted an interest in industrial disputes and public lands.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

Reform was a coalition, not a movement. The Progressives included a professional middle class, urban and immigrant workers, organized women, and Black reformers who agreed on the need for action and differed on its aims.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-yawp-progressive]

The reforms were limited and exclusionary. The same years completed Black disfranchisement, segregated the federal service, enforced immigration exclusion, and displaced people from conservation lands.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-conservation]

Women won ground but not equality. Suffrage advanced in the states and reorganized nationally, but the amendment was not yet won and protective-labor law treated women as a class apart.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-justia-muller]

The courts set the boundary. The Supreme Court permitted protective laws for women and children while invalidating general hours laws, and it later struck down the federal child-labor law.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-oyez-hammer]

The reforms did not redistribute authority within the workplace. The Triangle investigation produced safety law and workmen’s compensation but no share of control for the workers, and the owners were acquitted.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-yawp-progressive]

### 6.2 What is open

Who drove reform? One position holds that reform came from a displaced or anxiously rising middle class seeking order. Another holds that it came from working-class and immigrant communities and their urban allies. A third holds that business leaders themselves sought regulation to stabilize competition.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe]

Did regulation serve the public or the regulated? Roosevelt and Wilson presented regulation as a curb on corporate power. Kolko and Sklar argued that the regulated industries shaped the regulation to their own advantage. Each position should be stated at its strongest before particular measures are judged.[^ch29-glencoe-historians]

Was it one movement or many? Wiebe’s organizational synthesis treats reform as a single response to disorder. Later work treats it as many movements with different constituencies. McGerr argues for a coherent middle-class crusade. Leonard argues that the shared commitments of the reformers included eugenics and exclusion.[^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe][^ch29-mcgerr][^ch29-check-debate-works]

Where did the ideas come from? Rodgers locates American reform in a transatlantic traffic of social policy. Other accounts stress domestic conditions and institutions. The exchange was a real influence, but it did not make American reform a European import.[^ch29-check-debate-works][^ch29-check-sources]

What did the era leave? One reading stresses the durable regulatory and welfare institutions. Another stresses the era’s exclusions and the distrust of government that followed. The evidence supports holding both, and the chapter does not let the later achievements excuse the exclusions.[^ch29-mcgerr][^ch29-check-conservation]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which Progressive reform at once widened the public’s power over concentrated wealth and narrowed who counted as the public. The positions overlap rather than form teams.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-mcgerr]

A worker who won a nine-hour day, a woman who won a protective law, and a Black clerk who lost a federal post each encountered the same enlarging state differently. The synthesis holds the gains and the exclusions together without claiming that one caused the other.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

The reformers built an administrative state that could arbitrate a strike, inspect a factory, and manage a forest. They built it with the era’s racial and gender order inside it. The state’s new capacity and its new exclusions were products of the same movement.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-table2]

## Legacies

The administrative state that the Progressives built outlasted them. The Federal Reserve, the Federal Trade Commission, the income tax, the direct election of senators, and the labor and safety codes of the states became durable parts of American government. The conservation program left a permanent federal presence in the West.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-nps-tr-conservation]

The exclusions outlasted the reformers too. Federal segregation, the enforcement of Asian exclusion, and the disfranchisement of Black southerners continued after 1916. The immigration restriction laws of the 1920s, which belong to Chapter 32, built on the exclusion regime described here.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-ch32]

The reform impulse also left a double legacy. It left a confidence that expert government can correct concentrated power. It left a skepticism bred by the movement’s moralism and its exclusions. McGerr argues that this ambivalence about politics and the state has roots in these decades. That is an interpretation, not a verdict.[^ch29-mcgerr]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** Historians such as Rodgers have located American reform in a transatlantic traffic in social insurance, city planning, and labor law, and reformers traveled and translated across the Atlantic. The Progressive movement was national and international at once. The comparison belongs in a note rather than in a claim that reform was imported.[^ch29-check-debate-works][^ch29-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** The investigating commissions, the magazines, and the presidential papers are rich, while the testimony of the workers, tenants, and detainees they describe survives unevenly. The commission reports show what inspectors recorded, not what every worker believed. That asymmetry belongs in the record rather than being filled with invented voices.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-silences]

## Summary

- On October 3, 1902, Roosevelt summoned the anthracite operators and the United Mine Workers to the temporary White House, and though the conference failed, arbitration ended the 163-day strike with a 10 percent award and a nine-hour day.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- “Progressive” named a coalition of a professional middle class, urban and immigrant workers, organized women, and Black reformers, not a single movement.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-yawp-progressive]
- The states adopted the direct primary, the initiative, the referendum, and the recall, and the Seventeenth Amendment provided for the direct election of senators in 1913.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots][^ch29-archives-17th]
- The muckraking magazines — *McClure’s* and its rivals — made corruption and corporate power national scandals, and Roosevelt coined the name “muckraker” in 1906.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-loc-muckrakers]
- Sinclair’s *The Jungle* (1906) prompted the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, signed on June 30, 1906, regulating food rather than the labor Sinclair had meant to expose.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]
- Hull-House, founded by Addams and Starr in 1889, became the model settlement and a base for the investigations that produced labor law.[^ch29-hullhouse]
- The Triangle Shirtwaist fire of March 25, 1911, killed 145 workers by the Factory Investigating Commission’s count, and the resulting investigation produced workmen’s compensation and a revised state safety code.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-dol-fic]
- Roosevelt’s Square Deal included the Northern Securities suit, the Elkins and Hepburn Acts, the Reclamation Act of 1902, and the reservation of about 230 million acres under the Forest Service and the Antiquities Act.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-usbr-reclamation]
- The Ballinger–Pinchot affair and the tariff split the Republicans, and in 1912 Wilson won 435 electoral votes with about 42 percent of the popular vote while Roosevelt took 88 and Taft 8.[^ch29-taft-domestic][^ch29-archives-1912][^ch29-app-1912]
- Wilson’s New Freedom produced the Underwood–Simmons tariff, the Federal Reserve Act, the Federal Trade Commission and Clayton Acts, and the Adamson Act, while child labor and racial equality remained beyond it.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-archives-keating-owen][^ch29-oyez-hammer]
- Wilson allowed federal segregation, Trotter protested it at the White House in 1914, and the NAACP, founded in 1909, fought it through the courts and *The Crisis*.[^ch29-postal-segregation][^ch29-whitehouse-trotter][^ch29-naacp-history]
- The Angel Island Immigration Station, opened in 1910, enforced Asian exclusion through detention and interrogation, and the Ludlow Massacre of April 20, 1914, showed the violence that reform had not ended.[^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-nps-ludlow]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1900 and 1916 the United States enlarged the authority of its government. A president arbitrated a coal strike in the name of the public; investigators wrote factory conditions into law; federal agencies took charge of the food supply, the currency, and the forests; and a new party wrote direct democracy into its platform. The same years segregated the federal service, barred and interrogated immigrants at Angel Island, completed the disfranchisement of Black southerners, and left workers to be shot in a Colorado tent colony. The chapter’s question — who benefited when reformers enlarged public authority? — is answered by a record in which the gains and the exclusions were built by the same hands.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-nps-ludlow]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in August 1914, when the steamship *Ancon* made the first official transit of the Panama Canal. The canal crossed a country the United States had helped create in 1903, and it opened two weeks into a European war. The reforming state that Roosevelt and Wilson had built at home would now be turned outward, into the Caribbean, the Pacific, and eventually the European war. Chapter 30 takes up the United States and the world, and asks what kind of power a nation exercises when it rules without annexing.[^ch29-check-ch30]
