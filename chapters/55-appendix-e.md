# Appendix E. Historiographical Essay and Guide to Further Reading

This file specifies the essay’s argument and architecture, the primary collections, the era and thematic guides, the digital-source and permissions rules, and the verification queue. It is not a draft of the appendix and does not certify every recommendation.*

## 1. How Appendix E Works

### 1.1 Purpose and reader-facing form

- Appendix E has two jobs. The essay explains how the questions American historians ask, and the evidence they accept, have changed. The guide gives a selective route into the literature and the primary collections. It is not a bibliography of every work cited in the fifty chapters, and it does not replace their evidence notes.[^app-e-plan]
- The appendix should be readable from beginning to end by an attentive general reader, but it should also work as a reference. A reader should be able to turn to an era or a theme, find two to four works, understand why each is there, and know what it cannot do.
- The appendix has a short opening note, clearly marked sections, and a closing note on selection. It has no epigraphs, no In Focus box, no debate table, no roadmap, no summary, and no study questions. Those are chapter apparatus, and the appendix is reference material.[^app-e-plan]
- No table or illustration is planned. The seed says so, and the allowance is for prose; adding a table would require a separate production decision. If a comparison table of historiographical schools is later judged necessary, it must be registered in the README, assigned a display number, and budgeted; it must not appear as an unregistered convenience.[^app-e-plan]
- This outline was prepared with the README, AGENTS.md, and the Stage 3 outline for Appendix D open for consistency; the Appendix B and C entries were checked through the README. The chapter debate tables named in §8 were checked for the positions the essay summarizes.[^app-e-acknowledgments]
- The appendix’s claims about historiography are arguments, not neutral summaries. They should be supported by the works themselves, by substantive reviews, or by the profession’s own state-of-the-field accounts. A publisher’s description can verify a book’s existence and its publisher’s characterization; it cannot verify the book’s argument.[^app-e-method]

### 1.2 How to use this guide

- A reader who wants a first book should begin with a national synthesis that is explicit about its exclusions, then a field-defining monograph, then a primary collection. The guide marks these choices as “Start with” and “Then read,” not as a canon.[^app-e-lepore][^app-e-blackhawk]
- A reader who wants to research a topic should begin with the primary collections in §3, then the relevant era or theme in §§4–5, then the chapter’s combined notes. The chapter notes give the exact passage; the appendix gives the route.[^app-e-plan]
- A teacher should use the essay for the shape of the field and the era and theme guides for assignable books. The annotations state the value and the limit of each work, so that a syllabus does not turn a single interpretation into the period.[^app-e-aha-lesson]
- A skeptic should read §2.10 and the “what is open” notes in the chapters. The appendix does not ask the reader to accept a synthesis; it asks the reader to see what each synthesis includes and leaves out.[^app-e-plan]

### 1.3 Selection rules and annotations

- The guide will carry about 50 to 55 entries. No era or theme will carry more than four in the final selection. Each annotation will run roughly 40 to 60 words: what the work argues, why it is worth the reader’s time, and what it does not establish.[^app-e-plan]
- Prefer works that are available in paperback, a stable library edition, or an accessible digital edition. Note when a standard work is out of print, abridged, or available only through a research library. A recommendation the reader cannot obtain is not a useful recommendation.[^app-e-method]
- Include at least one work per section that challenges the book’s own interpretation. The guide is not a gallery of agreement. Where a field is divided, the annotations should say so.[^app-e-plan]
- Include Indigenous, Black, women’s, labor, environmental, borderlands, and transnational scholarship as fields with their own questions, not as supplementary “voices” added to a fixed national story.[^app-e-aha-native]
- Do not list a work merely because it is famous. Explain what question it changed, what evidence it opened, or what synthesis it made possible. A list of classics without annotations is a shelf, not a guide.[^app-e-method]
- Mark the verification status of every entry in the production record. The final appendix will not print the marks, but the editor must know whether an entry was read, consulted in part, or recommended on the strength of a publisher’s description or a review.[^app-e-method]
- Give full bibliographic details at first mention and a short form thereafter. Do not use “ibid.” Do not send the reader to a separate bibliography; the combined notes are the evidence trail.[^app-e-plan]

### 1.4 The essay’s thesis

- The central claim is that American historiography has not moved in a straight line from error to truth. It has moved through a series of arguments about who counts as an actor, what counts as evidence, and what scale of history is appropriate. Those arguments were shaped by the country’s political conflicts and by the changing composition of the historical profession.[^app-e-higham][^app-e-bender]
- Three long shifts organize the essay. The first is from providential and national narrative to a professional discipline with archives, seminars, journals, and standards of evidence. The second is from conflict to consensus and back to conflict. The third is from a nation-centered, elite-centered history to a history of multiple actors and scales: Indigenous nations, enslaved people, women, workers, migrants, and the environment.[^app-e-higham][^app-e-foner-new]
- A fourth shift is methodological. Historians now work with census microdata, oral history, material culture, archaeology, linguistics, film, sound, and digital archives. The expansion of evidence is not a technical footnote to the intellectual history; it is one of the reasons the questions changed.[^app-e-method]
- The book’s five through-lines are a provisional answer to the fragmentation of the field. They do not claim that the field has converged on one synthesis. They claim that a national history can be written if it is explicit about land, labor, belonging, inequality, and power, and about the people each of those categories includes and excludes.[^app-e-plan]
- The essay’s normative claim is modest and demanding: good history states its scale, its evidence, and its silences. It does not pretend that a national narrative is the only scale, and it does not pretend that a microhistory is the only honest one.[^app-e-bender]

### 1.5 What the essay must not do

- It must not be a parade of “turns.” The linguistic turn, the Indigenous turn, the transnational turn, and the history-of-capitalism turn overlapped, disagreed internally, and left older questions standing. A sequence of labels is not an explanation.[^app-e-method]
- It must not claim that each new school refuted the last. The consensus school did not refute the Progressive historians; it changed the questions. Social history did not refute consensus history; it changed the actors. The cultural turn did not refute social history; it changed the meaning of experience.[^app-e-higham]
- It must not be a presentist scorecard. Historians of the 1890s, the 1950s, and the 1980s asked the questions their evidence and their politics made available. The essay can criticize their blind spots without pretending that the present has none.[^app-e-novick]
- It must not substitute for the chapters’ evidence. A guide can say that a field has moved; only the chapter can show that a particular claim about a particular event is supported.[^app-e-plan]
- It must not claim that recent history is impossible. It must explain the specific evidentiary limits of the recent past: incomplete records, classification, litigation, partisanship, and the absence of archival distance.[^app-e-recent]
- It must not be a “great books” list organized by reputation. The guide’s criterion is usefulness to a reader who wants to understand a question, not the celebration of a canon.[^app-e-method]

### 1.6 Verification status and footnote policy

- This outline uses four status labels. **Consulted** means that the text or the relevant portion was opened and read for this outline. **Publisher/catalog** means that a publisher page, library catalog, or institutional description was read for bibliographic details and the publisher’s characterization, not for the book’s full argument. **Recommended** means that the work is included for the reader but was not read for this outline. **Target** means that the work is identified but its edition or URL still needs verification.[^app-e-method]
- A claim about what a book argues requires the book or a substantive review. A publisher’s blurb is evidence of what the publisher wanted to say, not of what the book proves. A search snippet is a lead, not a source. An AI summary is a lead, not a source.[^app-e-method]
- Quotations in the final appendix will be collated against the identified edition, with a page or equivalent locator. This outline quotes only from sources that were read closely enough to support the quotation.[^app-e-method]
- Primary collections are described from their own about pages, finding aids, or terms-of-use pages. Where a site blocked automated access, the limitation is recorded rather than hidden.[^app-e-access]
- Some book notes in this outline name a publisher description or catalog record without giving a URL. Those are Stage 4 verification tasks, not claims that the link was checked. The final note must include a direct URL or DOI, the edition, and the access date.[^app-e-method]
- The final draft must recheck every URL, edition, and attribution. A link that opens is not proof that its contents support the sentence.[^app-e-method]

## 2. The Essay: How American History Changed Its Questions

### 2.1 From providential history to a professional discipline

- Nineteenth-century American history was often a national and providential story. George Bancroft, Francis Parkman, William H. Prescott, and John Lothrop Motley wrote national narratives that a republic found useful. Native peoples appeared as obstacles, foils, or vanishing races; the nation appeared as the natural unit of history.[^app-e-national-narratives][^app-e-bender]
- Thomas Bender dates the first national history of the United States to David Ramsay’s *History of the American Revolution*, published in 1789 after the Constitution was ratified. Bender’s point is not that Ramsay invented American history. It is that national history was from the beginning a project of making citizens, and that the project shaped what counted as a story.[^app-e-bender]
- The professional discipline took shape in the late nineteenth century. The American Historical Association was founded in 1884; graduate seminars, research libraries, journals, and doctoral degrees created a cadre of historians who claimed a scientific method. The archive became the profession’s sacred place, and objectivity became its professional ideal.[^app-e-aha-founded][^app-e-novick]
- Peter Novick’s *That Noble Dream* is the standard account of that ideal and its troubles. Objectivity was never a single doctrine. It was elaborated, challenged, revised, and defended as the profession changed. The book’s title names the aspiration and the irony: the dream of a view from nowhere was always entangled with the historian’s place in the world.[^app-e-novick]
- Frederick Jackson Turner’s 1893 essay, “The Significance of the Frontier in American History,” made the frontier the crucible of American democracy. It was a powerful answer to the question of American distinctiveness, and it was also a way of writing Indigenous dispossession as national character. The essay’s influence is part of the evidence for how a national narrative can organize a profession.[^app-e-turner]
- Charles A. Beard’s *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution of the United States* (1913) and Vernon L. Parrington’s *Main Currents in American Thought* (1927–30) made conflict the engine of American history. Beard asked whose property the Constitution protected. Parrington asked which ideas were liberal and which were reactionary. Their questions were material and ideological, not providential.[^app-e-progressive-books]
- The paradox is that professional history did not escape the national story. It formalized it. The archive, the monograph, and the seminar gave the nation a more rigorous story, but the story’s center remained the nation. The later turns are in part a long argument about that center.[^app-e-bender]

### 2.2 Conflict, consensus, and the postwar settlement

- The Progressive generation wrote conflict into the center of American history. Turner’s sections, Beard’s classes, and Parrington’s ideas gave the past a plot of struggle. The plot was not always accurate, but it made institutions and interests visible.[^app-e-higham]
- The post–World War II consensus school reacted against that plot. Louis Hartz’s *The Liberal Tradition in America* (1955) argued that the United States had never had a feudal order and therefore had never developed a genuine conservative tradition. Daniel J. Boorstin’s *The Genius of American Politics* (1953) celebrated American practicality and the absence of ideology. Richard Hofstadter’s *The Age of Reform* (1955) treated Populism and Progressivism as nostalgic movements and the New Deal as the real break.[^app-e-higham]
- John Higham’s 1959 essay, “The Cult of the ‘American Consensus’: Homogenizing Our History,” is the classic critique. Higham did not deny that the consensus historians had found real continuities. He argued that they had turned a complicated past into a “placid, unexciting” one, that they had dissolved genuine conflicts into “climates of opinion,” and that the result was conservative in effect even when it was not conservative in intention.[^app-e-higham]
- Higham’s essay is also a warning about method. The consensus historians were not fabricating evidence. They were selecting continuities and describing them as the essence of the national experience. The lesson is not that consensus history was a conspiracy. The lesson is that a question can be answered honestly and still be the wrong question.[^app-e-higham]
- The New Left of the 1960s reopened the conflict. William Appleman Williams’s *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959) made the Open Door and economic expansion central to American foreign policy. Gabriel Kolko and James Weinstein examined corporate liberalism and the state. Eugene Genovese’s *Roll, Jordan, Roll* (1974) made the enslaved a historical subject, not a problem for White actors. The civil rights movement and the Vietnam War made the consensus picture hard to sustain.[^app-e-newleft]
- The debate was not only academic. It was about whether the United States was exceptional, whether class mattered, and whether dissent was legitimate. The historians’ arguments traveled into textbooks, classrooms, and public memory. That is one reason the historiography of this period cannot be separated from the political history of the period.[^app-e-higham]

### 2.3 Social history and the recovery of ordinary people

- The next shift was from the actions of elites to the experience of ordinary people. Labor historians, community historians, family historians, and historians of slavery and women began to ask how work, kinship, neighborhood, and daily life changed. The phrase “history from below” named the ambition, though not every social historian used it.[^app-e-foner-new]
- The new social history expanded the archive. Census manuscripts, tax lists, probate inventories, parish registers, court records, ships’ manifests, letters, diaries, and oral testimony made it possible to reconstruct the lives of people who did not write books. Quantification made some of those lives visible in the aggregate; close reading made others visible in detail.[^app-e-method]
- The gains were real. Enslaved people became actors in the history of slavery, not merely victims or abstractions. Workers became political actors, not merely labor costs. Women became historical subjects, not merely the wives and mothers of citizens. Immigrants and their children became agents of community formation, not merely objects of assimilation.[^app-e-foner-new]
- The costs were also real. Social history could fragment the past into a series of community studies, each excellent on its own ground and silent on the whole. Synthesis lagged. Textbooks added boxes and sidebars rather than changing the narrative. Bender’s complaint that new scholarship was “bracketed” rather than integrated describes the problem precisely.[^app-e-bender]
- Eric Foner’s edited volume *The New American History* (1990) is a useful marker of the moment. Its essays surveyed the state of several fields and described how traditional fields were being expanded and redefined. Foner described “the continuing vitality and creativity of the study of the past, how traditional fields are being expanded and redefined even as new ones are created.”[^app-e-foner-new]
- The volume is also a marker of the problem it described. Each field had its own narrative, and the sum was not yet a new national narrative. The question of synthesis passed to the next generation, and it has not been settled.[^app-e-bender]

### 2.4 The cultural and linguistic turn

- The cultural turn shifted attention from experience to meaning. Historians asked how people made sense of their world, how categories such as race, gender, class, and nation were produced, and how language and representation shaped what counted as reality. The shift was not a rejection of social history; it was an argument about how social experience is formed.[^app-e-scott]
- Joan Wallach Scott’s 1986 essay, “Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis,” is a central text. Scott argued that gender is not a synonym for women and not a simple reflection of biology. It is a “social category imposed on a sexed body,” and it is a way of signifying relationships of power. The definition made gender available for the history of politics, war, and the state, not only the history of the family.[^app-e-scott]
- Kimberlé Crenshaw’s 1989 essay, “Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex,” supplied another central concept. Crenshaw argued that antidiscrimination law and feminist theory often treated race and gender as separate axes, and that this single-axis framework erased Black women. “Because the intersectional experience is greater than the sum of racism and sexism,” she wrote, “any analysis that does not take intersectionality into account cannot sufficiently address the particular manner in which Black women are subordinated.”[^app-e-crenshaw]
- The gains were substantial. Categories that had seemed natural became historical. “Woman,” “Black,” “white,” “heterosexual,” and “citizen” were shown to be constructed, contested, and enforced. The history of identity became a history of power.[^app-e-scott][^app-e-crenshaw]
- The costs were also substantial. Some cultural history became obscure, self-referential, or indifferent to material life. Labor historians, environmental historians, and economic historians pushed back. The argument was not whether culture mattered; it was whether culture was all that mattered. The best work after the turn treated culture and political economy together.[^app-e-hilt]
- The turn also changed the historian’s evidence. Paintings, photographs, novels, films, advertisements, buildings, and bodies became sources for the history of meaning. The expansion was liberating and hazardous: an image can be read in many ways, and the historian must show why one reading is better than another.[^app-e-method]

### 2.5 Indigenous sovereignty and the remaking of American history

- The historiography of Native America did as much as any field to change the frame of American history in the last half century. The old story placed Native peoples on the wrong side of modernity: obstacles to settlement, victims of progress, or candidates for assimilation. The new history placed Indigenous nations at the center of their own histories and made their diplomacy, economy, and sovereignty central to the American story.[^app-e-aha-native]
- Vine Deloria Jr.’s *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969) was a public and intellectual provocation. It attacked the arrogance of White anthropologists, the failures of federal policy, and the assumption that Native peoples were a problem to be solved. It was not a work of academic historiography, but it changed the terms in which academic historiography was written.[^app-e-deloria]
- The “new Indian history” of the 1970s and 1980s drew on ethnohistory and community studies. Richard White’s *The Middle Ground* (1991) showed how Algonquian peoples and French, British, and American empires created a shared world of meaning and exchange in the Great Lakes region. William Cronon’s *Changes in the Land* (1983) showed how colonial property regimes transformed New England ecology. Both books made Indigenous agency and environmental change part of the same story.[^app-e-white-middle][^app-e-cronon]
- By 2012, the American Historical Association could describe an “indigenous turn” in early American history. The old trivialization of Native Americans as “myopic, prepolitical actors” and “mere speed bumps” was gone, at least from the best scholarship. The creation of America was now a multilateral process that created new worlds for all.[^app-e-aha-native]
- Ned Blackhawk’s *The Rediscovery of America* (2023) carried the argument into a full national synthesis. Blackhawk’s publisher describes the book as a retelling that recognizes Native Americans as “essential to understanding the evolution of modern America.” The title’s word is “unmaking”: the book does not add Native people to an intact national story; it argues that the story was never intact without them.[^app-e-blackhawk]
- Pekka Hämäläinen’s *The Comanche Empire* (2008) supplied a different corrective. It presented the Comanche as an imperial power that controlled a vast region and shaped the policies of European and American states. The book was criticized for overstating Comanche power, but it made the case that Indigenous history is a history of power, not only of victimization.[^app-e-hamalainen]
- The methodological challenge is as important as the interpretive one. Tribal archives, oral histories, and material culture are not simply additional sources for a national history. They carry protocols of consent, community review, and cultural property. The Hiawatha Belt is not a free illustration; it is the visual constitution of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, a record of an internal peace rather than a treaty with outsiders. A history that ignores those protocols is not more objective; it is less careful.[^app-e-care]

### 2.6 Race, slavery, and the reinterpretation of capitalism

- The history of slavery is the clearest case of a field that changed both the evidence and the national story. W. E. B. Du Bois’s *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) treated Reconstruction as a democratic experiment and the formerly enslaved as political actors. It was marginalized for decades and is now foundational.[^app-e-dubois]
- The next generation combined social history, labor history, and political economy. Eugene Genovese’s *Roll, Jordan, Roll* (1974) examined the slaveholders’ world and the enslaved’s moral economy. Eric Foner’s *Reconstruction* (1988) made emancipation and the struggle over labor central to the postwar state. Ira Berlin’s *Many Thousands Gone* (1998) traced the making of African American society across regions and centuries. Walter Johnson’s *Soul by Soul* (1999) reconstructed the New Orleans slave market as a site of commerce, law, and human terror.[^app-e-genovese-berlin][^app-e-foner-recon][^app-e-johnson]
- The “new history of capitalism” of the 2000s and 2010s pushed the argument further. Edward E. Baptist’s *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014) argued that slavery was central to the making of American capitalism and that the torture of enslaved workers was part of the story of productivity. Sven Beckert’s *Empire of Cotton* (2014) placed slavery and colonial expropriation at the center of a global cotton economy. The books were widely reviewed and reached general readers.[^app-e-baptist][^app-e-beckert]
- The field is also contested. Eric Hilt’s 2017 survey of ten works in the new history of capitalism praised their moral seriousness and their attention to coercion, but argued that some of them overstated their claims, ignored economic-history methods, and failed to test alternative explanations. Hilt’s critique is not a defense of slavery; it is an argument about evidence. A guide that omitted the critique would misrepresent the field.[^app-e-hilt]
- The public battle over the 1619 Project made the academic debate visible. The *New York Times Magazine* project placed slavery at the center of American origins and provoked both praise and criticism.[^app-e-1619] Leslie M. Harris, a historian of slavery who helped fact-check the project, criticized its claim that the Revolution was fought primarily to preserve slavery. She also wrote that “the United States was not, in fact, founded to protect slavery—but the *Times* is right that slavery was central to its story.”[^app-e-harris]
- The political response was not a historiographical argument. The President’s Advisory 1776 Commission’s *The 1776 Report* (2021) presented a patriotic counter-narrative. The American Historical Association condemned it as a report “written hastily in one month” that “fails to engage a rich and vibrant body of scholarship that has evolved over the last seven decades.” The AAUP called it “based on falsehoods but poses as history” and noted that it lacked the ordinary apparatus of citations.[^app-e-aha-1776][^app-e-aaup-1776]
- The lesson for the appendix is double. Public history can bring a field to a mass audience, and public history can distort a field. The guide should recommend works that show the evidence, not only the argument, and it should distinguish a journalistic synthesis from a scholarly monograph.[^app-e-harris]

### 2.7 Gender, sexuality, and the politics of the family

- Women’s history became gender history through a series of arguments about separate spheres, work, and power. Nancy Cott’s *The Bonds of Womanhood* (1977) examined the ideology of domesticity in New England. Linda Kerber’s *Women of the Republic* (1980) placed women in the political history of the Revolution and the early republic. Laurel Thatcher Ulrich’s *A Midwife’s Tale* (1990) used a diary to reconstruct the ordinary life of a Maine midwife. Each book showed that the family was a political institution, not a private refuge.[^app-e-cott][^app-e-kerber][^app-e-ulrich]
- Scott’s 1986 essay supplied the theoretical hinge. If gender is a way of signifying power, then the history of politics, war, and the state is also a history of gender. The claim did not make women’s history obsolete; it made the whole field larger.[^app-e-scott]
- The history of sexuality developed on a parallel track. John D’Emilio’s “Capitalism and Gay Identity” (1983) argued that wage labor and urban life made new forms of gay identity possible.[^app-e-demilio] George Chauncey’s *Gay New York* (1994) reconstructed a working-class homosexual world before the closet. Margot Canaday’s *The Straight State* (2009) showed how the federal government used immigration, military, and welfare policy to produce a heterosexual citizen. The field made sexuality a category of citizenship, not a private matter.[^app-e-chauncey][^app-e-canaday]
- Crenshaw’s intersectionality was a warning to both fields. A history of women that focused on white, middle-class, native-born women could reproduce the very exclusions it criticized. A history of race that focused on men could do the same. The best work after Crenshaw treated race, gender, class, and sexuality as mutually constituting, not as separate variables to be added.[^app-e-crenshaw]
- The challenge to the book’s through-lines is direct. “Liberty” and “unfreedom” look different when marriage, reproduction, domestic labor, and sexual regulation are included. A history of the republic that treats the household as a private unit will miss a great deal of the public order.[^app-e-scott]

### 2.8 Environment, capitalism, and the state

- Environmental history made the land an actor. Alfred Crosby’s *The Columbian Exchange* (1972) showed that the most important consequences of 1492 were biological: the movement of crops, animals, pathogens, and weeds in both directions across the Atlantic. William Cronon’s *Changes in the Land* (1983) showed how colonial property regimes changed New England’s ecology. Donald Worster’s *Rivers of Empire* (1985) argued that the American West was built by a hydraulic state and a culture of aridity.[^app-e-crosby][^app-e-cronon][^app-e-worster]
- The field’s great contribution is to refuse the separation of nature and society. A forest is a human artifact as well as an ecological system. A river is a political institution as well as a waterway. A crop is a commodity, a diet, and a landscape. The environment is not a backdrop to American history; it is one of its conditions.[^app-e-cronon]
- The history of capitalism developed a parallel critique. Alfred D. Chandler Jr.’s *The Visible Hand* (1977) showed how managerial enterprise organized the modern economy. David Montgomery’s *The Fall of the House of Labor* (1987) showed how workers contested managerial authority. Martin J. Sklar’s *The Corporate Reconstruction of American Capitalism* (1988) showed how law and corporate organization remade the market. The field’s later “new history of capitalism” pushed slavery, finance, and coercion into the center.[^app-e-chandler][^app-e-montgomery][^app-e-sklar]
- The state is the hinge between the two fields. The New Deal, the wartime economy, and the management of western water were projects of public power.[^app-e-katznelson][^app-e-worster] A history of capitalism without the state is as incomplete as a history of the environment without property.
- The synthesis problem is real. Environmental history can romanticize pre-capitalist landscapes; capitalism history can flatten the state into a tool of business; both can lose the household and the body. The best work keeps land, labor, law, and money in the same frame.[^app-e-hilt]

### 2.9 Borderlands, empire, and the world

- Borderlands history challenged the national container. The older Spanish-borderlands framework gave way to histories of Indigenous nations, imperial competition, and hybrid communities. Richard White’s *The Middle Ground* is a borderlands book as well as an Indigenous history. Pekka Hämäläinen’s *The Comanche Empire* and Brian DeLay’s *War of a Thousand Deserts* made Indigenous power central to the history of the U.S.–Mexico borderlands.[^app-e-white-middle][^app-e-hamalainen][^app-e-delay]
- The transnational turn made a different argument. Thomas Bender’s *A Nation Among Nations* (2006) proposed “the end of American history as we have known it.” The nation, he argued, “is not free-standing and self-contained; it is connected with and partially shaped by what is beyond it.” The book’s title is a program: the United States is one nation among others, not the measure of all histories.[^app-e-bender]
- The history of empire connected the transnational and the borderlands. William Appleman Williams’s *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959) made economic expansion central to American foreign policy. Walter LaFeber’s *The New Empire* (1963) traced the commercial and strategic roots of American expansion.[^app-e-lafeber] Michael H. Hunt’s *Ideology and U.S. Foreign Policy* (1987) showed how racial hierarchy and ideas of civilization shaped the American encounter with the world. Paul A. Kramer’s “Power and Connection” (2011) surveyed the field and called for a history that connects metropole and colony.[^app-e-williams][^app-e-hunt][^app-e-kramer]
- The best work in this field refuses the choice between “the nation” and “the world.” It studies the state’s borders, the movement of people and goods, the international circulation of ideas, and the empires that shaped both. The nation is not a natural container; it is an institution that has to be explained.[^app-e-bender]
- The danger is a transnational history that loses the state and a borderlands history that celebrates mobility while ignoring coercion. The guide should recommend works that keep power in view.[^app-e-kramer]

### 2.10 Public history and the recent past

- Public history is where historiography becomes politics. The 1619 Project and the 1776 Report offered competing origin stories. The AHA and the AAUP defended professional standards against the second. PEN America documented a wave of school book bans, and the AHA condemned the removal of 381 books from the Naval Academy’s Nimitz Library in 2025. These are not side issues; they are part of the history of how Americans argue about the past.[^app-e-harris][^app-e-aha-1776][^app-e-pen][^app-e-aha-military]
- The AHA’s 2024 report, *American Lesson Plan*, presents a more complicated picture than the culture-war coverage: its endorsers describe most high-school U.S. history teaching as reflecting norms of inquiry, evidence, and political neutrality. The report is a useful corrective to the assumption that the profession’s arguments have captured the classroom.[^app-e-aha-lesson]
- The recent past has its own evidentiary limits. Records are incomplete, classified, or still being created. Litigation is ongoing. Participants are alive and have interests. A commission report is both a body of evidence and an argument about responsibility. The 9/11 Commission Report, the Financial Crisis Inquiry Report, and the January 6 Select Committee’s Final Report are indispensable, and none is a neutral record.[^app-e-9-11][^app-e-fcic][^app-e-jan6]
- The historian’s responsibility in this terrain is to distinguish what is known, what is alleged, and what is contested. It is not to pretend that the evidence is evenly distributed, and it is not to turn a political preference into a historical verdict. The recent past is history with a shorter evidentiary leash.[^app-e-recent]

### 2.11 What the essay concludes

- The profession has not converged on a single synthesis. It has become more plural, more empirical, and more self-conscious about scale. The old national narrative is still available, but it is no longer the only respectable way to write American history.[^app-e-bender]
- The book’s five through-lines are a provisional synthesis. They are a way of asking questions across chapters, not a claim that the questions have been settled. A reader who wants a different synthesis should use the guide to find one.[^app-e-plan]
- The reader’s route is a loop, not a ladder: start with a problem, read a synthesis, read a monograph, go to a primary source, and return to the problem with better questions. The appendix is designed to make that loop easier.[^app-e-method]
- The essay ends with a question rather than a verdict: what would it mean to write a national history that does not naturalize the nation? The book’s answer is provisional, and the guide’s answer is to keep reading.[^app-e-bender]

## 3. Primary Collections

The collections below are organized by type, not by era. Each entry says what the collection is best for and what it cannot do. The list is selective; the chapter notes identify the specific documents used in the chapters.

### 3.1 Founding and constitutional documents

- **Founders Online** (National Archives and University of Virginia Press) brings together the annotated papers of George Washington, Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, Alexander Hamilton, John Jay, James Madison, and Thomas Jefferson. It is the best first stop for a founding-era letter or draft. It is an editorial project, not a complete archive, and it covers seven founders rather than every participant.[^app-e-founders]
- **The National Archives’ Milestone Documents** collection presents selected high-profile documents with transcriptions and context. It is a good starting point for a general reader. It is a curated set, not a complete record, and its selections reflect the anniversary projects that created it.[^app-e-nara-milestones]
- **A Century of Lawmaking for a New Nation** (Library of Congress) covers congressional documents and debates from 1774 to 1875, including the Continental Congress, the Constitutional Convention, the *Annals of Congress*, and the *American State Papers*. It is indispensable for the early republic. The site blocked automated access during this outline; the description rests on the Library’s own catalog page.[^app-e-loc-lawmaking]
- **The Documentary History of the Ratification of the Constitution** (Center for the Study of the American Constitution, University of Wisconsin–Madison) is the definitive edition of the ratification debate. Its editors have drawn on more than 70,000 documents, including town and county meetings, convention journals, letters, newspapers, and pamphlets, and have published thirty-four volumes. The digital edition is by subscription; check the print edition at a research library.[^app-e-csac]
- **The Avalon Project** (Yale Law School) provides transcriptions of treaties, statutes, and diplomatic documents. It is convenient and widely used. It is a transcription, not a facsimile, and the reader should check the edition and the date against the original.[^app-e-avalon]
- **The Constitution Annotated** (Library of Congress) combines the constitutional text with legal analysis and case law. **GovInfo** provides the *Statutes at Large*, the *Congressional Record*, and agency publications. **Congress.gov** provides bills, public laws, and member records. **The Supreme Court** and **Oyez** provide opinions and oral arguments. **The Federal Judicial Center** provides judicial history. Use the official text for the law and the case record for the holding.[^app-e-constitution][^app-e-govinfo][^app-e-congress][^app-e-scotus][^app-e-oyez][^app-e-fjc]

### 3.2 Indigenous nations, treaties, and oral history

- **The National Museum of the American Indian** (Smithsonian) is a museum and a digital resource, not a tribal archive. Its exhibitions and collections can orient a reader, but the nation’s own protocols govern the use of cultural material.[^app-e-nmai]
- **The National Archives’ American Indian and Alaska Native Records** page describes records from 1774 through the mid-1990s, organized by tribe, topic, and federal agency. Many records are not digitized, and records less than 75 years old may be restricted under FOIA’s personal-privacy exemption. The archive is the record of the federal government, not a neutral record of Native life.[^app-e-nara-native]
- **Kappler’s *Indian Affairs: Laws and Treaties*** (seven volumes, 1904–1941) is the standard printed compilation of U.S. treaties, laws, and executive orders concerning Native nations. It is a starting point for treaty research, not a substitute for the enrolled treaty or the nation’s own record.[^app-e-kappler]
- **The Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center** (Dickinson College) digitizes student files, photographs, publications, and records from the Carlisle Indian Industrial School. It is valuable for the history of assimilation and for family history. It is also a record of a school that separated children from their families, and the records must be read with that history in view.[^app-e-carlisle]
- **The Plateau Peoples’ Web Portal** (Washington State University) is a collaboratively curated and reciprocally managed archive of Plateau cultural materials. It is a model of tribal review and community control. It is not a general archive of the Plateau, and access to some materials may be restricted.[^app-e-plateau]
- Use nation-specific archives and tribal historians first. Treaties, oral histories, and material culture are not interchangeable sources. A federal treaty record can establish what the United States promised; it cannot establish what a nation understood the promise to mean.[^app-e-nara-native]

### 3.3 Colonial and early American records

- **The Library of Congress Digital Collections** include colonial newspapers, maps, broadsides, and the papers of major figures. **The Massachusetts Historical Society** holds colonial and Revolutionary manuscripts. **Virtual Jamestown** provides transcribed documents and maps for the Virginia colony. **The New Netherland Institute** provides translations and guides for Dutch New Netherland. **The Maryland State Archives** and **the Pennsylvania State Archives** provide colonial and Revolutionary records.[^app-e-colonial]
- **Documenting the American South** (University of North Carolina) provides texts, images, and oral histories for Southern history. It is especially strong for slavery, the Civil War, and Southern memory. It is a curated digital library, not a complete archive.[^app-e-docsouth]
- Colonial archives are fragmented by colony, county, town, and church. A single document rarely tells the whole story. Probate inventories, tax lists, parish registers, and court records can reconstruct land, labor, and family life. Indigenous voices are often mediated by colonial scribes, and the historian must say so.[^app-e-colonial]
- **Early English Books Online** and **Eighteenth Century Collections Online** are subscription databases of printed works. They are invaluable for print culture and for the history of ideas. They do not replace the manuscript archive, and their metadata can be uneven.[^app-e-colonial]

### 3.4 Slavery, emancipation, and freedom

- **SlaveVoyages** is the digital home of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, the Intra-American Slave Trade Database, and the African Origins project. It documents more than 12 million enslaved Africans and the ships that carried them. It is a database of voyages, not a census of every enslaved person, and it does not capture the experience of the middle passage.[^app-e-slavevoyages]
- **The Freedmen and Southern Society Project** (University of Maryland) is producing a documentary history of emancipation from 1861 to 1867. Its editors selected some 50,000 documents from the National Archives and transcribed, organized, and annotated them. The published volumes and the online selections are a first stop for freedpeople’s letters, labor contracts, and testimony.[^app-e-freedmen]
- **Freedom on the Move** collects advertisements for self-emancipated people and related records. It is a database of resistance and pursuit, not a complete record of freedom. **Born in Slavery** (Library of Congress) provides the Federal Writers’ Project narratives of formerly enslaved people. The narratives are invaluable and shaped by the interviewers, the aging of the interviewees, and the politics of the 1930s.[^app-e-fotm][^app-e-born-slavery]
- **The Colored Conventions Project**, **the Black Abolitionist Archive**, and **the Georgetown Slavery Archive** provide records of Black political organizing, abolitionism, and universities’ entanglement with slavery. Each is a curated project with its own limits. Read the editors’ introductions before quoting.[^app-e-black-archives]
- The archive of slavery was made by enslavers, merchants, and the state. It records property, not personhood. The historian must read against the grain, use the testimony of the enslaved where it survives, and say what the record cannot show.[^app-e-freedmen]

### 3.5 Congress, law, and administration

- **Congress.gov** and **GovInfo** are the first stops for bills, public laws, the *Congressional Record*, the *Statutes at Large*, and agency publications. **A Century of Lawmaking** covers the earlier period. **The Supreme Court** and **Oyez** provide opinions, oral arguments, and case summaries. **The Caselaw Access Project** provides historical case text. **The Federal Judicial Center** provides judicial biography and court history.[^app-e-congress][^app-e-govinfo][^app-e-scotus][^app-e-oyez][^app-e-fjc]
- The law on the books is not the law in practice. Pair a statute with the regulations, the enforcement records, the litigation, and the administrative reports. A civil-rights act without the voting registrars’ records is a promise without a history.[^app-e-method]
- **The Congressional Research Service**, **the Government Accountability Office**, **the Congressional Budget Office**, and inspectors general provide analysis and oversight. They are not neutral; they are institutional voices with their own mandates. Use them for the record, not for the verdict.[^app-e-govinfo]
- **The Federal Register** provides rules and notices. **The National Archives Catalog** provides record descriptions and digitized documents. **FOIA reading rooms** at the CIA, the FBI, and other agencies provide released records. Each has gaps, redactions, and delays.[^app-e-nara-catalog][^app-e-cia]

### 3.6 Census, labor, and economic series

- **The U.S. Census Bureau** provides decennial census publications, the American Community Survey, and current population estimates. **IPUMS USA** harmonizes census microdata from 1790 to 2010 and the ACS from 2000 to the present. **NHGIS** provides summary tables and GIS boundary files from 1790 to the present. These are the core sources for population, household, and geography.[^app-e-census][^app-e-ipums][^app-e-nhgis]
- **Historical Statistics of the United States** (Millennial Edition) is the standard reference for long-run economic, demographic, and social series. In 2026, Cambridge University Press licensed the Millennium Edition to the Economic History Association, removing authentication and making the tables openly available. It remains a compilation with its own definitions and revisions.[^app-e-hsus]
- **The Bureau of Labor Statistics** provides employment, wages, prices, and productivity series. **FRED** (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis) provides more than 850,000 economic time series from many sources. **The NBER Macrohistory Database** covers the pre–World War II and interwar economy. **County Business Patterns** provides subnational employment and payroll by industry. **The Bureau of Economic Analysis** provides GDP, income, and regional accounts. **MeasuringWorth** provides comparators for historical money.[^app-e-bls][^app-e-fred][^app-e-nber][^app-e-cbp][^app-e-bea][^app-e-measuringworth]
- Definitions matter more than the download button. A poverty rate, an unemployment rate, a wage series, and a GDP series each have an observation year, a universe, a denominator, and a revision history. Do not infer individual behavior from a county aggregate. Do not compare series with incompatible boundaries. Appendix B’s tables are the book’s checked data; the raw series are here.[^app-e-hsus]

### 3.7 Diplomacy, war, and intelligence

- **Foreign Relations of the United States** (Office of the Historian, Department of State) is the official documentary record of major U.S. foreign-policy decisions. It is selective, declassified with delays, and organized by administration and topic. It is the first stop for diplomacy, not a complete record of every conversation.[^app-e-frus]
- **The National Archives** holds military and diplomatic records. **The CIA’s FOIA Electronic Reading Room** provides released intelligence records. **The Wilson Center’s Digital Archive** and the **Cold War International History Project** provide documents from multiple archives, including former Soviet and Eastern European collections. **The Pentagon Papers** are available in full through the National Archives.[^app-e-nara-catalog][^app-e-cia][^app-e-wilson][^app-e-pentagon]
- **The 9/11 Commission Report**, **the Iraq Study Group Report**, and **the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence’s study of the CIA’s detention and interrogation program** are commission and congressional records. They are indispensable and they are arguments. Read the dissents, the footnotes, and the underlying documents.[^app-e-9-11][^app-e-ssci]
- The **Afghanistan Papers** (Washington Post, 2019) are a collection of interview transcripts and documents from the U.S. war in Afghanistan. They are valuable and they are a journalistic selection. Use them with the inspectors general’s reports and the congressional record.[^app-e-afghanistan]

### 3.8 Incarceration, migration, and civil rights

- **Densho** preserves Japanese American incarceration history through oral histories, photographs, and documents. **The Japanese American National Museum** provides collections and exhibitions. **The National Archives’ Japanese Relocation records** provide the government’s files. The government record and the community record tell different parts of the story.[^app-e-densho][^app-e-janm][^app-e-nara-catalog]
- **The Civil Rights Digital Library** (University of Georgia) brings together photographs, news film, oral histories, and documents from Georgia and the South. **The SNCC Digital Gateway** provides a documentary history of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. **The King Papers** (Stanford) provide the edited papers of Martin Luther King Jr. **The Southern Oral History Program** provides more than 6,000 interviews on the U.S. South.[^app-e-crdl][^app-e-sncc][^app-e-king][^app-e-sohp]
- **The South Asian American Digital Archive** and **the ACT UP Oral History Project** show how community archives preserve histories that mainstream archives neglected. Each has its own consent, access, and interpretation rules.[^app-e-saada][^app-e-actup]
- Oral history is memory, not a transcript of fact. The interviewer shapes the record; the transcript is an interpretation; the archive’s selection shapes what survives. Use oral history with documents, and say what the interview can and cannot establish.[^app-e-sohp]

### 3.9 9/11, January 6, and the recent record

- **The 9/11 Commission Report** (2004) is the official account of the attacks, the intelligence failures, and the commission’s recommendations. It is a post hoc commission report, not a neutral record of every victim or every decision.[^app-e-9-11]
- **The Select Committee to Investigate the January 6th Attack on the United States Capitol’s Final Report** (December 22, 2022) is available through GovInfo with supporting materials, including videos, transcripts, and documents. It is a congressional committee record and a partisan process record. Use it for the sequence and the evidence, not for a verdict on responsibility.[^app-e-jan6]
- **The Financial Crisis Inquiry Report** (2011) is the official report of the National Commission on the Causes of the Financial and Economic Crisis in the United States. It combines evidence and causal argument. Its dissents and its underlying interviews are part of the record.[^app-e-fcic]
- **Court records** (PACER, CourtListener), **Department of Justice** releases, **inspectors general** reports, and **GAO** reports provide the documentary spine for the recent past. **The Mueller Report** (2019) is a Department of Justice report with its own legal and evidentiary limits. **The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence’s study** is a redacted executive summary of a much longer classified report.[^app-e-recent][^app-e-ssci]
- The recent record is fragile. Save the PDF and the URL; note the access date; record the version. A page that changes after publication is a source problem, not a minor inconvenience.[^app-e-access]

### 3.10 Oral history and community archives

- **The Southern Oral History Program**, **StoryCorps**, **the Veterans History Project**, **Densho**, **the ACT UP Oral History Project**, **SAADA**, and tribal oral-history projects preserve voices that official archives often missed. They are indispensable for the history of work, family, migration, illness, and political organizing.[^app-e-sohp][^app-e-storycorps][^app-e-vhp][^app-e-densho][^app-e-actup][^app-e-saada]
- Oral history is a collaboration. The narrator consenting to an interview is not consenting to every later use. The interviewer’s questions, the editing of the transcript, and the archive’s access rules are part of the evidence. Read the project’s methods page before quoting.[^app-e-sohp]
- Community archives are not automatically more authentic than official archives. They have their own selections, silences, and power relations. The point is not to replace one archive with another; it is to read both with the same care.[^app-e-method]

## 4. Era Guides

The entries below are a candidate pool, not the final table of contents. The final guide will select two or three works for each era, cross-refer to the thematic guide where a work does double duty, and keep the total inside the word budget. The annotations below state why a work is a candidate; the final draft will shorten them and cut the rest.

### 4.1 Before 1607

- **Start with.** Charles C. Mann, *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus* (2005). A vivid synthesis of the hemispheric revision that made the pre-Columbian Americas populous, urban, and ecologically managed. It is journalism, not a primary authority; read it with Denevan and Krech.[^app-e-mann]
- **Then read.** William M. Denevan, “The Pristine Myth: The Landscape of the Americas in 1492” (1992). The essay that named the myth of the empty continent and argued for a continuum of human modification. It is the corrective to both the wilderness fantasy and the “universal garden.”[^app-e-denevan]
- **Then read.** Shepard Krech III, *The Ecological Indian: Myth and History* (1999). A caution against the romantic stereotype of the conservationist Native American. It has been criticized for testing a stereotype as a single claim; read it as a corrective, not a verdict.[^app-e-krech]
- **Then read.** Ned Blackhawk, *The Rediscovery of America* (2023). A national synthesis that places Native peoples at the center of five centuries of American history. It is the best single book for a reader who wants the long Indigenous frame.[^app-e-blackhawk]
- **Primary collections:** National Park Service archaeology pages, UNESCO World Heritage descriptions, tribal museums, and the National Museum of the American Indian. Dates are contested; do not print a single origin date as settled.[^app-e-nmai]

### 4.2 Colonial period

- **Start with.** Edmund S. Morgan, *American Slavery, American Freedom: The Ordeal of Colonial Virginia* (1975). The classic argument that Virginia’s freedom and its slavery were made together. It is a powerful synthesis and a product of its moment; read it with the critics who complicate the “unthinking decision.”[^app-e-morgan]
- **Then read.** Richard White, *The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815* (1991; 2nd ed., 2011). The book that made accommodation and creative misunderstanding central to colonial history. It is dense, and its model does not fit every region.[^app-e-white-middle]
- **Then read.** William Cronon, *Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England* (1983; rev. ed., 2003). The book that launched environmental history and showed how property regimes change landscapes. It is New England–specific; do not universalize it.[^app-e-cronon]
- **Then read.** Kathleen M. Brown, *Good Wives, Nasty Wenches, and Anxious Patriarchs: Gender, Race, and Power in Colonial Virginia* (1996). A study of how gender and race were made together in colonial law and household life. It is a necessary corrective to histories that treat the household as private.[^app-e-brown]
- **Primary collections:** Library of Congress Digital Collections, Massachusetts Historical Society, Virtual Jamestown, New Netherland Institute, Maryland State Archives, Pennsylvania State Archives, and Documenting the American South.[^app-e-colonial]

### 4.3 Revolution and early republic

- **Start with.** Bernard Bailyn, *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (1967). The classic account of the radical-Whig ideas that made independence thinkable. It is an intellectual history, not a social history, and it underplays slavery and Indigenous dispossession.[^app-e-bailyn]
- **Then read.** Gordon S. Wood, *The Radicalism of the American Revolution* (1992). The argument that the Revolution transformed social relations as well as political institutions. It is a broad synthesis; use it with Nash and Taylor.[^app-e-wood-radical]
- **Then read.** Gary B. Nash, *The Unknown American Revolution* (2005). A history of the Revolution from below, including laborers, sailors, enslaved people, and Native Americans. It restores actors that the ideological account leaves out.[^app-e-nash]
- **Then read.** Alan Taylor, *American Revolutions: A Continental History, 1750–1804* (2016). A continental account that treats the Revolution as a conflict among empires, settlers, and Indigenous nations. It is the best single corrective to a thirteen-colonies story.[^app-e-taylor]
- **Constitutional debate:** Charles A. Beard, *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution* (1913); Robert E. Brown, *Charles Beard and the Constitution* (1956); Forrest McDonald, *We the People* (1958); Gordon S. Wood, *Power and Liberty* (2021); David Waldstreicher, *Slavery’s Constitution* (2009); Sean Wilentz, *No Property in Man* (2018). The chapter’s Table 1 in Ch. 12, §7.4 maps the positions.[^app-e-constitution-debate]
- **Primary collections:** Founders Online, the Documentary History of the Ratification of the Constitution, the Avalon Project, the National Archives’ Milestone Documents, and the Library of Congress’s *Century of Lawmaking*.[^app-e-founders][^app-e-csac][^app-e-avalon][^app-e-nara-milestones][^app-e-loc-lawmaking]

### 4.4 Antebellum and Civil War

- **Start with.** Daniel Walker Howe, *What Hath God Wrought: The Transformation of America, 1815–1848* (2007). A synthesis of the market revolution, communication, reform, and westward expansion. It is elegant and long; it is also a synthesis with a point of view.[^app-e-howe]
- **Then read.** David M. Potter, *The Impending Crisis, 1848–1861* (1976, completed by Don E. Fehrenbacher). The standard account of the political crisis that led to secession. It is a political narrative; pair it with social and economic histories.[^app-e-potter]
- **Then read.** James M. McPherson, *Battle Cry of Freedom: The Civil War Era* (1988). The standard one-volume history of the war. It is a military and political synthesis; it does not make slavery the only cause, but it does not marginalize it.[^app-e-mcpherson]
- **Then read.** Walter Johnson, *Soul by Soul: Life Inside the Antebellum Slave Market* (1999). A close study of the New Orleans slave market that shows how commerce, law, and violence made slavery. It is a model of reading the archive against the grain.[^app-e-johnson]
- **Then read.** Edward E. Baptist, *The Half Has Never Been Told: Slavery and the Making of American Capitalism* (2014), and Sven Beckert, *Empire of Cotton: A Global History* (2014). Two ambitious arguments for slavery’s centrality to capitalism. Read them with Eric Hilt’s critique and with the economic-history literature.[^app-e-baptist][^app-e-beckert][^app-e-hilt]
- **Primary collections:** SlaveVoyages, the Freedmen and Southern Society Project, Freedom on the Move, Born in Slavery, and the Library of Congress’s Civil War collections.[^app-e-slavevoyages][^app-e-freedmen][^app-e-fotm][^app-e-born-slavery]

### 4.5 Reconstruction and the Gilded Age

- **Start with.** Eric Foner, *Reconstruction: America’s Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988). The synthesis that made emancipation and the freedpeople’s political agency central to Reconstruction. It is the standard account and still the place to begin.[^app-e-foner-recon]
- **Then read.** W. E. B. Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935). The foundational revision that treated Reconstruction as a democratic experiment and the enslaved as political actors. It was marginalized for decades; read it as historiography as well as history.[^app-e-dubois]
- **Then read.** C. Vann Woodward, *The Strange Career of Jim Crow* (1955; commemorative ed., 2002). The book that made segregation’s timing a historical problem. Its thesis has been revised, but the question it asked remains central.[^app-e-woodward]
- **Then read.** David W. Blight, *Race and Reunion: The Civil War in American Memory* (2001). A history of how White reunion was purchased by segregating the memory of emancipation. It is the best book on the memory of the war.[^app-e-blight]
- **Then read.** Richard White, *The Republic for Which It Stands: The United States during Reconstruction and the Gilded Age, 1865–1896* (2017). A synthesis that treats the period as a single transformation of state, economy, and society. It is long and argumentative.[^app-e-white-republic]
- **Primary collections:** Freedmen and Southern Society Project, the Library of Congress, the National Archives, and the census. For the Gilded Age, add the *Congressional Record*, the *Statutes at Large*, and the labor and capital series in Appendix B.[^app-e-freedmen][^app-e-congress][^app-e-govinfo]

### 4.6 Reform, world wars, and the New Deal

- **Start with.** Richard Hofstadter, *The Age of Reform: From Bryan to F.D.R.* (1955). The classic argument that Populism and Progressivism were status anxieties and nostalgic movements. It is brilliant and contested; read it with its critics in Ch. 27 and Ch. 29.[^app-e-hofstadter]
- **Then read.** William E. Leuchtenburg, *Franklin D. Roosevelt and the New Deal, 1932–1940* (1963). The standard narrative of the New Deal’s achievements and limits. It is sympathetic to the New Deal and should be paired with Brinkley and Katznelson.[^app-e-leuchtenburg]
- **Then read.** Alan Brinkley, *The End of Reform: New Deal Liberalism in Recession and War* (1995). The argument that New Deal liberalism turned from structural reform to compensation and consumption. It is a key revision.[^app-e-brinkley]
- **Then read.** Ira Katznelson, *When Affirmative Action Was White: An Untold History of Racial Inequality in Twentieth-Century America* (2005). The argument that New Deal and postwar programs were racially selective and built a White middle class. It is a necessary corrective to a colorblind New Deal.[^app-e-katznelson]
- **Then read.** Lizabeth Cohen, *Making a New Deal: Industrial Workers in Chicago, 1919–1939* (1990). A social history of workers’ politics and the New Deal coalition. It shows how the New Deal was made and unmade at the local level.[^app-e-cohen]
- **Primary collections:** FRUS, the National Archives, the FDR Library, the Library of Congress, and the labor and economic series in §3.6.[^app-e-frus][^app-e-nara-catalog]

### 4.7 Cold War

- **Start with.** John Lewis Gaddis, *The Cold War: A New History* (2005). A short, lucid synthesis by a leading post-revisionist. It is a good first book and a partial one; read it with Williams, Leffler, and Westad.[^app-e-gaddis]
- **Then read.** Melvyn P. Leffler, *A Preponderance of Power: National Security, the Truman Administration, and the Cold War* (1992). The argument that American leaders sought a preponderance of power to secure the nation’s security and the international order. It is the fullest statement of the national-security interpretation.[^app-e-leffler]
- **Then read.** William Appleman Williams, *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959). The Open Door interpretation that made economic expansion central to American foreign policy. It is a classic of revisionism and a product of its moment.[^app-e-williams]
- **Then read.** Odd Arne Westad, *The Cold War: A World History* (2017). A global history that treats the Cold War as an ideological confrontation with deep roots and worldwide consequences. It is the best single book for the international turn.[^app-e-westad]
- **Then read.** Ellen Schrecker, *Many Are the Crimes: McCarthyism in America* (1998). A history of domestic anticommunism that shows how the Cold War shaped American politics and civil liberties. It is a necessary complement to the diplomatic story.[^app-e-schrecker]
- **Primary collections:** FRUS, CIA FOIA, the Wilson Center Digital Archive, the National Archives, and the Pentagon Papers.[^app-e-frus][^app-e-cia][^app-e-wilson][^app-e-pentagon]

### 4.8 Civil rights and the 1960s

- **Start with.** Jacquelyn Dowd Hall, “The Long Civil Rights Movement and the Political Uses of the Past” (2005). The essay that reframed the movement’s chronology and its political uses. It is the best single introduction to the historiographical debate.[^app-e-hall]
- **Then read.** Charles M. Payne, *I’ve Got the Light of Freedom: The Organizing Tradition and the Mississippi Freedom Struggle* (1995). A local history of organizing that shows how ordinary people made the movement. It is the corrective to a top-down, King-centered narrative.[^app-e-payne]
- **Then read.** David J. Garrow, *Bearing the Cross: Martin Luther King, Jr., and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference* (1986). The standard biography of King and the SCLC. It is detailed and sympathetic; pair it with Payne and Hall.[^app-e-garrow]
- **Then read.** Sundiata Keita Cha-Jua and Clarence Lang, “The ‘Long Movement’ as Vampire: Temporal and Spatial Fallacies in Recent Black Freedom Studies” (2007). The strongest critique of the long-movement framework. It argues that the framework collapses periods, erases the differences between civil rights and Black Power, and blurs regional distinctions.[^app-e-chajua]
- **Then read.** Thomas J. Sugrue, *The Origins of the Urban Crisis: Race and Inequality in Postwar Detroit* (1996). A history of deindustrialization, housing discrimination, and racial violence that explains the urban crisis from the ground up. It is essential for the history after 1965.[^app-e-sugrue]
- **Primary collections:** the Civil Rights Digital Library, the SNCC Digital Gateway, the King Papers, the Southern Oral History Program, and the National Archives.[^app-e-crdl][^app-e-sncc][^app-e-king][^app-e-sohp]

### 4.9 Late twentieth century

- **Start with.** Bruce J. Schulman, *The Seventies: The Great Shift in American Culture, Society, and Politics* (2001). A synthesis that treats the 1970s as a turning point rather than a malaise. It is a good first book and a broad one.[^app-e-schulman]
- **Then read.** Jefferson Cowie, *Stayin’ Alive: The 1970s and the Last Days of the Working Class* (2010). A history of working-class politics and culture in the 1970s. It explains how the New Deal coalition came apart.[^app-e-cowie]
- **Then read.** Judith Stein, *Pivotal Decade: How the United States Traded Factories for Finance in the Seventies* (2010). The argument that the 1970s saw a structural shift from manufacturing to finance. It is a political economy of the decade.[^app-e-stein]

### 5.1 Indigenous history

## 6. Digital Sources, Editions, and Permissions

### 6.1 Identifying the edition actually consulted

- Record the title page, copyright page, ISBN or DOI, publisher, series, editor or translator, and date. A reprint, an abridgment, a translation, and a digital facsimile are different editions, even when the title is the same.[^app-e-method]
- Distinguish a facsimile from a transcription and a transcription from an OCR text. A facsimile can show the document’s material form; a transcription can silently modernize spelling and punctuation; an OCR text can misread names, numbers, and dates. State which one was used.[^app-e-method]
- For a newspaper, record the issue date, page, and column. For a manuscript, record the collection, box, folder, and item. For a dataset, record the version, DOI, observation year, variables, universe, and denominator. For a web page, record the URL and the access date.[^app-e-access]
- A citation that gives only a website or a book title is not a locator. The reader must be able to find the passage.[^app-e-method]

### 6.2 Catalog metadata, publisher copy, and evidence

- A library catalog record can verify publication details. It cannot verify the book’s argument. A publisher’s description can verify the publisher’s characterization. It cannot verify the book’s evidence. A review can characterize a book. It is not the book.[^app-e-method]
- A primary source can establish that someone said or wrote something. It cannot establish that the statement was true. A commission report can establish what the commission found. It cannot establish that the finding is beyond dispute.[^app-e-method]
- A search snippet is a lead. An AI summary is a lead. Neither is a source. Open the document, read the relevant passage, and cite the locator.[^app-e-access]
- A link that opens is not proof that its contents support the sentence. Check the page’s text, date, and context. If the page has changed, record the version consulted.[^app-e-access]

### 6.3 Rights and permissions for the registered assets

- U.S. federal government works are generally not protected by copyright under 17 U.S.C. §105, but not every document in a federal archive is a federal work. A donated photograph, a licensed map, or a third-party illustration may still be protected. The National Archives says that most digitized images are in the public domain, that a small percentage may be subject to copyright, and that the user is responsible for clearing rights.[^app-e-nara-rights]
- The Library of Congress does not own the rights to all material in its collections and does not license it. “No known restrictions” is a statement about the Library’s knowledge, not a license. Check the item’s rights advisory and the underlying work.[^app-e-loc-rights]
- Smithsonian Open Access provides millions of images and data for reuse, but not everything in the Smithsonian is open access. Check the item-level license. A CC0 mark is a dedication; a CC BY mark requires attribution; a CC BY-SA mark requires share-alike.[^app-e-si-open][^app-e-cc]
- RightsStatements.org provides twelve standardized statements for cultural heritage. Use them to describe the rights status of an item, not to substitute for a license. A statement such as “No Copyright — United States” is not the same as a CC0 dedication.[^app-e-rightsstatements]
- Indigenous cultural rights are not exhausted by copyright. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance call for collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics. Mukurtu is a platform built with Indigenous communities to manage cultural heritage according to community protocols.[^app-e-care][^app-e-mukurtu] A Hiawatha Belt image, a sacred site photograph, or an oral history may require community authorization even when the copyright has expired.
- The book’s CC0 dedication covers the book’s original text. It does not grant rights to third-party images, maps, or datasets. Each registered asset needs its own rights record, credit line, and permission status.[^app-e-plan]
- A credit line should identify the creator, title, institution, identifier, license or rights statement, access date, and any modification. “Courtesy of the Library of Congress” is a start, not a complete credit.[^app-e-loc-rights]

### 6.4 Link rot, preservation, and evidence

- Save a PDF or page image for every source that carries a consequential claim. Use a DOI or a permalink where one exists. The Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine can preserve a page, but it is not a guarantee that the page was the version consulted.[^app-e-access]
- Record the access date in the note. If a page changes after the access date, the note still identifies the version used. If the page disappears, the saved copy is the evidence.[^app-e-access]
- Do not cite a search result as if it were the source. Do not cite a snippet as if it were the passage. Do not cite an AI summary as if it were a reading.[^app-e-method]
- Data requires the same care. Record the series identifier, the observation year, the units, the denominator, the vintage, and the transformations. A chart is not a source; the underlying series is.[^app-e-hsus]

### 6.5 What the appendix will not do

- It will not print a master bibliography. The combined notes and the chapter notes are the evidence trail; the guide is a selection.[^app-e-plan]
- It will not present unread works as verified evidence. The annotations will say what was read and what was recommended.[^app-e-method]
- It will not reproduce images or maps. The registered assets belong to the chapters and Appendix C.[^app-e-plan]
- It will not settle the debates it describes. It will give the reader the positions and the evidence to pursue them.[^app-e-higham]
