# Chapter 19. The Coming of the Crisis (1850–1859)

> “This Fourth July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.”[^ch19-yawp-douglass]
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave is the Fourth of July?,” address delivered at Corinthian Hall, Rochester, New York, July 5, 1852.[^ch19-yawp-douglass]

> “[T]he people have the lawful means to introduce it, or exclude it as they please, for the reason that slavery cannot exist a day or an hour anywhere, unless it is supported by local police regulations.”[^ch19-nps-freeport]
>
> — Senator Stephen A. Douglas, answer at the Freeport debate, Illinois, August 27, 1858.[^ch19-nps-freeport]

> “A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free.”[^ch19-nps-house-divided]
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, speech accepting the Illinois Republican convention’s nomination for the Senate, Springfield, Illinois, June 16, 1858.[^ch19-nps-house-divided]

> “One school, the fundamentalists, emphasizes the intrinsic, inevitable conflict between slavery and free labor. The other, the revisionists, emphasizes discrete events and political structures rather than slavery itself.”[^ch19-ayers-causes]
>
> — Edward L. Ayers, “What Caused the Civil War?,” *North & South* 8, no. 5 (2005).[^ch19-ayers-causes]

Four voices, four ways of naming the same crisis. An abolitionist told a Rochester audience that the nation’s birthday was not his. A senator told Illinois voters that a territory could starve slavery by withholding the police regulations that sustained it. A prairie lawyer said the house could not stand half slave and half free. A historian said the quarrel between “fundamentalists” and “revisionists” still shapes the argument. They disagree about whether slavery, party politics, or contingency drove the crisis, and about who had standing to decide. The question is not which voice was sincere. It is why a decade of settlements failed to contain slavery’s expansion.[^ch19-check-epigraphs]

William Henry, known in Syracuse as Jerry, was a cooper and an enslaved man who had escaped from Missouri. He had lived in Syracuse since 1843. On October 1, 1851, federal marshals seized him at work on a warrant that said he owed “service and labor” to a claimant in Missouri. They told him at first that he was wanted for theft. Only after they had put him in manacles did they say that the warrant came under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]

The warrant had been issued by U.S. Commissioner Joseph Sabine on September 29. Henry struggled furiously when he understood the charge. He was subdued and brought before Sabine for a hearing. An attempt to free him in the commissioner’s office failed, but he broke away and reached one of the bridges over the Erie Canal. He was recaptured there and taken to the police justice’s offices.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry]

By then a crowd had gathered. The Syracuse University Libraries account puts it at about 2,500, many in the city for an antislavery convention; the Onondaga Historical Association says a large crowd gathered outside. On a signal from the leaders, the crowd broke in. A few pistol shots were fired, but the crowd’s force, not gunfire, overcame the officers, and Henry was free.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]

Jermain Wesley Loguen, a formerly enslaved Methodist minister, and Samuel J. May, a Unitarian minister, helped organize the rescue. Henry was hidden in the city and then taken to Kingston, Ontario, where he lived until his death a few years later. May, speaking two weeks later, recalled the crowd’s anger at seeing “a man dragged through the streets, chained and held down in a cart by four or six others.” He also recalled that Daniel Webster had declared that the law would be enforced there, even during the antislavery convention.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry]

A grand jury in Onondaga County indicted Deputy Marshal Henry W. Allen for kidnapping under an 1840 New York law; his case was set for trial in June 1852 and became a vehicle for challenging the federal act. The two fullest accounts differ on the officers and first rescue attempt; they agree on the warrant, hearing, recapture, and storming. The weather, the crowd’s words, and Henry’s thoughts are not preserved. The rescue was organized resistance by Black activists and White allies, an answer to a federal law with a claim about who belonged to the city.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry][^ch19-check-scene]

Why did compromise fail to contain slavery’s expansion?

This chapter covers the decade between the Compromise of 1850 and John Brown’s execution on December 2, 1859. It follows the settlement of 1850 and its enforcement; the fugitive cases and the antislavery response; the Kansas-Nebraska Act, Bleeding Kansas, and party realignment; the expansion schemes of the mid-1850s; *Dred Scott* and the Lincoln-Douglas debates; the slave South and northern free Black communities; and Harpers Ferry. It does not re-narrate the Mexican-American War or the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which belong to Chapter 18, or the election of 1860 and secession, which belong to Chapter 20.[^ch19-check-scope][^ch19-check-ch18][^ch19-check-ch20][^ch19-check-dates]

It draws on the statutes and resolutions, the court opinions, the congressional debates and votes, newspapers and speeches, the abolitionist press, the records of the fugitive cases, and the scholarship on the coming of the Civil War. It treats “popular sovereignty,” “Bleeding Kansas,” and what contemporaries called the Slave Power — the political influence they attributed to slaveholders — as the era’s own contested vocabulary, not neutral description. Enslaved people were the objects of a political crisis they also acted in. Free Black communities and White abolitionists were agents of resistance, not bystanders. A story that reports only the politicians, or only the violence, will be incomplete.[^ch19-check-scope][^ch19-check-names][^ch19-check-race]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 follows the Compromise of 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act, and the first enforcement crisis. §2 turns to the fugitive cases in Table 1, the vigilance committees, *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*, and Black abolitionism. §3 follows the Kansas-Nebraska Act, Bleeding Kansas, the collapse of the Whigs, the rise of the Republican Party, the election of 1856 in Table 2, Map 15, Lecompton, and the expansion schemes. §4 examines *Dred Scott*, its reception, the Freeport Doctrine, and the Lincoln-Douglas debates. §5 turns to the slave South, northern free Black communities, and John Brown. §6 asks how historians explain the failure of compromise. The chapter begins in Syracuse on October 1, 1851, where a crowd rescued a man the law meant to return to slavery.[^ch19-check-scope]

## 1. The 1850 settlement

### 1.1 Clay, Douglas, and the five statutes

The Compromise of 1850 was not a single bargain. It was five statutes enacted in September 1850, and the most explosive of them was a stronger fugitive-slave law. The settlement is often named for Henry Clay, but it was assembled from separate bills and passed only after Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois took charge of them.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]

The crisis had been building since the conquest of Mexico. California’s request to enter the Union as a free state threatened the balance between “slave” and “free” states that had existed since the Missouri Compromise of 1820. On January 29, 1850, Clay of Kentucky proposed a series of resolutions to “settle and adjust amicably all existing questions of controversy … arising out of the institution of slavery.”[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-check-ch18]

The five statutes admitted California as a free state. They organized the territories of New Mexico and Utah and left the slavery question to their inhabitants. They fixed the Texas boundary and paid Texas $10 million. They abolished the slave trade, but not slavery, in the District of Columbia. And they amended the Fugitive Slave Act.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]

The Senate debated Clay’s package for seven months and rejected it. Douglas then steered substitute bills through both houses. Clay’s resolutions themselves were never adopted; the enacted statutes are what became the Compromise of 1850.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]

The compromise postponed the sectional reckoning. It also made the fugitive question national and explosive. A law that had been intended to protect the Union became the test of whether free-state communities would enforce slavery.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-check-ch18]

### 1.2 In Focus: the price of a certificate

**In Focus.** Section 8 of “An Act to amend, and supplementary to, the Act … respecting Fugitives from Justice, and Persons escaping from the Service of their Masters,” approved September 18, 1850, set the commissioner’s fee. It provided that “he shall be entitled to a fee of ten dollars in full for his services in each case, upon the delivery of the said certificate to the claimant, his agent or attorney; or a fee of five dollars in cases where the proof shall not, in the opinion of such commissioner, warrant such certificate and delivery.” The same act provided that “in no trial or hearing under this act shall the testimony of such alleged fugitive be admitted in evidence.”[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]

The arithmetic is the argument. A commissioner earned twice as much for returning an accused person as for freeing one. The person whose liberty was at stake could not testify. Section 5 commanded “all good citizens” to aid in executing the law and set a fine of up to $1,000 and imprisonment up to six months for obstructing it or aiding escape, plus $1,000 in civil damages for each person lost. Section 10 made a court record from the state of alleged escape “full and conclusive evidence” of the claim.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]

The act’s language of “service or labor” is the era’s euphemism for slavery. The clause lets the arithmetic and the silence speak: a law that paid for a verdict and excluded the accused from the hearing.[^ch19-check-focus]

### 1.3 Enforcement, resistance, and the price of union

The act’s machinery reached into northern communities. It expanded the number of federal commissioners empowered to issue certificates of removal. It told them to hear claims “in a summary manner.” It required federal and local law enforcement in all states to arrest suspected fugitives. And it commanded bystanders to help.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-ev-burns]

Resistance began at once. In Boston, vigilance committees helped the Crafts evade recapture in 1850, and a crowd rescued Shadrach Minkins from the courthouse in February 1851. In Syracuse, the rescue of Jerry answered the law with a crowd. In Christiana, Pennsylvania, a Black community fought off a posse.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-christiana-society]

The act did not produce northern submission. It produced rescues, antislavery literature, and a political realignment. It also made the 1850 settlement a continuing test of federal authority in the free states.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-ev-burns]

## 2. Fugitive cases and abolitionism

Some cases ended in rescue through organized force or crowd action. Others ended in rendition under heavy guard. The difference usually tracked the strength of the local vigilance network and the timing of federal preparation, not the merits of the claim.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims]

The cases made the law’s costs visible: an enslaver killed at Christiana, a Boston courthouse draped in chains, a man marched to a ship through the streets of a free city. ### 2.2 Vigilance committees and the politics of rescue

The rescues were organized work, and Black-led vigilance committees were often at their core. In Boston, the vigilance committee formed to resist the Fugitive Slave Act helped the Crafts and tried to free Anthony Burns. Its courthouse assault in May 1854 killed Deputy Marshal James Batchelder. Boston authorities then guarded Burns with hundreds of armed men.[^ch19-ev-burns]

In Syracuse, the rescue rested on a network that included Loguen, May, Gerrit Smith, and Frederick Douglass. In Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, the Christiana Resistance was a Black community’s armed defense of four self-emancipated men. About 141 people were arrested and 38 indicted. The first defendant, Castner Hanway, was acquitted of treason in December 1851.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-christiana-society]

The fugitive crisis was also a crisis of families. In the winter of 1856 Margaret Garner crossed the frozen Ohio River from Kentucky to Cincinnati with her family. Federal marshals captured them. Rather than see her two-year-old daughter returned to slavery, Garner killed the child. The family lost its freedom suit and was returned to slavery; Garner died in Mississippi in 1858. The case exposed the sexual coercion and family separation that the law protected, though the record does not settle every question about the Garner household.[^ch19-kchr-garner][^ch19-aaihs-garner]

Rescue politics made the fugitive question a test of local and federal authority. It also pushed many northerners toward the Republican Party. The committees were institutions, not spontaneous crowds.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-check-race]

### 2.3 *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*

Harriet Beecher Stowe turned the fugitive question into mass literature. Stowe had lived in Cincinnati, across the river from Kentucky. She began the story as a serial in the *National Era*, where it ran from June 5, 1851, to April 1, 1852. The two-volume book was published on March 20, 1852. More than 10,000 copies sold in the first week, and about 300,000 in the United States by the end of the year. British sales soon reached more than 1.5 million, according to the Vassar exhibition.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]

Stowe answered southern accusations that she had invented her material with *A Key to Uncle Tom’s Cabin* in 1853, a collection of testimony, newspaper advertisements, and reports. The novel also inspired more than 20 “Anti-Uncle Tom” novels defending slavery.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]

*Uncle Tom’s Cabin* did not cause the crisis, but it transformed the audience for it. It made slavery’s domestic cruelty a national subject, and it gave northern readers a vocabulary for the fugitive cases they were reading in the newspapers.[^ch19-vassar-stowe][^ch19-check-focus]

### 2.4 Black abolitionism: Douglass and Tubman

The decade’s antislavery politics were led in significant part by Black activists whose own freedom was precarious. Frederick Douglass told an abolitionist audience in Rochester on July 5, 1852, that the Fourth of July was theirs, not his: “This Fourth July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.” He added that “the sunlight that brought light and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me.”[^ch19-yawp-douglass]

Harriet Tubman escaped enslavement in Maryland in 1849. Over the next decade she made about 13 trips into Maryland to lead family and friends out, and she gave instructions to about 70 more who freed themselves. She later worked with John Brown and, during the Civil War, with Union forces in South Carolina.[^ch19-nps-tubman]

By the 1850s about 200,000 free Black people lived in the North in a state of “unfreedom.” Black laws denied them citizenship, suffrage, and property rights. Massachusetts had barred Black residence, Ohio required a $500 bond of Black entrants, and Indiana barred Black testimony and voting. Churches, schools, and vigilance committees formed the infrastructure of the freedom struggle.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom]

Black abolitionism gave the fugitive crisis its moral clarity and its personnel. The Syracuse crowd, the Christiana defenders, and the vigilance committees belonged to this broader movement.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-check-race]

## 3. Kansas, party realignment, and expansion schemes

### 3.1 The Kansas-Nebraska Act

The Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed the Missouri Compromise and reopened the slavery question in territory that had been closed to it. It also broke the party system that had contained the conflict.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]

Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois wanted a northern route for a transcontinental railroad. On January 4, 1854, he introduced a bill to organize the Nebraska territory “with or without slavery, as their constitutions may prescribe.” That formula was popular sovereignty: the settlers of a territory would decide whether to permit slavery.[^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]

Southern senators led by David Atchison of Missouri demanded an explicit repeal of the 1820 line. Douglas agreed. “I will incorporate it into my bill,” he told Atchison, “though I know it will raise a hell of a storm.”[^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]

The Senate passed the bill 37 to 14 in the early morning hours of March 4, 1854. It became law on May 30. It created the territories of Kansas and Nebraska and left slavery to the settlers.[^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska]

The act outraged antislavery northerners. It destroyed the remnants of the Whig coalition and helped produce the Republican Party. It also produced the violent settlement race known as Bleeding Kansas.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]

### 3.2 Bleeding Kansas and the caning of Sumner

The Kansas settlement race turned the territorial question into open violence, and the violence spread into Congress itself. On May 21, 1856, a proslavery posse surrounded and sacked the free-state town of Lawrence, Kansas. The posse flew a blood-red flag inscribed “Southern Rights,” and the inhabitants fled.[^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]

Three days earlier, Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts had denounced “the Crime Against Kansas.” He named Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina as a principal culprit and called slavery “the harlot, Slavery.” On May 22, Representative Preston Brooks of South Carolina, Butler’s kinsman, entered the Senate chamber after adjournment and beat Sumner with a metal-topped cane until the cane broke. Sumner was carried away bleeding and did not fully recover for years. Brooks survived a House censure, resigned, and was immediately reelected.[^ch19-senate-sumner][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]

On the night of May 24–25, 1856, John Brown and four of his sons dragged five proslavery men from their cabins near Pottawatomie Creek and killed them with broadswords. Brown had come to Kansas to fight for a free territory.[^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]

The violence in Kansas and the caning in the Senate taught both sections that the conflict would be settled by force at least as often as by law.[^ch19-senate-sumner][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]

### 3.3 The collapse of the Whigs and the making of the Republican Party

Kansas-Nebraska broke the second party system. The Whig coalition disintegrated under the sectional strain. A nativist movement, the American or Know-Nothing Party, briefly competed for its voters. The Democratic Party survived but divided increasingly along sectional lines.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska]

Antislavery opponents of Kansas-Nebraska founded the Republican Party in 1854. Its 1856 platform, adopted June 18, denied the authority “of Congress, of a Territorial Legislation, of any individual, or association of individuals, to give legal existence to Slavery in any Territory of the United States.” It denounced slavery and polygamy as “twin relics of barbarism.”[^ch19-gop-platform-1856]

The Republicans offered northern voters a party organized against slavery’s expansion. The realignment set the stage for the 1856 election and, eventually, for 1860.[^ch19-gop-platform-1856][^ch19-election-1856]

### 3.4 Table 2: the election of 1856

The election of 1856 confirmed the sectional realignment. A Democrat won the presidency while a new Republican Party carried most of the North.[^ch19-election-1856]

James Buchanan won 174 electoral votes and 1,836,072 popular votes, about 45.3 percent. John C. Frémont, the Republican, won 114 electoral votes and 1,342,345 popular votes, about 33.1 percent. Millard Fillmore, the Whig-American, won eight electoral votes and 873,053 popular votes, about 21.6 percent.[^ch19-election-1856]

Buchanan won with less than a majority of the popular vote. His support was concentrated in the slave states, and Frémont’s was concentrated in the North. The result showed a party system reorganizing itself on sectional lines.[^ch19-election-1856]

### 3.6 Lecompton, the English bill, and Douglas’s break with Buchanan

The Lecompton Constitution forced a split between the Democratic president and the Democratic senator whose doctrine of popular sovereignty Kansas was supposed to vindicate.[^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-english-bill]

A proslavery convention at Lecompton wrote a state constitution in 1857. A ratification election in December 1857, boycotted by free-state settlers, approved it. A second election in January 1858 overwhelmingly defeated it.[^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-english-bill]

Congress balked. The English bill of 1858 offered Kansas admission conditioned on a vote on a land grant and constitution. Kansans rejected the offer in August 1858 by an overwhelming margin.[^ch19-english-bill][^ch19-lecompton]

Douglas broke with President James Buchanan over Lecompton. He defended popular sovereignty against the proslavery constitution. The rupture made Douglas unpopular in the South and set him on the collision course that shaped 1860.[^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-nps-freeport]

### 3.7 Expansion schemes: Gadsden, Ostend, and the filibusters

The crisis coincided with an aggressive, and ultimately frustrated, campaign to extend slavery’s reach beyond the continental United States.[^ch19-state-gadsden][^ch19-state-territorial-expansion]

The United States and Mexico concluded the Gadsden Purchase in 1853–54. The United States paid Mexico $10 million for 29,670 square miles that became part of Arizona and New Mexico, providing a route for a southern transcontinental railroad. The Senate ratified a revised treaty on April 25, 1854, and Santa Anna signed it on June 8, 1854.[^ch19-state-gadsden][^ch19-check-gadsden]

The Ostend Manifesto was a different kind of document. In 1854 three American diplomats drafted a statement urging the acquisition of Cuba and framing it as essential to American interests. When it became public, it embarrassed the Pierce administration and drew condemnation. The Republican platform of 1856 rejected its “highwayman’s plea, that ‘might makes right.’”[^ch19-state-territorial-expansion][^ch19-gop-platform-1856]

Ostend was evidence of a slaveholding expansionist current, not an official policy the government adopted. Private armed expeditions pursued slavery’s extension outside official diplomacy. Narciso López’s filibustering expeditions against Cuba ended with his execution in 1851; William Walker’s expedition to Nicaragua began in 1855 and briefly made him president of that country. They mostly failed and produced local hostility to American expansion.[^ch19-state-territorial-expansion]

The expansion schemes show that the sectional argument was not confined to the territories. For many slaveholders, slavery’s security required new ground, and the republic’s boundaries were argued over as a question of slavery’s future.[^ch19-state-territorial-expansion][^ch19-check-race]

## 4. *Dred Scott* and the debates

### 4.1 The *Dred Scott* decision

In *Dred Scott v. Sandford*, decided March 6, 1857, the Supreme Court held that enslaved people were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in a federal territory. The decision appeared to make the Republican position unconstitutional.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott]

Dred Scott and his wife Harriet sued for their freedom in St. Louis in 1846. They argued that they had been held in a free territory. What began as a private suit became an 11-year legal struggle that grew as slavery became the central issue of national politics.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott]

Chief Justice Roger B. Taney’s majority opinion stated that the descendants of enslaved Africans, whether free or enslaved, were not “citizens” within the meaning of the Constitution. It described enslaved people as “beings of an inferior order … and so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect.” It held that Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in the territories.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott]

The decision went beyond the case. It struck at the Republican Party’s central plank. Rather than settling the territorial question, it made the question constitutional and intensified the political conflict.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

### 4.2 Reaction: Lincoln’s reply and the Republican plank

Republicans treated *Dred Scott* as a partisan and constitutional catastrophe. Lincoln answered it by appealing to the Declaration of Independence.[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

In a speech at Springfield on June 26, 1857, Lincoln said the decision “declares two propositions—first, that a Negro cannot sue in the U.S. Courts; and secondly, that Congress cannot prohibit slavery in the Territories.” He added that it was made by a divided court.[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

Lincoln argued that the Declaration’s “all men are created equal” set a standard for free society, even if it did not describe present conditions. He conceded that a Black woman was not his equal “in some respects,” but insisted that “in her natural right to eat the bread she earns with her own hands without asking leave of any one else, she is my equal, and the equal of all others.”[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

The argument had limits. Lincoln did not claim that Black and White Americans were equal in every respect, and he continued to support colonization. The speech’s force lies in the standard it set, not in a modern egalitarianism.[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply][^ch19-check-race]

*Dred Scott* gave the Republicans a grievance and a target. The party’s 1856 platform had already denied any authority to give slavery legal existence in a territory. After 1857 the argument was against the Court as well.[^ch19-gop-platform-1856][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

### 4.3 The Freeport Doctrine

Douglas answered *Dred Scott* with a doctrine of local police power. At the second Lincoln-Douglas debate at Freeport, Illinois, on August 27, 1858, Lincoln asked whether the people of a territory could lawfully exclude slavery before forming a state constitution.[^ch19-nps-freeport]

Douglas said the people “have the lawful means to introduce it, or exclude it as they please, for the reason that slavery cannot exist a day or an hour anywhere, unless it is supported by local police regulations.” Territorial legislatures could not forbid slavery directly. They could make it practically impossible by withholding the laws slavery required.[^ch19-nps-freeport]

The answer helped Douglas in Illinois and cost him the South. Southern Democrats demanded a federal slave code for the territories. That demand split the Democratic convention at Charleston in April 1860, which is Chapter 20’s opening scene.[^ch19-nps-freeport][^ch19-check-ch20]

### 4.4 The Lincoln-Douglas debates and the House Divided

The 1858 debates made Lincoln a national figure. They also framed the crisis as a choice between a nation all free and a nation all slave.[^ch19-nps-house-divided][^ch19-nps-freeport]

Accepting the Illinois Republican nomination on June 16, 1858, Lincoln said, “A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free.” He predicted that the Union would become all one thing or all the other.[^ch19-nps-house-divided]

Lincoln and Douglas met in seven debates in the late summer and fall of 1858. National coverage carried their words by telegraph and reprinted them in newspapers. Douglas, the incumbent, defended popular sovereignty; Lincoln argued that the territories should be kept free.[^ch19-nps-freeport]

Douglas was reelected to the Senate by the narrowest of margins. The campaign made Lincoln a national candidate and made the South read Douglas as an enemy of slavery.[^ch19-nps-freeport]

By 1858 the political argument had narrowed to the question Lincoln had named: whether a republic half slave and half free could endure.[^ch19-nps-house-divided][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

## 5. The slave South, free Black communities, and John Brown

### 5.1 The slave South: cotton, expansion, and political power

The crisis of the 1850s was rooted in a slave South whose wealth and political power grew with cotton and depended on slavery’s expansion.[^ch19-yawp-cotton]

By 1860 the South produced about two billion pounds of cotton, more than 60 percent of all United States exports. Enslaved people were the central capital asset of the cotton economy. Prime field hands averaged $1,600 at market by 1850, and land and enslaved labor were pledged as collateral for further expansion.[^ch19-yawp-cotton]

About four million enslaved people lived in the South in 1860, nearly 2.5 million of them in the Cotton Belt. The internal slave trade moved people from the Upper South to the Southwest and separated families. Slaveholding wealth underwrote political power, and proslavery ideology defended slavery as the foundation of order.[^ch19-yawp-cotton]

For the slaveholding South, the territories were not an abstraction. The question of whether slavery could expand was the question of whether the institution could remain politically and economically secure. The chapter presents the slaveholding interest at its strongest, and the enslaved majority as actors within it.[^ch19-yawp-cotton][^ch19-check-race]

### 5.2 Free Black communities and northern “unfreedom”

The North’s free Black communities lived in a legal “unfreedom” that made them both the targets of the fugitive law and the organizers of resistance.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom]

By the 1850s about 200,000 free Black people lived in the North. Black laws denied them citizenship, suffrage, and property rights and exposed them to violence without recourse. Massachusetts had barred Black residence, Ohio required a $500 bond of Black entrants, and Indiana barred Black testimony and voting.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom]

The Black convention movement began with the American Society of Free Persons of Color, which held its first convention in Philadelphia in 1830. It spread westward with Black migrants. Churches, schools, and vigilance committees formed the infrastructure of the freedom struggle.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-ev-burns]

The chapter does not treat the North as simply free. The same decade that produced the Fugitive Slave Act also produced the black laws, and the same communities that suffered both led the resistance.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-check-race]

### 5.3 John Brown from Pottawatomie to Harpers Ferry

John Brown carried the argument over slavery from the Kansas plains to a federal armory in Virginia. His raid made the coming conflict visible.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

Brown arrived at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, on July 3, 1859. He raised money from abolitionists and rented the Kennedy farm in Maryland under the alias Isaac Smith. His force numbered 21 at the time of the raid. He studied the armory, the trains, and the town.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

On the night of October 16, 1859, Brown and his men took the bridges, the U.S. Armory and Arsenal, and the Rifle Works. They seized hostages, including the enslaver Lewis Washington, and freed the people Washington enslaved. The next day, townspeople and militia fired on the raiders. The first person killed was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railroad employee.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

On October 18, Colonel Robert E. Lee and a company of U.S. Marines stormed the engine house where Brown and his surviving men had holed up. Sixteen people died in the raid, including ten of Brown’s men. Brown’s trial began on October 27, 1859, and lasted five days. The jury convicted him on all charges, and he was sentenced to hang on November 2. He was executed on December 2, 1859.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

Brown told an armory watchman, “I came here from Kansas and this is a slave state; I want to free all the negroes in this state.” In his final statement he wrote, “I, John Brown, am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood.”[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

### 5.4 The aftermath of Harpers Ferry

Harpers Ferry accelerated the disunion it intended to prevent. It hardened the South and elevated Brown in northern memory.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

A contemporary newspaper account quoted by the National Park Service concluded that “the Harpers Ferry invasion has advanced the cause of disunion more than any other event that has happened since the formation of the Government.” Brown’s bearing at trial and execution made him a martyr to many northern abolitionists.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

Brown’s raid and his final words became a permanent part of the antislavery argument. His execution widened the breach between the sections.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

By December 1859 the party system, the courts, and the territorial settlements had all failed to contain the conflict. The next year’s election and secession are Chapter 20’s subject.[^ch19-check-ch20][^ch19-nps-freeport]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The evidence supports several conclusions. The Compromise of 1850 deferred rather than resolved the conflict. Congress passed five statutes in September 1850, admitting California free and strengthening the Fugitive Slave Act. The fugitive law became the flashpoint.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]

The fugitive cases are documented. Rescues at Syracuse and Boston, the armed resistance at Christiana, and renditions at Boston in 1851 and 1854 are established in court and institutional records.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-sims][^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-christiana-society]

Kansas-Nebraska reopened the territorial question and broke the party system. The act of May 30, 1854, repealed the Missouri Compromise, and the violence known as Bleeding Kansas followed.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]

Slavery stood at the center of the political argument. *Dred Scott*, the Lincoln-Douglas debates, and the Freeport Doctrine all turned on slavery’s extension, whatever else divided the parties.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-nps-freeport][^ch19-gop-platform-1856]

Harpers Ferry escalated the crisis. Brown’s raid and execution, and the reaction to them, narrowed the space for compromise.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]

The costs were borne unequally. Enslaved people, free Black communities, and fugitives bore the law’s violence; the politicians argued over it. The chapter keeps that asymmetry central.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-yawp-cotton][^ch19-check-race]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the conflict inevitable? If slavery was the fundamental and unbridgeable division, war was near-inevitable. If the political system failed at specific moments, the outcome was contingent. The chapter weighs both without dissolving one into the other.[^ch19-ayers-causes][^ch19-cwmonitor-reading]

What weight should party politics carry? As the *Civil War Monitor*’s reading guide summarizes, Michael Holt emphasizes the collapse of the national parties, and Marc Egnal recasts the party story as an economic one in which East–West ties displaced North–South ones. Potter emphasizes slavery’s centrality. The chapter tests each against the elections, the conventions, and the votes.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]

How much did slavery’s expansion drive the crisis? For many slaveholders, expansion was a condition of security. For many northerners, the issue was the spread of an institution they opposed without intending Black equality. The chapter distinguishes antislavery from anti-Black prejudice.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-race]

Whose agency mattered most? Fugitives, vigilance committees, and Black abolitionists forced the issue; politicians responded. The chapter does not let the politicians’ records eclipse the acts of the enslaved and free Black actors.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-tubman][^ch19-check-race]

Whose voices survive? Statutes, court opinions, speeches, and newspapers document the politicians and the cases. The enslaved left fewer direct records, and the law excluded the accused fugitive’s testimony. The chapter names that silence.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-yawp-cotton][^ch19-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the failure of compromise was overdetermined but not foreordained. Slavery’s extension was the central and unbridgeable question. The specific failures of party, court, and settlement made the rupture more likely without dictating its timing or form.

The fundamentalist position, as Ayers summarizes it, emphasizes the intrinsic, inevitable conflict between slavery and free labor. Read critically, it names the central issue but can make the outcome seem automatic and obscure the choices available to actors.[^ch19-ayers-causes]

The revisionist and party-centered position, as the *Civil War Monitor*’s reading guide describes it, locates the crisis in the collapse of the national two-party system. Michael Holt argues that the breakdown made Americans see internal enemies. Marc Egnal recasts the party story as an economic one, in which East–West economic ties displaced North–South ones. These accounts restore contingency and structural depth but can understate slavery’s moral and political force.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]

A third position emphasizes the slaveholders’ offensive. As the *Civil War Monitor*’s reading guide describes it, Charles Dew’s study of secession commissioners finds that the maintenance of racial dominance was the dominant argument for disunion. William W. Freehling’s *The Road to Disunion*, volume 2, supplies the broad account of the southern role. Dew’s commissioners spoke for a movement, not for every southerner. These works strengthen the case for slavery as the cause while keeping southern agency in view.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]

The synthesis for this chapter begins with David M. Potter’s *The Impending Crisis*. The book treats slavery as pervading the sectional conflict without reducing the crisis to a single cause. The chapter presents the debate as overlapping positions, not teams, and tests each against the cases, the votes, and the violence.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]

## Legacies

The settlements did not hold. The Compromise of 1850 bought a decade at the price of a fugitive-slave law that many northern communities refused to enforce. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 tore up the line that had contained the conflict since 1820. *Dred Scott* made the Republican position unconstitutional in the Court’s eyes, and the Freeport Doctrine answered the Court with a doctrine of local police power. Each settlement made the next one less credible.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-nps-freeport]

Harpers Ferry gave the crisis its martyr and its warning. Brown’s raid failed militarily, but his words and his execution entered the antislavery argument permanently. The South read the northern response as proof that the free states would not protect slavery. Within a year and a half, the election of 1860 and secession carried the conflict past the reach of compromise.[^ch19-nps-john-brown][^ch19-check-ch20]

The decade’s lasting legacy is a question about the republic itself. A nation that claimed to govern by consent enforced a law that denied the accused a hearing and paid its officials more to enslave a person than to free one. The people who answered it — in Syracuse, in Boston, at Christiana, and in Kansas — did not wait for the politicians to resolve the contradiction. The chapter’s question, why compromise failed, has its sharpest answer in their acts as much as in the votes.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-christiana-society][^ch19-check-race]

## Summary

- The Compromise of 1850 was five statutes passed in September 1850; it admitted California free, organized New Mexico and Utah, fixed the Texas boundary, abolished the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and strengthened the Fugitive Slave Act.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- The Fugitive Slave Act required free-state enforcement, paid commissioners more for returning than for freeing an accused person, and denied the accused a voice in the hearing; it made the settlement a continuing test of federal authority.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]
- A small set of fugitive cases — Minkins and Sims in Boston, Christiana in Pennsylvania, the Jerry Rescue in Syracuse, Glover in Wisconsin, and Burns in Boston — turned the statute into a national drama of rescue and rendition.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims][^ch19-christiana-society][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-glover]
- *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*, published in book form on March 20, 1852, sold about 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year and made the fugitive question mass literature.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]
- The Kansas-Nebraska Act of May 30, 1854, repealed the Missouri Compromise, introduced popular sovereignty, destroyed the Whig coalition, and produced Bleeding Kansas.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]
- On May 22, 1856, Representative Preston Brooks beat Senator Charles Sumner into unconsciousness; on the night of May 24–25, 1856, John Brown and four of his sons killed five proslavery men at Pottawatomie Creek.[^ch19-senate-sumner][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]
- In the election of 1856, Buchanan won 174 electoral votes and about 45 percent of the popular vote, Frémont carried most of the North with 114 electoral votes, and Fillmore won eight.[^ch19-election-1856]
- On March 6, 1857, the Supreme Court in *Dred Scott v. Sandford* held that enslaved people were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories; Lincoln answered that the Declaration’s equality set a standard for free society.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]
- Douglas’s Freeport Doctrine of August 27, 1858, held that a territory could exclude slavery by withholding local police regulations, splitting him from the South.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- On October 16–18, 1859, John Brown’s raid on Harpers Ferry failed; he was tried, convicted, and executed on December 2, 1859, and the raid widened the breach between the sections.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- The debate turns on whether the war was inevitable, a contingent failure of party politics, a slaveholders’ offensive, or some combination, and the honest answer holds several positions together.[^ch19-check-tables][^ch19-cwmonitor-reading]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The decade between the Compromise of 1850 and John Brown’s execution is the record of a republic repeatedly promising itself that the slavery question had been settled. Each settlement — the statutes of 1850, the territorial compromises, the Court’s opinion of 1857 — changed the terms of the conflict without ending it, and each made the next settlement harder to believe. The people who refused to wait for the politicians, from the Syracuse crowd to the men and women who freed themselves, forced the issue into the open. The chapter’s question is why compromise failed, and its answer begins with the law’s own machinery and the courage of those who defied it.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-john-brown]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at the Democratic National Convention in Charleston, South Carolina, in April 1860, where the demand for a federal slave code for the territories split the party Douglas had led. That demand was the direct answer to his Freeport Doctrine, and the split helped elect Abraham Lincoln. Chapter 20 takes up the election of 1860, secession, and the coming of the war.[^ch19-check-ch20][^ch19-nps-freeport]
