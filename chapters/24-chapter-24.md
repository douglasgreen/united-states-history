# Chapter 24. The Second Industrial Revolution (1865–1900)

> “This association of poverty with progress is the great enigma of our times. It is the central fact from which spring industrial, social, and political difficulties that perplex the world, and with which statesmanship and philanthropy and education grapple in vain.”[^ch24-yawp-george]

> — Henry George, *Progress and Poverty*, 1879.[^ch24-yawp-george]

> “We accept and welcome, therefore, as conditions to which we must accommodate ourselves, great inequality of environment, the concentration of business, industrial and commercial, in the hands of a few, and the law of competition between these, as being not only beneficial, but essential for the future progress of the race.”[^ch24-yawp-carnegie]

> — Andrew Carnegie, “Wealth,” *North American Review*, June 1889.[^ch24-yawp-carnegie]

> “In many sectors of the economy the visible hand of management replaced what Adam Smith referred to as the invisible hand of market forces.”[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

> — Alfred D. Chandler Jr., *The Visible Hand: The Managerial Revolution in American Business*, 1977.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

A land reformer called the coupling of wealth and want the great riddle of the age. A steelmaker welcomed the concentration of business as the engine of progress. A business historian argued that the decisive change was organizational: salaried managers, not market prices, increasingly coordinated the economy. The three disagree about whether industrial progress was the problem, the price of progress, or a question of institutional design. This chapter asks how private enterprises acquired public power.[^ch24-check-epigraphs]

At 9 o’clock on Sunday morning, November 18, 1883, by the old local time, James Hamblet stopped the pendulum of the standard clock in Room 48 of the Western Union Telegraph Building in New York. The clock rested for three minutes and 58.38 seconds. Its electric tickers, which drove the time signals in jewelry and watch stores across the city, fell silent with it. Then Hamblet started the pendulum again. “Mr. Hamblet had changed the time of New York City and State,” the *New York Times* reported the next morning.[^ch24-nyt-standard-time]

The change was not a municipal ordinance or an act of Congress. It was the local implementation of a decision by the General Time Convention, an association of railroad companies. On October 11, 1883, the convention had met at the Grand Pacific Hotel in Chicago. William F. Allen, the convention’s secretary and the publisher of the *Traveler’s Official Railway Guide*, presented a plan for five time zones: Intercolonial, Eastern, Central, Mountain, and Pacific. The zones would follow the mean sun-time on the 75th, 90th, 105th, and 120th meridians west of Greenwich, one hour apart. The railroads adopted the plan and set the new standard for noon on November 18.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-loc-two-noons]

In New York, the New York Central Railroad took the new time at 10 o’clock, becoming the first railroad in the country to do so. The city’s tower clocks and show clocks were stopped for four minutes and started again. At noon, the Western Union time ball fell on the new standard, giving shipmasters a signal on the 75th meridian. A crowd gathered at City Hall to watch the clock under the figure of Justice. When the hands moved again after their brief pause, one disappointed spectator concluded that the whole thing was “a sell.”[^ch24-nyt-standard-time]

Not every city complied. The *Times* reported that Chicago’s authorities had refused the new standard, perhaps because the Chicago meridian had not been chosen. The federal government did not make standard time law until the Standard Time Act of 1918, which also created daylight saving time and gave the Interstate Commerce Commission authority over zone boundaries. For thirty-five years, the railroads’ private standard governed the nation’s clocks.[^ch24-nyt-standard-time][^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]

A private association of companies had standardized the public’s time. How did private enterprises acquire public power?[^ch24-check-scene]

This chapter covers the thirty-five years from the end of the Civil War to 1900. It asks how the railroad, the steelworks, the refinery, the investment bank, and the trust became institutions with power over the conditions of citizenship. It draws on railroad and corporate records, statutes and court decisions, census and immigration series, the reports of the United States Strike Commission, labor and reform newspapers, the accounts of workers and reformers, and the company’s own town and lease forms. It treats “progress,” “trust,” “monopoly,” “robber baron,” and “new immigration” as terms that require definition rather than as neutral descriptions. It distinguishes what the corporations claimed from what the record shows. The era produced both an extraordinary increase in productive capacity and a heavy concentration of power over time, work, and place.[^ch24-check-scope][^ch24-check-names][^ch24-strike-commission]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the railroads, the Crédit Mobilier, and the standardization of time. §2 turns to steel, oil, finance, and the trust. §3 follows immigration, Chinese exclusion, and the industrial city. §4 examines the labor conflicts from 1877 through Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman. §5 asks how Americans argued about the inequality the new order produced. §6 asks how historians explain the rise of corporate power. The chapter begins in New York on November 18, 1883, when the nation’s clocks were reset to railroad time.

## 1. Railroads, Crédit Mobilier, and standard time

The railroad was the first American business to require large fixed capital, professional management, and coordinated operation at a distance. It became the model for the corporation that followed, and its need for timetables made it the agent of the century’s most intimate standardization.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand][^ch24-gilder-railroads]

### 1.1 The railroad as the first big business

At the end of the Civil War the United States had about 35,000 miles of railroad track. By 1890 it had about 167,000. The network consumed enormous quantities of steel, coal, and timber and employed a workforce that reached across the continent. Its fixed costs were high, its traffic was uneven, and its managers had to coordinate trains, freight, and repairs across hundreds of miles. The railroad therefore developed the accounting, the divisional organization, and the salaried hierarchy that later corporations copied.[^ch24-gilder-railroads][^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

Alfred D. Chandler Jr. called this the “visible hand” of management. In many sectors of the economy, he argued, the salaried managers of large enterprises coordinated production and distribution more efficiently than market prices could. The claim is an argument about efficiency, not a moral verdict. It explains why the corporation grew; it does not by itself explain who paid for it, who was excluded from it, or who was coerced into its service. Those questions require the rest of the chapter.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

**Figure 4** plots the three measures that made the railroad the center of the new economy: route mileage, steel output, and the urban share of the population. The panels are separate because the units are not comparable. Rail mileage is a stock of track; steel output is an annual flow of tons; the urban share is a percentage of a changing population, defined by a Census threshold that moved during the period. A chart that places the three on one axis would conceal the definitions it depends on.[^ch24-check-figure][^ch24-census-urbanization]

### 1.2 Public subsidy and the Crédit Mobilier

The transcontinental railroads were not built by private capital alone. The Pacific Railroad Acts of 1862 and 1864 chartered the Union Pacific and authorized federal land grants and bonds for the line. Because no railroad yet existed to generate revenue, the money was made in constructing the road. That structure created an incentive to bill the company for the work rather than to finish it cheaply.[^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]

Union Pacific officials created Crédit Mobilier of America to build the road. The construction company charged the railroad more than the work cost, and the profits went to the same men who controlled both companies. One of them, Representative Oakes Ames of Massachusetts, sold shares at bargain rates to roughly a dozen high-ranking colleagues, including Schuyler Colfax, a former Speaker of the House and by 1872 the vice president, to buy political protection. On September 4, 1872, the *New York Sun* exposed the scheme. Speaker James G. Blaine appointed a select committee chaired by Representative Luke Poland in December, and on February 27, 1873, the House censured Ames and Representative James Brooks for using their political influence for personal financial gain.[^ch24-house-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]

The scandal mattered beyond the fate of two congressmen. It showed that a charter, a land grant, and a bond guarantee could be turned into private profit, and that the men who wrote the rules could be the men who benefited from them. The railroad was not merely a business that used the state. It was a business that had been created by the state, and the line between public purpose and private enrichment ran through the same boardroom.[^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier][^ch24-check-sources]

### 1.3 Standard time and the standardization of the nation

The standardization of time was a private achievement with public consequences. Charles F. Dowd proposed a system of national time zones in 1863, and William F. Allen adapted the idea for the railroads. The General Time Convention adopted Allen’s plan on October 11, 1883, and set the change for November 18. Both the United States Naval Observatory and the Allegheny Observatory in Pittsburgh sent telegraph signals to help the railroads synchronize their clocks. Railroad officials visited city governments to persuade them to adopt the new standard, and newspapers printed maps and instructions for resetting watches.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-loc-two-noons]

Contemporary opinion divided. Many welcomed the convenience. Some objected to what they considered a violation of the natural order. The *Indianapolis Sentinel* published a condemnation that captured the stakes: “People will have to marry by railroad time and die by railroad time. Ministers will be required to preach by railroad time — banks will open and close by railroad time — in fact, the Railroad Convention has taken charge of the time business, and the people may as well set about adjusting their affairs in accordance with its decree.”[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]

The federal government did not codify standard time until the Standard Time Act of 1918. The act placed the authority to set zone boundaries with the Interstate Commerce Commission and created daylight saving time. Until then, the railroads’ standard was the nation’s standard, adopted city by city and state by state. The pattern would repeat: a private institution sets a rule that organizes public life, and the state ratifies it later.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]

### 1.4 Regulation: *Wabash*, the Interstate Commerce Act

The railroads set prices, excluded competitors, and favored large shippers with rebates. They often charged more for a short haul than for a longer one over the same line, a practice that fell hardest on farmers and small shippers. State legislatures, many under Granger pressure, passed regulatory laws in the 1870s, and in *Munn v. Illinois* (1877) the Supreme Court upheld the state’s power to regulate a business affected with a public interest.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce]

In *Wabash, St. Louis & Pacific Railway Co. v. Illinois* (1886), the Court held that a state could not regulate interstate railroad rates. The decision left the federal government as the only regulator. Congress responded with the Interstate Commerce Act, approved on February 4, 1887. The act created a five-member Interstate Commerce Commission, required that rates be “reasonable and just,” prohibited rebates and preferences, forbade the long-haul/short-haul discrimination, and banned pooling. It was the first federal independent regulatory commission.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]

The act was a beginning, not a solution. It did not give the commission independent power to set rates, and it relied on the courts to enforce its orders. Its terms often contradicted one another, and the railroads contested its authority. The commission’s most effective powers were the requirement that railroads file annual reports and the ban on special rates arranged among the railroads themselves. The first federal regulation of a private industry was a response to the industry’s own power, and it was weaker than the problem it addressed.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]

## 2. Steel, oil, finance, and trusts

The railroad created the demand; the steelworks, the refinery, and the investment bank organized the supply. Each translated a technical or financial advantage into control over a market, and each raised the question of whether the advantage was earned, subsidized, or imposed.[^ch24-gilder-railroads][^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

### 2.1 Steel and the Bessemer process

The Bessemer process, patented in the 1850s, made steel cheap enough for rails and structures. Andrew Carnegie built the Edgar Thomson Works at Braddock, Pennsylvania, in 1875; it was his first steel mill and the first large Bessemer steel plant in the area. Carnegie had learned management in the Pennsylvania Railroad, and he applied its lessons to steel.[^ch24-steel-museum-edgar-thomson][^ch24-gilder-railroads]

Carnegie’s advantage was vertical integration. His plants stood at the end of a chain of operations he owned outright or controlled through partnerships: iron ore mines, ships that carried the ore across the Great Lakes, railway lines, coal lands, coke ovens, and plants that turned iron ore into iron and steel. Control over the sources and transportation of raw materials guaranteed a reliable flow of supplies at predictable prices and might deny them to competitors. His motto was “Cut the prices; scoop the market; run the mills full.”[^ch24-gilder-railroads]

The result was a formidable industrial machine. It was also a labor regime. Carnegie’s cost cutting depended on managers who drove crews and on workers who had little power to resist. The technological achievement and the labor discipline were not separate stories. Homestead, in §4.3, is where the same company used the same plant to break the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers.[^ch24-gilder-railroads][^ch24-loc-homestead]

### 2.2 Rockefeller and Standard Oil

John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil on refining, cost control, and railroad rebates. He formed the Standard Oil Company on January 10, 1870. In the first three months of 1872 he bought out, shut down, or bankrupted 22 of his 26 Cleveland competitors. Standard Oil’s size let it negotiate railroad rebates in exchange for guaranteed volume, and smaller refiners could not match the discount. By 1881 Rockefeller and his partners controlled some forty refineries and about 90 percent of the nation’s refining capacity. The word “monopoly” was already contested: Standard Oil described its position as the result of efficiency, while its critics used the word to describe a single seller’s control of a market.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-gilder-railroads]

The trust was the legal form that made the combination durable. On January 2, 1882, Standard Oil’s attorney Samuel Dodd devised the trust: stockholders of the component companies transferred their shares to a board of nine trustees and received trust certificates in return. The trustees ran all the companies as one, elected their directors and officers, and divided the profits. The form spread to other industries.[^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-loc-standard-oil]

Standard Oil’s control of the market helped prompt the antitrust laws of the 1880s and 1890s. It also made the company the era’s most visible example of a private government: a board of trustees, accountable to certificates rather than voters, deciding how an essential commodity would be refined, transported, and sold.[^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-loc-standard-oil]

### 2.3 Finance and the investment banker

As the trusts grew, the investment banker became the organizer and disciplinarian of American industry. By the late 1880s John Pierpont Morgan had emerged as the nation’s leading investment banker. Railroad executives came to him for capital, and he often insisted on reorganization to simplify corporate structures and combine separate lines into larger, centrally controlled systems. He also insisted that a representative of the House of Morgan join the company’s board, so that the company would follow policies that protected his investors’ returns. Some called the process “Morganization.”[^ch24-gilder-railroads]

Morgan’s power rested on access to capital and on the dependence of companies on his judgment. It was a form of private government over corporations: the banker decided which lines would survive, which would be merged, and who would sit on the board. By the early 1900s, such reorganization had created twelve large railroad systems that controlled more than half of the country’s railroad mileage, with twenty others operating most of the rest.[^ch24-gilder-railroads]

The narrative stops at 1900, so the great merger into United States Steel in 1901, and the Northern Securities episode that followed, belong as clearly labeled consequence rather than as the main story. They show where the logic led: the banker who reorganized railroads would soon organize steel.[^ch24-gilder-railroads][^ch24-check-dates]

### 2.4 The trust and the Sherman Act

The trust was a legal device for combining competitors, and the Sherman Anti-Trust Act of 1890 was Congress’s first attempt to forbid it. Section 1 declared illegal “every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States.” Section 2 forbade monopolization. The Senate passed the act 51–1 on April 8, 1890; the House passed it 242–0 on June 20; President Benjamin Harrison signed it on July 2.[^ch24-archives-sherman]

The statute was broad and loosely worded. It did not define “trust,” “combination,” “conspiracy,” or “monopoly,” and it left enforcement to the courts. In *United States v. E. C. Knight Company* (1895), the Supreme Court held that a company controlling about 98 percent of sugar refining had not violated the law, because control of manufacture was not control of trade. The ruling seemed to end federal trust regulation until the twentieth century.[^ch24-archives-sherman]

The episode is the clearest example of the gap between law and power. Congress could declare a principle; the courts decided what it meant; the corporations reorganized faster than either could respond. The antitrust impulse was real, but it did not break the trusts before 1900.[^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-check-dates]

## 3. Immigration and cities

The industrial order needed labor, and the labor came from the countryside and from abroad. Between 1870 and 1900 nearly 12 million immigrants arrived in the United States. They filled the mills, the packinghouses, the garment shops, and the construction crews, and they made the industrial city the characteristic American place.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

### 3.1 Old and new immigration

In the 1870s and 1880s the great majority of European immigrants came from Germany, Ireland, and England, the principal sources before the Civil War. By 1900 the migration had shifted to the south and east, and most newcomers came from Italy, the Czarist empire, Romania, and other parts of southern and eastern Europe. The phrase “new immigration” became a contested label, used by nativists to distinguish the newcomers from the older stock and by scholars to describe a real shift in origins. The categories were partly invented, and both streams continued.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-gilder-immigration]

More than 70 percent of immigrants entered through New York City, most of them Europeans. Most arrived at the Castle Garden depot at the tip of Manhattan until the federal government opened a new processing center on Ellis Island on January 1, 1892. Ellis Island operated until 1954 and processed more than 12 million steamship passengers. Mexican immigrants also began arriving in the late nineteenth century, primarily to work on the railroads, and they created enclaves as far north as Chicago. Smaller numbers came from Syria and Lebanon, the Punjab, Japan, the Philippines, and the Caribbean; they entered particular trades and regions, and they complicate any single story of “the immigrant.” The gateways shaped the cities: New York, Chicago, Boston, and Philadelphia became immigrant cities, and the immigrant neighborhood became the first American home for millions.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-nps-ellis][^ch24-gilder-immigration][^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

### 3.2 Chinese exclusion as labor and immigration policy

Chinese immigrants had worked in the gold mines, in agriculture, and in the garment industry, and they were “particularly instrumental in building railroads in the American west.” As their numbers grew, anti-Chinese sentiment among other workers grew with them. The hostility was economic and cultural as well as racial: the Chinese were accused of lowering wages, of refusing to assimilate, and of belonging to a different civilization.[^ch24-state-chinese-immigration][^ch24-loc-immigration]

Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act, officially “An act to execute certain treaty stipulations relating to Chinese,” and President Chester A. Arthur signed it on May 6, 1882. It suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years. Its preamble declared that “the coming of Chinese laborers to this country endangers the good order of certain localities within the territory thereof.” The act defined “Chinese laborers” to include skilled and unskilled workers and Chinese employed in mining.[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion]

Congress extended exclusion for another ten years in the Geary Act of 1892 and made it permanent in 1902. The ban was not repealed until 1943, when the United States and China were allies in the Second World War. Chinese exclusion belongs to this chapter as labor and immigration policy: it was a national decision about which workers could enter the labor market, made in response to the conflicts that industrialization produced. The later Angel Island immigrant station, which opened in 1910, belongs to Chapter 29; it is not part of this pre-1900 narrative.[^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-state-chinese-immigration][^ch24-check-ch29]

### 3.3 The tenement and the industrial city

Industrial cities grew faster than their housing and sanitation. The Census counted 29.0 percent of Americans in places of 8,000 or more in 1890, and 32.9 percent in such places in 1900. On the 4,000-person threshold the bureau also reported for 1900, the figure was 37.3 percent. Later compilations that apply a 2,500-person threshold backward put the 1890 share at 35.1 percent. The numbers differ because the definition moved, not because the country changed twice.[^ch24-census-1900-urban][^ch24-census-urbanization]

Jacob Riis, a Danish immigrant and police reporter, published *How the Other Half Lives* in 1890. He wrote that three-fourths of New York’s people lived in tenements, and that “the fifteen thousand tenant houses that were the despair of the sanitarian in the past generation have swelled into thirty-seven thousand, and more than twelve hundred thousand persons call them home.” The tenement was the characteristic form of working-class urban life: a building that packed families into small rooms, shared water and privies, and turned the street into an extension of the home.[^ch24-yawp-riis]

Riis’s account combines documented figures with reform rhetoric and photographs. It is evidence of a reformer’s argument as well as of the conditions he described. Read that way, the numbers are real, the framing is purposeful, and the photographs were part of the argument. The tenement was not a neutral fact; it was a problem that reformers, landlords, and tenants defined differently.[^ch24-yawp-riis][^ch24-check-race]

### 3.4 The urban political economy

The industrial city was also a political order. The same decades that produced the trust produced the ward boss, the ethnic parish, the settlement house, and the mass-circulation press. In New York, Tammany Hall’s Democratic machine provided jobs, legal help, and personal favors to immigrant constituents while its public works contracts funded graft. George Washington Plunkitt, a Tammany ward heeler, explained the difference between “honest graft” and “dishonest graft” and described a day spent bailing out a saloonkeeper, finding jobs for the unemployed, attending an Italian funeral, and dropping in on a Jewish wedding.[^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

The machine was corrupt and useful at the same time. It gave immigrants a path to the state when the state was otherwise distant, and it made the party, rather than the city government, the agency of incorporation. Beside it stood institutions that immigrants built themselves: churches, synagogues, mutual-aid societies, unions, and newspapers. Those institutions carried the old country into the new city and gave the newcomers a public voice that the ward boss could not monopolize.[^ch24-yawp-industrial-america][^ch24-loc-immigration]

The city was also a racial and religious hierarchy. Chinese immigrants were excluded from the nation, and many settled in their own neighborhoods, where Chinatowns became the target of rumor and regulation. Black migrants from the rural South arrived in the same decades, and they faced a racial order that segregation and disfranchisement were hardening; that history belongs to Chapter 26, but it was part of the industrial city’s political economy. The era’s immigration law drew racial lines, and those lines shaped who could enter, who could become a citizen, and who could live where.[^ch24-check-race][^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

## 4. Labor from 1877 through Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman

The new order was built against resistance. The great strikes of 1877, 1886, 1892, and 1894 were national events, and each ended with the state or the courts on the employers’ side. The defeats did not prove that workers were powerless. They proved that the republic’s coercive power was available to the corporations when the terms of work were at stake.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-loc-homestead][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

### 4.1 The Great Railroad Strike of 1877

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 began among railroad workers over wage cuts. In mid-July 1877, after another reduction, workers on the Baltimore & Ohio walked out at Martinsburg, West Virginia, and stopped freight trains from passing. The strike spread along the line into Maryland, Pennsylvania, New York, and beyond. Governors called up militia, and President Rutherford B. Hayes issued proclamations for West Virginia on July 18, Maryland on July 21, and Pennsylvania on July 23.[^ch24-loc-1877]

In Pittsburgh the militia fired into the crowd, killing twenty strikers, and strikers set fire to the city, destroying dozens of buildings, more than a hundred engines, and more than a thousand cars. The official death toll in Pittsburgh was 26, but it was likely higher. At Reading, Pennsylvania, at least ten were killed. By September the state governments and federal troops had suppressed the strike. It had begun as a wage protest and became a general uprising of industrial workers, the first multi-state strike in the United States.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

The strike was suppressed, and it left no national organization capable of consolidating a victory. It showed both employers and the state that industrial conflict would be met with force. It also showed workers that isolated craft organizations could not withstand a national coalition of railroads and governments. The lesson pointed toward the national unions of the 1880s.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

### 4.2 Haymarket and the eight-hour movement

The Haymarket affair of 1886 grew out of the eight-hour-day movement. On May 1, 1886, Chicago unionists, socialists, anarchists, and ordinary workers made the city the center of the national movement for an eight-hour day. Some 35,000 workers walked off their jobs, and tens of thousands more joined them on May 3 and 4. Police clashed with strikers at least a dozen times. At the McCormick reaper plant on May 3, police fired on strikers and killed at least two.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket]

Anarchists called a protest meeting at the West Randolph Street Haymarket for the evening of May 4. The crowd on Des Plaines Street was peaceful, and Mayor Carter H. Harrison, who attended, instructed police not to disturb it. When a speaker urged the crowd to “throttle” the law, 176 officers under Inspector John Bonfield marched to the meeting and ordered it to disperse. Someone threw a bomb at the police, killing one officer instantly. Police fired into the crowd; sixty officers were injured, and eight eventually died. An undetermined number of the crowd were killed or wounded.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket]

Eight anarchists were tried and convicted. Four were hanged on November 11, 1887. One killed himself in his cell, two had their sentences commuted, and in 1893 Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors after criticizing the trial. The Knights of Labor, associated with Haymarket in the public mind, declined sharply after 1886. The bombing gave employers and newspapers a vocabulary of radicalism that they used against the entire labor movement.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket-trial][^ch24-loc-organized-labor]

### 4.3 The Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor

The two leading organizations of the 1880s represented different strategies. The Knights of Labor, founded in 1869 and led by Terence V. Powderly, tried to organize all workers, skilled and unskilled. It welcomed women as well as men and, at least in principle, crossed the lines of race and ethnicity. By 1886 it claimed more than 700,000 members. Its vision was a cooperative, producer-centered society in which labor, not capital, received the reward.[^ch24-georgia-knights][^ch24-yawp-industrial-america]

The American Federation of Labor organized skilled craft unions and bargained for concrete gains. In 1886 the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions became the AFL, led by Samuel Gompers and other craft-union officials. It concentrated on wages, hours, and working conditions for skilled workers and avoided the Knights’ broad program. It grew from about 140,000 members in 1886 to nearly 3 million in 1924.[^ch24-loc-organized-labor][^ch24-aflcio-gompers]

The AFL’s craft strategy gave it leverage with employers, but it also set boundaries. Under Gompers the federation reversed its founding pledge and disallowed Black members, and it set itself against Chinese workers. Its affiliated unions organized skilled trades that were overwhelmingly White and male. The AFL’s narrower base made the house of labor smaller than the working class.[^ch24-aflcio-gompers][^ch24-check-race]

The limits of the craft strategy appeared at Homestead, Pennsylvania, in 1892. The Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers had won a contract at Carnegie Steel in 1889. When it expired in 1892, Henry Clay Frick, the company’s president, and Andrew Carnegie set out to break the union. Frick erected fences around the plant — workers called it “Fort Frick” — and hired 300 Pinkerton agents. On July 6, the Pinkertons tried to enter by river barge; workers and their families met them at the bank, and a gun battle followed. Seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed. On July 12, 8,500 National Guard troops arrived at Frick’s request and cleared the town. The Amalgamated collapsed in November.[^ch24-aflcio-homestead][^ch24-loc-homestead]

The state had not merely kept order; it had decided which side would be allowed to organize. The company had won, and the state had made the win enforceable. The same plant that had made Carnegie’s steel cheap now made his labor policy an example for other employers.[^ch24-aflcio-homestead][^ch24-loc-homestead]

### 4.4 In Focus: a Pullman lease

**In Focus.** In 1885 the economist Richard T. Ely described the town of Pullman, Illinois, and the lease on which every tenant held a house. “Every tenant holds his house on a lease which may be terminated on ten days’ notice,” Ely wrote. The form left the dates blank and ran “unless sooner cancelled,” and it added that “it is not necessary that any reason be assigned for the notice.” Rent paid in advance, it warned, “shall not be a waiver of the right to put an end to the term and tenancy under this lease by such notice.”[^ch24-ely-pullman]

Ely recorded rents of $14 to $25 a month, exclusive of water, and $17 for a five-room cottage. The company retained everything: “No private individual owns to-day a square rod of ground or a single structure in the entire town. No organization, not even a church, can occupy any other than rented quarters.” The town was meant to yield six percent on the investment.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-nps-pullman-story]

The lease shows how far the property right reached. It governed the home, the water, the gas, and the church, not only the workplace. A worker who lost his job could lose his house on ten days’ notice, and rent paid in advance was no defense. The company did not need to own the worker; it owned the ground under his feet.[^ch24-ely-pullman]

Ely’s 1885 article transcribes the form but does not reproduce a signed, dated lease. The box therefore reads the recorded form, not a particular family’s contract. An original executed lease, if found, would show the form as it was actually signed.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-check-focus]

### 4.5 Pullman, 1894, and the injunction

After the depression of 1893, Pullman cut wages by about a quarter while rents held steady. The workers’ statement to the American Railway Union convention in June 1894 described five reductions in wages between May and December 1893, the last nearly 30 percent, and added, “our rents had not fallen.” It compared $15 a month in Pullman with $8 in Roseland and observed that “no man or woman of us all has ever owned or can ever hope to own one inch of George M. Pullman’s land.”[^ch24-pullman-workers-statement][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

The workers walked off the job on May 11, 1894. In June the ARU, led by Eugene V. Debs, called a boycott of Pullman cars, and by late June at least 125,000 ARU members had walked out in support. The boycott halted freight and mail traffic across much of the country. When Governor John P. Altgeld refused to request federal troops, Attorney General Richard Olney sought the first federal injunction against a strike, and President Grover Cleveland sent about 2,000 troops to Illinois to enforce it. Violence followed, and Debs and other leaders were arrested.[^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman][^ch24-fjc-debs]

In 1895 the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the injunction in *In re Debs*. The Court held that the federal government could act to protect interstate commerce and the mails. Justice David Brewer wrote that the United States “may remove everything put upon highways, natural or artificial, to obstruct the passage of interstate commerce, or the carrying of the mails.” Debs served six months for violating the injunction, and the ARU was broken.[^ch24-inre-debs][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

The injunction became one of the courts’ most powerful instruments of industrial discipline. It allowed a judge to order a strike to stop and to punish its leaders for contempt. The railroad brotherhoods might bargain; the industrial union that challenged the railroads would face the federal courts. The lesson was not lost on the next generation of labor organizers.[^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman][^ch24-inre-debs]

## 5. Inequality and its defenders and critics

The second industrial revolution produced both unprecedented wealth and visible poverty. Americans argued about whether the inequality was the price of progress, the result of unjust law, or a problem to be fixed by organization. The argument was not a sideshow to the industrial story. It was the way Americans decided what the new order meant.[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-yawp-carnegie]

### 5.1 The distribution question

Defenders treated competition and concentration as natural and beneficial. Critics treated land monopoly and the wage system as the causes of poverty. Organizers treated collective action as the remedy. The debate was not simply “robber barons versus reformers”: the defenders included men who believed they were philanthropists, and the critics included men who wanted a different distribution of property, not the abolition of property.[^ch24-yawp-carnegie][^ch24-yawp-george]

The era had no modern national income accounts, and its statistics of income and wealth were fragmentary. Contemporaries argued from wage data, census returns, tax records, and observation. Later estimates of inequality in the period are contested, and this account reports them as estimates with their methods rather than as settled facts.[^ch24-check-numbers]

### 5.2 The defenders

William Graham Sumner, a Yale sociologist, gave the defenders a philosophy. In “The Absurd Effort to Make the World Over” (1894), he argued that “the burden of proof is on those who affirm that our social condition is utterly diseased and in need of radical regeneration.” The existing order, he wrote, was the product of natural forces, and “the possession of the requisite ability is a natural monopoly.” Reform was an “absurd effort” because it mistook the results of nature for the failures of law.[^ch24-nlnrac-sumner]

Andrew Carnegie gave the defenders a gospel. In “Wealth” (1889), he wrote that the concentration of business “in the hands of a few” was “not only beneficial, but essential for the future progress of the race.” The rich man was “but a trustee for the poor,” entrusted for a season with a part of the community’s increased wealth and administering it “for the community far better than it did, or would have done, of itself.” The duty of wealth was to use its surplus for the general good, not to dissolve the inequality that made the surplus possible.[^ch24-yawp-carnegie]

The defenders’ strongest case was that competition disciplined costs, rewarded ability, and produced abundance. Their weakness was that they treated the existing distribution as the result of ability alone. They did not weigh the land grants, the rebates, the injunctions, or the private armies that had helped to make the fortunes they defended. Nor did they ask what a worker could do when the company owned the town, the water, and the lease.[^ch24-yawp-carnegie][^ch24-nlnrac-sumner]

### 5.3 The critics

Henry George gave the critics a system. In *Progress and Poverty* (1879), he called “the association of poverty with progress” the “great enigma of our times,” blamed it on the monopoly of land, and proposed a single tax on land values. Political liberty, he wrote, when the equal right to land is denied, becomes “merely the liberty to compete for employment at starvation wages.” In 1886 he ran for mayor of New York City as the candidate of the United Labor Party, a sign of how far the critique had entered politics.[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-pbs-george]

Edward Bellamy gave the critics a picture of the future. His novel *Looking Backward, 2000–1887* (1888) imagined a cooperative society in which production was organized for use rather than profit. It became one of the best-selling novels of the era; by one library exhibit’s account, only *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* outsold it in its first few years. The novel gave readers a systematic picture of an alternative to the wage system. Bellamy’s future was orderly, humane, and administered; its appeal lay in the promise that industrial organization could be turned from private gain to public purpose.[^ch24-uci-bellamy]

The critics’ strength was that they treated poverty as a structural problem, not a personal failure. Their weakness was that their remedies were easier to imagine than to enact. The single tax won readers and a New York mayoral campaign, but not a national majority, and Bellamy’s cooperative commonwealth required a transformation of property that no party could deliver. Both critics also wrote as if the working class were unified, when the era’s labor movement was divided by skill, race, and gender.[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-uci-bellamy][^ch24-check-race]

### 5.4 The measure

The measure gives the inequality debate a quantitative footing without overclaiming. Where this account uses a figure, it names the source series and the observation year; any inflation adjustment must also name the price index and base year. The series behind Figure 4 and Appendix B, Table 7 are the anchors; the text does not combine incompatible estimates or present a contested range as a settled count.[^ch24-check-figure][^ch24-check-numbers]

The most important measure is also the most modest. The United States became richer and more productive between 1865 and 1900, and the gains were distributed unevenly. The exact distribution is debated, and the debate is part of the history. A single number cannot settle whether the era’s inequality was the price of progress, the result of law, or both.[^ch24-check-numbers]

## 6. The Debate

The argument over how corporate power was built began while it was being built. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand][^ch24-white-republic]

### 6.1 What is settled

The scale and concentration of industry are documented. Railroad mileage, steel output, and the urban share of the population all rose sharply between 1865 and 1900, and firms such as Standard Oil and Carnegie Steel dominated their industries.[^ch24-gilder-railroads][^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-loc-homestead]

Public subsidy and public standardization were real. The transcontinental railroads received federal land grants and bonds, and the railroads set a standard of time that the federal government adopted only in 1918.[^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-two-noons]

Labor conflict was pervasive. The great strikes of 1877, 1886, 1892, and 1894 were national events, and the state and the courts intervened on the side of employers.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-loc-homestead][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

The law lagged the corporations. The Interstate Commerce Act and the Sherman Act were real federal efforts, but both were limited in wording and narrowed by the courts before 1900.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-archives-sherman]

Immigration restriction was racialized. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 singled out one nationality for exclusion, and its extension in 1892 and permanence in 1902 fixed the principle for decades.[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion]

### 6.2 What is open

Why did corporate consolidation happen? Historians disagree about whether it was mainly an efficiency gained through management, a product of public subsidy and the law, or an outcome of the struggle between capital and labor.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand][^ch24-white-republic]

How much did the state build the market? The weight of land grants, tariffs, courts, and coercion in creating the corporate order remains debated.[^ch24-white-republic][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]

How should the labor upheavals be explained? Whether the great strikes were defensive reactions to wage cuts, expressions of a working-class republicanism, or the birth pangs of modern unionism is contested.[^ch24-check-sources][^ch24-loc-organized-labor]

How much did “new immigration” change the country, and how new was it? The categories were partly nativist inventions, and their analytic value is debated.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-check-names]

When should the era be said to end? The 1900 endpoint is a planning choice. The merger movement, U.S. Steel, and the great antitrust actions fall at or just after it, and the narrative labels them as consequence rather than as the main story.[^ch24-check-dates][^ch24-check-scope]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

Alfred D. Chandler Jr. explained the rise of the modern corporation as a problem of coordination. Large firms and salaried managers lowered costs and coordinated production and distribution more efficiently than markets could, and the visible hand of management replaced the invisible hand of market forces in many sectors. The explanation is powerful because it accounts for the internal logic of the corporation across railroads, steel, and distribution. Its risk is to treat efficiency as the whole story and to understate the subsidies, the coercion, and the labor conflicts that made the corporation’s advantages possible.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

Richard White’s synthesis puts the state at the center. In his account, the Gilded Age was the seedbed of modern America, and corporate power was built with the state: land grants, tariffs, courts, and coercion organized the economy and its exclusions. The emphasis restores law and public subsidy to the story and connects industrial change to race, immigration, and the West. Its risk is the opposite of Chandler’s: a broad political economy can make the market’s own logic disappear.[^ch24-white-republic]

A third position, represented in Table 1 by the labor historians, asks how workers built their own organizations and what their defeats foreclosed. A fourth, represented by the legal historians, treats the corporation as a creation of law and antitrust as a reordering of property rights rather than an attack on business. The four positions overlap more than they compete. Corporate power was acquired, not simply earned: through management, through law and subsidy, and through the suppression of labor’s claims. The evidence supports that synthesis, and the table is a map of the arguments rather than a set of teams.[^ch24-check-table][^ch24-white-republic][^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]

## Legacies

The second industrial revolution gave the United States the productive capacity and the corporate institutions that would define the twentieth century. It also concentrated power over time, work, and place in private hands. The railroads set the nation’s clocks before Congress did; the trusts organized whole industries before the antitrust laws could reach them; and the courts made the injunction an instrument that ended the great strikes.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

The era’s exclusions were as durable as its achievements. Chinese exclusion fixed a racial principle in immigration law that lasted until 1943. The AFL’s craft unions organized a narrow slice of the working class and, under Gompers, disallowed Black members. The tenement and the company town organized the lives of millions who had come for the promise of the “Golden Door.”[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-aflcio-gompers][^ch24-yawp-riis]

The era’s lasting legacy is a set of questions that passed to the twentieth century: whether a republic could govern corporations it had chartered and subsidized, whether a wage was enough to make a worker free, and whether the standardization of time and the concentration of capital had made a new kind of citizenship. Those questions passed to the West that Chapter 25 describes, and to the reform movements that Chapter 29 takes up.[^ch24-check-ch25][^ch24-check-ch29][^ch24-gilder-immigration]

## Summary

- On November 18, 1883, the railroads standardized the nation’s time on their own authority, a change Congress did not make law until 1918, and the day was called “the Day of Two Noons.”[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- The transcontinental railroads were built with federal land grants and bonds, and the Crédit Mobilier scandal of 1872 diverted construction profits to private and political hands; the House censured Oakes Ames and James Brooks in 1873.[^ch24-house-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- Rockefeller formed Standard Oil in 1870, controlled about 90 percent of American refining by 1881, and organized the Standard Oil Trust in 1882; the Sherman Anti-Trust Act of 1890 was narrowed by *E. C. Knight* in 1895.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-gilder-railroads]
- The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 created the first federal independent regulatory commission after *Wabash v. Illinois* (1886) struck down state rate regulation.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]
- Nearly 12 million immigrants arrived between 1870 and 1900, most through New York; Ellis Island opened on January 1, 1892; and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 suspended Chinese labor immigration for ten years, extended in 1892 and made permanent in 1902.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-nps-ellis][^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion]
- The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 began at Martinsburg and was suppressed by militia and federal troops; Haymarket (1886) produced a bombing, a mass trial, and the hangings of four anarchists in 1887.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket-trial]
- The Knights of Labor reached more than 700,000 members by 1886 and declined after Haymarket, while the American Federation of Labor, founded the same year under Samuel Gompers, organized skilled craft workers and disallowed Black members.[^ch24-georgia-knights][^ch24-loc-organized-labor][^ch24-aflcio-gompers]
- At Homestead in 1892, seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed and 8,500 National Guard troops occupied the town; the union was broken.[^ch24-aflcio-homestead][^ch24-loc-homestead]
- The Pullman lease tied housing to company control, the strike of 1894 joined a company town to a nationwide boycott, and *In re Debs* (1895) upheld the federal injunction that broke the strike.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman][^ch24-inre-debs]
- Henry George called the association of poverty with progress the “great enigma of our times” and proposed a single tax on land, while Andrew Carnegie defended the concentration of business as “essential for the future progress of the race.”[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-yawp-carnegie]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The second industrial revolution made the United States one of the world’s leading industrial powers and made the corporation the organizing institution of American life. It standardized time, built the trust, filled the cities, and broke the great strikes. It left a republic with more productive capacity than it had institutions to govern it. The questions it raised — about the boundary between private and public power, the meaning of a wage, and who belonged to the industrial nation — were not settled in 1900.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-loc-organized-labor][^ch24-gilder-immigration]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in the Black Hills in 1874, with the expedition that reported gold and with the claims of the Lakota and other nations whose land the industrial order was already reaching. The chapter takes up conquest, extraction, and the end of the frontier that the same industrial order was consuming. Chapter 25 takes up the West.[^ch24-check-ch25][^ch24-check-bridge]
