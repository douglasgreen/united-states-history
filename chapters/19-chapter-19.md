## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The man and the place.** William Henry, known as Jerry, was an escaped enslaved man and cooper who had lived in Syracuse, New York, since 1843. He had escaped from Missouri; the warrant alleged that he owed “service and labor” to a party there.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **The arrest.** On October 1, 1851, Deputy U.S. Marshal Henry W. Allen executed a warrant issued by U.S. Commissioner Joseph Sabine and seized Jerry, initially on the pretext of theft. The warrant dated to September 29 or 30, 1851. Jerry “put up substantial resistance” once told the Fugitive Slave Law was the ground of his arrest, and was subdued and taken for a hearing.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **The first attempt.** Abolitionists tried to free him in the commissioner’s office; he broke away and reached one of the bridges over the Erie Canal before being recaptured and taken to the police justice’s offices. The Syracuse University Libraries account distinguishes these stages: the office attempt, the recapture at the canal bridge, and the storming of the police offices.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry]
- **The crowd.** A crowd variously reported as about 2,500 people — many in the city for an antislavery convention — surrounded the building. On a signal, the crowd broke in, freed Jerry, and helped him escape. A few pistol shots were fired, but the account states the crowd’s force, not gunfire, overcame the federal officers.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **The organizers and the aftermath.** The ministers Jermain Wesley Loguen, a formerly enslaved man from Tennessee, and Samuel J. May helped organize the rescue. Jerry was hidden in the city and then taken to Kingston, Ontario, where he lived until his death a few years later. In June 1852 Deputy Marshal Allen was tried in Onondaga County under an 1840 New York law for kidnapping; the prosecution used the case to challenge the constitutionality of the Fugitive Slave Act. Samuel J. May’s speech to an Onondaga County citizens’ convention two weeks after the rescue records the crowd’s outrage, and Gerrit Smith and Frederick Douglass later made the “Jerry level” a byword of the antislavery movement.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **The provocation.** May’s account records that Daniel Webster had told a Syracuse audience the previous June that the Fugitive Slave Law “should be enforced here,” even in the midst of the next antislavery convention. The rescue was, in part, an answer to that threat.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry]

### The scene’s argument

- **A rescue, not a riot.** The scene should open on the arrest at Jerry’s workplace and narrow to the storming of the police offices. The point is not that a mob won but that ordinary people, backed by a Black-led and interracial antislavery network, refused to let a federal law be enforced in their city.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **The law and the claim.** The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 pressed free-state citizens into the machinery of enslavement and denied the accused a hearing; Syracuse answered with a rescue. The chapter should let the collision stand: a republic of consent enforcing a law many of its citizens regarded as kidnapping.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: why did compromise fail to contain slavery’s expansion?
- **The turn.** The rescue did not settle the constitutional question; it made the enforcement of the 1850 settlement a recurring test of federal authority in the free states, and it showed that resistance could succeed.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims]
- **The widening.** The scene stands in Syracuse in October 1851. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader back to the Compromise of 1850 and outward to Boston, Kansas, Illinois, and Harpers Ferry.[^ch19-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The date, the warrant, the arrest, the attempted rescue, the recapture, the storming, and the escape are documented. The day’s weather, what Jerry or Loguen said in the moment, and what the marshals intended are not attested in the cited sources; do not stage them.[^ch19-check-scene][^ch19-syracuse-jerry]
- **No exact crowd figure stated as fact.** The crowd is commonly given as about 2,500; present the figure with its source rather than as a precise count. The number who entered the building and the number who gathered outside are not separately documented.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **No harmonizing of the accounts.** The Syracuse University Libraries and Onondaga Historical Association accounts differ in detail — for example, on the number of marshals and the sequence of the first rescue attempt. Report the discrepancies or follow the fuller institutional account.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-cnyhistory-jerry]
- **No modern phrasing in their mouths.** Quotations come from the consulted texts character by character; do not modernize or paraphrase inside quotation marks.[^ch19-check-epigraphs]

## 1. The 1850 settlement

### 1.1 Clay, Douglas, and the five statutes

- **The claim.** The Compromise of 1850 was not a single bargain but five statutes passed in September 1850, and its most explosive element — a stronger fugitive-slave law — was the price of union.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- **The crisis.** By 1850 sectional conflict over slavery strained the union. California’s request to enter as a “free state” threatened the balance between “slave” and “free” states established in 1820. Senator Henry Clay of Kentucky proposed a series of resolutions, introduced January 29, 1850, to “settle and adjust amicably all existing questions of controversy … arising out of the institution of slavery.”[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- **The statutes.** The five acts admitted California as a free state; established territorial governments for New Mexico and Utah with the slavery question left to the inhabitants; fixed the Texas boundary and paid Texas $10 million; abolished the slave trade — not slavery — in the District of Columbia; and amended the Fugitive Slave Act.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- **The passage.** The Senate debated Clay’s solution for seven months, first rejected his package, and then passed Senator Stephen A. Douglas’s substitute bills, which cleared both houses. Clay’s resolutions themselves were not adopted; the enacted statutes are what became the Compromise of 1850.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- **The consequence.** The compromise postponed the sectional reckoning and made the fugitive question national and explosive. Its enforcement is the subject of §§1.3 and 2.[^ch19-check-ch18][^ch19-archives-compromise1850]

### 1.2 In Focus: one clause of the Fugitive Slave Act

- **The claim.** The Fugitive Slave Act’s fee clause made the return of a fugitive profitable and the finding of freedom costly, and it must be read beside the clause denying the accused a voice in the hearing.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]
- **In Focus: the fee clause.** **In Focus.** The box reads a single text: Section 8 of “An Act to amend, and supplementary to, the Act … respecting Fugitives from Justice, and Persons escaping from the Service of their Masters” (approved September 18, 1850). It provided that the commissioner “he shall be entitled to a fee of ten dollars in full for his services in each case, upon the delivery of the said certificate to the claimant, his agent or attorney; or a fee of five dollars in cases where the proof shall not, in the opinion of such commissioner, warrant such certificate and delivery.” The same act provided that “in no trial or hearing under this act shall the testimony of such alleged fugitive be admitted in evidence.”[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]
- **How to read it.** The clause is an instrument for reading federal power at its most mechanical. A commissioner earned twice as much for returning an accused person as for freeing one, and the person whose liberty was at stake could not testify. The box should let the arithmetic and the silence speak.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]
- **The related sections.** Section 5 required “all good citizens … to aid and assist in the prompt and efficient execution of this law” and set a fine of up to $1,000 and imprisonment up to six months for obstructing it or aiding escape, plus $1,000 in civil damages for each person lost. Section 10 allowed a court record from the state of alleged escape to stand as “full and conclusive evidence” of the claim. The box should note these as context, not as additional objects.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- **The text and its limits.** The quotations are transcribed from the Avalon Project text of the *Statutes at Large* and must be collated against the enrolled statute. The act’s language of “service or labor” is the era’s euphemism; the box should name what it conceals.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-check-focus]

### 1.3 Enforcement, resistance, and the price of union

- **The claim.** The act’s requirement that free-state officials and citizens enforce it turned every northern community into a possible site of the fugitive crisis.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-ev-burns]
- **The machinery.** The act expanded the number of federal commissioners empowered to issue certificates of removal, charged them to hear claims “in a summary manner,” and required both federal and local law enforcement in all states to arrest suspected fugitives.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-ev-burns]
- **The resistance.** Vigilance committees formed to defeat the law; in Boston they helped the Crafts evade recapture in 1850, and an armed crowd rescued Shadrach Minkins from the courthouse the same year. The pattern of rescue and rendition is the subject of §2.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims]
- **The consequence.** The act made the 1850 settlement a continuing test of federal authority in the free states. It did not produce northern submission; it produced the rescues, the antislavery literature, and the political realignment that followed.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-ev-burns]

## 2. Fugitive cases and abolitionism

### 2.1 Table 1: selected fugitive cases, 1851–1854

- **The claim.** A small number of well-documented cases turned the Fugitive Slave Act from a statute into a national drama of enforcement and resistance.[^ch19-check-tables]
- **The cases.** The table should include the Boston cases (Shadrach Minkins, February 1851, rescued; Thomas Sims, April 1851, remanded), the Christiana Resistance (September 1851, the enslaver Edward Gorsuch killed, the defendants acquitted of treason), the Jerry Rescue (Syracuse, October 1851, rescued), the Joshua Glover case (Racine and Milwaukee, March 1854, rescued), and the Anthony Burns case (Boston, May–June 1854, remanded).[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-christiana-society][^ch19-glover]
- **The pattern.** Some cases ended in rescue through organized force or crowd action; others ended in rendition under heavy guard. The distinction usually tracked the strength of the local vigilance network and the timing of federal preparation, not the merits of the claim.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims]
- **The consequence.** The cases made the law’s costs visible: a federal marshal killed at Christiana, a Boston courthouse draped in chains, a man marched to a ship through the streets of a free city. They are the chapter’s clearest evidence that the settlement of 1850 was unenforceable without coercion.[^ch19-christiana-society][^ch19-nps-sims]
- **The source limits.** The case records must be built from direct court and archive sources, not from summaries alone. ### 2.2 Vigilance committees and the politics of rescue

- **The claim.** The rescues were organized work, and Black-led vigilance committees were often their core.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims]
- **Boston.** The Boston Vigilance Committee, formed to resist the Fugitive Slave Act, helped the Crafts and tried to free Anthony Burns; its failed courthouse assault in May 1854 killed a deputy marshal. Boston authorities then guarded Burns with hundreds of armed men.[^ch19-ev-burns]
- **Syracuse and Philadelphia.** The Syracuse rescue rested on an abolitionist network that included Loguen, May, Smith, and Douglass. The Christiana Resistance in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, was a Black community’s armed defense of four self-emancipated men, and it produced the largest treason trial in the nation’s history; the first defendant, Castner Hanway, was acquitted in December 1851.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-christiana-society]
- **The consequence.** Rescue politics made the fugitive question a test of local and federal authority and pushed many northerners toward the Republican Party. The chapter should show the committees as institutions, not spontaneous crowds.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-check-race]

### 2.3 *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*

- **The claim.** *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* turned the fugitive question into mass literature and made slavery’s domestic cruelty a national subject.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]
- **The publication.** Harriet Beecher Stowe, who had lived in Cincinnati across the river from Kentucky, began the story as a serial in the *National Era*, where it ran from June 5, 1851, to April 1, 1852. The two-volume book was published on March 20, 1852; more than 10,000 copies sold in the first week, and about 300,000 in the United States by the end of the year. More than a million and a half copies sold in England in the following year.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]
- **The response.** Stowe answered southern accusations that she had invented her material with *A Key to Uncle Tom’s Cabin* (1853), a collection of testimony, newspaper advertisements, and reports. The novel also inspired more than 20 “Anti-Uncle Tom” novels defending slavery.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]
- **The consequence.** The novel did not cause the crisis, but it transformed the audience for it. The chapter should read *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* alongside the Fugitive Slave Act’s clause — in the surrounding argument, not as a second object in the In Focus box.[^ch19-check-focus][^ch19-vassar-stowe]

### 2.4 Black abolitionism: Douglass and Tubman

- **The claim.** The decade’s antislavery politics were led in significant part by Black activists whose own freedom was precarious.[^ch19-yawp-douglass][^ch19-nps-tubman]
- **Douglass.** In his July 5, 1852, address in Rochester, Frederick Douglass told a white abolitionist audience that the Fourth of July was theirs, not his: “This Fourth July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.” He added that “The sunlight that brought light and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me,” and made American slavery, not national celebration, his subject.[^ch19-yawp-douglass]
- **Tubman.** Harriet Tubman escaped enslavement in Maryland in 1849 and spent the next decade making about 13 trips into Maryland to lead family and friends out, and giving instructions to about 70 more who freed themselves. She later worked closely with John Brown and, during the Civil War, with Union forces in South Carolina.[^ch19-nps-tubman]
- **The communities.** By the 1850s about 200,000 free Black people lived in the North in a state of “unfreedom,” denied citizenship, suffrage, and property rights by “black laws,” and organizing through conventions and vigilance committees. The chapter should treat these communities as the base of the abolitionist movement, not as its audience.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-check-race]
- **The consequence.** Black abolitionism gave the fugitive crisis its moral clarity and its personnel. The chapter should connect the Syracuse crowd, the Christiana defenders, and the vigilance committees to this broader movement.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom]

## 3. Kansas, party realignment, and expansion schemes

### 3.1 The Kansas-Nebraska Act

- **The claim.** The Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed the Missouri Compromise and reopened the slavery question in territory that had been closed to it, destroying the party system that had contained the conflict.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]
- **The bill.** Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, seeking a northern route for a transcontinental railroad, introduced a bill on January 4, 1854, to organize the Nebraska territory “with or without slavery, as their constitutions may prescribe” — the doctrine of popular sovereignty. Southern senators led by Missouri’s David Atchison demanded an explicit repeal of the 1820 line, and Douglas agreed: “I will incorporate it into my bill … though I know it will raise a hell of a storm.”[^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]
- **The passage.** The Senate passed the bill 37 to 14 in the early morning hours of March 4, 1854, and it became law on May 30, 1854. It created the territories of Kansas and Nebraska and left slavery to the settlers.[^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska]
- **The consequence.** The act outraged antislavery northerners, destroyed the remnants of the Whig coalition, and helped produce the Republican Party. It also produced the violent settlement race known as Bleeding Kansas.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]

### 3.2 Bleeding Kansas and the caning of Sumner

- **The claim.** The Kansas settlement race turned the territorial question into open violence, and the violence spread into Congress itself.[^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie][^ch19-senate-sumner]
- **Lawrence.** On May 21, 1856, a proslavery posse surrounded and sacked the free-state town of Lawrence, Kansas, flying a blood-red flag inscribed “Southern Rights,” while the inhabitants fled.[^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]
- **Sumner.** Three days earlier, Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts had denounced the “Crime Against Kansas” and named Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina as a principal culprit, calling slavery “the harlot, Slavery.” On May 22, 1856, Representative Preston Brooks of South Carolina, Butler’s kinsman, entered the Senate chamber after adjournment and beat Sumner with a metal-topped cane until the cane broke; Sumner was carried away bleeding and did not fully recover for years.[^ch19-senate-sumner][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]
- **Pottawatomie.** On the night of May 24–25, 1856, John Brown and four of his sons dragged five proslavery men from their cabins near Pottawatomie Creek and killed them with broadswords. Brown, who had come to Kansas to fight for a free territory, treated the killings as an act of war.[^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]
- **The consequence.** The violence in Kansas and the caning in the Senate taught both sections that the conflict would be settled by force at least as often as by law.[^ch19-senate-sumner][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]

### 3.3 The collapse of the Whigs and the making of the Republican Party

- **The claim.** Kansas-Nebraska broke the second party system, and the Republican Party was built from its fragments as a northern, free-soil coalition.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-gop-platform-1856]
- **The collapse.** The Whig coalition disintegrated under the sectional strain; a nativist American Party and a revived Know-Nothing movement briefly competed for its voters; the Democracy survived but divided increasingly along sectional lines.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska]
- **The party.** Antislavery opponents of Kansas-Nebraska founded the Republican Party in 1854; its first statewide convention under the name met at Jackson, Michigan, on July 6, 1854. Its 1856 platform, adopted June 18, 1856, denied the authority of Congress, of a territorial legislature, or of any individual to give legal existence to slavery in any territory, and denounced slavery and polygamy as “twin relics of barbarism.”[^ch19-gop-platform-1856]
- **The consequence.** The Republicans offered northern voters a party organized against slavery’s expansion. The realignment set the stage for the 1856 election and, eventually, for 1860.[^ch19-gop-platform-1856][^ch19-election-1856]

### 3.4 Table 2: the election of 1856

- **The claim.** The 1856 election confirmed the sectional realignment: a Democrat won the presidency while a new Republican Party carried most of the North.[^ch19-election-1856]
- **The result.** James Buchanan won 174 electoral votes and 1,836,072 popular votes, about 45.3 percent. John C. Frémont, the Republican, won 114 electoral votes and 1,342,345 popular votes, about 33.1 percent. Millard Fillmore, the Whig-American, won eight electoral votes and 873,053 popular votes, about 21.6 percent.[^ch19-election-1856]
- **The meaning.** Buchanan won with less than a majority of the popular vote, and the state returns show his support concentrated in the slave states while Frémont’s was concentrated in the North. The result showed a party system reorganizing itself on sectional lines.[^ch19-election-1856]

### 3.6 Lecompton, the English bill, and Douglas’s break with Buchanan

- **The claim.** The Lecompton Constitution forced a split between the Democratic president and the Democratic senator whose doctrine of popular sovereignty Kansas was supposed to vindicate.[^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-english-bill]
- **The constitution.** A proslavery convention at Lecompton produced a state constitution that a rigged election ratified in December 1857 while free-state settlers boycotted the vote. A second election in January 1858 overwhelmingly defeated it.[^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-english-bill]
- **The English bill.** Congress balked; the English bill of 1858 offered Kansas admission conditioned on a vote on a land grant and constitution. Kansans rejected the offer on August 21, 1858, by 11,812 to 1,926.[^ch19-english-bill][^ch19-lecompton]
- **The split.** Douglas broke with President Buchanan over Lecompton, defending popular sovereignty against the proslavery constitution. The rupture made Douglas unpopular in the South and set him on the collision course that shaped 1860.[^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The source limits.** The Lecompton and English bill figures are presently drawn from a local historical site and a reference work and must be verified against the congressional record and the Kansas territorial records.[^ch19-check-sources][^ch19-lecompton][^ch19-english-bill]

### 3.7 Expansion schemes: Gadsden, Ostend, and the filibusters

- **The claim.** The crisis coincided with an aggressive, and ultimately frustrated, campaign to extend slavery’s reach beyond the continental United States.[^ch19-state-gadsden][^ch19-state-territorial-expansion]
- **The Gadsden Purchase.** The United States and Mexico concluded a treaty in 1853–54 in which the United States paid Mexico $10 million for 29,670 square miles that became part of Arizona and New Mexico, providing a route for a southern transcontinental railroad. The Senate ratified a revised treaty on April 25, 1854, and Santa Anna signed it on June 8, 1854. Chapter 18 owns the Mexican-American War; this chapter owns the Gadsden sequel as part of slavery-expansion politics.[^ch19-state-gadsden][^ch19-check-gadsden]
- **The Ostend Manifesto.** In 1854 three American diplomats drafted a document urging the acquisition of Cuba and framing it as essential to American interests. When it became public it embarrassed the Pierce administration and drew condemnation; the Republican platform of 1856 rejected its “highwayman’s plea, that ‘might makes right.’” The chapter should treat Ostend as evidence of a slaveholding expansionist current, not as an official policy the government adopted, and must verify the document’s exact wording before quotation.[^ch19-state-territorial-expansion][^ch19-gop-platform-1856]
- **The filibusters.** Private armed expeditions — Narciso López’s against Cuba in 1851 and William Walker’s in Nicaragua from 1855 — pursued slavery’s extension outside official diplomacy. They mostly failed and produced local hostility to American expansion.[^ch19-state-territorial-expansion]
- **The consequence.** The expansion schemes show that the sectional argument was not confined to the territories. For many slaveholders, slavery’s security required new ground, and the republic’s boundaries were argued over as a question of slavery’s future.[^ch19-state-territorial-expansion][^ch19-check-race]

## 4. *Dred Scott* and the debates

### 4.1 The Dred Scott decision

- **The claim.** In *Dred Scott v. Sandford*, decided March 6, 1857, the Supreme Court held that enslaved people were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in a federal territory, apparently making the Republican position unconstitutional.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott]
- **The case.** Dred Scott and his wife Harriet sued for their freedom in St. Louis in 1846, on the ground that they had been held in a free territory. What began as a private suit became an 11-year legal struggle that grew as slavery became the central issue of national politics.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott]
- **The opinion.** Chief Justice Roger B. Taney’s majority opinion stated that the descendants of enslaved Africans, whether free or enslaved, were not “citizens” within the meaning of the Constitution; that the class of enslaved people and their descendants had been “regarded as beings of an inferior order … and so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect”; and that Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in the territories.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott]
- **The consequence.** The decision went beyond the case to strike at the Republican Party’s central plank. Rather than settling the territorial question, it made the question constitutional and intensified the political conflict.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

### 4.2 Reaction: Lincoln’s reply and the Republican plank

- **The claim.** Republicans treated *Dred Scott* as a partisan and a constitutional catastrophe, and Lincoln answered it by appealing to the Declaration of Independence.[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]
- **The reply.** In a speech at Springfield on June 26, 1857, Lincoln said the decision “declares two propositions — first, that a Negro cannot sue in the U.S. Courts; and secondly, that Congress cannot prohibit slavery in the Territories,” and that it was made by a divided court. He argued that the Declaration’s “all men are created equal” set a standard for free society, even if it did not describe present conditions.[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]
- **The claim about rights.** Lincoln conceded that the Black woman was not his equal “in some respects,” but insisted that “in her natural right to eat the bread she earns with her own hands without asking leave of anyone else, she is my equal, and the equal of all others.” The chapter should present the argument’s limits as well as its force.[^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply][^ch19-check-race]
- **The consequence.** *Dred Scott* gave the Republicans a grievance and a target. The party’s 1856 platform had already denied any authority to give slavery legal existence in a territory; after 1857 the argument was against the Court as well.[^ch19-gop-platform-1856][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

### 4.3 The Freeport Doctrine

- **The claim.** Douglas answered *Dred Scott* with the doctrine that a territory could exclude slavery by withholding the local police regulations slavery required — a position that satisfied Illinois voters and enraged the South.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The question.** At the second Lincoln-Douglas debate at Freeport, Illinois, on August 27, 1858, Lincoln asked whether the people of a territory could lawfully exclude slavery before forming a state constitution.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The answer.** Douglas said the people “have the lawful means to introduce it, or exclude it as they please, for the reason that slavery cannot exist a day or an hour anywhere, unless it is supported by local police regulations.” Territorial legislatures could not forbid slavery directly, but could make it practically impossible.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The consequence.** The answer helped Douglas in Illinois and cost him the South, which demanded a federal slave code for the territories. That demand split the Democratic convention at Charleston in April 1860 — Chapter 20’s opening scene.[^ch19-nps-freeport][^ch19-check-ch20]

### 4.4 The Lincoln-Douglas debates and the House Divided

- **The claim.** The 1858 debates made Lincoln a national figure and framed the crisis as a choice between a nation all free and a nation all slave.[^ch19-nps-house-divided][^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The speech.** Accepting the Illinois Republican nomination on June 16, 1858, Lincoln said, “A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free,” and predicted the Union would become all one thing or all the other.[^ch19-nps-house-divided]
- **The debates.** Lincoln and Douglas met in seven debates between August 21 and October 15, 1858, with national coverage carried by telegraph and reprinted in newspapers. Douglas, the incumbent, opened and closed four of the seven.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The outcome.** Douglas was reelected to the Senate by the narrowest of margins, but the campaign made Lincoln a national candidate and made the South read Douglas as an enemy of slavery.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- **The consequence.** By 1858 the political argument had narrowed to the question the chapter’s title names: whether a republic half slave and half free could contain the crisis.[^ch19-nps-house-divided][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]

## 5. The slave South, free Black communities, and John Brown

### 5.1 The slave South: cotton, expansion, and political power

- **The claim.** The crisis of the 1850s was rooted in a slave South whose wealth and political power grew with cotton and depended on slavery’s expansion.[^ch19-yawp-cotton]
- **The economy.** By 1860 the South produced about two billion pounds of cotton, more than 60 percent of all United States exports. Enslaved people were the central capital asset of the cotton economy; prime field hands averaged $1,600 at market by 1850, and land and enslaved labor were pledged as collateral for further expansion.[^ch19-yawp-cotton]
- **The society.** About four million enslaved people lived in the South in 1860, nearly 2.5 million of them in the Cotton Belt; the internal slave trade moved people from the Upper South to the Southwest and separated families. Planters converted wealth into political power, and proslavery ideology defended slavery as the foundation of order.[^ch19-yawp-cotton]
- **The consequence.** For the slaveholding South, the territories were not an abstraction. The question of whether slavery could expand was the question of whether the institution could remain politically and economically secure. The chapter should present the slaveholding interest at its strongest, and the enslaved majority as actors within it.[^ch19-yawp-cotton][^ch19-check-race]

### 5.2 Free Black communities and northern “unfreedom”

- **The claim.** The North’s free Black communities lived in a legal “unfreedom” that made them both the targets of the fugitive law and the organizers of resistance.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom]
- **The numbers and the law.** By the 1850s about 200,000 free Black people lived in the North. “Black laws” denied them citizenship, suffrage, and property rights and exposed them to violence without recourse: Massachusetts barred Black residence, Ohio required a $500 bond of Black entrants, and Indiana barred Black testimony and voting.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom]
- **The institutions.** The Black convention movement began with the American Society of Free Persons of Color, which held its first convention in Philadelphia in 1830; it spread westward with Black migrants. Churches, schools, and vigilance committees formed the infrastructure of the freedom struggle.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-ev-burns]
- **The consequence.** The chapter must not treat the North as simply “free.” The same decade that produced the Fugitive Slave Act also produced the black laws, and the same communities that suffered both led the resistance.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-check-race]

### 5.3 John Brown from Pottawatomie to Harpers Ferry

- **The claim.** John Brown carried the argument over slavery from the Kansas plains to a federal armory in Virginia, and his raid made the coming conflict visible.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The preparation.** Brown arrived at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, on July 3, 1859, raised money from abolitionists, and rented the Kennedy farm in Maryland under the alias Isaac Smith. His force numbered 21 at the time of the raid, and he studied the armory, the trains, and the town.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The raid.** On the night of October 16, 1859, Brown and 18 men took the bridges, the U.S. Armory and Arsenal, and the Rifle Works, and seized hostages including the enslaver Lewis Washington; the people they enslaved were freed. The next day, townspeople and militia fired on the raiders; the first person killed was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railroad employee.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The capture and trial.** On October 18, Colonel Robert E. Lee and a company of U.S. Marines stormed the engine house where Brown and his surviving men had holed up, killing or capturing the raiders. Sixteen people died in the raid, including ten of Brown’s men. Brown’s trial began October 27, 1859, and lasted five days; the jury convicted on all charges, and Brown was sentenced to hang on November 2. He was executed on December 2, 1859.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The words.** Brown told his captors, “I came here from Kansas and this is a slave state; I want to free all the negroes in this state.” In his final statement he wrote, “I, John Brown, am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood.” Both quotations must be collated against the trial record and the printed statements.[^ch19-nps-john-brown][^ch19-check-epigraphs]

### 5.4 The aftermath of Harpers Ferry

- **The claim.** Harpers Ferry accelerated the disunion it intended to prevent, hardening the South and elevating Brown in northern memory.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The reaction.** A contemporary newspaper account quoted by the National Park Service concluded that “the Harpers Ferry invasion has advanced the cause of disunion more than any other event that has happened since the formation of the Government.” Brown’s bearing at trial and execution made him a martyr to many northern abolitionists.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The memory.** Brown’s raid and his final words became a permanent part of the antislavery argument, and his execution widened the breach between the sections.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The consequence.** By December 1859 the party system, the courts, and the territorial settlements had all failed to contain the conflict. The next year’s election and secession are Chapter 20’s subject.[^ch19-check-ch20][^ch19-nps-freeport]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The 1850 settlement deferred rather than resolved the conflict.** Congress passed five statutes in September 1850 admitting California free and strengthening the Fugitive Slave Act; the fugitive law became the flashpoint.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]
- **The fugitive cases are documented.** Rescues at Syracuse, Boston, and Christiana, and renditions at Boston in 1851 and 1854, are established in court and institutional records.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-sims][^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-christiana-society]
- **Kansas-Nebraska reopened the territorial question and broke the party system.** The act of May 30, 1854, repealed the Missouri Compromise, and the violence known as Bleeding Kansas followed.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]
- **Slavery stood at the center of the political argument.** *Dred Scott*, the Lincoln-Douglas debates, and the Freeport Doctrine all turned on slavery’s extension, whatever else divided the parties.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-nps-freeport][^ch19-gop-platform-1856]
- **Harpers Ferry escalated the crisis.** Brown’s raid and execution, and the reaction to them, narrowed the space for compromise.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- **The costs were borne unequally.** Enslaved people, free Black communities, and fugitives bore the law’s violence; the politicians argued over it. The chapter must keep that asymmetry central.[^ch19-nps-northern-unfreedom][^ch19-yawp-cotton][^ch19-check-race]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the conflict “irrepressible”?** If slavery was the fundamental and unbridgeable division, the war was near-inevitable; if the political system failed at specific moments, the outcome was contingent. The chapter should weigh both without dissolving one into the other.[^ch19-ayers-causes][^ch19-cwmonitor-reading]
- **What weight should party politics carry?** Holt and Egnal emphasize the collapse of the national parties and changing economic ties rather than a moral crusade; Potter emphasizes slavery’s centrality. The chapter should test each against the elections, the conventions, and the votes.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]
- **How much did slavery’s expansion drive the crisis?** For many slaveholders, expansion was a condition of security; for many northerners, the issue was the spread of an institution they opposed without intending Black equality. The chapter should distinguish antislavery from anti-Black prejudice.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-race]
- **Whose agency mattered most?** Fugitives, vigilance committees, and Black abolitionists forced the issue; politicians responded. The chapter should not let the politicians’ records eclipse the acts of the enslaved and free Black actors.[^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-tubman][^ch19-check-race]
- **Whose voices survive?** Statutes, court opinions, speeches, and newspapers document the politicians and the cases; the enslaved left fewer direct records, and the law excluded the accused fugitive’s testimony. The chapter must name that silence.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-yawp-cotton][^ch19-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the failure of compromise was overdetermined but not foreordained: slavery’s extension was the central and unbridgeable question, and the specific failures of party, court, and settlement made the rupture more likely without dictating its timing or form.
- **The fundamentalist position.** Edward Ayers summarizes the “fundamentalist” school as emphasizing the intrinsic, inevitable conflict between slavery and free labor. Read critically, it names the central issue but can make the outcome seem automatic and obscure the choices available to actors.[^ch19-ayers-causes]
- **The revisionist and party-centered position.** Michael Holt argues that the collapse of the national two-party system provoked the crisis by making Americans see internal enemies; Marc Egnal recasts the party story as an economic one, in which East–West economic ties displaced North–South ones. These accounts restore contingency and structural depth but can understate slavery’s moral and political force.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading]
- **The slaveholders’ offensive.** Charles Dew’s study of secession commissioners finds that the maintenance of racial dominance was the dominant argument for disunion, and William W. Freehling stresses the South’s internal divisions and the contingency of secession. They strengthen the case for slavery as the cause while insisting that southerners were not unanimous.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** David M. Potter’s *The Impending Crisis* remains the starting point, treating slavery as pervading the sectional conflict without reducing the crisis to a single cause. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not teams, and test each against the cases, the votes, and the violence.[^ch19-cwmonitor-reading][^ch19-check-sources]

## Legacies

The settlements did not hold. The Compromise of 1850 bought a decade at the price of a fugitive-slave law that northern communities refused to enforce, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 tore up the line that had contained the conflict since 1820. *Dred Scott* made the Republican position unconstitutional in the Court’s eyes, and the Freeport Doctrine answered the Court with a doctrine of local police power. Each settlement made the next one less credible.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850][^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-nps-freeport]

Harpers Ferry gave the crisis its martyr and its warning. Brown’s raid failed militarily, but his words and his execution entered the antislavery argument permanently, and the South read the northern response as proof that the free states would not protect slavery. Within a year and a half, the election of 1860 and secession carried the conflict past the reach of compromise.[^ch19-nps-john-brown][^ch19-check-ch20]

The decade’s lasting legacy is a question about the republic itself. A nation that claimed to govern by consent enforced a law that denied the accused a hearing and paid its officials more to enslave a person than to free one. The people who answered it — in Syracuse, in Boston, at Christiana, and in Kansas — did not wait for the politicians to resolve the contradiction. The chapter’s question, why compromise failed, has its sharpest answer in their acts as much as in the votes.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-christiana-society][^ch19-check-race]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The crisis was entangled with Cuba, Nicaragua, Mexico, and Britain: the Ostend Manifesto and the filibusters pursued slavery’s extension abroad, the Gadsden Purchase fixed the southern border, and the cotton economy bound the South to Liverpool and Manchester. The chapter should place American sectionalism in its Atlantic setting.[^ch19-state-gadsden][^ch19-state-territorial-expansion][^ch19-yawp-cotton]

## Summary

- The Compromise of 1850 was five statutes passed in September 1850; it admitted California free, organized New Mexico and Utah, fixed the Texas boundary, abolished the slave trade in the District of Columbia, and strengthened the Fugitive Slave Act.[^ch19-archives-compromise1850]
- The Fugitive Slave Act required free-state enforcement, paid commissioners more for returning than for freeing an accused person, and denied the accused a voice in the hearing; it made the settlement a continuing test of federal authority.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act]
- A small set of fugitive cases — Minkins and Sims in Boston, Christiana in Pennsylvania, the Jerry Rescue in Syracuse, Glover in Wisconsin, and Burns in Boston — turned the statute into a national drama of rescue and rendition.[^ch19-ev-burns][^ch19-nps-sims][^ch19-christiana-society][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-glover]
- *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*, published in book form on March 20, 1852, sold about 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year and made the fugitive question mass literature.[^ch19-vassar-stowe]
- The Kansas-Nebraska Act of May 30, 1854, repealed the Missouri Compromise, introduced popular sovereignty, destroyed the Whig coalition, and produced Bleeding Kansas.[^ch19-archives-kansas-nebraska][^ch19-senate-kansas-nebraska]
- On May 22, 1856, Representative Preston Brooks beat Senator Charles Sumner nearly to death; on the night of May 24–25, 1856, John Brown and four of his sons killed five proslavery men at Pottawatomie Creek.[^ch19-senate-sumner][^ch19-pbs-pottawatomie]
- In the election of 1856, Buchanan won 174 electoral votes and about 45 percent of the popular vote, Frémont carried most of the North with 114 electoral votes, and Fillmore won eight.[^ch19-election-1856]
- On March 6, 1857, the Supreme Court in *Dred Scott v. Sandford* held that enslaved people were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories; Lincoln answered that the Declaration’s equality set a standard for free society.[^ch19-archives-dred-scott][^ch19-lincoln-dred-scott-reply]
- Douglas’s Freeport Doctrine of August 27, 1858, held that a territory could exclude slavery by withholding local police regulations, splitting him from the South.[^ch19-nps-freeport]
- On October 16–18, 1859, John Brown’s raid on Harpers Ferry failed; he was tried, convicted, and executed on December 2, 1859, and the raid widened the breach between the sections.[^ch19-nps-john-brown]
- The debate turns on whether the war was an irrepressible conflict, a contingent failure of party politics, a slaveholders’ offensive, or some combination, and the honest answer holds several positions together.[^ch19-check-tables][^ch19-cwmonitor-reading]

> *A closing note.* The decade between the Compromise of 1850 and John Brown’s execution is the record of a republic repeatedly promising itself that the slavery question had been settled. Each settlement — the statutes of 1850, the territorial compromises, the Court’s opinion of 1857 — changed the terms of the conflict without ending it, and each made the next settlement harder to believe. The people who refused to wait for the politicians, from the Syracuse crowd to the men and women who freed themselves, forced the issue into the open. The chapter’s question is why compromise failed, and its answer begins with the law’s own machinery and the courage of those who defied it.[^ch19-fugitive-slave-act][^ch19-syracuse-jerry][^ch19-nps-john-brown]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at the Democratic National Convention in Charleston, South Carolina, in April 1860, where the demand for a federal slave code for the territories split the party Douglas had led. That demand was the direct answer to his Freeport Doctrine, and the split helped elect Abraham Lincoln. Chapter 20 takes up the election of 1860, secession, and the coming of the war.[^ch19-check-ch20][^ch19-nps-freeport][^ch19-check-bridge]
