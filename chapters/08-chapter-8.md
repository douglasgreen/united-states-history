# Chapter 8. Imperial Rivalries and the Great War for Empire (1713–1763)

> “As to the summons you send me to retire, I do not think myself obliged to obey it.”
>
> — Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre, reply to Robert Dinwiddie, December 1753, as recorded in George Washington’s journal[^ch08-wash-journal-bfi]

> “… it is just and reasonable, and essential to our Interest, and the Security of our Colonies, that the several Nations or Tribes of Indians with whom We are connected, and who live under our Protection, should not be molested or disturbed in the Possession of such Parts of Our Dominions and Territories as, not having been ceded to or purchased by Us, are reserved to them … as their Hunting Grounds.”
>
> — George III, Royal Proclamation, October 7, 1763[^ch08-proc-avalon]

> “Coming to grips with the Seven Years’ War as an event that decisively shaped American history, as well as the histories of Europe and the Atlantic world in general, may therefore help us begin to understand the colonial period as something more than a quaint mezzotint prelude to our national history.”
>
> — Fred Anderson, *Crucible of War: The Seven Years’ War and the Fate of Empire in British North America, 1754–1766* (New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 2000), Introduction[^ch08-anderson-past]

The three quotations disagree about who owned the interior and what the war meant. Saint-Pierre answers a Virginia governor with the flat refusal of a rival empire. George III speaks as a sovereign who claims to protect the nations whose lands he also claims. Anderson steps back and argues that the war was a hinge of American history, not a decorative preface to the Revolution. Together they frame the chapter’s question: why did victory destabilize the empire that won it?[^ch08-check-epigraphs]

On October 31, 1753, George Washington left Williamsburg carrying a letter from Lieutenant Governor Robert Dinwiddie to the commander of the French forts on the Ohio. Washington was 21, a major in the Virginia militia, and a member of a family with land to gain in the West. He rode by Fredericksburg and Winchester to Wills Creek, where he hired Christopher Gist to guide him and four traders to handle the baggage; Jacob Van Braam went along as interpreter. At Logstown, on the Ohio, he met Tanacharison, the Seneca leader the English called the Half-King, and asked for an escort. On December 4 the party reached Venango, where the French officer Philippe-Thomas Chabert de Joncaire told Washington that it was the French “absolute Design to take Possession of the Ohio.” At Fort Le Boeuf, on French Creek, Washington delivered the letter to Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre, inspected the fort’s eight six-pound guns and its canoes, and received a reply that was polite and absolute. Saint-Pierre would not retire.[^ch08-wash-journal-mdgazette][^ch08-wash-journal-bfi]

The return nearly killed him. The journal recorded “excessive Rains and vast Quantity of Snow” and, later, “one continued Series of cold wet Weather.” The Allegheny was running with ice. Washington and Gist built a raft, were caught in the floes, and Washington was thrown into ten feet of water; he saved himself by catching a log. Gist’s fingers froze. They reached Williamsburg on January 16, 1754, and Dinwiddie had Washington’s report printed. The *Maryland Gazette* carried it in March, and a London edition followed with materials for a map. Readers in Williamsburg and London could now follow a young Virginian into the Ohio Country and hear a French commander refuse to leave.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-check-scene]

The question the chapter follows is why victory destabilized Britain’s empire — why the war made it larger, poorer, and harder to govern.

This chapter covers the imperial rivalry between Britain and France in North America from the Peace of Utrecht in 1713 to the settlement of 1763, and the war that turned the rivalry into a global conflict. It draws on Washington’s printed journal, governors’ and officers’ reports, treaties and the Royal Proclamation, the maps and land records of the competing empires, and the scholarship that reads those records against the grain. It treats “empire,” “alliance,” “middle ground,” and “frontier” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter must establish. It uses historical scholarship for what the records cannot show and names the silences left by archives that recorded Native diplomacy through European interpreters and preserved the victors’ accounts. It does not present the British victory as inevitable, the interior as empty, or the war as a mere prelude to the American Revolution.[^ch08-check-names][^ch08-nps-middle-ground]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines New France’s fur-trade, alliance, and Catholic-seigneurial order and compares the two empires. §2 turns to the Ohio Country and the Indigenous brokers both empires courted. §3 follows Washington’s mission into the war’s beginning at Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity, the Albany Congress, and the campaigns of 1755. §4 explains colonial mobilization, William Pitt’s strategy, the fall of Quebec and Montreal, and the war beyond North America. §5 weighs the Peace of Paris, Pontiac’s War, and the Royal Proclamation of 1763. §6 asks how historians explain the war and its consequences for independence. The chapter begins in the Ohio Country, where a young Virginian carried a letter that two empires would answer with war.

## 1. New France’s networks

### 1.1 The fur trade and the alliance system

New France was not a colony of dense settlement. It was a network of forts, missions, and trading posts bound to Indigenous nations by trade, kinship, and diplomacy. Its power rested less on people than on relationships. The St. Lawrence was the spine; the Great Lakes, the Ohio, and the Mississippi were the reaches. French traders exchanged manufactured goods for furs, and French officers distributed gifts, medals, and gunpowder to allied leaders. In the Great Lakes region and the pays d’en haut, this produced what the historian Richard White has called a “middle ground”: a space in which neither Europeans nor Native nations could fully impose their will, and in which both accommodated each other’s customs.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground]

The alliance was not a French command. It was a negotiation among partners with their own interests. France’s ability to govern a vast territory with few settlers depended on controlling waterways and on cultivating strong relationships with powerful nations, including the Wyandot, Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, and their neighbors. When those relationships held, a small population could project power far beyond its numbers. When they failed, the colony’s weakness was exposed.[^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance]

Fur was the export that paid for the network. It accounted for about 70 percent of New France’s exports as late as the 1730s, and the trade drew French traders farther into the interior in search of suppliers and new routes. The same traffic that made the colony valuable made it dependent on the nations who hunted, processed, and carried the furs. New France’s strength and its fragility came from the same source: it could not defend the interior alone, and its Native allies had their own interests in the balance of empires.[^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance][^ch08-nps-middle-ground]

### 1.2 Governance, the seigneurial order, and the Catholic church

New France organized its settler society around seigneurial land tenure, the Catholic church, and royal administration, a different order from the charter colonies to the south. The seigneurial system, established in 1627 and abolished only in 1854, divided land into seigneuries: large grants held by seigneurs who owed services and dues to the crown and who in turn settled and rented plots to habitants. The plots were usually long, narrow river lots, laid out so that each family had access to the water. Habitants paid the cens, a small annual due that acknowledged the seigneur’s authority, the rente in cash or kind, and the banalités owed for grinding grain at the seigneur’s mill; they also owed days of labor, or corvée. Most of the population — 75 to 80 percent, by one estimate — lived on seigneurial land.[^ch08-canadianencyc-seigneurial]

The church ran parishes, schools, and hospitals, and Catholic clergy held public authority. Government combined a royal governor, who commanded the military and conducted external affairs, with an intendant, who managed justice, police, and finances, and a Sovereign Council that served as a court of appeal and registered the king’s edicts. The system was hierarchical, but it was not simply imposed from above: the availability of land gave habitants room to choose where to settle, and seigneurs who were too demanding or too absent failed to attract tenants.[^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance][^ch08-canadianencyc-seigneurial]

Settlement concentrated along the St. Lawrence, leaving a vast interior held by alliance rather than by population. That was exactly the structure the war would test. New France could put farmers in the river valley and traders and soldiers at the posts, but it could not put colonists across the continent. Its empire was a set of relationships strung along water.[^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance]

### 1.3 The demographic imbalance and what it meant

The British colonies outnumbered New France by roughly twenty to one in the 1750s, yet France held the interior for decades. The standard compiled estimates put the total population of the future thirteen colonies at about 1,170,760 in 1750 and 1,593,625 in 1760, combining White and Black populations. New France held perhaps 70,000 people at the start of the Seven Years’ War, by one recent estimate; other compilations give 75,000 to 90,000 colonists, including Acadians and the settlers of Louisiana. The totals rest on colonial counts and estimates, not a modern census, and the French figures cover a scattered and differently defined population. They describe a scale, not an exact count.[^ch08-pop-hs][^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-check-population]

Numbers alone did not decide the contest. France’s interior position, Indigenous alliances, and control of rivers offset British population, and British colonists were reluctant to fund and man distant campaigns. The imbalance was a problem the British had to solve, not a guaranteed victory. Table 1 presents the comparison.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-check-table1]

## 2. The Ohio Country and Indigenous brokers

### 2.1 A contested middle ground

The Ohio Country was not an empty frontier between empires but a set of Indigenous homelands whose nations played the rivals against each other. The region between Lake Erie and the Ohio River held Mingo, Shawnee, Delaware (Lenape), and Wyandot communities, among others, many of them recent migrants pushed west by earlier colonial expansion. They traded with both empires and were courted by both.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-check-names]

The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy claimed authority over the Ohio nations and dealt with the British through the Covenant Chain, so the contest for the interior was also a contest among Native powers. That claim was real in diplomacy and contested on the ground. It remained a claim, not a settled fact, and the Haudenosaunee were nobody’s proxies.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]

Whoever won the favor of the Ohio nations could control the river, and the nations knew it. This is the ground on which Washington’s mission and the war’s first shots were fought. It is also the ground on which the middle-ground model must be used carefully: White’s model describes the Great Lakes region, and the Ohio Country was a related but distinct space of migration, trade, and rivalry.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-white-middle-ground]

### 2.2 The Ohio Company and Virginia’s land claims

The war grew partly from a private land company’s ambition. Virginia’s claim to the Ohio Country was pressed by investors who expected to profit from the lands they claimed. In 1748 Thomas Lee and George Washington’s half-brothers Lawrence and Augustine Washington organized the Ohio Company of Virginia, whose investors included Robert Dinwiddie himself. In 1749 the crown granted the company 200,000 acres on the Ohio, with another 300,000 to follow if it settled a hundred families and built a fort; the grant was rent- and tax-free for ten years. From its post at Wills Creek, the company planned settlements and began an eighty-mile wagon road toward the Monongahela.[^ch08-ohio-company-wiki][^ch08-nps-prelude]

The company hired Christopher Gist in 1750 to explore and survey the Ohio Valley, and Gist’s journals described the country from the Alleghenies toward the Miami villages. In 1752 the company signed a treaty of friendship at Logstown with nations of the region, though Virginia and Pennsylvania continued to dispute the same lands. Dinwiddie’s demand that the French leave the Ohio was made on behalf of a colony and an investment at once. The double interest was real, but the war was not only a land deal.[^ch08-ohio-company-wiki][^ch08-mountvernon-timeline]

### 2.3 Tanacharison and the brokers

The war’s opening depended on Native diplomats and warriors whose decisions were their own. Neither empire could act in the Ohio Country without them. Tanacharison, a Seneca leader the English called the Half-King, had been sent by the Haudenosaunee Confederacy to speak for the Ohio nations in dealings with the empires. French expansion threatened Haudenosaunee influence, and Tanacharison’s own account of his reception at the French fort suggests why he turned toward the British.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-wash-journal-mdgazette]

Washington’s journal preserves the speech Tanacharison said he had made to the French commander: “Fathers, both you and the English are white, we live in a Country between; therefore the Land belongs to neither one nor to other; But the Great Being Above allow’d it to be a Place of Residence for us …” He asked the French to withdraw, as he said he had asked the English to do, and he closed with a challenge: the empire that showed “the greatest Regard to it” would have his people’s support. The commander’s reply, as Tanacharison reported it, was contemptuous: he would go down the river, and he had forces sufficient to “tread under my Feet all that stand in Opposition.” The words reach us through Washington’s transcription of Tanacharison’s report, not from a French record, and they should be read with that mediation in mind.[^ch08-wash-journal-mdgazette]

Tanacharison allied with the British because the alliance served his nations’ interests, not because he accepted British sovereignty. His presence on Washington’s 1753 journey and his warriors at Jumonville Glen show how much of the war’s course ran through Indigenous decisions. To credit the brokers is not to romanticize them or to pretend that the records preserve their voices fully. What survives is filtered through European reports.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-check-names]

### 2.4 The Céloron expedition and the French counter-claim

France answered Virginia’s claim with its own, marked on the land. The rival claims were not settled by law but by forts and war. In 1749 Pierre Joseph Céloron de Blainville set out from Montreal to mark the bounds of French claims, burying lead plates at the mouths of key rivers, including at the forks of the Ohio. In 1753 the French built a series of forts — Presque Isle, Le Boeuf, and Machault — to link Canada to Louisiana and block British expansion.[^ch08-mountvernon-timeline][^ch08-washingtonstrail]

When Dinwiddie sent men to build a fort at the forks of the Ohio in early 1754, a larger French and Canadian force under Claude-Pierre Pécaudy de Contrecœur arrived in April, compelled the small British party to leave, tore down its works, and began Fort Duquesne. Washington had already seen the forks and judged them “extremely well situated for a Fort,” with “the absolute Command of both Rivers.” By the spring of 1754 two empires were building forts on the same ground, and the question of who would fire first belonged to the next section.[^ch08-wash-journal-mdgazette][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]

## 3. Washington, Albany, and the war’s beginning

### 3.1 The mission’s violent sequel

The war began not on a European battlefield but in the Ohio Country, in a chain of decisions taken by a colonial governor and a young officer without London’s direction. In March 1754 Dinwiddie sent Washington back to the frontier with authority to restrain anyone obstructing Virginia’s works and, “in Case of resistance[,] to make Prisoners of or kill & destroy them.” The historian Fred Anderson describes those instructions, issued without the knowledge or direction of the British government, as “an invitation to start a war.”[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]

Washington marched west with a small Virginia force, built Fort Necessity in the Great Meadows, and waited for instructions. The French held the forks and Fort Duquesne, and both sides sent small parties into the same disputed ground. The fort was a flimsy circular stockade in an open field, designed as a supply depot rather than a fortress, and it could not withstand a sustained attack. Washington would learn that in July.[^ch08-mountvernon-fortnecessity][^ch08-nps-prelude]

### 3.2 Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity

The first bloodshed of the war in North America came on May 28, 1754, when Washington’s party and Tanacharison’s warriors ambushed a French detachment near Jumonville Glen. Washington had about forty soldiers and a dozen of Tanacharison’s warriors. A shot was fired before the encircling party was fully in position; whether a Frenchman or a Virginian fired it has been disputed ever since. The fight lasted about fifteen minutes. By Washington’s own count, ten Frenchmen were killed and twenty-one taken prisoner; the National Park Service counts seventeen killed or wounded. Among the dead was the French officer Joseph Coulon de Villiers de Jumonville, killed by Tanacharison in the confusion that followed the surrender. The manner of his death is uncertain, and the accounts conflict.[^ch08-nps-jumonville][^ch08-mountvernon-fortnecessity][^ch08-check-dating]

On July 3 a larger French and Native force under Jumonville’s brother, Louis Coulon de Villiers, attacked Fort Necessity in heavy rain. Washington surrendered that night. The surrender document, which he signed without reading French, used the word “assassinated” for Jumonville’s death, a charge Washington later insisted was a translation error. He was the only military surrender of his career. He marched his men out on July 4.[^ch08-mountvernon-fortnecessity][^ch08-check-dating]

News of the two fights reached London, and the British government decided to send an army the following year. What had begun as rival claims to a river valley was becoming an imperial war. The sequence matters for the chapter’s argument: the war did not begin with a declaration or a European decision. It began with a colonial governor’s instructions, a young officer’s choices, and a Native leader’s decision to strike.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-nps-jumonville]

### 3.3 The Albany Congress and the Plan of Union

While Washington fought in the Ohio Country, seven colonies met at Albany in June and July 1754 to secure the Haudenosaunee alliance and to consider a common government. The congress met chiefly to repair a breakdown in negotiations between New York and the Mohawk nation and to set a common policy toward the Haudenosaunee.[^ch08-state-albany]

Benjamin Franklin proposed a general government with a crown-appointed President-General and a Grand Council chosen by the colonial assemblies, with power to treat with the nations, raise forces, and tax. The delegates adopted a version on July 10, 1754. The plan failed at both ends. The colonial assemblies thought it took too much of their independence, and British officials thought it took too much for the colonies. Franklin later blamed the assemblies’ provincial outlook and their jealousy of any central taxing authority. His *Join, or Die* woodcut, published in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* in May 1754, pictured the colonies as segments of a severed snake.[^ch08-albany-avalon][^ch08-join-or-die]

Albany showed that a shared danger could produce a proposal for union and that the proposal could still fail. The plan resurfaced in later debates, but it was not the inevitable ancestor of the Constitution. The colonies that could not agree on a common government in 1754 would have to fight a war without one.[^ch08-state-albany]

### 3.4 1755 and the widening war

In 1755 Britain sent a general and a plan of four offensives, and the most important of them ended in disaster. The war widened from a frontier quarrel into a contest of regular armies. Major General Edward Braddock led about 1,459 regulars and provincials toward Fort Duquesne. On July 9, 1755, French and Native forces ambushed his column near the Monongahela. Braddock was mortally wounded, 977 of his men were killed or wounded, including sixty-three officers, and the survivors fled. Washington, serving as an aide-de-camp, helped rally the retreat.[^ch08-monongahela-mountvernon]

Britain also struck at sea. In June 1755 Admiral Edward Boscawen fired on the French ship *Alcide* and captured troop transports bound for Canada. France and Britain declared war on each other in the spring of 1756, marking the formal start of the Seven Years’ War, of which the American fighting was one theater. Map 8 traces the campaigns, forts, and theaters from 1754 to 1763, and it distinguishes imperial claims from the Indigenous homelands the empires claimed.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki][^ch08-check-map]

The disasters of 1754–55 gave way to years of French success. 1755 was not the story’s ending; the war’s outcome was still open. Braddock’s defeat showed that regulars and provincials could be destroyed in the woods, and it showed how much of the fighting would depend on Native allies and on who controlled the routes into the interior.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki]

## 4. Campaigns and colonial mobilization

### 4.1 French victories, 1756–57

For the first years of the war the French and their allies held the advantage, and the British suffered a string of defeats that made the war’s outcome uncertain. French regulars, Canadian militia, and Native allies used interior lines and river routes to strike at British frontier posts. In August 1757 a French force under Louis-Joseph de Montcalm besieged Fort William Henry at the southern end of Lake George, and its commander, George Monro, surrendered on August 9 after several days of bombardment.[^ch08-fwh-wiki]

Montcalm’s Native allies violated the terms of surrender and attacked the departing British column, which had been deprived of ammunition. About 200 people were killed or wounded in the attack that followed. The episode became one of the war’s most notorious incidents and poisoned British attitudes toward Native people. It was a specific failure of imperial control over diverse allies, not evidence about Native warfare in general. Montcalm had promised protection and could not deliver it; the alliance that gave France its reach also limited its command.[^ch08-fwh-wiki]

### 4.2 Pitt’s strategy and British mobilization

The war turned when William Pitt took direction of the British war effort and committed money and troops to North America on a new scale, while subsidizing allies in Europe and attacking France’s empire worldwide. From 1758 Pitt’s ministry financed Prussia and others to keep French armies busy in Europe, expanded the Royal Navy, and dispatched large forces to North America. Britain’s ability to borrow and tax at home let it pay for a war the colonies could not fund alone. The strategy was expensive, and its cost would outlast the victory.[^ch08-pitt-wiki][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]

A British and provincial expedition under John Forbes took Fort Duquesne in November 1758, and other forces captured Louisbourg and struck French posts on the frontier. Colonial assemblies raised provincial regiments, but they did so unevenly and on their own terms. Mobilization was uneven, and the war was not a display of unified loyalty.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki][^ch08-state-taxation]

British mobilization changed the war’s scale. The colonies that helped win it would, within a generation, be asked to help pay for it. That is the connection the next chapter takes up, and this chapter states it as a consequence rather than a cause.[^ch08-state-taxation]

### 4.3 The fall of Quebec and Montreal

The decisive year was 1759, when a British army took Quebec after a siege and a short battle on the Plains of Abraham. On September 13 British troops under James Wolfe defeated French troops and Canadian militia under Montcalm on the plateau outside the city. British forces numbered about 4,400 regulars and colonial rangers against about 3,400 French and Canadian troops; the engagement lasted about an hour; both Wolfe and Montcalm were mortally wounded.[^ch08-plains-wiki]

Quebec surrendered after the battle, and Montreal fell in 1760, ending French military power in Canada. The war in Canada was also a war of supply, disease, and winter, and the victory at Quebec was the outcome of a campaign that had already taken Louisbourg and cut the St. Lawrence.[^ch08-plains-wiki][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]

By 1760 Britain had won the main object of the war in North America, though the war continued elsewhere and the peace was still three years away. The gap mattered, because the settlement would be shaped by victories and defeats far from Canada.[^ch08-plains-wiki][^ch08-state-paris]

### 4.4 The war beyond North America

The American fighting was one theater of a global war. Britain’s victories in the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and Europe shaped the peace as much as the campaigns in Canada. During the war British forces fought in India and captured French and Spanish colonies in the Caribbean. In 1762 Britain took Havana, the capital of Spanish Cuba, which strengthened its hand in the negotiations.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-paris-avalon]

Because the war was global, the peace required exchanges across the world. Britain would return some captured islands and choose which conquests to keep, a decision that shaped the North American settlement. The 1763 settlement was a global bargain, not a purely American outcome. The map of North America was drawn in Paris, but the bargaining that drew it reached from the Caribbean to India.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-paris-avalon]

## 5. Paris, Pontiac’s War, and the Proclamation

### 5.1 The Peace of Paris and the reshuffling of empires

The Treaty of Paris of February 10, 1763, ended the war and redrew the map of North America. France ceded Canada and all its claims east of the Mississippi to Britain, except the town of New Orleans and its island, which remained French. Spain, an ally of France, ceded Florida to Britain and received Louisiana from France through the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau of November 3, 1762. France had first ceded Louisiana to Spain to keep it out of British hands. France kept fishing rights and the small islands of Saint Pierre and Miquelon.[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-state-paris]

The treaty also settled the wider war. Britain restored Guadeloupe, Martinique, Belleisle, and Gorée to France, and it kept Canada, Grenada, Senegal, and Florida. British leaders chose to keep Canada and the trans-Appalachian interior while returning Guadeloupe and Martinique, the most valuable captured sugar islands, a choice contemporaries debated and historians still argue about. Colin Calloway’s study of 1763 treats the peace as the beginning of a cascade of consequences across the continent; a reviewer summarized the book’s argument by saying that the decision “set people and events in motion.”[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-calloway-review]

Britain now claimed an interior it did not control and a debt it could not easily pay. Table 2 lays out the settlement.[^ch08-check-table2][^ch08-check-fontainebleau]

### 5.2 Pontiac’s War and the Indigenous answer

The British victory did not end the war in the interior. In 1763 a broad Indigenous coalition attacked British forts and settlements from the Great Lakes to the Ohio Valley, and the British had to fight for the ground they had just won. British officials halted the gift-giving and trade practices that had sustained the French alliance, alarming interior nations. A Delaware (Lenape) prophet, Neolin, preached a vision in which the Master of Life told him to reject colonial ways and drive the Europeans out; his message influenced the Odawa leader Pontiac (Obwandiyag), who helped build a coalition of Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot, Seneca, and others.[^ch08-pontiac-battlefields][^ch08-pressbooks-debt][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]

Beginning in May 1763, warriors attacked British posts. Pontiac laid siege to Fort Detroit, and other nations attacked Fort Pitt. Most of the small posts fell; the British held Fort Pitt and Detroit. Hundreds of colonists were killed or captured, and many more fled. The war continued into 1766, when Pontiac agreed to a treaty signed at Fort Ontario on July 25, after it became clear that the French would not return. It was the first broad, cross-nation Indigenous war against the British, and it forced Britain to reconsider how it would govern the interior.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]

The frontier war also turned inward. In December 1763, Scots-Irish frontier settlers called the Paxton Boys attacked the Conestoga of the Susquehannock nation in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. They killed six residents of Conestoga Indian Town in their homes and, on a second attack, fourteen more who had been placed under protective custody. No one was ever prosecuted. The killings showed how far frontier violence outpaced imperial control, and they showed that the war’s violence could be turned against Native people who had taken no part in it.[^ch08-zinn-conestoga][^ch08-pressbooks-debt]

### 5.3 In Focus: the Royal Proclamation of 1763

**In Focus.** On October 7, 1763, King George III issued a proclamation that created four new governments — Quebec, East Florida, West Florida, and Grenada — and reserved the interior for the Native nations. The document declares that it is “just and reasonable, and essential to our Interest, and the Security of our Colonies, that the several Nations or Tribes of Indians with whom We are connected, and who live under our Protection, should not be molested or disturbed in the Possession of such Parts of Our Dominions and Territories as, not having been ceded to or purchased by Us, are reserved to them … as their Hunting Grounds.” It forbids colonial governors to grant land beyond the bounds of the new governments, forbids settlement on reserved lands, and requires that any purchase of Native land be made by the crown at a public meeting. Read closely, the Proclamation does two things at once. It recognizes Indigenous possession of the interior — a real concession that Native nations would later cite — and it asserts British sovereignty over those same nations and lands, reserving to the crown the exclusive right to buy them. It also angered colonists who had expected the war to open the West, and it proved unenforceable almost at once. Its line ran along the sources of the Atlantic-flowing rivers, the divide contemporaries called the Appalachian Mountains. The document was neither pure protection nor pure land grab.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line][^ch08-check-focus]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The war was decided by British money, sea power, and mobilization, not by population alone. Britain’s ability to borrow and to subsidize allies let it sustain a global war that France could not match.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-pitt-wiki]

France lost its mainland empire east of the Mississippi in 1763, and Spain acquired Louisiana west of it. The Treaty of Paris transferred Canada and the interior east of the Mississippi to Britain; the separate Treaty of Fontainebleau had already ceded Louisiana to Spain.[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-state-paris]

The settlement did not end conflict in the interior. Pontiac’s War followed the peace, and the Proclamation of 1763 tried and failed to control settlement beyond the Appalachians.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]

The war was global. Fighting in North America was one theater of a conflict that reached Europe, the Caribbean, West Africa, and India.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki][^ch08-state-paris]

The war left a large British debt and an enlarged empire. Britain’s debt and the cost of defending the interior became central political problems after 1763.[^ch08-pressbooks-debt][^ch08-state-taxation]

### 6.2 What is open

Whether the war led to the Revolution is open. Did the Seven Years’ War set the American Revolution in motion by creating the debt and the imperial reforms, or is that a retrospective story that reads independence back into a war fought for empire? Anderson’s answer is that the war created the conditions of the later crisis without intending or foreseeing it; the fiscal interpretation answers that the debt made reform unavoidable. Both answers remain in play.[^ch08-anderson-past][^ch08-state-taxation]

How much agency the Native nations had is open. Were they junior allies of European empires, or independent powers whose decisions shaped the war and the peace? The middle-ground and Indigenous-agency interpretations answer that they were independent, and the question is how far the evidence supports the strongest form of that claim.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-white-middle-ground]

What the interior was like after 1763 is open. Did the Proclamation protect Native lands, or did it mainly transfer the right to buy them to the crown? The document supports both readings.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]

The scale of the fighting is open. Troop strengths, casualty figures, and the number of forts destroyed vary across sources, and the numbers must be given as estimates with attributed sources.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-check-dating]

What the archives cannot show is part of the problem. Native diplomacy and warfare reach us mainly through European reports and interpreters; Pontiac’s own words survive in translation, and the nations’ decisions are visible chiefly through their effects. Naming those mediations is not a reason to ignore Indigenous agency.[^ch08-check-names][^ch08-pontiac-battlefields]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which empires, colonists, and Native nations all made decisions inside structures none of them controlled, and in which the outcome was contingent at every stage. Fred Anderson’s *Crucible of War* (2000) treats the war as a decisive event in its own right and insists that historians who look ahead to the Revolution lose the contingency of the moment. The war created the conditions of the later crisis without intending or foreseeing it.[^ch08-anderson-past]

Richard White’s *The Middle Ground* (1991) and Colin Calloway’s *The Scratch of a Pen* (2006) read the war and the 1763 settlement from Native ground, showing that the nations were neither passive nor simply defeated. White’s model explains how a small French colony held a large interior by accommodation as well as force; Calloway’s study of 1763 shows how the peace set a cascade of consequences in motion. Both arguments have limits: the middle ground is bounded to a region and period and can understate violence and coercion, and a focus on 1763 can make the year carry more causal weight than any single year can bear.[^ch08-white-middle-ground][^ch08-scratch-calloway][^ch08-calloway-review]

Read together, the positions say that British victory was real but incomplete: it changed who claimed the interior without settling who would control it. The imperial and Indigenous accounts belong together rather than in a contest for a single cause. The best history refuses both the story of an inevitable British triumph and the story of the war as a mere prelude to independence, and it refuses to let the empires stand in for the nations whose homelands they claimed.[^ch08-anderson-past][^ch08-white-middle-ground]

## Legacies

The war left an empire that was larger and more expensive than the one that began it. Britain’s national debt nearly doubled, from about £75 million in 1756 to about £133 million in 1763, and interest payments consumed much of the national budget; the cost of defending the new interior and the former French posts added a continuing drain. When Parliament turned to the colonies for revenue, first through stricter enforcement and then through the Stamp Act, it was acting on the war’s bill. That is the ground on which Chapter 9 begins.[^ch08-pressbooks-debt][^ch08-state-taxation][^ch08-check-ch9]

The Proclamation of 1763 left a double inheritance. It recognized Native possession of the interior and reserved the crown’s exclusive right to buy it, a framework that Native nations and later treaty law would cite; and it failed to stop settlers from crossing the Appalachians, so that the line became a grievance rather than a boundary. In the Great Lakes and Ohio Country, the middle ground that had sustained French power eroded after 1763, and the pressure that Pontiac’s War had answered only grew.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line][^ch08-nps-middle-ground]

The war also became a national memory. Generations of Americans learned it as the French and Indian War, a story of Braddock’s defeat, Wolfe’s victory, and a young Washington’s first fame, told as a prelude to independence. That memory is not the whole history, since the same war was for Native nations a war for their homelands and for France the end of a mainland empire. The victors’ story and the nations’ story are not the same story, and both remain in view.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-calloway-review][^ch08-pontiac-wiki]

## Optional threads

- **The global frame.** Pitt’s subsidies to Prussia, the naval war, the capture of Havana and the Caribbean islands, and the fighting in India all placed the American war inside a world conflict whose peace was negotiated far from the Ohio Country. The 1763 settlement was a global bargain.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- **Archives and silences.** Washington’s journal, Dinwiddie’s letters, and British officers’ reports preserve the imperial side in detail; the Native nations and their diplomats reach us mainly through European interpreters and after-the-fact accounts, and Pontiac’s words survive in translation. Naming those mediations is not a reason to ignore Indigenous agency.[^ch08-wash-journal-mdgazette][^ch08-pontiac-battlefields][^ch08-check-names]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 6 carries the imperial wars to 1713 and the Peace of Utrecht; Chapter 7 describes the provincial society that produced the war; Chapter 9 owns the fiscal crisis the war created. Later chapters will carry the Proclamation Line into the Revolution and the erosion of the middle ground into removal.[^ch08-check-ch7][^ch08-check-ch9][^ch08-check-scope]

## Summary

- New France was a small settler colony that governed a vast interior through the fur trade, alliance, and the accommodations historians call the middle ground.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance]
- The British colonies outnumbered New France by roughly twenty to one in the 1750s, yet France held the interior for decades, so numbers alone did not decide the contest.[^ch08-pop-hs][^ch08-canadianencyc-newfrance]
- Virginia’s claim to the Ohio Country was pressed by the Ohio Company, whose investors included Governor Dinwiddie and George Washington’s family.[^ch08-ohio-company-wiki]
- George Washington’s 1753 mission carried Dinwiddie’s demand to Fort Le Boeuf, where Saint-Pierre refused to withdraw; the journal Washington wrote was printed and widely read.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]
- The war’s first bloodshed in North America came at Jumonville Glen on May 28, 1754, and Washington surrendered at Fort Necessity on July 3, 1754.[^ch08-nps-jumonville][^ch08-mountvernon-fortnecessity]
- The Albany Congress of 1754 adopted Franklin’s plan for a common government, and both the colonies and the crown rejected it.[^ch08-state-albany][^ch08-albany-avalon]
- Braddock’s army was nearly destroyed at the Monongahela on July 9, 1755, and the war widened into the global Seven Years’ War.[^ch08-monongahela-mountvernon][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- French forces won early victories, including the siege of Fort William Henry and the attack on the departing column in August 1757.[^ch08-fwh-wiki]
- William Pitt’s strategy of subsidies abroad and large forces in North America turned the war; Quebec fell in 1759 and Montreal in 1760.[^ch08-pitt-wiki][^ch08-plains-wiki]
- The Treaty of Paris of February 10, 1763, gave Britain Canada and the interior east of the Mississippi, while Spain ceded Florida and acquired Louisiana through the separate Treaty of Fontainebleau.[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-state-paris]
- Pontiac’s War and the Paxton Boys’ massacre of the Conestoga in 1763 showed that the peace did not end conflict in the interior.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-zinn-conestoga]
- The Royal Proclamation of 1763 reserved the interior for Native nations under British sovereignty and forbade settlement beyond the Appalachians, satisfying almost no one.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]

> *A closing note.* The years from 1713 to 1763 made a British empire in North America and left it unstable. A young Virginian carried a letter into the Ohio Country and returned with a printed journal; a skirmish at Jumonville Glen became a world war; the fall of Quebec and Montreal ended French power in Canada; and the Peace of Paris gave Britain a continent it could not yet govern. Pontiac’s War and the Royal Proclamation of 1763 showed that the interior belonged to nations that had never been conquered, and the war’s debt promised that the colonists who had helped win it would soon be asked to pay. The story begins with a mission and ends with an empire larger, deeper in debt, and less settled than the victory suggested.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-pressbooks-debt][^ch08-pontiac-wiki]
