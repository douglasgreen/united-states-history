## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On the night of August 4, 1964, two United States destroyers, the USS *Maddox* and the USS *C. Turner Joy*, reported being attacked by North Vietnamese torpedo boats in the Gulf of Tonkin, in international waters off North Vietnam. Two nights earlier, on August 2, North Vietnamese torpedo boats had attacked the *Maddox*, which was conducting an electronic-eavesdropping patrol (a DESOTO patrol) in support of South Vietnamese commando raids on the North.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- **The doubts within the report.** The *Maddox*’s captain, John J. Herrick, first reported a torpedo attack and then cabled that “freak weather effects on radar and overeager sonarmen may have accounted for many reports.” Secretary of Defense Robert S. McNamara did not pass the captain’s doubts to President Johnson.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]
- **The response.** Johnson ordered retaliatory air strikes on North Vietnamese targets and addressed the nation on television on the evening of August 4, characterizing the attacks as unprovoked aggression. He asked Congress for a resolution of support.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- **The resolution.** Congress passed the joint resolution (H.J. Res. 1145), the “Southeast Asia Resolution,” on August 7, 1964; Johnson signed it as Public Law 88-408 on August 10, 1964 (78 Stat. 384). The Senate vote was 98 to 2, with Wayne Morse of Oregon and Ernest Gruening of Alaska dissenting; the House vote was unanimous. It authorized the president “to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression,” and to assist any SEATO member or protocol state requesting help. It became the legal basis for the Johnson and Nixon administrations’ prosecution of the war and was repealed in January 1971.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]
- **What the later investigation found.** In 2005 and 2006 the National Security Agency released more than 140 formerly classified documents on the incident. The release included the agency historian Robert J. Hanyok’s study, which concluded that the August 2 attack occurred but that the August 4 attack did not, and that agency reporting had been “skewed” toward the administration’s claim. Hanyok wrote that “[t]he overwhelming body of reports, if used, would have told the story that no attack occurred.”[^ch41-nsa-tonkin][^ch41-hanyok]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the reports, the doubts, the strikes, the resolution, the votes, the text, and the later signals-intelligence finding. It cannot show what Johnson privately believed that night, what McNamara intended in withholding the captain’s cable, or that the resolution alone caused the escalation that followed. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch41-check-scene][^ch41-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** On August 4, 1964, no one in the room knew that the August 4 attack had not happened, that the war would reach 536,000 American troops by 1968, or that the resolution would be repealed. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch41-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A report opens a question.** The scene’s claim is not that a single false report caused the war but that a contested report became a mandate, and that the gap between the report and the evidence was known to some of the men who acted on it.[^ch41-hanyok][^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]
- **The stakes.** The resolution transferred to the president a broad authority that Congress believed it was granting narrowly; the movement from a naval incident to a near-declaration of war took three days.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: why did a strategy of containment produce an escalating war it could not resolve?[^ch41-check-scope]
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the Gulf of Tonkin back to Iran and Guatemala, forward to Geneva and the client state, and on to the escalation, the draft, and 1968.[^ch41-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented dialogue or interior thought.** The reports, the captain’s cable, the strikes, and the votes are documented. The scene may not stage a fabricated exchange on the bridge of the *Maddox*, an unrecorded remark by Johnson in the Oval Office, or a private thought attributed to McNamara.[^ch41-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the 2005 NSA finding, the 1971 repeal, or the war’s outcome, and it must not present the August 2 attack as if it were the same event as the disputed August 4 report.[^ch41-check-scene][^ch41-hanyok]
- **No single-hero or single-villain framing.** The scene must not reduce the crisis to McNamara alone, nor treat the two attacks as one event; the record distinguishes the August 2 attack from the August 4 report.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-check-silences]

## 1. The “cheap” Cold War: Iran, Guatemala, Suez, Cuba, and the Congo (1953–1962)

### 1.1 The claim: containment practiced on the cheap

- **The claim.** Faced with a Soviet nuclear arsenal it would not risk a general war to match, the Eisenhower and Kennedy administrations pursued containment through covert action, covertly armed clients, and regional crises rather than direct great-power war; these interventions were “cheap” in American lives and dollars and expensive in the sovereignty of the countries they touched, and they set the pattern that Vietnam would follow and finally break.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive][^ch41-guatemala-frus][^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1953 Iran coup through the 1954 Guatemala operation, the 1956 Suez crisis, the Cuban crises of 1961–62, and the Congo from 1960 to 1965. Each case is a bounded episode; together they show the instruments — covert action, surrogate armies, economic pressure, and the threat of force — that the United States carried into Indochina.[^ch41-suez-milestone][^ch41-congo-milestone]

### 1.2 Iran, 1953

- **The operation.** In August 1953, the United States and Britain backed the overthrow of Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddeq of Iran, whose government had nationalized the British-owned oil industry. The covert operation was code-named TPAJAX (known as Operation Ajax). The CIA’s internal history, *The Battle for Iran*, written in the mid-1970s, states that the agency planned and helped carry out the coup; the agency’s first formal public acknowledgment of its role came with the release of documents in 2013.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive]
- **The evidentiary caution.** The National Security Archive notes that fundamental questions — who hatched the plot, who carried it out, who supported it inside Iran, and how it succeeded — remain in dispute, and that most CIA records on the coup were reportedly lost or destroyed in the early 1960s. The chapter should present the coup as a joint Anglo-American operation with significant Iranian participation, not as an American act alone.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive]

### 1.3 Guatemala, 1954

- **The operation.** In June 1954, a CIA-organized operation code-named PBSUCCESS overthrew the elected government of President Jacobo Árbenz of Guatemala, whose land-reform program had threatened the holdings of the United Fruit Company. The operation combined psychological warfare, a small exile force, and the threat of American military force.[^ch41-guatemala-frus]
- **The institutional record.** The State Department’s documentary history records that the plan of operations called for cutting off military aid to Guatemala, increasing aid to its neighbors, and exerting diplomatic and economic pressure. The retrospective FRUS volume on Guatemala and the CIA’s own declassified account are the principal published records; the chapter should distinguish what the documents show about the operation from what they leave in dispute.[^ch41-guatemala-frus]

### 1.4 Suez, 1956

- **The crisis.** On July 26, 1956, Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company, the British-French enterprise that had operated the canal since 1869. In October, Israeli forces invaded the Sinai, and Britain and France landed troops under the pretext of protecting the canal. The Eisenhower administration, worried about Soviet intervention and about being associated with European colonialism, pressured its allies into a cease-fire on November 6 and voted for United Nations resolutions condemning the invasion.[^ch41-suez-milestone]
- **The consequence.** The episode ended Britain’s and France’s ability to act independently in the region and prompted the Eisenhower Doctrine, which extended American power in the Middle East. It also showed the United States using pressure on allies rather than force on the adversary, another version of the “cheap” approach.[^ch41-suez-milestone]

### 1.5 Cuba: the Bay of Pigs and the missile crisis, 1961–1962

- **The Bay of Pigs.** In April 1961, President John F. Kennedy carried out a CIA plan inherited from the Eisenhower administration and altered it; about 1,500 anti-Castro Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on April 17 and were quickly defeated by Castro’s forces. The cover story collapsed, and the United States was exposed as the sponsor. The administration continued to press for Castro’s removal through the CIA-backed Operation Mongoose.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller]
- **The missile crisis.** In October 1962, American reconnaissance detected Soviet ballistic missiles and nuclear forces in Cuba, and Kennedy imposed a naval “quarantine,” backing it with the threat of force. After several days at the brink, the Soviets agreed to withdraw the missiles; the United States promised not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to withdraw missiles based in Turkey. The blockade was lifted on November 20, 1962.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller]
- **The tie to Vietnam.** Both crises raised the value of “credibility” in American planning and deepened the belief that a small commitment, if abandoned, would invite larger challenges. That belief would be carried into Southeast Asia.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-debate-works]

### 1.6 The Congo, 1960–1965

- **The crisis.** The former Belgian Congo gained independence on June 30, 1960. Within days the army mutinied, Belgium sent troops, Katanga province seceded, and the United Nations created an intervention force. The United States, fearing Soviet influence, moved to remove Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba. He was arrested in September 1960 and killed on January 17, 1961; the State Department’s account records that plans to assassinate Lumumba were developed, and that Colonel Joseph Mobutu, later backed by American funds and support, took power.[^ch41-congo-milestone]
- **The pattern.** The Congo showed the same repertoire — covert action, client building, and the subordination of a newly independent state’s politics to Cold War categories — that would recur in Vietnam. The chapter should use it to establish the recurring logic, not to suggest that the cases were identical.[^ch41-congo-milestone][^ch41-check-debate-works]

## 2. Geneva and the first Vietnam war (1945–1956)

### 2.1 The claim: a colonial war becomes an American commitment

- **The claim.** The first Vietnam war was a Vietnamese war of independence against France, but by 1954 the United States was paying a large share of its cost and had begun to treat Vietnam as a Cold War test; the Geneva settlement of July 1954 partitioned the country and scheduled elections, and the United States supported the southern regime’s refusal to hold them.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-dienbienphu-frus]
- **The chronology.** The section runs from Hồ Chí Minh’s 1945 declaration of independence from France through the French war, the siege of Dien Bien Phu in 1954, the Geneva Conference, and the cancellation of the 1956 reunification elections.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 2.2 The French war and Dien Bien Phu

- **The war.** After World War II, Hồ Chí Minh declared Vietnamese independence from France in 1945. American leaders, suspicious of his communism, supported French efforts to retain Vietnam as a colony even as the United States pressed France to grant genuine independence. By the early 1950s the United States was financing a large share of the French war effort.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-dienbienphu-frus]
- **The cost share.** A 1954 account in *Proceedings* of the United States Naval Institute describes the American government as bearing “almost three-fourths of the financial cost” of the French war. The exact American share, its base years, and its definition must be established from a documentary source — the FRUS volume on Indochina for 1952–1954 or the Pentagon Papers — before the figure is asserted.[^ch41-dienbienphu-frus][^ch41-check-numbers]
- **The defeat.** The Viet Minh besieged and defeated the French garrison at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, ending French hopes of holding Indochina and opening the Geneva negotiations.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 2.3 The Geneva settlement

- **The settlement.** The 1954 Geneva Accords partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with Viet Minh forces regrouping to the North and the State of Vietnam’s forces to the South, and scheduled elections in 1956 to reunify the country. The State Department’s own history of the period records that American planners immediately considered how the Western signers might “circumvent or avoid the elections.”[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]
- **The American response.** The United States did not accept the settlement as a permanent partition and moved to strengthen a noncommunist state in the South, invoking the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization, created in 1954. The chapter should distinguish the terms the signers signed from the interpretations the Americans placed on them.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

### 2.4 The canceled elections and the SEATO frame

- **The elections.** Elections scheduled for July 1956 to reunite the country were never held; Prime Minister Ngô Đình Diem of the South, with American support, canceled them, arguing that free elections were impossible in the North. The State Department’s history concludes that the communists seemed likely to win, chiefly through organization and rural appeal, and that the United States supported Diem’s refusal.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- **The consequence.** By the late 1950s a communist guerrilla force in the South, the Viet Cong, was fighting to overthrow Diem’s government, with material support from the North; the Second Indochina War had begun.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

## 3. The client state (1954–1963)

### 3.1 The claim: aid built a state its own people did not trust

- **The claim.** American aid and advice created a South Vietnamese state and army that depended on the United States, but Diem’s government never won the loyalty of a majority of southerners; his repression of Buddhists and political opponents and his failure on land reform strengthened the insurgency, and the 1963 coup that removed him left a succession of unstable governments.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-diem-coup-miller]
- **The structure.** The section runs from Diem’s establishment of his government after 1954 through the Buddhist crisis of 1963, the American debate over a coup, and the coup and assassination of November 1–2, 1963.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller]

### 3.2 Diem’s regime and American aid

- **The regime.** Diem was a Catholic leading a predominantly Buddhist country; he governed with a military supplied and trained by the United States and with substantial American economic assistance. The State Department’s account concludes that he failed to capture the loyalties of southerners the way Hồ Chí Minh had in the North, and that his rural policies and ambivalence toward land reform bolstered the Viet Cong.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- **The advisory buildup.** Kennedy inherited and expanded the advisory mission; by the end of 1963 there were about 16,000 American military advisers in South Vietnam. The chapter should attribute the buildup to decisions across two administrations, not to one president.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 3.3 The Buddhist crisis, 1963

- **The crisis.** In the spring of 1963, government forces cracked down on Buddhist monks; on June 11 a monk, Thích Quảng Đức, burned himself to death in a Saigon street in protest. Diem imposed martial law, and special forces directed by his brother, Ngô Đình Nhu, raided Buddhist pagodas.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller]
- **The American dilemma.** Kennedy pressed Diem for reforms while trying to preserve the regime as the best available barrier to communism; advisers divided over whether the United States should acquiesce in or support a coup. The JFK Library’s account and the FRUS documents on the Buddhist crisis are the principal records and must be consulted directly rather than paraphrased from memory.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-check-scene]

### 3.4 The 1963 coup and the death of two presidents

- **The coup.** On November 1, 1963, South Vietnamese army officers led by General Dương Văn Minh overthrew and killed Diem and Nhu. At a meeting on October 29, Kennedy’s advisers had been divided; Secretary of State Dean Rusk urged caution, and Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy expressed doubts about breaking with Diem. The killing occurred with the tacit approval of the Kennedy administration.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- **The consequence.** The coup produced not stability but a succession of governments. Eighteen days later, President Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas, and the war continued under new leadership in both countries. The chapter should not present the coup as a single turning point that determined the escalation, since the record shows neither side settled the war in the eighteen months after it.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

## 4. Tonkin, Americanization, the draft, and the supply war (1964–1967)

### 4.1 The claim: a narrow mandate becomes a land war

- **The claim.** Between August 1964 and the end of 1967 the United States moved from advising and supporting a client to fighting its own ground war, on the strength of a congressional resolution that Johnson used as the functional equivalent of a declaration of war; the strategy of gradual pressure and attrition did not force Hanoi to negotiate, and the draft carried the war into American homes.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-escalation-miller][^ch41-draft-michigan]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the resolution through Operation Rolling Thunder and the first Marine landings in 1965, the July 1965 decision, the attrition strategy and the supply war, and the draft; it closes with the In Focus on the resolution and Map 32.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-map32]

### 4.2 The Tonkin Resolution and the mandate

- **The mandate.** Johnson used the resolution as a functional equivalent to a declaration of war, though critics argued he should have asked for a formal declaration. Congress supported it on the assumption that the president would return before further escalation.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]
- **The plan.** Johnson approved National Security Action Memorandum 273 in November 1963, directing the government to help South Vietnam “win their contest against the externally directed and supported communist conspiracy,” and NSAM 288 in March 1964, calling for a greater use of American force, including planning for air strikes on the North. Operation Plan 34-A, approved in January 1964, shifted stepped-up covert operations against the North from the CIA to the military.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 4.3 Rolling Thunder, the Marines, and the July 1965 decision

- **The bombing.** After Communist attacks on American installations, Johnson authorized a program of sustained bombing of North Vietnam, code-named Operation Rolling Thunder. The Office of the Historian and the Miller Center date the authorization to February 13, 1965, and the start of sustained operations to March 2, 1965; the discrepancy must be reconciled in the text.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]
- **The troops.** On March 8, 1965, two Marine battalions, about 3,500 troops, landed near Da Nang to protect the air base, the first American combat forces sent to mainland Asia since the Korean War. By mid-April the Marines had moved to offensive operations; in June, General William C. Westmoreland requested an additional 44 battalions. By November 1965 there were more than 175,000 American troops; by the end of Johnson’s presidency, 536,000.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]
- **The decision.** In July 1965, Johnson announced that draft inductions would rise from 17,000 to 35,000 per month, and the administration committed to a land war it had said it would avoid; Johnson had campaigned in 1964 on a peace platform.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]
- **The “peace” offer.** On April 7, 1965, at Johns Hopkins University, Johnson coupled the bombing with an offer of economic development for the Mekong region and a willingness to discuss a settlement: “We will not be defeated. We will not grow tired. We will not withdraw either openly or under the cloak of a meaningless agreement.” Hanoi rejected the terms.[^ch41-escalation-miller]

### 4.4 Attrition, the body count, and the supply war: Map 32

- **The strategy.** Westmoreland’s strategy of attrition measured progress in enemy “body counts,” a metric that rewarded reported kills and could not demonstrate that the insurgency was being defeated. The chapter should show what the metric could and could not establish, and should not present a single body-count figure as a settled measure.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-numbers]
- **The bombing’s scale.** Estimates of the tonnage dropped on North Vietnam vary with scope and period; one Navy history gives roughly 864,000 tons of bombs and missiles, while a commercial history gives about 643,000 tons for 1965–68. The chapter should give any figure with its period, target set, and definition.[^ch41-rollingthunder-navy][^ch41-check-numbers]
- **The supply war.** The Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia carried men and matériel south despite the bombing, and the United States expanded operations in Laos and along the border to interdict it. Map 32, “Southeast Asia and supply routes, 1954–1968,” is the registered visual for this section; it must distinguish the borders of North Vietnam, South Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, and label bases and the trail, with the map’s date stated rather than implied.[^ch41-check-map32][^ch41-rollingthunder-navy]
- **The map’s limits.** The map cannot show the volume or the success of infiltration; a boundary line is not a measure of a route’s use. Cite the Pentagon Papers, the FRUS volumes, and military maps, and reconcile with Appendix C’s catalog entry and the production record `repo-map32`.[^ch41-check-map32][^ch41-pentagon-papers-nara]

### 4.5 The draft and the home front

- **The conscription system.** Between 1964 and 1973 the military drafted about 2.2 million men out of an eligible pool of about 27 million; only about 25 percent of the force in combat zones were draftees, but the draft led many others to volunteer to choose their service, and deferments favored the college-educated and the connected.[^ch41-draft-michigan]
- **The inequality.** About 80 percent of enlisted men came from poor or working-class families, and a similar share had only a high-school education; the chapter should give these as the sourced proportions and attribute them to their studies rather than to the whole force.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-appy]
- **The protest link.** The draft brought the war home: it intensified the antiwar movement, and the first campus teach-in was held at the University of Michigan on March 24–25, 1965. The chapter should treat the draft as both a policy and a cause of protest.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-antiwar-mcny]

### 4.6 In Focus: the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution

- **The object.** The In Focus reads one document: the Tonkin Gulf Resolution (H.J. Res. 1145, Public Law 88-408, 78 Stat. 384), passed on August 7, 1964, and signed on August 10.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]
- **The close reading.** The box should read Section 1’s grant of authority (“all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression”) and Section 2’s extension of that authority to SEATO members and protocol states, and note Section 3’s termination clause. The point is the gap between a narrow naval-reprisal resolution and a general authorization that a president could and did use for a land war.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]
- **The distinction the box must make.** It must distinguish the resolution from the August 4 report it rested on, and from the later finding that the August 4 attack did not occur. It must not present the resolution as the war’s cause; it authorized measures that a series of later decisions expanded.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-hanyok]
- **The rights and register record.** The National Archives holds the resolution and the Senate roll-call tally sheet; the chapter lists no image, so the box is a textual close reading unless a reproduction is registered with an exact object locator, caption, source line, and rights record. Do not assume that a host’s public-domain statement covers every object it displays.[^ch41-check-focus][^ch41-check-rights]

## 5. Tet, My Lai, protest, and the 1968 decision (1968)

### 5.1 The claim: a battlefield decision that became a political one

- **The claim.** In 1968 the war’s political foundations cracked. The Tet offensive, though it failed militarily, showed Americans that victory was not near; My Lai showed what the war had done to those fighting it; the antiwar movement grew while public approval fell; and on March 31 Johnson withdrew from the presidential race and limited the bombing. The year did not end the war, but it ended the strategy of escalation.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]
- **The structure.** The section runs from Tet through the post-Tet troop request, My Lai and its cover-up, the growth of protest and the “credibility gap,” and the March 31 speech.[^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]

### 5.2 Tet, January–February 1968

- **The offensive.** In late January 1968, during the lunar new year holiday, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces launched coordinated attacks on towns, cities, and government sites across South Vietnam, including a breach of the outer wall of the United States Embassy in Saigon. A second phase struck smaller cities and towns in May and June, and a third began in August.[^ch41-tet-milestone]
- **The result.** American and South Vietnamese forces retook the territory and inflicted heavy losses on the insurgents, but the offensive showed that the enemy was not as weak as the Johnson administration had claimed and that victory was not imminent. Both sides claimed victory; the political effect in the United States was a sharp fall in confidence.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]
- **The right lesson.** The chapter should avoid the common claim that Tet was purely a military victory and a political defeat to be applauded or deplored; it was both a costly failure for Hanoi and a decisive shift in American politics.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The troop request and the review

- **The request.** After Tet, General Westmoreland requested about 206,000 additional troops beyond the authorized 525,000 level. The request set off a debate within the administration about whether to expand the war or to begin disengaging.[^ch41-westmoreland-request][^ch41-tet-milestone]
- **The review.** Johnson asked advisers, including the new Secretary of Defense, Clark Clifford, to reassess. The review concluded that the troop request could not be met without mobilizing reserves and damaging the economy and the dollar, and it marked the turn toward de-escalation. The chapter should attribute the review’s conclusions to the documentary record rather than to a single adviser.[^ch41-lbj-march31-miller][^ch41-westmoreland-request]

### 5.4 My Lai, March 16, 1968

- **The massacre.** On March 16, 1968, soldiers of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Infantry Brigade, Americal Division, killed several hundred unarmed South Vietnamese civilians in the hamlets of My Lai (Son My) in Quảng Ngãi province. Estimates of the dead range from 347 to 507; a South Vietnamese report in 1968 alleged more than 400.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]
- **The response and cover-up.** The first American investigation by Colonel Oran Henderson concluded that 20 civilians had been killed accidentally; a second, parallel investigation by Lieutenant Colonel Frank Barker, whose own task force was the subject, concurred. The killings became known through the efforts of the soldier Ronald Ridenhour, whose March 1969 letter to officials and Congress triggered an Army inquiry and the Peers inquiry; the episode became public in 1969.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]
- **The evidentiary caution.** The chapter should give the range of estimates with the investigation that produced each, distinguish the Army’s initial account from the later inquiries, and not reduce the massacre to one officer’s actions; the timeline documents command pressure, faulty intelligence, and the conduct of multiple platoons.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]

### 5.5 Protest and the “credibility gap”

- **The movement.** Opposition grew from campus teach-ins in 1965 to mass demonstrations: on April 15, 1967, as many as 400,000 people marched in New York from Central Park to the United Nations, the largest antiwar demonstration in American history to that date; Martin Luther King Jr. delivered “Beyond Vietnam” at Riverside Church on April 4, 1967, denouncing “the giant triplets of racism, extreme materialism, and militarism.” Veterans and active-duty soldiers also protested.[^ch41-antiwar-mcny]
- **The public.** Gallup’s series shows approval of Johnson’s handling of the war falling from 39 percent before Tet to 26 percent after it, approval of his handling of the presidency from 48 to 36 percent, and the share calling the war a mistake rising from 45 to 49 percent; the share identifying as “hawks” fell from 60 to 41 percent. The chapter must cite the actual survey questions, dates, and populations, not a summary. (The README specifically warns that this chapter’s approval figures need direct survey citations, not Appendix B’s election table.)[^ch41-pubopinion-digitalhistory][^ch41-check-polls][^ch41-check-numbers]
- **The “credibility gap.”** Contemporary critics coined the term for the distance between the administration’s optimistic statements and the war’s realities; the gap widened after Tet and shaped the politics of 1968. The chapter should treat the term as a period description and test it against the documented statements rather than adopting it as neutral analysis.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-names]

### 5.6 March 31, 1968

- **The speech.** On March 31, 1968, Johnson announced a unilateral reduction in the bombing of North Vietnam, renewed the offer of talks, and, at the end, withdrew from the presidential race: “I have concluded that I should not permit the Presidency to become involved in the partisan divisions that are developing in this political year.” He said he would not seek, and would not accept, his party’s nomination.[^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]
- **The context.** Six weeks after Tet, the first presidential primary in New Hampshire brought Senator Eugene McCarthy a strong showing against the president, and Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race days later. The chapter should connect the decision to the wartime politics without claiming that any one event caused it.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-debate-works]
- **The unresolved war.** The speech did not end the war. The chapter ends with the war still being fought and the political system in crisis, handing the election and the convention to Chapter 42.[^ch41-check-ch42][^ch41-check-bridge]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The United States escalated by a series of decisions, not one.** The documentary record establishes the shift from advice and support to combat, the bombing campaign, the Marine landings, and the troop buildup to 536,000 by 1968.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]
- **The August 4, 1964, attack did not occur.** The NSA’s declassified signals-intelligence study concluded that the August 4 attack did not take place and that reporting had been skewed toward the administration’s claim.[^ch41-hanyok][^ch41-nsa-tonkin]
- **The resolution became the legal basis for the war and was repealed in 1971.** The text and the votes are documented.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]
- **Tet changed American politics without deciding the war.** The offensive was a costly failure for Hanoi and a decisive blow to American confidence; the war continued for years.[^ch41-tet-milestone]
- **My Lai happened and was investigated.** The massacre and the inquiries are documented, with a range of victim estimates.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]
- **The human cost is documented.** The National Archives’ casualty file records 58,220 American deaths, including 16,899 in 1968 and 47,434 classed as hostile; Vietnamese and broader Indochinese losses are estimates that must be given as such and are not part of this chapter’s 1968 balance sheet.[^ch41-casualties-nara][^ch41-check-numbers]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the escalation avoidable?** Logevall argues the United States had viable alternatives in 1964–65 and chose war; accounts centered on the Cold War consensus argue the constraints were tighter than the choice model allows. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-herring]
- **Was the war winnable?** The revisionist account associated with Mark Moyar argues that the United States could have prevailed, and dates the decisive turn to the 1963 coup against Diem and to American political failure rather than military failure; the orthodox account argues the war was unwinnable at acceptable cost. The chapter should present the revisionist case fairly and test it against the documents.[^ch41-moyar][^ch41-herring]
- **How much of the war was American and how much Vietnamese?** Lien-Hang Nguyen and Pierre Asselin, using Hanoi’s archives, argue that North Vietnamese leaders pursued their own strategy, often differing with their Chinese and Soviet patrons, and that American intervention ended on terms shaped as much in Hanoi as in Washington. This is a corrective to the American-centered account, not a denial of American power.[^ch41-nguyen-hanoi][^ch41-asselin]
- **Was the failure bureaucratic or structural?** H.R. McMaster argues that the Joint Chiefs and civilian leaders failed to debate the war honestly, producing “lies that led to Vietnam”; other accounts locate the failure in the structural belief in containment and credibility that no honest debate could have dislodged. The chapter should separate the two kinds of explanation.[^ch41-mcmaster][^ch41-logevall]
- **Did the media lose the war?** A common charge holds that television and reporting turned the public against a war the military was winning after Tet; the record shows Tet’s political effect and the administration’s own loss of confidence, and the chapter should not treat a contested causal claim as settled.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-check-debate-works]
- **What counts as the war’s measure?** Body counts, pacification indexes, bombing tonnage, and election results each measure different things; the chapter should say what each can and cannot establish rather than choosing the one that supports a verdict.[^ch41-check-numbers][^ch41-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the United States chose war within real constraints — a Cold War consensus that made abandonment politically costly, a client state that could not stand alone, and a bureaucracy that suppressed dissent — and in which Vietnamese actors, not only American ones, made the war what it was.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-nguyen-hanoi][^ch41-mcmaster]
- **Contingency.** The August 1964 resolution, the July 1965 troop decision, the November 1963 coup, and the post-Tet troop request were decisions people made and could have made otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome. The chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-westmoreland-request][^ch41-diem-coup-miller]
- **Structure and choice together.** The synthesis should hold structural conditions — the Cold War, decolonization, the credibility doctrine, and the client state’s weakness — together with the contingent choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation over the other.[^ch41-check-scope][^ch41-herring]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that between 1953 and 1968 the United States extended containment through covert action and client states, chose to escalate in Vietnam rather than accept a negotiated settlement, and discovered that the same strategy could not produce the outcome it sought; the war’s costs fell on Vietnamese peasants and soldiers, on American working-class draftees, and on the American political system that had chosen it.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-appy][^ch41-tet-milestone]

## Legacies

The years from 1953 to 1968 left a pattern of American power that outlasted the war. The covert interventions, client states, and credibility reasoning of the early Cold War became the template that Vietnam tested to destruction; the war then reshaped how Americans judged later uses of force, a caution later called the “Vietnam syndrome,” and produced the 1973 War Powers Resolution, which sought to require congressional approval for sustained hostilities.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive][^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]

The war also left a human aftermath on several continents. Vietnamese refugees, wounded veterans, and families of the dead carried the war into the following decades; the 1982 Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington became a national site of mourning; and the questions of Agent Orange, post-traumatic stress, and the fate of the missing continued long after the shooting stopped. Those developments belong to later chapters and are named here only as consequences, with their dates.[^ch41-casualties-nara][^ch41-check-ch42]

The years left a memory and a dispute. The war became, for its critics, proof that containment had been a trap and that the government had not told the truth, and for its defenders, proof that the United States had been denied the victory it could have won; the chapter should present both verdicts as arguments to be tested against the record rather than as settled conclusions.[^ch41-hanyok][^ch41-moyar]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The war was made in Hanoi, Moscow, Beijing, Paris, and Saigon as well as Washington; the French colonial war, the Soviet and Chinese patrons, and the nonaligned states at Geneva all shaped it, and Chapter 40 treats the parallel Cold War competition over social provision. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch41-nguyen-hanoi][^ch41-check-ch40]

> *A closing note.* Between 1953 and 1968 the United States practiced containment through covert action, client states, and finally open war, and it discovered in Vietnam that the same instruments could not produce the outcome it sought. The war’s costs fell hardest on the Vietnamese countryside and on American working-class draftees, and its political costs fell on the presidents who chose it. The chapter’s question — why did a strategy of containment produce an escalating war it could not resolve? — is answered by the record of a choice that was made within real constraints and that no participant knew how to reverse.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-draft-michigan]

Chapter 42 takes up the election of 1968 and the political realignment, Vietnamization, the secret bombing of Cambodia, the Pentagon Papers, and the long unmooring of public trust. This chapter ends with the war still being fought and the president who escalated it out of the race; the next turns to the politics that the war remade.[^ch41-check-ch42][^ch41-check-bridge][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]
