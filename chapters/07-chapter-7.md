## Epigraphs

> “The God that holds you over the Pit of Hell, much as one holds a Spider, or some loathsome Insect, over the Fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked…”[^ch07-edwards-sinners]

> “It is a Principle among Printers that when Truth has fair Play, it will always prevail over Falsehood.”[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

> “What historians call ‘the first Great Awakening’ can best be described as a revitalization of religious piety that swept through the American colonies between the 1730s and the 1770s.”[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

- **Why these three.** Edwards speaks the language of an angry God and a sinner suspended over the fire. Franklin answers with the printer’s confidence that open argument will defeat falsehood, a very different theory of how minds are changed. Heyrman steps back to describe the revival as a movement historians have named and dated. The three disagree about fear, persuasion, and whether the awakenings were a single event or a category later imposed on scattered revivals.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Edwards’s wording is taken from the 1741 Boston printing of the sermon; the printer’s mark and the text must be collated against the consulted edition before it is set in type. Franklin’s sentence is quoted from a National Humanities Center selection that modernizes spelling and punctuation, so the original *Pennsylvania Gazette* column must be checked. Heyrman’s line is quoted from her essay in the National Humanities Center’s *Divining America* series.[^ch07-edwards-sinners][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc][^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-check-epigraphs]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that Edwards’s hearers were terrified into conversion, that colonial printers were free from commercial and political pressure, or that the “Great Awakening” was one coordinated movement. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and the date.** On July 8, 1741, Jonathan Edwards, minister of Northampton, preached a sermon at Enfield that would be printed later that year in Boston as “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God.” Enfield then lay within Massachusetts; Connecticut annexed the town in 1749, so a modern reader must not place the scene in present-day Connecticut.[^ch07-edwards-sinners][^ch07-enfield-wiki][^ch07-sinners-wiki]
- **The text and its text.** The printed sermon takes its text from Deuteronomy 32:35, “Their Foot shall slide in due Time,” and argues that nothing but God’s pleasure keeps the unconverted out of hell. The most concrete image is that of a spider or “some loathsome Insect” held over the fire.[^ch07-edwards-sinners]
- **The sermon’s place in a series.** Edwards had preached a version to his own congregation in Northampton before the Enfield occasion, and the awakening was already under way in the Connecticut valley when he preached in 1741. The chapter should present Enfield as one documented occasion inside a wider revival, not as the movement’s origin.[^ch07-sinners-wiki][^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The printed artifact.** The sermon survives because it was printed and reprinted, not because a transcript of the spoken delivery survives. What can be read closely is the printed text and its audience among readers, which is exactly the print culture §4 will develop.[^ch07-edwards-sinners]

### The scene’s argument

- **A documented hour, not a tableau.** The scene should open at Enfield on July 8, 1741, move through the sermon’s most concrete figures, and rest on the printed page that carried the words to readers who never heard them. Every action and image named should be one the record supports.
- **The caution about the delivery.** The familiar story that the congregation groaned and cried out is reported in later accounts; unless a contemporary source is found and quoted, the scene must not narrate the crowd’s reaction as established fact. Describe what the text says and what the printing did.[^ch07-check-scene]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: What connected these increasingly different colonies?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that religion unified the colonies or that the colonies were one society. Credit, migration, coerced labor, print, and household economies bound colonies that were growing further apart, and the revival both crossed and deepened their divisions.
- **The widening.** The scene sits at Enfield in 1741. The framing paragraph follows immediately and sends the reader to the rice lowcountry, the tobacco Chesapeake, the ports, and the trials at Salem, all inside one interdependent but unequal world.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-stono-wiki]

### What must not be invented

- No invented weather, meetinghouse detail, dialogue, or thoughts, and no reenacted conversion. The text and its printing are the evidence.[^ch07-check-scene]
- No claim about the size or behavior of the Enfield congregation unless a contemporary source supports it.
- No claim that Edwards’s sermon alone caused conversions or that it was unusual for its violence; its imagery belonged to a wider revival idiom.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- No claim that the awakenings created a single American religious identity; Heyrman stresses both their transatlantic reach and the “sharp” religious polarization they left behind.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

## 1. Staples, trade, and credit

### 1.1 The staple economies and their regions

- **The claim.** The colonies grew more different from one another because they grew different crops for an Atlantic market. Tobacco shaped the Chesapeake, rice and indigo the Carolina lowcountry, and provisions, grain, and the carrying trade the middle colonies and New England.
- **Tobacco.** Chesapeake planters sold tobacco into a consignment system; by about 1730 a large share of the crop was shipped through English factors and commission agents rather than sold locally, which left planters dependent on distant merchants and prices they could not control.[^ch07-tobacco-encyclopediava]
- **Rice and the lowcountry.** The Carolina rice economy rested on the knowledge and labor of enslaved Africans and on a task system, and it made the lowcountry a slave-majority region distinct from the Chesapeake. This section should state the regional contrast and leave Stono to §3.
- **Provisions and the carrying trade.** The middle colonies and New England fed plantations and islands, built and sailed ships, and sold flour, meat, and ships’ stores, so the very diversity of staples created a division of labor among colonies.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution]
- **The consequence.** No single colonial economy existed, but the staples, shipping, and credit tied the regions together through a shared imperial market.[^ch07-check-credit]

### 1.2 The Navigation system and the empire of trade

- **The claim.** The Navigation Acts did not simply impoverish the colonies, but they did channel colonial trade to serve the empire’s shipping and revenue. The argument should neither adopt “beneficial neglect” as fact nor treat the acts as a total system of control.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution]
- **What the acts did.** They reserved colonial trade to English and colonial vessels and required enumerated goods — including tobacco and rice — to be shipped first to England before re-export. They made the colonies a market and a supply area for the imperial economy.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution]
- **How it worked in practice.** Enforcement was uneven, smuggling was common, and the acts were only one influence on colonial prosperity. Historians disagree about the balance of cost and benefit, and the section should present the dispute rather than resolve it by assertion.[^ch07-check-credit]
- **The consequence.** By 1750 the colonies were integrated into an Atlantic economy that enriched some colonists, indebted others, and depended throughout on enslaved labor.

### 1.3 Credit, money, and the chain of debt

- **The claim.** Colonial growth ran on credit, and credit tied planters, shopkeepers, merchants, and the empire into a chain in which a distant failure could ruin a farm.
- **The mechanism.** Planters bought on credit against the next crop and often fell into debt to English merchants; farmers and artisans borrowed from storekeepers; and colonial governments issued paper money to lubricate exchange.[^ch07-clevelandfed-currency]
- **Paper currency.** Massachusetts issued the first colonial paper money in 1690, and the colonies experimented with land banks and bills of credit. Parliament later regulated colonial paper money through the Currency Acts of 1751 and 1764, a restraint that would become politically charged after the war.[^ch07-currency-wiki][^ch07-clevelandfed-currency]
- **The household at the end of the chain.** The credit system reached into ordinary households through debts, wage labor, and the accounts kept for servants and the enslaved, which §5 takes up. A runaway or a fire could erase a family’s solvency, as the *Pennsylvania Gazette*’s columns of loss and debt suggest.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **The consequence.** Credit made the colonies interdependent while concentrating risk at the bottom, and it made the merchant’s ledger one of the era’s most important documents.[^ch07-check-credit]

## 2. Population and migration

### 2.1 Growth by numbers and the limits of the numbers

- **The claim.** The colonial population grew rapidly between 1700 and 1750, through both natural increase and immigration, and it grew more African. The chapter must state estimates as estimates and define the geographic unit.[^ch07-check-population]
- **The series.** The standard estimates from *Historical Statistics of the United States* put the total population of the future thirteen colonies at about 250,888 in 1700, 466,185 in 1720, 629,445 in 1730, 905,563 in 1740, and 1,170,760 in 1750. These combine white and Black populations and rest on colonial counts and estimates rather than a modern census.[^ch07-population-hs]
- **The caution.** The README directs that population totals remain research questions until they are geographically defined and sourced. State the series, its compiler, and its limits, and do not present a single figure as exact.[^ch07-check-population][^ch07-population-hs]

### 2.2 Natural increase and immigration

- **The claim.** Natural increase and immigration together drove growth, and the sources of immigration became less English and more Scots-Irish, German, and African.
- **The migration streams.** Scots-Irish Presbyterians from Ulster began arriving in large numbers after 1718, and German-speaking migrants from the Rhineland and neighboring lands entered chiefly through Philadelphia. About 7,500 Scots-Irish and Irish migrants reached Pennsylvania before 1740, with many more in the following decades. Philadelphia grew into the largest colonial city, its population drawn from England, Ireland, Germany, Africa, and the Caribbean.[^ch07-phila-immigration]
- **The enslaved stream.** The forced migration of enslaved Africans continued and grew; the lowcountry and the Chesapeake received the largest numbers, and their presence reshaped the populations of those regions. This section should state the direction and scale of the traffic and leave its law and its resistance to §3.[^ch07-check-population]
- **The consequence.** Migration made the colonies more plural and less British, and it made “the people” of any colony harder to describe in a single phrase.[^ch07-phila-immigration][^ch07-check-names]

### 2.3 Mobility, land, and the frontier of settlement

- **The claim.** Population growth pushed settlement westward into Indigenous homelands, and land hunger became a force that Chapter 8 will carry into the Ohio Country.
- **The pattern.** Settlers moved from the older seaboard into the piedmont and the backcountry, where they pressed against Native nations and the claims of colonial governments. The movement is a cause of conflict, not simply a backdrop.[^ch07-check-ch8]
- **The consequence.** By 1750 the colonies were not only larger and more plural but pressed harder against Indigenous territory, setting up the imperial war that the next chapter owns.[^ch07-check-ch8]

## 3. Continental slavery and Stono

### 3.1 The plantation orders of the mainland

- **The claim.** By 1750 slavery was continental and the enslaved population was growing, deepening the racial order Chapter 5 had written into law. The chapter treats Native and African slavery as one developing labor regime without collapsing their distinct histories.
- **The regions.** The Chesapeake and the lowcountry held the largest enslaved populations; the middle colonies and New England held smaller numbers in households, workshops, and ports. The institutions differed, but the law of property in persons reached all of them.
- **The daily reality.** Enslaved people worked in tobacco and rice fields, as domestic servants, and as skilled artisans, and they resisted through flight, work slowdowns, and open revolt. This chapter does not repeat Chapter 5’s statutory analysis but shows the regime in operation.[^ch07-stono-wiki]

### 3.2 The Stono rising of 1739

- **The claim.** On Sunday, September 9, 1739, a group of enslaved people rose near the Stono River in St. Paul’s Parish, South Carolina, in the largest slave rebellion of the colonial South. It failed, but it terrified white colonists across British America.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-stono-nhc]
- **The course.** About twenty enslaved people gathered near the Stono River, broke into a store that sold firearms, killed the shopkeepers, and marched south toward Spanish Florida, recruiting others along the way. A white official’s account and a later family account of Cato disagree in detail, and the numbers vary among sources; the chapter must present about fifty as an estimate, not a count.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-check-dating]
- **The inspiration.** The marchers were reported to be Kongolese, some Catholic and some Portuguese-speaking, and several proclamations from St. Augustine had promised freedom to fugitives from British colonies, giving the rising a destination as well as a grievance. Spain’s offer and the war between Britain and Spain helped shape the timing.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-stono-pbs]
- **The suppression.** The militia caught the main body near the Edisto River; more were killed or captured, and several of the leaders’ heads were displayed on posts. The South Carolina assembly had already passed a Security Act that spring, and the rising pushed the colony toward harsher law.[^ch07-stono-pbs][^ch07-stono-nhc]
- **The consequence.** Stono showed that enslaved people would choose freedom and that the colony would answer with law. It is the hinge from the labor regime to the code of 1740.[^ch07-stono-wiki]

### 3.3 The Negro Act of 1740 and the racial order

- **The claim.** South Carolina’s law of 1740, usually called the Negro Act, codified the racial order by restricting movement, assembly, literacy, and manumission. It reproduced the word “Negro” in the statute’s own title and text; the chapter should reproduce the term only in the law’s name and in quotation, and explain that it is historical.
- **Its provisions.** The act barred enslaved people from learning to read and write, from assembling in groups, from growing their own food, and from earning money, and it made it harder for enslavers to free the people they held. It placed all “negroes, Indians, mulattoes and mustizoes” on the footing of property.[^ch07-negro-act-zinn]
- **The pattern.** The law answered a specific rising by generalizing suspicion, and it linked Blackness to bondage in statute across the region. It is the mainland counterpart to the codes Chapter 6 traced to 1713.[^ch07-negro-act-zinn]
- **The consequence.** Stono and the act of 1740 together show the two faces of the era’s slave society: the refusal to accept bondage and the determination to make bondage permanent in law.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-negro-act-zinn]

## 4. Revivals and print

### 4.1 The awakenings and the Atlantic revival

- **The claim.** In the 1730s and 1740s a wave of evangelical revival moved through Britain and its colonies, cutting across denominations and dividing them. Heyrman describes it as a “revitalization of religious piety” that ran from the 1730s to the 1770s, part of a much wider Atlantic movement.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The beginnings.** The earliest revivals appeared among Presbyterians in the middle colonies, led by the Tennent family and their “Log College,” and then spread to the Congregationalists and Baptists of New England. Once revival began, preachers like Edwards used vivid images of hell to move hearers.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The theology and style.** The revival stressed the “new birth” and conversion of the heart rather than the head, and it used emotional, often extemporary preaching. This was a real shift in religious practice, and the modern writer must not reduce it to mere spectacle.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The consequence.** The awakenings created a common evangelical identity while splitting congregations and denominations into those who welcomed them and those who did not.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-fga-wiki]

### 4.2 Whitefield, itinerancy, and the making of a public

- **The claim.** George Whitefield turned revival into a public event that drew thousands and made him, in Heyrman’s phrase, the public figure whose name was known to virtually all colonials. His travel and the press that followed him helped create an intercolonial audience.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The crowds.** Whitefield landed at Lewes Town in November 1739 and reached Philadelphia on November 8; on November 15, 1739, the *Pennsylvania Gazette* reported that he had preached from the Court-House gallery to “near 6000 People” standing in the street.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **The opposition.** Moderate and conservative clergy objected to the emotionalism and especially to “itinerants” who criticized local ministers, and some went further, objecting when “some white women and African Americans shed their subordinate social status long enough to exhort religious gatherings.” The line is worth quoting because it names the social boundary that revival crossed.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The press and the man.** Franklin published Whitefield’s sermons and journals and advertised them in the *Gazette*, so the revival and the print market grew together. Whitefield also took up land on the forks of the Delaware for a “Negro School,” a scheme the *Gazette* carried the same month as his farewell sermon, showing how revival, philanthropy, print, and slavery met in one paper.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **The consequence.** The awakenings created new audiences and new divisions at once, and the links they forged ran through the press as much as the pulpit.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

### 4.3 In Focus: one issue of the *Pennsylvania Gazette*

**In Focus.** On November 15, 1739, Benjamin Franklin’s *Pennsylvania Gazette* reported, in its own voice, that the Rev. Mr. Whitefield had begun to preach from the Court-House gallery in Philadelphia “about six at Night, to near 6000 People before him in the Street, who stood in an awful Silence to hear him.” The same paper carried shipping intelligence, commercial advertisements, and subscription notices, and the issues around it advertised enslaved people for sale, reported the death of an enslaved child, and warned creditors against trusting a runaway wife. Read closely, one issue of the *Gazette* is not merely a record of the revival; it is a market object in which religion, trade, and bondage sit in adjacent columns. Franklin’s press sold sermons and slave sales as the same kind of news. This is the chapter’s best single document for showing that print connected the colonies — and connected them through commerce, not despite it. The chapter should name the issue, confirm its number and page against the digitized run, and read the columns together rather than selecting only the edifying item. This box is registered as the chapter’s one In Focus close reading, and its object and page remain a research check.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc][^ch07-check-focus]

## 5. Households, coverture, and Salem

### 5.1 The household as an economic and legal unit

- **The claim.** The household, not the individual, was the basic unit of production, labor, and authority in the colonial order. Farms, shops, and plantations ran on family labor, servants, and enslaved people under the legal headship of the householder.
- **The work inside.** Women’s labor — food, textiles, dairy, nursing, and the management of servants and the enslaved — was essential to the household economy even when it was not counted in wages. Midwives and healers held knowledge and authority that the law did not register.
- **The dependence.** Servants and enslaved people lived inside the household and under the same authority, which tied the family to the labor regime of §3. The section should show that the household was an institution of power as well as affection.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

### 5.2 Coverture and the law of women

- **The claim.** English common law made a married woman a *feme covert*, her legal existence merged into her husband’s, while an unmarried woman remained a *feme sole* who could own property and make contracts. Coverture structured what women could own, sue for, and keep.
- **The doctrine.** Under coverture a married woman generally could not hold property, sign contracts, or keep wages in her own name, and the doctrine was inherited by the American colonies as part of English law.[^ch07-coverture-wiki]
- **How it worked in practice.** The rule was unevenly enforced: widows, women who worked in trades, and some married women acted beyond it, and the law itself created exceptions. The chapter should present coverture as a legal framework and a lived negotiation, not as an absolute.[^ch07-coverture-nwhm]
- **The paper trail.** The *Gazette* shows the doctrine in daily operation: in December 1742 a Philadelphia man printed a notice that his wife had “misused her said Husband, and doth run him in debt unnecessarily,” warning all persons against trusting her, and then published a retraction a week later. Read carefully, the exchange is a slaveholder-adjacent household quarrel conducted in public print.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **The consequence.** Coverture made marriage a transfer of legal capacity, and it gave the household its legal shape. Its reform would wait for the nineteenth-century married women’s property acts.[^ch07-coverture-wiki]

### 5.3 Salem, 1692

- **The claim.** The Salem witch trials of 1692 grew from local conflict and religious fear and ended with the execution of accused people on the testimony of the afflicted. Salem is the chapter’s case for social strain inside a godly community.
- **The outbreak.** In late February 1692, the daughter and niece of the minister Samuel Parris fell into fits and were diagnosed as bewitched, touching off accusations that spread through Salem Village and its neighbors.[^ch07-tituba-uva]
- **Tituba.** Tituba, an enslaved woman in the Parris household, was one of the first three accused and the only one of the three to survive the year. Her confession helped legitimize the accusations, but the popular picture of her as the “Black Witch of Salem” rests on tradition and fiction rather than on the court record, and the chapter should say so.[^ch07-tituba-uva]
- **The toll.** Nineteen people were hanged, and Giles Corey was pressed to death with stones for refusing to enter a plea; others died in custody, and roughly 200 were accused. Verify the count and names before stating them.[^ch07-check-salem]
- **The social explanation.** Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum’s *Salem Possessed* (1974) reads the trials out of a factional and economic conflict between a village faction tied to agriculture and the Putnam family and a faction tied to the mercantile town and the Porter family. The book is a model of local record work, but its focus on communal conflict can lose the religious, gendered, and individual forces that also mattered.[^ch07-salem-possessed-review]
- **The consequence.** Salem shows how a community under stress can turn fear into law; it belongs in this chapter as a case of social strain, not as an oddity apart from colonial society.[^ch07-salem-possessed-review][^ch07-tituba-uva]

## 6. Social strain

### 6.1 Poverty and the laboring sort

- **The claim.** Colonial growth produced inequality and poverty, especially in the port cities, and the poor were a visible part of the era’s society rather than an exception to it.
- **The evidence.** Wage labor, seafaring, and the casual poor of Philadelphia, New York, and Boston appear in poor-relief records, almshouse accounts, and the criminal courts. The chapter should name the sources and not infer a modern class politics from them.[^ch07-check-strain]
- **The interpretation.** Gary B. Nash’s *The Urban Crucible* (1979) argues that the seaport towns became more stratified over the eighteenth century and that the laboring classes developed a political consciousness before the Revolution. This is an argument to present, not a settled fact.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]
- **The consequence.** Inequality was one of the forces that made the colonial order, and it complicates any claim that the colonies were a broadly egalitarian society.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

### 6.2 The New York conspiracy of 1741

- **The claim.** In 1741 a series of fires in Manhattan touched off a panic that courts turned into the largest execution of enslaved and poor people in the northern colonies, on evidence that many historians now doubt. It belongs beside Stono as a case of resistance, fear, and law.
- **The events.** Thirteen fires burned in lower Manhattan in the spring of 1741, including one inside Fort George; about 200 people were arrested, and a court under Daniel Horsmanden tried them for a plot linking enslaved people with poor Whites and, later, with Catholic Spain.[^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]
- **The evidence.** Much of the case rested on the testimony of Mary Burton, a sixteen-year-old Irish indentured servant, who named people only under pressure. Thirty to forty enslaved people were hanged or burned, and four Whites were hanged, including John Ury and John Hughson. Historians disagree about whether any plot existed.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]
- **The record.** Horsmanden published his *Journal of the Proceedings* in 1744; it is both the principal source and a prosecution brief, and the chapter should read it as such.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder]
- **The consequence.** The panic of 1741 shows that the fear of insurrection, not only insurrection itself, shaped colonial law and society, and it links the northern ports to the slave societies of the South.[^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]

### 6.3 The strain inside the order

- **The claim.** The phrase “deferential society” describes a norm more than a fact. Colonial politics and religion ran on the expectation that ordinary people would follow their betters, but migration, revival, and poverty repeatedly strained that expectation.
- **The counterevidence.** Revival preaching let laypeople and even women and African Americans exhort, which Heyrman notes drew objections precisely because it disturbed “subordinate social status.” Frontier mobility weakened the hold of established churches and gentry. Together these pressures point away from a settled hierarchy.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The caution about Fischer.** Do not attribute a generic “deferential society” thesis to David Hackett Fischer without naming a supporting work; his *Albion’s Seed* (1989) argues regional folkways, not a general theory of deference, and the two should not be conflated.[^ch07-check-strain]
- **The consequence.** The era’s order was real and contested at once, which is the ground the Debate section takes up.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

- **The colonies were interdependent and unequal.** Staple trade, credit, migration, print, and coerced labor bound regions that grew more different, and the benefits were not shared equally.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **Slavery was continental.** Stono (1739) was the largest colonial-era slave rebellion in the South, and the Negro Act of 1740 answered it with harsher law.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-negro-act-zinn]
- **The awakenings happened and they divided people.** A transatlantic evangelical revival swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s and split denominations into New Lights and Old Lights.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-fga-wiki]
- **Salem ended in executions on contested evidence.** Nineteen were hanged and one pressed to death, and the accused included an enslaved woman whose confession helped drive the trials.[^ch07-tituba-uva][^ch07-check-salem]
- **The 1741 New York panic produced mass executions on doubtful proof.** Dozens were executed, and modern historians question whether a plot existed.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]

### 7.2 What is open

- **How much the colonies were deferential.** Was colonial society a hierarchy in which ordinary people deferred to their betters, or an arena of emerging class conflict and popular politics?[^ch07-bushman-king-people][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]
- **What the revivals did politically.** Did the awakenings democratize communication and challenge the elite, or were they a conservative continuation of older religion that historians have over-titled a “Great Awakening”? Christine Leigh Heyrman frames the split between Gary Nash and Rhys Isaac on one side and Heyrman, Christopher Jedrey, and Jon Butler on the other.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **Whether there was a colonial Enlightenment at all.** Does “Enlightenment” name a real movement in the colonies, or does it overstate the reach of a few printers and natural philosophers such as Franklin?[^ch07-check-sources]
- **The scale of the population and the slave trade.** The totals are estimates built from incomplete counts, and the enslaved traffic is reconstructed from shipping and merchant records that undercount it.[^ch07-population-hs][^ch07-check-population]
- **What the archives cannot show.** The poor, the enslaved, and women appear mainly in the records of those who governed, owned, or accused them. Salem reaches us through the interrogators, Stono through a white official and a family account, and the 1741 trials through a prosecutor’s journal.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which ordinary colonists, enslaved people, and Native nations made their own decisions inside structures they did not control. Edwards, Franklin, Whitefield, Tituba, the Stono marchers, and the accused of 1741 were actors, not symbols.
- **Bushman and the deferential order.** Bushman’s *King and People in Provincial Massachusetts* shows that monarchy was a whole political culture of deference, not just a form of government. It explains how authority worked, but it can understate conflict from below.[^ch07-bushman-king-people]
- **Nash and the critics.** Nash’s *Urban Crucible* and Rhys Isaac’s *The Transformation of Virginia* argue that revival and economic change let humbler people challenge their betters. Critics such as Butler, Heyrman, and Jedrey read the revivals as less transformative, and Butler went so far as to call the “Great Awakening” an interpretive fiction.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible][^ch07-nhc-awakening]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, the positions say that colonial society was ordered by hierarchy and credit, strained by migration, revival, and poverty, and contested by the people the order excluded. The chapter should hold those claims together rather than choose a single one.
- **The lesson.** The best history refuses both the story of a harmonious provincial society and the story of an early class revolution, and it refuses to let either the elite or the crowd stand in for everyone.[^ch07-bushman-king-people][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

## Legacies

The provincial order of 1750 became the raw material of the Revolution and the early republic. The credit chains, staple economies, and print networks that bound the colonies also gave them the grievances and the publics that would turn imperial reform into resistance. Franklin’s press and Whitefield’s revivals showed that a shared audience could be mobilized; within a generation such audiences would be mobilized for politics.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc][^ch07-nhc-awakening]

Slavery and its law outlived the empire that made them. Stono and the Negro Act of 1740 hardened the racial order of the lowcountry, and the enslaved population that grew through the eighteenth century would become the central fact of the nineteenth-century republic.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-negro-act-zinn]

Revival and reform left a durable inheritance. Evangelical religion became a permanent feature of American life, and the abolition and rights movements of later centuries would borrow its language and its networks. Coverture, meanwhile, survived the Revolution and was dismantled only by the married women’s property acts of the nineteenth century, a reminder that the era’s settlements changed some social terms and left others intact.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-coverture-wiki]

Salem and New York 1741 entered memory as warnings about fear and law. The trials became a byword for injustice, and the executions of 1741 remained for a long time largely outside the story the nation told about itself. The legacies are double: a society that built a public argument and a revival also built a racial order and a habit of executing the accused.[^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki][^ch07-salem-possessed-review]

## Optional threads

- **The Atlantic frame.** Whitefield’s transatlantic tours, the migration streams from Ulster and the Rhineland, the rice coast’s African knowledge, and the imperial credit system all placed the colonies inside an Atlantic world they did not control. The awakenings were British and German as much as American.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-phila-immigration][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **Archives and silences.** The poor appear in relief rolls, the enslaved in inventories and court minutes, and women in the records that governed them; the accused at Salem and the rebels at Stono reach us through the people who tried and killed them. The chapter should name these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-tituba-uva][^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 5 supplies the statutory making of hereditary slavery; Chapter 6 supplies the wars and the imperial order; Chapter 8 owns the Seven Years’ War and begins with Washington’s 1753 mission. Later chapters carry revival, print, and racial slavery into the Revolution and the republic.[^ch07-check-ch6][^ch07-check-ch8]

## Summary

- The colonies grew more different through staple economies — tobacco, rice, provisions, and shipping — yet were bound together by trade and credit.[^ch07-tobacco-encyclopediava][^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution]
- The Navigation Acts channeled colonial trade to the empire, with disputed costs and benefits, and colonial paper money was later restrained by the Currency Acts of 1751 and 1764.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution][^ch07-currency-wiki]
- The population of the future thirteen colonies rose from about 250,888 in 1700 to about 1,170,760 in 1750, an estimate that combines white and Black populations.[^ch07-population-hs]
- Scots-Irish, German, and African migration made the colonies more plural and less British, and Philadelphia became the largest colonial city.[^ch07-phila-immigration]
- On September 9, 1739, enslaved people rose near the Stono River in South Carolina, the largest colonial-era slave rebellion in the South, and were defeated near the Edisto.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-stono-nhc]
- South Carolina’s Negro Act of 1740 answered Stono with restrictions on literacy, assembly, movement, and manumission.[^ch07-negro-act-zinn]
- A transatlantic evangelical revival swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s, and Whitefield drew thousands when he preached in Philadelphia in November 1739.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- The awakenings divided denominations into New Lights and Old Lights and became a public event carried by the press.[^ch07-fga-wiki][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- The household was the era’s basic unit of labor and authority, and coverture merged a married woman’s legal identity into her husband’s.[^ch07-coverture-wiki]
- The Salem witch trials of 1692 ended with nineteen hanged and Giles Corey pressed to death, on evidence that grew from local conflict and fear.[^ch07-check-salem][^ch07-salem-possessed-review]
- The New York conspiracy of 1741 led to the execution of dozens on testimony many historians doubt.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]
- Historians disagree about whether deference, class conflict, revival, or print best explains colonial society, but they agree that the order was contested and unequal.[^ch07-bushman-king-people][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

### The economy, population, and migration

- **John J. McCusker and Russell R. Menard, *The Economy of British America, 1607–1789* (1985).** The standard synthesis of colonial economic growth, staples, and the Atlantic trade. Consult it for figures and for the debate over the Navigation Acts.

### Households, gender, and Salem

- **Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, *Good Wives* (1982).** A model study of women’s work, marriage, and household economy in northern New England. Use it to give the household section its texture.
- **Marylynn Salmon, *Women and the Law of Property in Early America* (1986).** The standard account of coverture and property law in the colonies and early states. Consult it before stating the legal rules.
- **Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum, *Salem Possessed* (1974).** The most influential social explanation of the witch trials. Pair it with work on gender and religion, which its communal focus can underplay.

### The debate

- **Richard L. Bushman, *King and People in Provincial Massachusetts* (1985).** The central statement of the deferential political culture. Consulted through the publisher’s description; read it before stating its thesis as fact.

- **Jon Butler, “Enthusiasm Described and Decried: The Great Awakening as Interpretive Fiction,” *Journal of American History* 69 (1982–83).** The most sweeping challenge to the “Great Awakening” as an event. Read it before using the term as an unproblematic category.
