# Chapter 46. September 11 and the War on Terror (2001–2008)

> “Every nation, in every region, now has a decision to make. Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists.”[^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]
>
> — George W. Bush, address to a joint session of Congress, September 20, 2001.[^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]

> “Preserving our freedom is one of the main reasons we are now engaged in this new war on terrorism. We will lose that war without firing a shot if we sacrifice the liberties of the American people.”[^ch46-feingold-2001]
>
> — Russ Feingold, statement on the USA PATRIOT Act, United States Senate, October 25, 2001.[^ch46-feingold-2001]

> “His Global War on Terror and his decision to confront Saddam Hussein were inspired by a fear of additional attacks and by the need to defend the United States, but concerns about the long-term exercise of U.S. power were ever-present.”[^ch46-leffler-response-2024]
>
> — Melvyn P. Leffler, response to the *Passport* roundtable on *Confronting Saddam Hussein*, January 2024.[^ch46-leffler-response-2024]

Three voices, three accounts of the same emergency. Bush described a global war and demanded that every nation choose a side. Feingold described a constitutional test and warned that the war could be lost at home by sacrificing the liberties it was fought to defend. Leffler described a decision shaped by fear and by a determination to preserve American power. They disagree about what the emergency was and what it required. Their disagreement frames the chapter’s question: how did emergency reorganize state power and public trust, and by what distinct steps?[^ch46-check-epigraphs]

On the morning of September 11, 2001, Lieutenant Michael Day of the United States Coast Guard watched a small boat at the lower tip of Manhattan take on passengers. “I thought the boat was going to flip over because so many people were trying to get on,” he later recalled, “and as I looked behind, they were just ten deep.” The Coast Guard put out a call over the radio: “All available boats, this is the U.S. Coast Guard aboard the Pilot Boat *New York*. Anyone wanting to help with the evacuation of lower Manhattan, report to Governor’s Island.”[^ch46-cgf-boatlift]

Within 15 to 20 minutes, more than 100 vessels responded. Ferries, tugboats, party boats, and private boats moved toward the sea wall. Robin Jones, an engineer aboard the *Mary Gellatly*, remembered the response in a sentence that became the day’s shorthand: “If it floated and it could get there, it got there.” In under nine hours, the boats carried more than 500,000 people off lower Manhattan.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift]

The record establishes the date, the radio call, the vessels, and an estimate of the number evacuated. It does not establish what each passenger felt, how each captain decided, or how many deaths the boats prevented. On that morning no one on the water knew that Congress would authorize the use of force three days later, that the PATRIOT Act would pass in six weeks, that the United States would invade Afghanistan in October and Iraq in 2003, or that a financial collapse would come in 2008. The scene holds only the first day: a city improvising a rescue while the institutions responsible for the nation’s defense were, as the 9/11 Commission later put it, unprepared. The question the day left open was how emergency would reorganize state power and public trust.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift][^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-check-scene]

This chapter covers the years from the attacks of September 11, 2001, through the financial collapse of 2008 and the enactment of the Troubled Assets Relief Program on October 3, 2008.[^ch46-check-dates-appD] It asks how emergency reorganized state power and public trust. Chapter 45 owns the 1990s and the disputed election of 2000; Chapter 47 owns the recovery response, the 2008 election, and the Obama administration. This chapter takes up the attacks, the legal and military architecture built in their shadow, the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, the Bush administration’s domestic program, Hurricane Katrina, and the credit crisis.[^ch46-check-scope][^ch46-check-ch45][^ch46-check-ch47]

The chapter draws on presidential addresses and the *Public Papers of the Presidents*, statutes and roll calls, the 9/11 Commission Report, the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence study, the Iraq Study Group report, the Financial Crisis Inquiry Report, federal budget and labor data, and the secondary literature. It treats “the war on terror,” “emergency,” “the Bush doctrine,” “enhanced interrogation,” “the surge,” and “the financial crisis” as contested names that require definition. The administration called its campaign “the war on terror”; the phrase described a political frame as much as a conflict with a fixed enemy. “Enhanced interrogation” was the administration’s term for coercive techniques that critics called torture. “The surge” was the name for the 2007 troop increase in Iraq, not a settled explanation of what followed it.[^ch46-check-scope][^ch46-check-names]

The record over-represents presidents, officials, commanders, and journalists. It under-represents the evacuees, the detainees, the Iraqi and Afghan civilians, and the displaced of the Gulf Coast. Where those voices do not survive, the chapter names the silence rather than filling it with a composite witness. It also distinguishes what a document said from what the evidence shows. A presidential speech establishes what the administration claimed. An intelligence estimate establishes what analysts concluded. Neither establishes that the claim was true.[^ch46-check-silences][^ch46-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the attacks, the toll, the rescue, and the framing of a global war. §2 examines the legal and institutional architecture of the emergency: the Authorization for Use of Military Force, the USA PATRIOT Act, the Department of Homeland Security, the detention and interrogation program, and the courts. §3 examines the war in Afghanistan, its political settlement, and its unfinished course. §4 examines the war in Iraq, the occupation, the insurgency, and the 2007 surge. §5 examines the tax cuts, the education law, the Medicare prescription-drug benefit, the federal response to Hurricane Katrina, and the housing and credit collapse, with Map 37. §6 asks what the emergency settled and what remains open about security, constitutional delegation, intelligence, and strategic choice. The chapter begins on the water off lower Manhattan, where an improvised evacuation carried half a million people to safety on the morning of September 11, 2001.[^ch46-check-scope]

## 1. Attacks and response (2001)

### 1.1 The claim: a transformation and the choices it opened

**The claim.** The attacks of September 11, 2001, killed 2,977 people and transformed American security policy. The responses that followed — war, statute, a new department, and a surveillance and detention program — were distinct choices made under emergency, and they must be told as separate institutional decisions rather than as one causal story.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec]

The section runs from the attacks and their toll through the rescue and the 9/11 Commission’s account of national unpreparedness to the president’s framing of a global war. The maritime evacuation is the chapter’s opening scene, not a policy explanation. The men and women on the water acted before the state had decided what kind of conflict it was in.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]

### 1.2 The attacks and the toll

The 9/11 Commission’s executive summary records the sequence. At 8:46 a.m., an airliner struck the North Tower of the World Trade Center. At 9:03, a second airliner struck the South Tower. At 9:37, a third struck the Pentagon. At 10:03, a fourth crashed in a field in southern Pennsylvania after passengers fought back. Both towers collapsed less than 90 minutes after the first impact. The Commission described the day as “a day of unprecedented shock and suffering in the history of the United States.”[^ch46-911report-exec]

The National September 11 Memorial & Museum records that the attacks killed 2,977 people, the single largest loss of life from a foreign attack on American soil, including 441 first responders. The Commission’s summary gives the distribution as more than 2,600 at the World Trade Center, 125 at the Pentagon, and 256 on the four planes, and notes that the deaths surpassed those at Pearl Harbor in December 1941. The counts differ by source and date: the Commission’s components sum to more than 2,981, while the memorial gives 2,977. Each is given with its source; the difference is not used to make an argument it cannot support.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-check-numbers]

### 1.3 The rescue and the Commission’s accounting

The rescue was improvised by civilians, mariners, and first responders. The 441 first responders who died were the greatest single-day loss of emergency responders in American history. The waterborne evacuation moved more than half a million people, according to the Coast Guard account; the figure is an estimate. These facts sit beside the Commission’s finding of institutional failure, not in place of it.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift][^ch46-911memorial-events]

The National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States was created by Congress and reported in July 2004. Its members, five Republicans and five Democrats, presented their account “without dissent.” The Commission concluded that “the nation was unprepared” and identified failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management. “The most important failure was one of imagination,” it wrote. “We do not believe leaders understood the gravity of the threat.”[^ch46-911report-exec]

One of the Commission’s most quoted sentences about bureaucracy followed from that finding. “It is therefore crucial,” it wrote, “to find a way of routinizing, even bureaucratizing, the exercise of imagination.” The sentence is a retrospective judgment about how large organizations think. It is not a description of what any official knew before the attacks, and it does not prove that a different organization would have prevented them. The Commission itself cautioned that it could not know whether any single step would have defeated the plot.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-911report-ch11]

The Commission is an official retrospective, not a neutral verdict. Its findings are evidence of what a bipartisan panel concluded after reading the record, not the events themselves. The chapter uses it for the sequence, the toll, and the judgment of unpreparedness, and distinguishes those uses from its own analysis.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The framing of a global war

On September 20, 2001, Bush addressed a joint session of Congress. He said the evidence pointed to al Qaeda, demanded that the Taliban turn over its leaders, and announced a Cabinet-level Office of Homeland Security under Tom Ridge. He also described the conflict in open-ended terms. “Our war on terror begins with al Qaeda, but it does not end there,” he said. “It will not end until every terrorist group of global reach has been found, stopped and defeated.” And he gave the world a choice: “Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists.”[^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]

The speech was a statement of the administration’s purposes, not a description the evidence settles. It named a method — military force, intelligence, law enforcement, and financial pressure — and an ambition without a terminus. It did not name Afghanistan, Iraq, or any other country as the next target. It did not ask Congress to declare war. It asked the world to choose sides and asked Americans to prepare for a lengthy campaign “unlike any other we have ever seen.”[^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]

The dissent belonged in the same moment. Feingold, a Wisconsin Democrat, voted for the authorization of force but against the PATRIOT Act. On the Senate floor on October 25, 2001, he said the country had to “redouble our vigilance” for security and for the Bill of Rights. “Preserving our freedom is one of the main reasons we are now engaged in this new war on terrorism,” he said. “We will lose that war without firing a shot if we sacrifice the liberties of the American people.” The argument was not that the threat was imaginary. It was that the response could destroy the thing it claimed to defend.[^ch46-feingold-2001]

The chapter must not treat September 11 as a single cause of the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, the PATRIOT Act, or the later surveillance programs. Each was a separate decision, made by identifiable officials, under conditions that changed over time. The attacks created the emergency. They did not dictate every use made of it.[^ch46-check-scope][^ch46-check-debate-works]

## 2. AUMF, PATRIOT Act, DHS, detention, torture, and surveillance (2001–2008)

### 2.1 The claim: emergency delegated power across statutes, agencies, and courts

**The claim.** In the years after the attacks, Congress and the executive branch built a legal and institutional architecture of emergency: an authorization for the use of force, a surveillance statute, a new department, and a detention and interrogation program. The architecture delegated broad authority, moved contested decisions into the executive branch, and was later partly constrained by the courts. It was not a single plan, and it did not arrive all at once.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-ssci-2014]

The section runs from the authorization through the PATRIOT Act and the Section 215 In Focus, the creation of the Department of Homeland Security, the detention and interrogation program and Abu Ghraib, and the detention cases in the Supreme Court. Each step had its own coalition, its own legal theory, and its own cost.

### 2.2 The Authorization for Use of Military Force

Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force on September 14, 2001. The Senate agreed 98–0, with two senators not voting. The House passed it 420–1, with Representative Barbara Lee of California casting the lone no vote. President Bush signed it on September 18 as Public Law 107-40, 115 Stat. 224.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-aumf-votes]

Section 2 authorized the president “to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks that occurred on September 11, 2001, or harbored such organizations or persons.” The resolution did not name Afghanistan, al Qaeda, or any country. It named a category and left the determination to the president. It also invoked the War Powers Resolution, framing the authorization as specific statutory authority for the use of force.[^ch46-aumf-2001]

The delegation was the constitutional question: Congress had authorized force without a declaration of war and without naming an enemy. The executive branch would later cite the resolution for operations far from Afghanistan, including the detention of American citizens and the military commissions the Supreme Court reviewed. Whether that reading was faithful to the text is a question about the scope of the delegation, not one the vote answers.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-hamdi-2004][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 The PATRIOT Act and the In Focus

The Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism Act became Public Law 107-56 on October 26, 2001, six weeks after the attacks. The Senate passed it 98–1, with Feingold the lone no vote. The House passed it 357–66. The act expanded surveillance authority in several areas: records searches under Section 215, delayed-notice searches under Section 213, the “significant purpose” standard for foreign-intelligence searches under Section 218, and pen-register and trap-and-trace authority under Section 214.[^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-patriot-votes][^ch46-patriot-aclu]

The haste is part of the record. The House passed the bill on October 24 and the Senate on October 25; the president signed it on October 26. The House vote came under a motion to suspend the rules, which required a two-thirds majority and limited debate. The administration pressed for passage as a security measure. That does not make the statute illegitimate. It explains why its provisions were later revisited.[^ch46-patriot-votes][^ch46-patriot-aclu]

**In Focus.** Section 215 replaced the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act’s business-records provision. It allowed the FBI director or a designee to seek an order requiring “the production of any tangible things (including books, records, papers, documents, and other items)” for “an investigation to protect against international terrorism or clandestine intelligence activities,” provided that an investigation of a United States person was not conducted “solely upon the basis of activities protected by the first amendment.” The judge was directed to enter the order “as requested, or as modified” if the application met the section’s requirements, and the recipient was forbidden to disclose that the FBI had sought or obtained the items.[^ch46-patriot-act]

The words carry the dispute. “Any tangible things” reached medical, library, and financial records. The 2001 text did not say the records had to be “relevant” to an investigation; that phrase entered the statute only in the 2006 reauthorization, which required “reasonable grounds to believe that the tangible things sought are relevant to an authorized investigation” and allowed recipients to challenge orders. The ACLU argued that the original section let the government compel records from anyone on a certification the judge could not reject, and that the gag order prevented challenges. The statute did require a judicial finding and permitted modification, but it set no relevance threshold and gave the recipient no explicit route to contest the order. What the government actually collected must be described from the court records, not inferred from the words alone.[^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-patriot-reauth-2006][^ch46-patriot-aclu]

### 2.4 The Department of Homeland Security

The Homeland Security Act of 2002 became Public Law 107-296 on November 25, 2002. It established the Department of Homeland Security as an executive department with a mission to prevent terrorist attacks, reduce vulnerability, and coordinate recovery. The department consolidated all or part of 22 federal departments and agencies. The Coast Guard, which had improvised the maritime evacuation of September 11, moved from the Department of Transportation to the new department in 2003. The consolidation created coordination problems that no organizational chart could solve.[^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-dhs-history][^ch46-cgf-boatlift]

### 2.5 Detention, interrogation, and Abu Ghraib

On November 13, 2001, Bush issued a military order on the detention, treatment, and trial of certain noncitizens in the war against terrorism. The order authorized detention at a location designated by the secretary of defense and trial by military commission for individuals the president determined were members of al Qaeda or had engaged in or aided international terrorism. It declared that such individuals would not be “privileged to seek any remedy or maintain any proceeding” in the courts of the United States or any foreign or international tribunal. Detainees were held at the naval base at Guantánamo Bay, Cuba.[^ch46-military-order-2001][^ch46-rasul-2004]

The CIA operated a separate detention and interrogation program. The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence conducted a multiyear review and released a declassified executive summary on December 9, 2014. The committee found that the CIA had detained at least 119 individuals, of whom at least 39 were subjected to the agency’s “enhanced interrogation techniques.” It found that at least 26 of the 119 were wrongfully held and did not meet the detention standard in the September 2001 memorandum of notification. It reviewed 20 of the CIA’s most prominent claims that the techniques had produced intelligence and found them “wrong in fundamental respects.” The report carried additional and minority views, and its findings concerned the agency’s program, not every detention in Afghanistan or Iraq.[^ch46-ssci-2014]

The abuse at Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq became public in April 2004, when photographs of American soldiers humiliating and mistreating detainees circulated. Secretary of Defense Donald H. Rumsfeld called the abuse “an exceptional, isolated” case; Bush spoke of “disgraceful conduct by a few American troops.” Human Rights Watch argued that the abuse followed from administration decisions to set aside interrogation rules, and that the photographs recorded a pattern rather than an exception. The chapter presents both framings and uses the investigations, not only the photographs. It separates what the legal memoranda authorized, what the interrogations did, and what the official investigations concluded.[^ch46-hrw-abu-ghraib][^ch46-ssci-2014]

### 2.6 Detention and the courts

The legal architecture did not go unchallenged. In *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld* (2004), the Supreme Court held that an American citizen detained in the United States as an enemy combatant had to be given “a meaningful opportunity to contest the factual basis for that detention before a neutral decisionmaker.” In *Rasul v. Bush* (2004), the Court held that federal courts had jurisdiction to hear habeas petitions from foreign nationals held at Guantánamo Bay. In *Hamdan v. Rumsfeld* (2006), the Court held that the military commissions established by the president’s order lacked authority because their structure and procedures violated the Uniform Code of Military Justice and the Geneva Conventions, and because Congress had not specifically authorized them.[^ch46-hamdi-2004][^ch46-rasul-2004][^ch46-hamdan-2006]

The holdings constrained the program without ending it. The Court did not order the release of the detainees or hold that the president lacked all authority to detain. It required a legal process and a congressional basis for the commissions. The episode shows how emergency power is contested: the executive acts, the courts review, and the political branches revise. It is not a story of one branch defeating another.[^ch46-hamdi-2004][^ch46-rasul-2004][^ch46-hamdan-2006]

## 3. Afghanistan (2001–2008)

### 3.1 The claim: a war that toppled a regime and left an unfinished state

**The claim.** The war in Afghanistan quickly removed the Taliban from power and disrupted al Qaeda, but the political settlement and the redirection of resources to Iraq left the war unfinished. By 2008 the United States had accomplished the overthrow of a regime and had not secured the state that replaced it.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]

The section runs from the October 2001 campaign through the Bonn settlement, the failure to capture or kill Osama bin Laden at Tora Bora, and the shift of attention and resources to Iraq.

### 3.2 Operation Enduring Freedom

On October 7, 2001, the United States and Britain began air strikes against al Qaeda training camps and Taliban military installations. Bush said the strikes were “designed to disrupt the use of Afghanistan as a terrorist base of operations, and to attack the military capability of the Taliban regime.” The United States supported the Northern Alliance, and after a series of victories its forces entered Kabul on November 13. The Taliban lost Kandahar on December 6.[^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]

The campaign was swift and its political results were real. The Taliban regime that had sheltered al Qaeda was gone from the capital within six weeks. But the victory rested on Afghan militias and American air power, not on a large occupying army. By May 2003, Rumsfeld declared that major combat operations in Afghanistan had ended, and only about 8,000 American soldiers were stationed there. The quick victory was not a settled outcome.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]

### 3.3 Bonn and the political settlement

In December 2001, Afghan factions meeting in Bonn under United Nations auspices signed the Agreement on Provisional Arrangements in Afghanistan Pending the Re-establishment of Permanent Government Institutions. The agreement created an interim authority, set a timetable for a constitution and elections, and established a legal framework for the transition. Hamid Karzai led the interim administration and was chosen to head the transitional government at the June 2002 loya jirga.[^ch46-bonn-agreement][^ch46-cfr-afghanistan]

The settlement rested on the factions present at Bonn and on continuing foreign military support. It did not include the Taliban or create a state able to secure its own territory. The constitution of January 2004 and the election later that year gave the government formal legitimacy, but did not resolve warlord power, corruption, or the Taliban’s ability to regroup.[^ch46-bonn-agreement][^ch46-cfr-afghanistan]

### 3.4 Tora Bora and the redirection

In December 2001, al Qaeda forces, including possibly Osama bin Laden, were cornered in the cave complex at Tora Bora. The Bush library’s account records that “the United States did not take part in the battle, which ended with bin Laden leaving Afghanistan for neighboring Pakistan.” The reliance on Afghan militias and the failure to block the escape routes became a symbol of the war’s limits.[^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement][^ch46-cfr-afghanistan]

The claim that Tora Bora could have captured bin Laden is a counterfactual. The record establishes that he left Afghanistan and that American forces did not participate in the battle. It does not establish that a different deployment would have captured him. The chapter marks the inference as an inference and does not turn a missed opportunity into a certainty.[^ch46-check-debate-works][^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]

By 2002 the administration’s attention and resources were moving toward Iraq. The Council on Foreign Relations timeline records the shift: the war in Afghanistan continued, but Pentagon planners began moving military and intelligence resources toward Saddam Hussein’s Iraq, and the operation at Shah-i-Kot in March 2002 was the largest since Tora Bora rather than the start of a broader effort. By January 2009, the United States and NATO had about 37,000 troops in Afghanistan. The war the administration had called finished in 2003 was not finished.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]

## 4. Iraq, occupation, insurgency, and the surge (2002–2008)

### 4.1 The claim: a war justified by claims the evidence did not support

**The claim.** The invasion of Iraq in March 2003 was justified principally by claims that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and posed a threat. The weapons were not found as claimed. The occupation and insurgency produced years of violence, and the 2007 surge reduced the violence without producing the political settlement it was meant to enable.[^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-isg-2006]

The section runs from the WMD case and the Iraq authorization through the invasion and the occupation to the insurgency, the Iraq Study Group, and the 2007 surge. The weapons reckoning belongs at the end because it is the test of the case that was made.

### 4.2 The case for war

Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force Against Iraq Resolution of 2002 as Public Law 107-243, 116 Stat. 1498. The House passed it 296–133 on October 10, 2002; the Senate passed it 77–23 the next day. Bush signed it on October 16. The resolution authorized force to “defend the national security of the United States against the continuing threat posed by Iraq” and to enforce United Nations Security Council resolutions.[^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-iraq-aumf-votes]

On February 5, 2003, Secretary of State Colin L. Powell presented the administration’s case to the Security Council. He said Iraq had failed to account for its weapons programs and was “concealing their efforts to produce more weapons of mass destruction.” He played intercepted conversations, showed satellite images, and described mobile biological-weapons laboratories. The presentation was the administration’s most detailed public case. It was evidence of what the administration asserted, not evidence that the weapons existed. The case rested on a real problem and a disputed inference: Iraq had used chemical weapons and obstructed inspectors, but the question was whether it retained stockpiles in 2003 and whether that capability justified an invasion.[^ch46-powell-un-2003][^ch46-duelfer-report]

### 4.3 The invasion and the occupation

On the night of March 19, 2003, Bush announced that coalition forces had begun striking targets in Iraq. He said the campaign was intended “to disarm Iraq, to free its people and to defend the world from grave danger.” American and British forces advanced from Kuwait, and Baghdad fell within weeks. On May 1, Bush declared from the deck of the USS *Abraham Lincoln* that “major combat operations in Iraq have ended.”[^ch46-bush-iraq-address-2003][^ch46-bush-mission-accomplished-2003]

The occupation began with decisions that shaped the insurgency. The Coalition Provisional Authority, under L. Paul Bremer III, issued an order in May 2003 removing senior Baath Party members from public employment and barring them from future government work. It also moved to dissolve the Iraqi army. Critics argue that de-Baathification removed the people who knew how to run the state and pushed tens of thousands of armed men into opposition. The chapter describes the orders from the record and presents the debate about their consequences.[^ch46-cpa-order1][^ch46-cpa-regulations-index][^ch46-check-debate-works]

The insurgency grew through 2003 and 2004. It drew on former regime elements, Sunni tribes, foreign fighters, and sectarian militias. The abuse at Abu Ghraib deepened the political cost. By the end of 2006, the American project in Iraq faced a violence it had not planned for and a government it could not control.[^ch46-isg-2006][^ch46-hrw-abu-ghraib]

### 4.4 The Iraq Study Group and the surge

The bipartisan Iraq Study Group, co-chaired by James A. Baker III and Lee H. Hamilton, issued its report on December 6, 2006. Its first sentence was a verdict: “The situation in Iraq is grave and deteriorating.” It recommended new diplomatic and political efforts in Iraq and the region and “a change in the primary mission of U.S. forces in Iraq” that would allow the United States to begin moving combat forces out. It rejected an open-ended commitment and proposed a phased redeployment.[^ch46-isg-2006]

Bush chose a different emphasis. On January 10, 2007, he announced a “New Way Forward.” He committed “more than 20,000 additional American troops to Iraq,” with five brigades to Baghdad and 4,000 more to Anbar Province. The strategy was counterinsurgency: clear neighborhoods, hold them, and build Iraqi capacity. It was also a bet that the Iraqi government would meet political benchmarks.[^ch46-bush-surge-2007]

Violence fell after 2007. Iraq Body Count, which records only deaths it can document from media reports, documented 25,774 to 27,599 civilian deaths in 2006, 22,671 to 24,295 in 2007, and 8,315 to 9,028 in 2008. The decline was real; its causes are contested. Supporters credit the surge and the change in strategy. Critics credit the Sunni Awakening, the sectarian separation of neighborhoods, and the Mahdi Army’s cease-fire. The chapter gives the figures with their definitions and does not assign the decline to one cause.[^ch46-iraqbodycount-2008][^ch46-bush-surge-2007]

The costs are large and method-dependent. A 2021 estimate by the Costs of War project at Brown University put the fiscal cost of the post-9/11 wars at about $8 trillion and the direct deaths at 897,000 to 929,000, including American service members, allied and opposition fighters, civilians, journalists, and aid workers. The estimate covers twenty years, not the 2003–2008 period alone, and excludes indirect deaths from disease, displacement, and lost access to food and water. It should be given as a retrospective estimate with its method and cutoff.[^ch46-costsofwar]

### 4.5 The weapons reckoning

The Iraq Survey Group, led by Charles Duelfer, released its report on September 30, 2004. It concluded that Saddam Hussein “wanted to end sanctions while preserving the capability to reconstitute his weapons of mass destruction when sanctions were lifted.” It also concluded that Iraq’s WMD capability “was essentially destroyed in 1991,” that Iraq had “unilaterally destroyed its undeclared chemical weapons stockpile in 1991,” and that Saddam “ended the nuclear program in 1991.” It found no evidence of concerted efforts to restart the nuclear program and no credible indication that Baghdad resumed chemical munitions production after 1991.[^ch46-duelfer-report]

The finding is more precise than the slogan that the weapons were never there. Iraq had the knowledge, the intent, and the dual-use infrastructure to reconstitute programs if sanctions ended. It did not have the stockpiles the administration described. The distinction between capability, intent, and deployed weapons is the heart of the weapons reckoning. The public case collapsed on the stockpiles. The strategic argument about intent survived in a different form, and it did not justify the war as it had been sold.[^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-check-debate-works]

## 5. Taxes, education, Medicare, Katrina, and the financial breakdown (2001–2008)

### 5.1 The claim: a domestic program and two failures of response

**The claim.** The administration’s domestic record combined large tax reductions, an expanded federal role in education and prescription drugs, and a response to Hurricane Katrina that its own review judged inadequate. The housing and credit boom ended in the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression. The section asks how a decade of war and tax reduction ended in collapse.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-fcic-report]

The section runs from the tax cuts through the education law and the Medicare benefit, the federal response to Katrina, and the housing and financial breakdown, with Map 37. It ends with the Troubled Assets Relief Program and the hand-off to Chapter 47.

### 5.2 The tax cuts and the fiscal turn

The Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001 became Public Law 107-16 on June 7, 2001. It created a new 10 percent bracket, phased in reductions of the 28, 31, 36, and 39.6 percent rates, increased the child tax credit, and phased out the estate tax. A second reconciliation act in 2003, the Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act, accelerated the rate reductions and lowered taxes on dividends and capital gains. It became Public Law 108-27 on May 28, 2003 and cost about $350 billion over eleven years.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-jgtrra-2003][^ch46-crs-budget-2004]

The fiscal turn was real. The federal government recorded a surplus of $236 billion in fiscal 2000, with receipts of $2,025 billion and outlays of $1,789 billion. In fiscal 2004 it recorded a deficit of $413 billion, with receipts of $1,880 billion and outlays of $2,292 billion. Receipts fell while outlays rose. The budget had moved from surplus to a deficit of more than $400 billion in four years.[^ch46-crs-budget-2004]

The tax cuts were not the only cause. The 2001 recession, the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, homeland security spending, and the automatic growth of existing programs all contributed. The Congressional Research Service attributed the deterioration to “the war on terrorism, the 2001 recession and the slow economic recovery,” policy changes including tax cuts and spending increases, and technical revisions. The chapter gives the figures and the competing causes rather than assigning the whole turn to one law.[^ch46-crs-budget-2004][^ch46-check-numbers]

### 5.3 No Child Left Behind and Medicare Part D

The No Child Left Behind Act of 2001 became Public Law 107-110 on January 8, 2002. It revised and reauthorized the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, required annual testing in reading and mathematics in grades 3 through 8, required states to define adequate yearly progress, and imposed consequences on schools that failed to meet the targets. It expanded federal testing and accountability requirements for elementary and secondary schools.[^ch46-nclb-2002]

The Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003 became Public Law 108-173 on December 8, 2003. It created a voluntary prescription-drug benefit under Medicare Part D, beginning in 2006, and replaced Medicare+Choice with Medicare Advantage. The House approved the conference agreement 220–215; the Senate approved it 54–44. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the conference agreement would increase mandatory spending by $394.3 billion from fiscal 2004 through fiscal 2013. The law also barred the secretary of health and human services from interfering in negotiations between drug manufacturers, pharmacies, and plan sponsors and from requiring a particular formulary or price structure.[^ch46-medicare-partd-2003][^ch46-crs-medicare-2004]

### 5.4 Hurricane Katrina and the federal response

Hurricane Katrina made landfall on August 29, 2005. The storm surge overwhelmed the hurricane protection system, and significant levee failures occurred on the 17th Street Canal, the Industrial Canal, and the London Avenue Canal. About 80 percent of New Orleans flooded. The White House’s own review called Katrina “the most destructive natural disaster in U.S. history” and estimated the damage at $96 billion.[^ch46-katrina-lessons]

The death toll was compiled over months. The White House report, written in February 2006, gave an estimated 1,330 dead and reported 2,096 people still missing. The National Weather Service’s later compilation gives 1,833 fatalities and about $108 billion in damage in 2005 dollars. The earlier count was incomplete; the later is the one to use for the final toll. Both sources agree that the levee breaks heightened the loss of life and property.[^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-katrina-deaths]

The federal response was judged inadequate by the administration’s own review. “The magnitude of Hurricane Katrina does not excuse our inadequate preparedness and response,” the report said. The failure fell hardest on those who could not leave: in Louisiana, about 71 percent of the victims were older than 60, and 47 percent of those were over 75. About 770,000 people were displaced. The record of who died, who stayed, and who returned is a record of inequality as well as engineering failure.[^ch46-katrina-lessons]

The chapter names the silences. The reports count bodies and damage; they do not recover the voices of the people who died in nursing homes, in their homes, or in the convention center. The failure was documented in the language of preparedness. Its human meaning has to be read from the records that survive, and from the absence of those who did not.[^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-check-silences]

### 5.5 The housing boom and the financial breakdown, with Map 37

The housing boom and the credit crisis were years in the making. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, created by Congress and reporting in 2011, concluded that the crisis was avoidable. It found “widespread failures in financial regulation,” “dramatic failures of corporate governance and risk management,” a combination of “excessive borrowing, risky investments, and lack of transparency,” and a government “ill prepared for the crisis.” The collapse of the housing bubble, “fueled by low interest rates, easy and available credit, scant regulation, and toxic mortgages,” was the spark.[^ch46-fcic-report]

The commission’s figures show the scale. From 2001 to 2007, national mortgage debt almost doubled, and mortgage debt per household rose more than 63 percent, from $91,500 to $149,500, while wages were essentially stagnant. Nearly one in ten borrowers in 2005 and 2006 took out “option ARM” loans that allowed payments so low that the balance could rise. The five major investment banks operated with leverage ratios as high as 40 to 1. The system had borrowed heavily against assets whose value depended on house prices that kept rising.[^ch46-fcic-report]

Ben S. Bernanke, then chairman of the Federal Reserve, distinguished the “triggers” of the crisis from its “vulnerabilities.” The most prominent trigger was the prospect of significant losses on subprime mortgages after house prices began to fall. The vulnerabilities were the structural weaknesses that propagated the shock, including the too-big-to-fail problem, short-term funding, and opaque exposures. The trigger was the mortgage losses; the reason they became a global crisis was the system that held them.[^ch46-bernanke-2010]

Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008. Credit markets seized. The unemployment rate, 4.9 percent in December 2007, reached 7.2 percent in December 2008. The crisis was a sequence of failures, rescues, and panics that turned a housing downturn into a recession.[^ch46-fcic-report][^ch46-bls-dec2008]

Map 37, “Housing boom and crisis, 2000–2008,” accompanies this section. It will show lending geography and mortgage exposure over a restricted 2000–2008 window, state its dates, geographic units, and data series, and distinguish measured lending from areas the data do not cover. It must not present a 2010 outcome before the chapter’s narrative endpoint or read a causal story off the map. The source task is the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act data, the American Community Survey, and dated foreclosure records, supported by the production record `repo-map37` and Appendix C.[^ch46-check-map37]

### 5.6 TARP and the response

The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008 became Public Law 110-343 on October 3, 2008. It authorized the Troubled Assets Relief Program to purchase troubled assets from financial institutions, with authority limited to $700 billion outstanding at any one time through a graduated process: $250 billion initially, $350 billion on presidential certification, and $700 billion after a report to Congress unless Congress disapproved.[^ch46-eesa-2008]

The vote was contingent. On September 29, the House rejected an earlier version of the rescue, 205–228. Four days later, it passed the revised bill, 263–171. The response to the crisis was not dictated by the crisis itself. It was a series of political choices made under pressure, and the choices could have gone the other way.[^ch46-tarp-votes][^ch46-eesa-2008]

The collapse and TARP are the chapter’s ending and the immediate prehistory of Chapter 47. The recovery response, the 2008 election, and the Obama administration belong to the next chapter. This chapter ends with the emergency measures of October 2008, not with the recovery they were meant to produce.[^ch46-check-ch47]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The September 11, 2001, attacks killed 2,977 people, including 441 first responders. Two hijacked airliners struck the World Trade Center, one struck the Pentagon, and one crashed in a field in Somerset County, Pennsylvania. The towers collapsed less than 90 minutes after the first impact.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec]

The Authorization for Use of Military Force became Public Law 107-40 on September 18, 2001, after passing the Senate 98–0 and the House 420–1. The USA PATRIOT Act became Public Law 107-56 on October 26, 2001. The Homeland Security Act became Public Law 107-296 on November 25, 2002.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-aumf-votes][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-homeland-security-act]

Operation Enduring Freedom began on October 7, 2001. The Iraq authorization became Public Law 107-243 on October 16, 2002. Bush announced the “New Way Forward” in Iraq on January 10, 2007.[^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement][^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-bush-surge-2007]

The Iraq Survey Group concluded in 2004 that Iraq had destroyed its chemical weapons stockpile and ended its nuclear program after 1991, while retaining the intent to reconstitute weapons programs if sanctions were lifted.[^ch46-duelfer-report]

The tax cut became Public Law 107-16 on June 7, 2001; the education law became Public Law 107-110 on January 8, 2002; and the Medicare benefit became Public Law 108-173 on December 8, 2003. Katrina made landfall on August 29, 2005. The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act became Public Law 110-343 on October 3, 2008.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-nclb-2002][^ch46-medicare-partd-2003][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-eesa-2008]

### 6.2 What is open

Did the emergency justify the delegations? One account holds that the threat was novel and broad authority necessary to prevent a second attack. Another holds that the statutes and detention program exceeded the law and damaged the nation’s standing. The chapter tests each against the AUMF’s text, the PATRIOT Act’s provisions, and the Supreme Court’s holdings, not the rhetoric of either side.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-hamdi-2004][^ch46-ssci-2014]

Was the Iraq war a necessary response, a strategic blunder, or a deliberate misrepresentation? Accounts stress ideology, credibility, intelligence failure, and individual choices. Leffler argues that Bush was moved by fear of another attack and by the need to preserve American power, and that officials treated Iraq’s weapons as a “gathering” threat rather than a proven imminent one. Critics argue the administration exaggerated the evidence. The chapter compares them and presents none as settled.[^ch46-leffler-response-2024][^ch46-powell-un-2003][^ch46-duelfer-report]

Did the surge work? Supporters read the decline in violence after 2007 as proof the strategy worked; critics read it as the product of the Awakening, sectarian separation, and other factors. Iraq Body Count’s figures establish that violence fell, not why. The chapter states each explanation and uses the figures with their definitions.[^ch46-bush-surge-2007][^ch46-iraqbodycount-2008][^ch46-check-debate-works]

Did the tax cuts cause the deficits and the collapse? One account links the fiscal turn and the crisis to tax reductions and deregulation; another links the crisis to global capital flows and private risk-taking. The budget figures show that receipts fell and outlays rose. They do not show that one policy caused the whole outcome.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-crs-budget-2004][^ch46-fcic-report][^ch46-check-debate-works]

What did Katrina prove about the state? The flood is read as evidence of a federal failure to protect, of a disaster that exceeded any response, or of the inequality the storm exposed. The White House review supports the first, the engineering record the second, and the mortality record the third. The chapter holds them together rather than choosing one.[^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-check-silences]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the emergency produced real institutional changes whose security value and legal cost are both contested. The attacks were real, the fear was real, and the measures were choices. The wars were justified by claims the evidence did not support, and the domestic program of tax reduction and expanded benefits ended in a financial collapse with multiple causes. The outcomes were made by people who could have decided otherwise.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-fcic-report]

The contingencies were specific. The AUMF passed with one dissenting House vote; the PATRIOT Act with one dissenting Senate vote; the Iraq authorization with 133 votes against it in the House. The Iraq Study Group offered a different course. The surge was a choice among alternatives. The first TARP vote failed. The tax cuts were legislation, not weather. Each is a place where the record shows a decision, not an inevitability.[^ch46-aumf-votes][^ch46-patriot-votes][^ch46-iraq-aumf-votes][^ch46-isg-2006][^ch46-tarp-votes]

Structure and choice must be held together. The altered threat, the global financial system, and the fiscal position set the conditions; the choices made within them decided the form. Between 2001 and 2008 the United States suffered the deadliest attack on its soil, fought two wars, built a new architecture of emergency, expanded federal education and health benefits while cutting taxes, failed in its response to a catastrophic flood, and ended the decade in a financial collapse. The chapter’s question is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-eesa-2008]

## Legacies

The emergency of 2001 left a legal and institutional architecture that outlasted the administration that built it. The authorizations, the surveillance statutes, the new department, and the detention program fixed frameworks that later governments would defend, revise, or abandon. The question of how far emergency may expand executive power became a permanent feature of American constitutional debate. These are documented changes, not a verdict on them.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-ssci-2014]

The wars left two countries and two American publics changed. The invasion of Iraq, undertaken on claims the evidence did not support, and the unfinished war in Afghanistan shaped a generation of American foreign policy and the lives of Iraqis and Afghans. The human and financial costs are large and method-dependent. The United States killed Osama bin Laden in 2011 and withdrew its last forces from Afghanistan in 2021, after the Taliban returned to power. Those outcomes were not known to the actors of 2001, and the chapter names them as later consequences rather than as the meaning of the earlier choices.[^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-costsofwar][^ch46-cfr-afghanistan]

The decade of tax reduction and expanded benefits ended in a financial collapse whose causes and whose response shaped the politics that followed. The housing boom and the credit crisis, the federal response to Katrina, and the program of tax reduction set terms that Chapter 47 takes up as it examines the recovery and the years after 2008. The emergency did not end in 2008. It changed hands.[^ch46-fcic-report][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-check-ch47]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 2001 and 2008 the United States suffered the deadliest attack on its soil, fought two wars, built a new legal and institutional architecture of emergency, expanded federal education and health benefits while cutting taxes, failed in its response to a catastrophic flood, and ended the decade in a financial collapse. The question — how did emergency reorganize state power and public trust, and by what distinct steps? — is answered by holding those developments together and refusing to collapse them into one causal story. The attacks did not cause everything that followed. They created a condition in which officials made choices, and the choices can be traced.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-eesa-2008]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Grant Park in Chicago on the night of November 4, 2008, where Barack Obama addressed supporters after his election as the 44th president. Chapter 47 takes up the recovery response, the reform of finance and health care, and the polarization that accompanied them. This chapter ends with the financial collapse and the passage of TARP; the next turns to the emergency’s aftermath.[^ch46-check-ch47][^ch46-eesa-2008]
