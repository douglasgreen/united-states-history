# Chapter 26. The South, Race, and the New Order (1877–1910)

> — George P. Melchior, delegate from Bolivar County, provisions referred to the Franchise Committee, *Journal of the Proceedings of the Constitutional Convention of the State of Mississippi*, 1890.[^ch26-ms-1890-journal]

> “The right to vote. Civic equality. The education of youth according to ability.”[^ch26-dubois-souls]
>
> — W. E. B. Du Bois, “Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others,” in *The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903.[^ch26-dubois-souls]

> “One of the strangest things about the career of Jim Crow was that the system was born in the North and reached an advanced age before moving South in force.”[^ch26-woodward-career]
>
> — C. Vann Woodward, *The Strange Career of Jim Crow*, 1955.[^ch26-woodward-career]

A Mississippi delegate said on the record that the convention existed to secure White supremacy. A Black scholar answered with the three things the new order denied. A historian argued that the order those two described was not an ancient southern custom but a recent invention, and a northern one first. The three disagree about whether the new order was a frank purpose, a contested demand, or a contingent construction. This chapter asks how racial domination became an institutional order after Reconstruction.[^ch26-ms-1890-journal][^ch26-dubois-souls][^ch26-woodward-career][^ch26-check-epigraphs]

On August 12, 1890, 134 delegates took their seats in the state capitol in Jackson to write a new constitution for Mississippi. The other 133 were White. The only Black delegate was Isaiah T. Montgomery of Bolivar County, a founder of the all-Black town of Mound Bayou and a man once enslaved by Joseph E. Davis, brother of the Confederate president. In February the legislature had called the convention, and Gov. John M. Stone had approved the call, in part because federal supervision of elections seemed possible. Democrats controlled the body. The roll, the call, and the partisan balance are documented; the weather and the mood of the chamber are not.[^ch26-ms-1890-journal][^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890][^ch26-kirwan-apportionment][^ch26-ms-independent-1890]

When the franchise article reached the floor, it did not mention race. It imposed a two-dollar poll tax, a criminal disqualification, a registration oath, and, after January 1, 1892, the requirement that every elector “shall … be able to read any section of the constitution of this State; or he shall be able to understand the same when read to him, or give a reasonable interpretation thereof.” A new registration was required before the next election. George P. Melchior of Bolivar County had submitted proposed provisions that were referred to the Franchise Committee, declaring “the manifest intention of this Convention to secure to the State of Mississippi, ‘white supremacy.’” The words were frank; the article was not.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-1890-journal]

Montgomery, who sat on the franchise committee, defended the report. According to the *Jackson Clarion-Ledger*, he estimated that the franchise provision would disqualify more than 123,000 Black voters and said he was willing to sacrifice them “upon the burning altar of liberty” for the easing of tension between the races. On November 1 the convention adopted the constitution by 129 votes to 8 and did not submit it to the voters. The document that disfranchised Black Mississippians for the next 75 years was never ratified by its voters.[^ch26-kirwan-apportionment][^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890][^ch26-ms-1890-journal][^ch26-ms-plan-1890]

The record preserves the roll, the article, the provisions, the newspaper report, the vote, and the signing. It does not preserve what Montgomery told himself as he voted, or what the other delegates said in the corners of the hall. What it shows is a state writing exclusion into its fundamental law in daylight, with a journal open and a vote recorded. What it cannot show is the order being completed, because on November 1, 1890, no one knew how far the model would travel or how long it would last.[^ch26-check-scene][^ch26-check-dates]

How did racial domination become an institutional order after Reconstruction?

This chapter covers the thirty-three years from the end of Reconstruction to 1910. Chapter 25 followed the instruments of conquest in the West; this chapter turns to the instruments of racial rule in the South. It asks how violence, credit, employment, electoral rules, law, and federal retreat worked together to build a racial order in the South, and how Black Southerners fought it.[^ch26-check-ch25] It draws on state constitutions and convention journals, the Supreme Court’s reports, the reports of the courts and the Bureau of Labor, census and agricultural data reported in the works cited, the anti-lynching pamphlets and Black newspapers, and the writings of Black leaders and the secondary accounts of their organizations.

It treats “Redemption,” “New South,” “Jim Crow,” “Fusion,” “the Negro problem,” and “the Solid South” as contested period terms that require definition rather than as neutral descriptions. “The Negro problem” was the period phrase for the question of Black citizenship, and “the Solid South” was the name for the Democratic Party’s dominance in the region after Reconstruction. The archive over-represents the officials, registrars, and mobs and under-represents the people they ruled, and the imbalance bears on the argument.[^ch26-check-scope][^ch26-check-names][^ch26-check-race]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines Redemption and the Supreme Court’s retreat. §2 follows the crop-lien, sharecropping, and convict-leasing economy. §3 turns to New South industry. §4 examines disfranchisement. §5 asks how segregation, lynching, and Black political response reshaped the South. §6 asks how historians have explained the making and enforcement of Jim Crow. The chapter begins in Mississippi in 1890, when a state convention wrote exclusion into a constitution.[^ch26-check-scope]

## 1. Redemption and the Court’s retreat

The new order did not begin in a courtroom. It began with the forcible end of Reconstruction government, and it was consolidated when the federal government stopped enforcing the amendments that Reconstruction had written into the Constitution. “Redemption” was the period term for the restoration of White Democratic rule; it described a seizure of power as a rescue. The Court supplied the legal language for the retreat, but the power to retreat had already been won in the streets.[^ch26-ms-plan-1875][^ch26-house-demise]

### 1.1 Redemption by violence and fraud

After Republicans carried Mississippi in 1874, White Democratic clubs and paramilitary leagues used violence and fraud to recapture the state. Over several days in September 1875, about 50 Black Mississippians were killed, along with White supporters, including a teacher who worked with the Black community at Clinton. Gov. Adelbert Ames asked President Ulysses S. Grant to intervene. Grant declined, and the violence and fraud continued. Other states copied the method, which became known as the Mississippi Plan.[^ch26-ms-plan-1875]

The federal retreat was general. Through 1874 and 1875, Black and Republican voters across the South faced stolen ballot boxes, fraudulent counts, armed intimidation, and violence. A federal elections bill passed the House in 1875 but was abandoned in the Senate, and federal enforcement collapsed before the 1876 election.[^ch26-house-demise]

The disputed presidential election of 1876 was resolved by an understanding that Southern Democrats would accept Rutherford B. Hayes in exchange for the withdrawal of the last federal troops from the South and a policy of leniency. When Hayes took office he withdrew the troops, and historians have largely treated that withdrawal as the end of Reconstruction. No single written text of the bargain survives. It was an understanding inferred from the withdrawal and the policy that followed, not a signed agreement.[^ch26-wikipedia-1877][^ch26-house-demise][^ch26-check-sources]

Redemption did not merely change officeholders. It transferred the protection of Black political and civil rights from the federal government to state governments that were committed to ending them. That transfer is the structural fact the rest of the chapter explains. A right that depends on the government that means to deny it is not a secure right, and the Court would soon confirm how little the federal government intended to do.[^ch26-house-demise][^ch26-civil-rights-cases]

### 1.2 The Supreme Court’s narrowing of the amendments

In the 1880s and 1890s the Supreme Court read the Reconstruction amendments narrowly. It denied Congress the power to reach private discrimination and upheld state measures that were neutral on their face but racial in operation. The Court’s retreat made the new order legally survivable.[^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-history-disfranchise]

The *Civil Rights Cases* of 1883 were a single decision covering five prosecutions under the Civil Rights Act of 1875. The Court held the first two sections of the act unconstitutional as applied to the states. The Fourteenth Amendment, the Court reasoned, is “prohibitory upon the states,” and the Thirteenth Amendment reaches only slavery “and its incidents.” The denial of equal accommodations, the Court said, could not “be justly regarded as imposing any badge of slavery or servitude.” The decision was eight to one. Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented, and his dissent became the moral counter-text that later lawyers would use.[^ch26-civil-rights-cases]

*Plessy v. Ferguson* followed in 1896. The Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring “equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races” on railroads. The phrase “separate but equal” became the doctrine’s name; the opinion itself spoke of equal but separate accommodations. *Plessy* was the validation of a practice the states had already begun after the 1887 Florida railroad-segregation law, not its origin. The Court did not invent segregation; it gave the states permission to perfect it.[^ch26-plessy-opinion][^ch26-archives-plessy]

*Williams v. Mississippi* in 1898 completed the legal work. The Court upheld Mississippi’s poll tax, literacy tests, and disfranchisement clauses on the ground that the constitution did not discriminate on its face and that “it has not been shown that their actual administration was evil; only that evil was possible under them.” The ruling licensed the spread of the Mississippi model. A state could write a neutral rule, apply it by race, and be safe from federal review so long as the rule’s text kept its balance and its administration left no record that a court would accept.[^ch26-williams][^ch26-history-disfranchise]

The Court’s reasoning left a door open for later litigation, but the door was narrow. A plaintiff had to show that a facially neutral law was administered with discriminatory effect, and the record of administration was kept by the officials who did the discriminating. Harlan’s *Plessy* dissent called the Constitution “color-blind,” and the phrase would be quoted for a century. In 1896, however, the dissent was a minority of one, and by 1898 the new order had its legal architecture.[^ch26-plessy-opinion][^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-check-dates]

## 2. Crop liens, sharecropping, and convict leasing

Emancipation gave Black Southerners their labor but not the land to live from. The new order in the countryside rested on contracts that converted free labor into debt and dependence, and on a punishment system that returned the region’s labor problem to the state.[^ch26-duke-sharecropping][^ch26-pbs-sharecropping]

### 2.1 Land, labor, and the failure of redistribution

At a Savannah meeting in 1865, Black leaders asked the government for land. Their spokesman, the Baptist minister Garrison Frazier, said that “the way we can best take care of ourselves, is to have land, and turn it and till it by our own labor.” Gen. William T. Sherman’s Special Field Order No. 15 set aside coastal land in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida — some 400,000 acres — but President Andrew Johnson rescinded the order in late 1865, and the promise of forty acres was never fulfilled.[^ch26-duke-sharecropping]

The sharecropping contract filled the gap. On January 1, 1867, in Marshall County, Mississippi, two formerly enslaved men, Cooper Hughes and Charles Roberts, contracted to work 40 acres of corn and 20 acres of cotton on I. G. Bailey’s land for one-third of the corn and one-half of the cotton, with stipends of meat, a mule, a garden plot, and cabins. The contract is evidence of both the freedpeople’s bargaining and the narrowness of their options. They could negotiate the terms of their labor; they could not negotiate the fact that someone else owned the land.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping]

Under the crop-lien system, a merchant or planter advanced supplies against the coming crop at interest and took first legal claim to the harvest. As cotton prices fell, families who could not pay could not leave, because the law and the debt bound them to the land. The mechanism was simple and relentless: advance, lien, crop, debt, and another advance to survive until the next crop. “Sharecropping” was not one institution but a family of arrangements, and the arrangements varied by county, crop, and contract. The direction was the same. The laborer bore the risk, and the merchant or planter held the claim.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-check-numbers]

### 2.2 Convict leasing and the criminal-exception economy

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery “except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted.” That exception became the legal doorway for a system that leased Black prisoners to private employers. Southern states leased prisoners to railways, mines, lumber yards, brick yards, factories, and large plantations. The state, county, or local government collected leasing fees; the prisoner earned no pay and faced dangerous, often deadly conditions.[^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing]

The system needed a supply of prisoners, and the law supplied it. Black Codes criminalized vagrancy, loitering, and failure to prove employment, and professional “crime hunters” were paid per arrest. Apprehensions rose when labor demand rose. Even people found innocent could be placed in the system if they could not pay their court fees. The line between arrest and employment was thin, and the people who crossed it were overwhelmingly Black.[^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing]

The Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company was among the largest users of prisoner labor, most of it Black men convicted of petty offenses, and convict labor became central to the iron and steel industry around Birmingham. Leasing belongs to the industrial story in §3, not beside it. The same political order that wrote a neutral franchise article leased its prisoners to the companies that were building the New South.[^ch26-loc-convict-leasing][^ch26-eoa-birmingham]

The system persisted into the 1930s and in places until after the Second World War. It left behind a thin record and a large silence. The silence is named here rather than filled with invented voices. What survives is the state’s ledger, the company’s account, and the newspaper’s notice; what does not survive, in most cases, is what the prisoner thought or said.[^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing][^ch26-check-race]

### 2.3 The cost of dependence

Debt, immobility, and the convict lease worked with disfranchisement to strip Black Southerners of the economic and legal standing that free citizenship implied, even as they remained the majority labor force in much of the region. A laborer who could not leave the land, could not vote, and could be arrested on a vagrancy charge and leased out had lost the instruments of self-defense that freedom had promised.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing]

This section supplies the material base that §4’s disfranchisement and §5’s violence presuppose. The new order was not only a set of legal rules. It was a distribution of land, credit, and punishment, and that distribution made the legal rules enforceable. A man who could not feed his family without the merchant’s advance could not easily risk the merchant’s anger at the polls.[^ch26-check-scope][^ch26-duke-sharecropping]

## 3. New South industry

The “New South” was a promotional program for northern investment and southern industrial growth. Its leading spokesman sold it as reunion while promoting White supremacy, and the region’s industrial growth rested on cheap labor, including the labor of children and of leased prisoners.[^ch26-yawp-grady][^ch26-nge-grady][^ch26-bls-child-labor]

### 3.1 Grady’s program and the promise of reunion

Henry W. Grady, managing editor of the *Atlanta Constitution*, won national recognition for his December 21, 1886, speech to the New England Society in New York City. Quoting Benjamin H. Hill, he declared: “There was a South of slavery and secession — that South is dead. There is a South of union and freedom — that South, thank God, is living, breathing, growing every hour.” The new South, he said, offered “a hundred farms for every plantation” and “a diversified industry that meets the complex needs of this complex age,” and that “The South has nothing to take back.”[^ch26-yawp-grady]

Grady used the *Constitution* to promote northern investment, industrial growth, diversified farming — and White supremacy. The two belong together; the economic program was not separable from the racial order it depended on. The mills and furnaces needed labor that could be paid little and controlled easily, and the political order that kept Black labor cheap was part of the business plan.[^ch26-nge-grady][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing]

Grady’s description is not analysis. “New South” was a slogan, and the test is wages, hours, land tenure, and who received the investment. The slogan’s promise of reunion was real in the sense that northern capital returned; it was false in the sense that the reunion was built on the surrender of Black political power.[^ch26-nge-grady][^ch26-check-names]

### 3.2 Textiles, iron, and steel

Central Alabama’s Jones Valley held closely associated deposits of iron ore, coal, limestone, and dolomite, giving the Birmingham District the lowest raw-material assembly costs in the country. Iron makers built vertically integrated companies, and the district grew as rapidly as Pittsburgh and Chicago in the last two decades of the nineteenth century. The labor force was drawn from impoverished freedmen and poor Whites who migrated from tenant farming and sharecropping.[^ch26-eoa-birmingham]

The textile industry grew rapidly after the Civil War. By 1900 about 25,000 of the nearly 100,000 textile workers in the South were children under 16. The figure is a single-year snapshot, and its definition of “child” and its source series must be stated before it is compared with other estimates. The number is large enough to establish the point: the mills that symbolized the New South depended on workers who could not legally contract for themselves.[^ch26-bls-child-labor][^ch26-check-numbers]

The industrial expansion did not lift the people whose labor made it possible. Its leading enterprises used the same racialized labor controls that governed the countryside: convict leases, child labor, company housing, and wages set in a market where Black workers had the fewest alternatives. The New South’s growth was real. So was the inequality that made it profitable.[^ch26-eoa-birmingham][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing][^ch26-bls-child-labor]

### 3.3 Who benefited

The New South enriched a narrow class of planters, merchants, and industrialists and left most farmers and workers in place. That is why the region’s politics after 1890 ran toward agrarian revolt. The farmers who did not share in the industrial promise turned to a movement that promised to organize them across the color line, and the promise terrified the men who had built the new order.[^ch26-nge-grady][^ch26-nge-watson]

Thomas E. Watson of Georgia began as a voice for poor farmers and sharecroppers of both races and depended on Black votes in his 1892 campaign. He later turned to disfranchisement and White supremacy, and by 1908 he was running for president on a White-supremacist platform. Watson’s career is a case study in the turn that Woodward placed at the center of the segregation story. The dependence and the turn belong here; the Populist movement belongs to Chapter 27.[^ch26-nge-watson][^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]

The agrarian revolt passes to Chapter 27; this section keeps its focus on the racial order that the New South’s leaders served. The two stories are connected, but they are not the same story. The New South’s boosters wanted northern capital and southern labor discipline; the farmers’ movement wanted credit, prices, and political power. For a moment the movements overlapped, and then the racial order divided them.[^ch26-check-ch27]

## 4. Disfranchisement

Disfranchisement was the new order’s central political achievement. It removed Black men from the electorate without naming them, and it did so through rules that could be defended as reforms. The Mississippi model spread because it worked, and it worked because the officials who applied it were chosen by the party that wrote it.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-history-disfranchise]

### 4.1 The Mississippi model

Mississippi’s 1890 constitution supplied the template because its restrictions were neutral in text and discriminatory in administration. The written rule and its enforcement must be kept distinct, as the evidence requires. The rule was published; the enforcement was local, discretionary, and largely unrecorded.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890]

The devices were cumulative. The poll tax of two dollars (Sec. 243) required payment and receipts. The criminal disqualification covered offenses that Black men were most likely to be charged with. The registration oath gave the registrar a written instrument to challenge. And Sec. 244 added the literacy test with its “understand the same when read to him, or give a reasonable interpretation thereof” alternative. A new registration and the discretion of the registrar turned neutral words into exclusion.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-williams]

The convention’s purpose was not hidden. Melchior’s provisions had named “white supremacy,” and James K. Vardaman later boasted that the convention had been held to eliminate Black men from politics. The attribution of the Vardaman sentence is disputed — some accounts assign it to Calhoon — but the intent it describes is not. The provisions and the article are the evidence; the later boast is not.[^ch26-ms-1890-journal][^ch26-ms-plan-1890][^ch26-check-attribution]

**In Focus.** Section 244 is a sentence that hands the ballot to a registrar. It says that after January 1, 1892, every elector must, “in addition to the foregoing qualifications, be able to read any section of the constitution of this State; or he shall be able to understand the same when read to him, or give a reasonable interpretation thereof.” The section does not say which section the registrar must choose, what counts as understanding, or what interpretation is reasonable. It says “any section,” and it leaves the choice to the official. A registrar who wanted to admit a voter could choose an easy section; one who wanted to exclude could choose a hard one. The “understanding” clause gave registrars a way to admit illiterate White men while excluding Black men, and the new registration requirement made every voter apply again. The section’s face is an education standard; its function is discretion. Mississippi did not remove it from the constitution until 1975. What the text cannot show is how each registrar used it, or how many people were turned away without a record.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890][^ch26-ms-independent-1890][^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-focus]

Its face is a reading requirement; its function was to place the power to admit or refuse a voter in the hands of an official chosen by the party that wrote the section. The text can show the instrument. It cannot show the registrar’s face, the number of refusals, or the families who gave up before they applied.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-check-focus]

### 4.2 The spread, 1890–1910

Between 1890 and 1910 most of the former Confederate states rewrote their constitutions or passed amendments that used the same neutral devices, and the Supreme Court’s *Williams* decision removed the last real federal check. The devices were combined differently in different states, but the repertoire was shared.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise]

The repertoire included the poll tax, the literacy and understanding test, the grandfather clause, the property qualification, the registration form filled out “without assistance,” the white primary, and felony disfranchisement. Georgia’s cumulative poll tax, adopted in 1877, cut Black turnout by about half before the 1890 wave even began. The devices were not invented in 1890; they were assembled into a system in the 1890s.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-numbers]

Louisiana held a convention in 1898 to write what its sponsors called a “disfranchisement constitution.” Before the calling referendum, registration had already been driven down: total registered voters fell from 294,432 in 1897 to 87,240 in 1898; White registrants fell from 164,088 to 74,133, and Black registrants from 130,344 to 12,902. The new constitution added an annual poll tax, a literacy test administered at the registrar’s discretion, a complicated “without assistance” registration form, a $300 property qualification, and a grandfather clause. The legislature put it into effect without submitting it to the voters. By 1900 the number of Black registrants had fallen to 5,320, and by 1922 to 598.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana]

The national scale is harder to measure than the Louisiana series, but the direction is clear. By 1910, according to figures attributed to the historian Donald G. Nieman, Black registration had fallen to about 15 percent in Virginia and under 2 percent in both Alabama and Mississippi. They are estimates with a year and a source, not a census of every county, and the same devices also removed many poor White voters. The text presents them as estimates.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-numbers]

### 4.4 The consequences of exclusion

Disfranchisement did more than remove Black voters. It helped complete a one-party South, shifted power toward White rural counties through apportionment on total population, and forced the region’s politics into a single racial channel. Because the disfranchised Black population was still counted for apportionment, White voters in majority-Black districts gained disproportionate power. Primaries became the only meaningful election in much of the South, and they were increasingly restricted to White voters. Candidates competed less on policy than on the intensity of their racial appeals.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-kirwan-apportionment]

The intent was stated in the conventions. John B. Knox, the president of Alabama’s 1901 convention, told the delegates that their goal was “within the limits imposed by the Federal Constitution, to establish white supremacy in this State.” He added that White supremacy had to be established “by law—not by force or fraud.” The sentence is the new order’s theory of itself: exclusion should be legal, orderly, and beyond federal reach.[^ch26-alabama-proceedings][^ch26-check-names]

The devices fell on poor White men as well, which is why the order required the grandfather clause and the property qualification to protect them. The new order sorted by race but controlled by class and party as well. A poor White man who could not pay the poll tax or pass the literacy test lost his vote too, and the grandfather clause was written to let him back in. The racial line was the point, but the class line was the mechanism.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-64parishes-louisiana]

## 5. Segregation, lynching, and Black response

Segregation and lynching were the new order’s public face. “Jim Crow” was the period name for the legal and customary system of racial separation; it is used here as a historical name, not as a neutral description. “Fusion” was the period term for the alliance of Republicans and Populists that contested Democratic rule in several southern states. Black Southerners did not wait for the system to finish before they fought it. They investigated, organized, published, litigated, and built institutions that outlasted the generation that made Jim Crow.[^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-durocher-wells]

### 5.1 Segregation as law and custom

Segregation after 1890 moved from custom and scattered statute to a system of state law that separated the races in transport, schools, and public life, and *Plessy* supplied its constitutional cover. The course began with an 1887 Florida railroad law, and states required separate accommodations in one public setting after another. The practice spread to schools and most public and semi-public facilities. Railway companies disliked the expense, and Black citizens saw each new law as a further repudiation of the Reconstruction amendments. When Louisiana proposed its separate-car bill in 1890, the Black community of New Orleans protested. The protest failed, and *Plessy* followed. The case began with a planned test of the law, not with a spontaneous act, and the Court turned the test into a doctrine.[^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-plessy-opinion]

Segregation was not ancient. Woodward’s argument that rigid segregation took shape only in the 1890s, and that earlier decades had seen more fluid race relations and more White dissent, is the position §6 tests rather than adopts. The evidence supports both a long history of racial separation and a short history of the legal system that made separation compulsory. The two histories are not the same.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-woodward-career]

### 5.2 Lynching and the “national crime”

Lynching was the enforcement arm of the new order, and Ida B. Wells produced the first sustained statistical and moral counter-record of it. In May 1892 Wells published an editorial in her Memphis paper, the *Free Speech*, after eight Black men were lynched. “Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread bare lie that Negro men rape white women,” she wrote. The word “Negro” is the period usage, reproduced here because the editorial’s language is part of the evidence. A White mob threatened the paper’s editors; the business manager fled, creditors took the office, and the *Free Speech* was “as if it had never been.” Wells, then in the North, could not return.[^ch26-wells-southern-horrors][^ch26-durocher-wells]

Wells delivered the lecture that became *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* in New York in October 1892. The pamphlet named the pattern of hanging followed by shooting the body and challenged the rape charge that justified it. In *A Red Record* (1895), she argued that “more than ten thousand Negroes have been killed in cold blood” in the thirty years since emancipation, that only three White men had been tried, convicted, and executed for those murders, and that in 1894 there were 132 executions by due process of law against 197 killings by mobs. She built the record from the *Chicago Tribune*’s compilations so that the evidence could not be dismissed as a Black source.[^ch26-wells-southern-horrors][^ch26-wells-red-record]

Wells showed that the justification for lynching shifted over time. The first excuse was the need to suppress alleged race riots; the second was “Negro domination”; the third was the charge of assaulting White women. Each excuse failed as the earlier one became implausible, and each was replaced by another that served the same purpose. Her analysis of the shifting excuse is the section’s argument about how violence was legitimated. The record was made by White compilers because Black testimony was disbelieved, and Wells turned the compilers’ own numbers against them.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-check-race]

### 5.3 Wilmington, 1898

In Wilmington, North Carolina, White supremacists destroyed a functioning Fusion government by massacre and coup, one of the most complete violent seizures of a city government in the period. Wilmington was a port of about 20,000 whose Black community had made itself a political force. The votes of 8,000 Black men helped a biracial Fusion alliance elect candidates of both races, and three of the ten aldermen were Black, along with the coroner, jailer, treasurer, and register of deeds.[^ch26-eji-wilmington][^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington]

White leaders targeted the Fusion officeholders and the Black-owned *Daily Record*. After a sexually charged editorial by its editor, Alex Manly, was reprinted across the state, about 500 White men assembled at the Wilmington Light Infantry armory at 8:00 a.m. on November 10, 1898. Led by the former congressman Alfred Moore Waddell, they moved on the *Record* office, and the crowd swelled to perhaps 2,000 as it crossed the city. Waddell had earlier urged that White citizens, if necessary, “choke the Cape Fear with carcasses.”[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington]

The number of people killed is disputed. The coroner reported 14, the 2006 state report settled on “as many as sixty,” and other accounts claim scores or hundreds. All reported victims were Black. The range is the honest report, and the uncertainty has causes: the dead were counted by local officials, the survivors fled, and the coup’s leaders wrote the first accounts. The event was called a race riot for a century before a state commission called it a coup. The name matters because it decides who acted and who was acted upon.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-eji-wilmington][^ch26-check-numbers]

### 5.4 Washington’s accommodation

Booker T. Washington’s 1895 Atlanta address made industrial education and social separation the price of White tolerance, and it became the most influential statement of Black strategy between 1895 and 1915. Speaking at the Cotton States and International Exposition in Atlanta on September 18, 1895, Washington told the story of the ship answered “Cast down your bucket where you are.” He urged Blacks to “dignify and glorify common labor” and said the races could “be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.” He argued that agitation for social equality was folly and that progress came through “severe and constant struggle, rather than of artificial forcing.”[^ch26-washington-atlanta][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]

The address won a standing ovation, was telegraphed across the country, and cemented Washington’s influence with White leaders and presidents alike. His control over Black school funding and political patronage was part of the strategy, not an aside. Washington built an institution, Tuskegee Institute, and a network of donors and officials; his accommodation was a way of surviving within the order while building power inside it. The strategy was real, and so was its cost.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise][^ch26-nps-washington]

### 5.5 Du Bois and the challenge

W. E. B. Du Bois’s 1903 critique challenged the assumption that accommodation was the only Black strategy and demanded the vote, civic equality, and education as matters of right. In “Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others,” Du Bois wrote that Washington represented “the old attitude of adjustment and submission,” that his program “practically accepts the alleged inferiority of the Negro races,” and that it asked Black people to give up “political power,” “insistence on civil rights,” and “higher education of Negro youth.” Against that, he set three demands: “The right to vote. Civic equality. The education of youth according to ability.”[^ch26-dubois-souls]

Du Bois called for the higher education of a “talented tenth” to lead the struggle, and *The Souls of Black Folk* rallied opposition to Washington in Black intellectual circles. The Washington–Du Bois disagreement was a debate about strategy and power, not a contest of personalities. Washington built institutions and negotiated with the powerful; Du Bois insisted that the rights Washington deferred were the point of the struggle. Both were responding to the same order, and neither could end it alone.[^ch26-dubois-souls][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]

### 5.6 The organizations, 1905 and 1909

The Niagara Movement and the NAACP converted the argument over strategy into permanent organizations, and Black women and men built them even as the new order closed the ballot box. Du Bois and other Black leaders met at Niagara Falls in 1905 and founded a movement demanding civil and political rights and offering an alternative to Washington’s accommodation. It was the immediate precursor of the NAACP.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise][^ch26-durocher-wells]

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People was founded in 1909. Wells was among its forty founders, though its White coordinators first removed her name from the founding committee before she forced it back on. The association launched *The Crisis* and chose Du Bois, not Wells, as its editor. The decision shows how the new organizations reproduced some of the inequalities they opposed. The movement against Jim Crow was not free of the assumptions of its time, and that fact belongs in the account without diminishing the achievement.[^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-race]

The 1905 and 1909 organizations belong to this chapter even though they are also milestones of the Progressive Era. Chapter 29 takes up Progressive reform and refers back to them rather than retelling their founding. The chapter’s endpoint is 1910, and the organizations it describes were built to last beyond it.[^ch26-check-ch29][^ch26-check-dates]

## 6. The Debate

The argument over how Jim Crow was made and enforced began while it was being made. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open. The debate is not a contest between villains and heroes; it is a disagreement about which mechanisms mattered most and how they fit together.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-check-table]

### 6.1 What is settled

The order was made by law as well as violence. Mississippi’s 1890 constitution, the 1883 *Civil Rights Cases*, *Plessy*, and *Williams v. Mississippi* together gave the new order a legal architecture, and the devices of disfranchisement were written as neutral rules applied by racial discretion.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-plessy-opinion][^ch26-williams]

The economic order was coercive. The crop-lien system bound laborers to the land through debt, and convict leasing leased Black prisoners to private employers under conditions that killed them. The coercion was not a residue of slavery that would disappear with time; it was organized by contracts, statutes, and courts.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing]

Lynching was enforced and documented. Wells’s record and the Wilmington casualty reports establish both the scale of the violence and the inadequacy of the surviving count. The record is incomplete, but the incompleteness is itself evidence of who was allowed to write.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington]

Black Southerners never stopped organizing. From Wells’s anti-lynching campaign to the Niagara Movement and the NAACP, Black men and women built institutions and arguments against the order throughout its making. They were not passive objects of the new order, and their resistance shaped what the order could do.[^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]

The order was regional and national at once. The South wrote the rules, but the Supreme Court, the national press, and northern investment made them durable. A southern order that had been rejected by the federal government at every turn could not have lasted; the retreat of the federal government was part of the order’s foundation.[^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-yawp-grady]

### 6.2 What is open

When did segregation begin? Woodward argued that rigid, statutory segregation took shape only in the 1890s, after a period of fluid and experimental race relations. His critics found segregation already present in the antebellum North, the antebellum urban South, and Reconstruction South Carolina. The question is both interpretive and chronological: what counts as segregation, and when did it become the rule?[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-woodward-career]

Why did the order arrive when it did? Woodward emphasized the reaction to the political disorder of the Populist Revolt by conservative Democrats and frustrated White Populists, and the turning of Populists against their Black allies. Later historians stress urbanization, gender and sexual anxiety, and the ambitions of a White urban middle class. Both mechanisms belong in the account and must be tested against the evidence. No single clock runs through every state.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-check-sources]

Who made disfranchisement? The “elite manipulation” account treats the disfranchising conventions as a project of political elites to remove a political threat. The “agrarian” account treats them as a response to the Populist revolt. Whether the decisive actor was the elite, the party, or the county registrar remains open, and the answer may vary by state.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-kirwan-apportionment]

How much agency did Black Southerners hold? Woodward’s early editions treated Black Southerners as “objects” rather than “subjects” of the segregation story, a charge his own later edition answered in part. Wells, Du Bois, and the organizers are the test. The evidence shows agency, but it also shows the limits that violence and debt placed on what agency could achieve.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-durocher-wells]

When should the era end? The 1910 endpoint is a choice of this chapter, not a claim that the order ended. 1896, 1898, 1901, and 1906 are competing markers, and anything after 1910 appears here as consequence. The endpoint is a claim about where this chapter stops.[^ch26-check-dates][^ch26-check-scope]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the new order was made at once by elite decision, by class conflict among Whites, by the mechanics of state law, by gendered and sexualized anxiety, and by the violence that enforced it — and in which Black Southerners shaped and resisted it throughout. No single explanation accounts for it, and the positions overlap rather than form teams.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-sources]

Woodward made segregation a problem with a history and a timeline, and gave the 1890s a central place in it. His book taught a generation that Jim Crow was not an eternal southern folkway but a political construction, and its title made the point in a phrase. The book’s influence is part of the history it describes.[^ch26-woodward-career][^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]

Work in the tradition of J. Morgan Kousser treats disfranchisement as a rational project of political actors who chose the device that removed the most opponents at the least cost to their own supporters. The state-building account explains why the conventions chose poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses rather than a simple racial bar: the devices were survivable in court and adaptable in administration.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-sources]

Glenda Elizabeth Gilmore and Grace Elizabeth Hale argue that the making of segregation ran through ideas about White womanhood, Black masculinity, and urban space, not through law alone. Their work, as summarized in the review cited here, explains the sexualized rhetoric of lynching and the popular culture of separation, and it connects the political history to the history of gender and the body.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]

Michael J. Pfeifer argues that lynching was a form of rough justice tied to local order, and the scholarship on Black resistance restores Wells, Du Bois, and the organizers to the center. The synthesis for this chapter is that the new order was built by law, capital, and violence and was contested at every step. The table that follows is a map of the arguments, not a set of teams.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-table]

## Legacies

The order built between 1877 and 1910 outlasted the people who made it. The disfranchising constitutions and amendments were not effectively struck down until the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and the segregation upheld in *Plessy* survived until *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954 and the civil rights laws that followed. The convict-lease system was abolished state by state, most before the Second World War but in places not until after it; its logic persisted in the prison labor and mass incarceration that followed.[^ch26-plessy-opinion][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-history-disfranchise]

The order also shaped where people could live. Disfranchisement and violence helped drive the Great Migration of the twentieth century, as Black Southerners left for northern and western cities. The migration was a consequence the evidence supports, not a single response to any one law. People left for work, safety, schools, and kin as well as for the ballot, and the order made every one of those reasons harder to pursue at home.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-check-sources]

The chapter’s central problem — how an order of racial domination was made and maintained — did not end in 1910. Its devices returned in new forms, and its memory was contested: Wilmington was called a race riot for a century before a state commission called it a coup, and the disfranchising conventions were described in their own time as reforms. That contested memory is part of the history, and the evidence on each side belongs in the account. The order was not a natural fact that historians later discovered; it was a political project that its makers defended in the language of law and reform.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-check-race]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The American South was not the only society to build legal segregation in these decades. South Africa’s segregationist order and its later apartheid ran on parallel ideas, and Woodward’s book itself compared the South to South Africa. The comparison belongs in a note rather than in a claim that the two systems were identical or that one caused the other.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Constitutions, court reports, registrars’ records, and newspaper accounts survive in abundance, while the words of the disfranchised and the lynched survive unevenly. Wells built her record from White compilations precisely because Black testimony was disbelieved. The asymmetry is named here rather than filled by speaking for the silenced.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 23 owns Reconstruction, Chapter 24 owns industrial transformation, Chapter 25 owns western conquest, Chapter 27 owns Populism and the 1898 empire, and Chapter 29 owns Progressive reform. These developments are cross-referenced, not re-narrated.[^ch26-check-ch23][^ch26-check-ch25][^ch26-check-ch27][^ch26-check-ch29]

## Summary

- In 1875 the “Mississippi Plan” used violence and fraud to restore White Democratic rule; about 50 Black Mississippians were killed, and Grant declined to intervene; the 1877 settlement withdrew the last federal troops.[^ch26-ms-plan-1875][^ch26-wikipedia-1877]
- The Supreme Court narrowed the Reconstruction amendments in the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883), upheld “separate but equal” in *Plessy* (1896), and validated Mississippi’s disfranchisement in *Williams* (1898).[^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-plessy-opinion][^ch26-williams]
- Emancipation left most Black Southerners without land; sharecropping and the crop-lien system bound them to the land through debt, and convict leasing leased Black prisoners to private employers under deadly conditions.[^ch26-duke-sharecropping][^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing]
- The “New South” promised northern investment and industrial growth; Birmingham became a major iron and steel center, but the region’s mills and furnaces rested on cheap child and convict labor.[^ch26-yawp-grady][^ch26-eoa-birmingham][^ch26-bls-child-labor]
- Mississippi’s 1890 constitution used a poll tax and Sec. 244’s literacy and understanding test to remove Black voters; the 1892 rolls listed 8,615 Black voters against 68,127 White voters, and one account puts the Black figure below 1,300 by the end of the decade.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-independent-1890][^ch26-ms-plan-1890]
- Other states followed, combining the poll tax, the literacy and understanding test, the grandfather clause, the property qualification, and the white primary; Louisiana’s Black registration fell from 130,344 in 1897 to 12,902 in 1898 and to 5,320 by 1900.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- Ida B. Wells documented lynching in *Southern Horrors* (1892) and *A Red Record* (1895), arguing that more than 10,000 Black people had been killed since emancipation and that mob deaths in 1894 outnumbered legal executions.[^ch26-wells-southern-horrors][^ch26-wells-red-record]
- In Wilmington in 1898 a White-supremacy campaign destroyed a Fusion government by massacre and coup; the death toll is disputed, from the coroner’s 14 to “as many as sixty” in the 2006 state report, all victims Black.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-eji-wilmington]
- Booker T. Washington’s 1895 Atlanta address made accommodation and industrial education the price of White tolerance; W. E. B. Du Bois answered in 1903 with the demand for “The right to vote. Civic equality. The education of youth according to ability.”[^ch26-washington-atlanta][^ch26-dubois-souls]
- The Niagara Movement (1905) and the NAACP (1909) turned the argument into organizations, with Wells among the NAACP’s forty founders and Du Bois editing *The Crisis*.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise][^ch26-durocher-wells]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1877 and 1910 the South’s leaders turned emancipation’s promise inside out. They redeemed the state by violence, leased the prisoners, kept the land, wrote exclusion into constitutions, upheld it in the courts, and enforced it with the rope. Yet the same decades produced Ida B. Wells’s record of the lynchings, Du Bois’s demand for the vote, and the organizations that carried the struggle into the next century. The chapter’s question — how racial domination became an institutional order — is answered by law, capital, violence, and the choices of people who refused to accept it.[^ch26-ms-1890-journal][^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-durocher-wells]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Chicago on July 9, 1896, where William Jennings Bryan told the Democratic National Convention that “you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.” The agrarian revolt that Bryan spoke for had once promised a politics that crossed the color line; Chapter 27 takes up what became of it, and of the republic’s turn to empire. The chapters overlap in time: this one carries the southern order past 1900, while Chapter 27 picks up the agrarian and imperial story in the 1890s.[^ch26-check-ch27]
