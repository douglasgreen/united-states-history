## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The orders and the expedition.** On June 8, 1874, former Union Brigadier General George Custer received written orders from General Alfred Terry to explore the Black Hills with his 7th Cavalry. The expedition, which set out from Fort Lincoln, Dakota Territory, on July 2, consisted of about 1,000 soldiers, 110 wagons, 70 Indian scouts, four reporters, and two gold miners.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **The treaty it violated.** The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 had granted the Lakota (Sioux) ownership of the Black Hills and established the Great Sioux Reservation. The expedition’s own scientific corps was legitimate under the treaty; the four journalists and two civilian miners were not.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-kirkby-black-hills][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **The gold report.** On August 2, 1874, the miners Horatio N. Ross and William T. McKay panned for gold, and Custer reported a discovery “right from the grass roots.” The *New York Times* published Custer’s report on August 22, describing gold “distributed throughout an extensive area within the Black Hills,” and reporters spread the news nationwide.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **The immediate doubt.** Members of the expedition were skeptical. Newton H. Winchell, the state geologist of Minnesota, later said he had not seen gold himself and doubted its quantity. Lieutenant Colonel Frederick Grant, the expedition’s second in command, wrote that the miners “showed the same pieces every day,” worth roughly two dollars, and concluded, “I don’t believe that any gold was found at all.” No member of the expedition left to prospect, and the rich deposits later mined at Deadwood and Lead lay in the northern hills, which the expedition never visited.[^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]
- **The name the Lakota gave it.** The expedition’s wagon-wheel track across the plains became known to the Lakota as the “Thieves Road.”[^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]
- **The aftermath.** In 1875 some 15,000 miners entered the Dakota Territory, many of them illegally; on November 3, 1875, President Ulysses S. Grant and his cabinet decided the Army would no longer stop settlers from entering the Black Hills; in December the Commissioner of Indian Affairs ordered all remaining Lakota outside the reservation to return by January 31, 1876, and declared those who refused hostile. The deadline passed, and the Great Sioux War began.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **What the scene can show.** The scene can show documented orders, a documented report of gold, documented public doubt, and a documented federal decision to stop enforcing the treaty. It cannot show the weather in the hills, the talk in Custer’s camp, or what any individual expedition member thought.[^ch25-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A report, not a discovery, opened the hills.** The scene’s claim is not that Custer found a fortune, but that a contested report of gold, published for a public still reeling from the Panic of 1873, was enough to license the taking of treaty land. The gap between what the miners could show and what the newspapers printed is the scene’s point.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question about title and enforcement: the Black Hills belonged to the Lakota by a treaty the United States had signed, the expedition entered in violation of that treaty, and the federal government soon declined to enforce it. Custer himself warned, in a letter to the *New York Times*, that the expedition was “goading the Indians to madness by invading their hallowed grounds.”[^ch25-kirkby-black-hills][^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: who held title to the West, and how was it enforced?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the Black Hills to the reservations, the railroads, the mines, the ranges, the schools, and the frontier story.

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The orders, the composition of the expedition, the gold report, the skeptics’ estimates, and the federal decisions are documented. The weather, the camp talk, and the private thoughts of Custer, his miners, or his officers must not be staged beyond what the record states.[^ch25-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** In 1874 no one knew that the Great Sioux War would begin in 1876, that Custer would die at the Little Bighorn, or that the Black Hills would be taken by an agreement of doubtful legality. The scene must preserve the uncertainty of the moment.[^ch25-check-scene][^ch25-check-dates]
- **No claim that the gold was a fraud or a windfall as a settled fact.** The evidence shows that gold traces were reported and that the quantity was doubted; the rich deposits lay elsewhere. The chapter should present the report, the doubt, and the later rush as three documented things, not collapse them into one verdict.[^ch25-kirkby-black-hills][^ch25-check-numbers]

## 1. Treaties and railroads

### 1.1 The treaty system and the Great Plains

- **The claim.** The United States governed the northern and central Plains through a treaty system that acknowledged Indigenous title even as it reduced it, and the treaties of 1851 and 1868 established the reservations that the wars and the allotment policy would later break apart.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **The 1868 treaty.** The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 opened, “From this day forward all war between the parties to this agreement shall for ever cease,” and set aside the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, for the Lakota. The government pledged to keep white settlers out of the reservation unless authorized.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie]
- **The provision that mattered.** The treaty restricted any further cession of Lakota land to a change approved by the required share of adult male Indians, which is why the later taking of the Black Hills was contested as a violation of its own terms. The exact article and its wording must be collated against the enrolled treaty before the chapter quotes the provision.[^ch25-check-treaty][^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie]
- **The consequence.** The treaty system gave the federal government a legal vocabulary for both recognizing and extinguishing Indigenous title. The chapter should present the treaties as instruments with real force that were broken by the same government that signed them.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]

### 1.2 The railroads and the land grants

- **The claim.** The transcontinental railroads were built with federal land grants and public credit across lands the United States had promised to Indigenous nations, and they made the extraction of the western interior possible.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-ch24]
- **The line.** The Central Pacific Railroad was chartered in 1862 to build the western portion of the first transcontinental railroad, and the two lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869. The chapter should cite the specific land-grant statutes and the railroad’s relationship to the reservation boundaries rather than describing “the railroad” in general.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-dates]
- **The consequence.** The rail line tied the mines, ranges, and farms of the interior to eastern and world markets, and it carried the soldiers, agents, and settlers of the reservation era. It is the material link between this chapter and Chapter 24’s industrial transformation.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-ch24]

### 1.3 Chinese labor on the iron road

- **The claim.** Chinese immigrants built much of the western transcontinental line under conditions of unequal pay and dangerous work, and their labor belongs at the center of the western story rather than in a footnote.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]
- **The workforce.** The Central Pacific hired its first 50 Chinese workers in 1864 and, after they dispelled the managers’ assumptions, hired thousands more; by the end of construction about 10,000 Chinese laborers had worked on the line. They were paid on average about 30 percent less than white workers, were segregated in camps, and had to pay for their own lodging, food, supplies, and equipment. On June 24, 1867, all Chinese railroad workers from Cisco to Truckee, California, stopped work — at the time the largest organized labor strike in the United States — demanding higher wages, a ten-hour day, and shorter tunnel shifts; the company broke the strike within a week by cutting off food and supplies.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]
- **The cost.** While no accurate count exists, it is estimated that over 1,000 Chinese laborers died building the Central Pacific. Rockslides, explosions, exposure, violence, and avalanches claimed lives in the Sierra Nevada tunnels.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]
- **The record and its silence.** Some historians call the Chinese workers “Silent Spikes,” because none of their correspondence or diaries are known to survive, and the railroad’s own records systemically anonymized them. The chapter should name this silence rather than invent individual voices.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-race]

## 2. Mining, cattle, irrigation, and labor

### 2.1 Mining and the mining law

- **The claim.** Western hardrock mining was a private enterprise licensed by federal law and financed by distant capital, and it reorganized landscapes, labor, and Indigenous homelands in a few decades.[^ch25-shpo-comstock]
- **The Comstock.** Silver was discovered near Virginia City, Nevada, in 1859, and the Comstock Lode — a deposit some two and a half miles long — made Virginia City boom for two decades, helped bring Nevada into the Union in 1864, and helped finance San Francisco. The chapter should give the production figures with their source and units, since the totals are reported inconsistently.[^ch25-shpo-comstock][^ch25-check-numbers]
- **The law.** The General Mining Act of 1872 governed the location and patenting of hardrock claims on federal land, and it remained the basic mining statute long after 1900. The chapter should cite the statute’s terms rather than a general description of “free mining.”[^ch25-check-numbers][^ch25-check-dates]
- **The limit.** The chapter should distinguish the mining rushes of the 1860s from the industrial mining of the 1880s and 1890s, and should name the Mexican and Chinese miners and the Indigenous nations whose lands the mines occupied.[^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican][^ch25-check-race]

### 2.2 The cattle range and its collapse

- **The claim.** The open-range cattle boom was a speculative, overstocked enterprise that destroyed itself against weather and barbed wire, and its collapse was an environmental event as much as a financial one.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]
- **The mechanism.** By the early 1880s the ranges of the southern Plains were overstocked and the grasses depleted. Ranchers built barbed-wire drift fences to keep cattle from drifting south in winter; by 1885 fences stretched across the northern Texas Panhandle.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]
- **The disaster.** Beginning in late December 1885, a series of blizzards struck the southern Plains. Cattle drifting south were stalled against the drift fences and froze or smothered in bunches, some 400 yards across; when the storms ended in January 1886, thousands of dead cattle were piled against the fences, and entire herds were almost wiped out. The industry never fully recovered the open range.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]
- **The limit.** The “Big Die-Up” was not simply a natural disaster: overstocking, speculation, and fencing were human decisions that made the herds vulnerable. The chapter should show that mechanism rather than treating the weather as an act of God.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]

### 2.3 Aridity and the irrigation question

- **The claim.** Much of the West could not be farmed like the East, and federal land law ignored that fact until reformers proposed a different geography of settlement.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-loc-powell]
- **Powell’s warning.** In his 1878 *Report on the Lands of the Arid Region of the United States*, John Wesley Powell argued that beyond the 100th meridian rainfall averages less than 20 inches a year, too little for most crops, and that the 160-acre homestead was badly adapted to aridity. He recommended smaller farms organized around communal irrigation and, more generally, a pastoral economy of sheep and cattle ranches, and he predicted that the Plains would suffer repeated, disastrous droughts.[^ch25-plains-powell]
- **The reception.** Federal policy proceeded largely without his advice, and irrigation development came later through the states and the Reclamation Act of 1902. The chapter should date the reclamation program correctly and not place it inside the pre-1900 narrative except as consequence.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-check-dates]
- **The consequence.** Aridity is the environmental limit the chapter’s later sections must respect: settlement, mining, and ranching all depended on water that the region did not reliably supply.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-worster-rivers]

### 2.4 Mexican and Chinese labor

- **The claim.** The mining, railroad, and agricultural economy of the Southwest and the Pacific coast depended on Mexican and Chinese labor, organized through segregated work camps and enforced wage discrimination.[^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican][^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]
- **The Mexican labor force.** Mexican-born workers filled the commercial agriculture, mining, smelting, and railroad jobs of the Southwest. They lived in segregated rural *colonias* and urban *barrios*, and they were paid less than workers of other groups similarly employed. By the account of a 1912 survey, Mexican section hands on the Santa Fe earned $1.00 to $1.25 a day, and the Mexican-born population of the border states rose from 66,312 in 1880 to 99,969 in 1900.[^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican]
- **The Chinese labor force.** Chinese immigrants worked in the gold mines, agriculture, and the garment industry as well as on the railroads, and they faced organized hostility as their numbers grew. The chapter should connect Chinese labor in the West to the exclusion policy that Chapter 24 treats as a national labor and immigration law.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-ch24]
- **The evidence and its limits.** The 1912 survey is a reform-era document that describes discrimination while reproducing its own racial assumptions; the chapter should read it as a period source with real data, not as a neutral description, and should name the missing voices in the record.[^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican][^ch25-check-race]

## 3. Plains and other western wars

### 3.1 Sand Creek and the terms of war

- **The claim.** The massacre at Sand Creek in 1864 set the terms of the wars that followed: a peaceful camp under federal protection was attacked by volunteer troops, and the survivors carried the memory into the reservation era.[^ch25-nps-sand-creek]
- **The event.** On the morning of November 29, 1864, about 750 Cheyenne and Arapaho people were encamped along Big Sandy Creek some 40 miles north of Fort Lyon, under leaders including Black Kettle and White Antelope. A Colorado volunteer force under Colonel John M. Chivington attacked the camp; more than 150 people, mostly women and children, were killed. The chapter should present Sand Creek as a prologue to the chapter’s period, clearly outside its 1865–1900 frame, and cite the National Park Service account with its own casualty qualifications.[^ch25-nps-sand-creek][^ch25-check-dates]

### 3.2 Red Cloud’s War and the 1868 settlement

- **The claim.** The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 was itself the settlement of a war: Lakota and allied resistance along the Bozeman Trail forced the United States to abandon its forts and recognize the Great Sioux Reservation.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-check-treaty]
- **The connection.** Red Cloud’s War and the 1868 treaty belong to the same decade as the transcontinental railroad and the treaties that opened the Plains. The chapter should state the sequence — trail, forts, war, treaty, reservation — and cite the treaty text for the promised boundaries.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-check-treaty]
- **The open check.** The chapter needs a direct, document-specific source for Red Cloud’s War and the commission’s negotiations; the consulted treaty text establishes the settlement, not the war’s course.[^ch25-check-sources]

### 3.3 The Great Sioux War and the Little Bighorn

- **The claim.** The federal decision to stop enforcing the 1868 treaty in the Black Hills produced the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, whose most famous battle was a Lakota and Cheyenne victory.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-nps-sitting-bull]
- **The course.** After the November 3, 1875, Cabinet decision and the December order to return by January 31, 1876, the deadline passed and the Great Sioux War began. On June 25, 1876, Custer and the 7th Cavalry were overwhelmingly defeated at the Battle of the Little Bighorn by Lakota and Cheyenne forces led by Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **Sitting Bull.** Sitting Bull, born into a prominent Hunkpapa Lakota family, had become a war leader and a spiritual figure, and he had said he had “seen nothing that the white man has … which is as good as our right to roam and live on the open plains as we choose.” He escaped to Canada in 1877, and Crazy Horse surrendered at Fort Robinson in Nebraska the same year. The chapter should give these dates against the sources and distinguish the military outcome from the political one.[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-check-dates]

### 3.4 The Nez Perce and Apache wars

- **The claim.** Beyond the northern Plains, the 1870s and 1880s wars in the Northwest and Southwest were fought over reservation boundaries and forced removal, and they produced long flights and lasting dispossession.[^ch25-nps-nez-perce][^ch25-nps-chiricahua]
- **The Nez Perce.** The Treaty of 1855 designated a Nez Perce reservation, and the Treaty of 1863 reduced it by 90 percent; even those who refused to sign were evicted. In 1877 the non-treaty Nez Perce, led by Chief Joseph, made a 126-day, 1,170-mile flight across four states. U.S. troops surprised a camp at the Big Hole on August 9, 1877, killing 60 to 90 Nez Perce men, women, and children, and the final battle came at Bear Paw, 40 miles south of the Canadian border, from September 30 to October 5, 1877. The people were separated afterward, some sent to Indian Territory in Oklahoma.[^ch25-nps-nez-perce]
- **The Apache wars.** In the Southwest, Geronimo’s final surrender came in Skeleton Canyon, Arizona, in the summer of 1886, after the U.S. Army committed thousands of troops to the pursuit over years. The Chiricahua Apache were held as prisoners of war rather than tried, taken to Fort Bowie and then by train to Florida, and kept apart from their families for years. The chapter should present the surrender as the end of a military campaign, not the end of Apache life.[^ch25-nps-chiricahua]

### 3.5 Wounded Knee, 1890

- **The claim.** The Ghost Dance of 1890 and the massacre at Wounded Knee were the violent culmination of the reservation and allotment policy, carried out amid drought and near starvation.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]
- **The context.** By 1890 reservations occupied poor land with little game, the bison herds had been destroyed, and the Ghost Dance — a revival blending Indigenous and millenarian teachings — spread across the Plains. Officials read the dance as insurrection; on November 13, 1890, President Benjamin Harrison ordered the army into the Sioux reservations, and one-third of the U.S. Army eventually deployed.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]
- **The killing.** On December 15, 1890, Indian police sent to arrest Sitting Bull shot and killed him. On December 28 a band of Ghost Dancers who had fled the Cheyenne River Reservation surrendered to Colonel James Forsyth’s 7th Cavalry at Wounded Knee Creek; the next morning, while troops searched for weapons, a shot was fired and the soldiers opened fire with rifles and four Hotchkiss guns on the mostly unarmed camp. Thirty troopers died; the burial party counted the bodies of 84 men, 44 women, and 18 children, and more died whose bodies were never counted. The chapter should call this a massacre, following the evidence, and present the casualty figures as reported counts with their sources.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee][^ch25-check-numbers]
- **The meaning.** The historian Louis S. Warren argues that Wounded Knee suppressed not a “naive” uprising but pragmatic people seeking a way forward under industrial capitalism and a growing federal bureaucracy. The chapter should use this interpretation to frame the massacre as policy, not accident.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

## 4. Boarding schools and the Dawes Act

### 4.1 Carlisle and the boarding-school policy

- **The claim.** The federal off-reservation boarding schools were an instrument of forced assimilation that separated children from their families and homelands and made the school itself part of the machinery of dispossession.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-dickinson-pratt]
- **The school.** The Carlisle Indian Industrial School, established in 1879 in Carlisle, Pennsylvania, was the first federally run, non-reservation boarding school for Indigenous children, and it operated until 1918. Its founder and first superintendent, Captain Richard Henry Pratt, popularized the phrase “kill the Indian in him, and save the man” in a speech delivered in 1892 to the National Conference of Charities and Correction in Denver.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-dickinson-pratt]
- **The mechanism.** Off-reservation schools forced the separation of children from their families, initiated them in English, Protestant, and market values, and framed citizenship as the reward for cultural surrender. The chapter should present this as a deliberate federal policy, not the work of one man.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-dickinson-pratt]

### 4.2 The Dawes Act of 1887

- **The claim.** The General Allotment Act of 1887 broke up reservation land held in common into individual plots, opened the “surplus” to non-Native settlement, and made allotment the legal engine of further land loss.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes]
- **The statute.** The act authorized the President to allot reservation lands in severalty: a quarter-section to each head of a family, an eighth-section to each single person over 18 and to each orphan, and a sixteenth-section to each other single person under 18. It held the allotted land in trust for 25 years, after which the United States would convey it in fee, and it declared that any Indian who received an allotment and adopted “the habits of civilized life” was a citizen of the United States.[^ch25-archives-dawes]
- **The consequence.** The policy stripped more than 90 million acres of tribal land, and the “surplus” land was sold to non-Native buyers. The allotments were often unsuitable for farming, and the acreages were comparable to those of the Homestead Act even though the Indigenous nations already controlled the land being divided. The chapter should contrast the two statutes.[^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-archives-homestead]
- **The limit.** The act exempted several nations and did not apply uniformly; the chapter should not describe allotment as a single nationwide event with a single date, and should note that Indigenous nations resisted, negotiated, and litigated.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-check-treaty]

### 4.3 In Focus: Choate’s paired portraits of Tom Torlino

- **The claim.** One pair of identified photographs shows how the boarding school staged its own claims about transformation, and how those photographs must be read as institutional evidence rather than as transparent records of one person.[^ch25-dickinson-torlino][^ch25-carrion-torlino]
- **In Focus: the object.** **In Focus.** Image 3 (`ch25-image-01`) is John N. Choate’s paired portraits of Tom Torlino, a Diné (Navajo) student at Carlisle, taken in 1882 and 1885. The caption printed with the pair reads: “TOM TORLINO – NAVAJO. AS HE ENTERED THE SCHOOL IN 1882. AS HE APPEARED THREE YEARS LATER.” The image appears in Choate’s *Souvenir of the Carlisle Indian School* (Carlisle, Pa.: J. N. Choate, 1902), held in the Dickinson College Archives and Special Collections as item CIS-I-0039.[^ch25-dickinson-torlino]
- **How to read it.** The art historian María Beatriz H. Carrión argues that the “before” pictures deliberately intensified the students’ cultural and racial markers, while the “after” photographs de-emphasized otherness by showing sitters in gendered uniforms and hairstyles; the school commissioned these photographs to promote its mission and raise funds. The box should read the pair as one composite, staged to manufacture a before and an after, and should say what the photographs cannot show: how Torlino understood the change, or what he lost.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-dickinson-torlino]
- **The evidence and its limits.** The archival record identifies the sitter, the photographer, the institution, and the print source, but the surviving descriptions are the school’s own. The chapter should not claim that the pictures document a real personal transformation, and it should name the silence around Torlino’s own account. If the reproductions cannot be cleared, the box reads the captions and the archival record in prose only.[^ch25-dickinson-torlino][^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-check-focus][^ch25-check-race]

### 4.4 The consequences of allotment and schooling

- **The claim.** Allotment, schooling, and the destruction of the bison worked together to destroy the material basis of Plains economies and to expose Indigenous nations to further land loss, while never extinguishing Indigenous political life.[^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]
- **The mechanism.** With the herds gone and the reservations allotted, many families could not support themselves on the land they received, and poverty and dependency compounded the effects of dispossession. The chapter should connect the boarding schools to the land question rather than treating them as separate subjects.[^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-carrion-torlino]
- **The resistance.** The chapter should state that the policy did not go unchallenged and that Indigenous nations continued to litigate, organize, and hold land and culture through the allotment era. The specific cases and their outcomes remain an open check against the sources.[^ch25-check-sources][^ch25-check-treaty]

## 5. Bison destruction, settlement, and the frontier story

### 5.1 The bison and the Plains economy

- **The claim.** The near-extinction of the American bison was a deliberate and rapid destruction of the material foundation of Plains nations, made possible by the railroad and the commercial hide trade.[^ch25-nps-bison]
- **The numbers.** In the early 1800s an estimated 30 to 60 million bison roamed North America; by 1900 fewer than 1,000 remained. The railroad transformed the plains by bringing large-scale commercial hunting and new markets for bison hides, meat, and bones to eastern cities.[^ch25-nps-bison]
- **The timing.** Bison hunting had effectively ceased by the early 1880s, and the only survivors lived in Yellowstone National Park, on a few private ranches, in Canada, or in zoos and Wild West shows. The chapter should give the estimates as estimates, with their sources and methods, since the figures vary widely.[^ch25-nps-bison][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee][^ch25-check-numbers]

### 5.2 Settlement: the Homestead Act and the land runs

- **The claim.** Federal land law converted the public domain into private farms and ranches, and the great land runs of 1889 opened lands previously reserved for Indigenous nations.[^ch25-archives-homestead][^ch25-okhistory-land-run]
- **The Homestead Act.** Passed on May 20, 1862, the Homestead Act granted adult heads of families 160 acres of surveyed public land for a minimal filing fee and five years of continuous residence and improvement.[^ch25-archives-homestead]
- **The land run.** On April 22, 1889, the government opened the two-million-acre “Unassigned Lands” of Indian Territory to settlement. An estimated 50,000 people surrounded the district; at noon, signals from troops, rifles, and cannon started the rush. An estimated 11,000 agricultural homesteads were claimed, and those who entered early — the “sooners” — generated hundreds of contests, one of which reached the Supreme Court in *Smith v. Townsend* (1892). The chapter should make plain that the land had been set aside for Indigenous nations and was being opened over their claims.[^ch25-okhistory-land-run]
- **The Exodusters.** Black southerners also sought the western land. The 1879 “Great Exodus” brought about 6,000 African Americans from Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas to Kansas, joining earlier migrants; Kansas’s Black population rose from about 16,250 in 1870 to 43,110 in 1880, and the town of Nicodemus became the best-known Black settlement. The chapter should present the Exodusters as people claiming the same land laws on their own terms, and should not treat the West as a White story.[^ch25-nps-exodusters][^ch25-check-race]

### 5.3 The Census of 1890 and the “closing” of the frontier

- **The claim.** The Census of 1890 reported that there was no longer a frontier line of settlement, and that official statement became the occasion for a new national story about the meaning of the West.[^ch25-yawp-turner][^ch25-tah-turner]
- **The statement.** Turner opened his 1893 address by quoting the Superintendent of the Census: “Up to and including 1880 the country had a frontier of settlement, but at present the unsettled area has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line.” Turner called this “the closing of a great historic movement.”[^ch25-yawp-turner][^ch25-tah-turner]
- **The turn.** The census line measured the density of White settlement, not the end of Indigenous presence or of conflict. The chapter should read the “closing” as a claim about a particular definition of settlement, exactly the kind of claim the debate section tests.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]

### 5.4 Buffalo Bill and the frontier story

- **The claim.** The Wild West show turned the West into a national and international entertainment, and it fixed a myth of conquest as adventure that outlived the events it depicted.[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull][^ch25-archive-limerick]
- **The showman.** William F. Cody (“Buffalo Bill”) built a Wild West exhibition that toured the United States and Europe, and he even presented Sitting Bull for a season after the Lakota leader’s surrender and years in Canada. The chapter should cite a document-specific source for the show’s history and for Cody’s relationship with Sitting Bull, which remains an open check.[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull][^ch25-check-sources]
- **The function.** The frontier story gave the conquest a heroic form at the very moment the census declared the frontier closed. The chapter should present the myth as evidence of how the conquest was remembered, not as a description of what happened.[^ch25-archive-limerick][^ch25-tah-turner]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The treaty system and its breach are documented.** The United States signed treaties that recognized Indigenous title, including the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty, and then took land, including the Black Hills, without the consent the treaties required.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- **The federal and corporate machinery of extraction is documented.** The railroads received public land and built across Indigenous homelands, and mining, ranching, and irrigation reorganized the western economy around distant markets.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-shpo-comstock][^ch25-plains-powell]
- **The violence is documented.** Sand Creek, the Great Sioux War, the Nez Perce and Apache wars, and Wounded Knee are documented campaigns and massacres, with reported casualty counts that can be cited.[^ch25-nps-sand-creek][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-nps-nez-perce][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]
- **The land loss is documented.** The Dawes Act allotted reservation land and opened the surplus, and the allotment policy stripped more than 90 million acres from Indigenous nations.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes]
- **The bison were destroyed.** The herds fell from an estimated 30 to 60 million to fewer than 1,000 by 1900, and Plains nations lost the economic base of their independence.[^ch25-nps-bison]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the West a process, a place, or a conquest?** Turner treated the frontier as a process that ended in 1890; Limerick and the New Western historians treated the West as a place whose conquest continued. The difference is not only interpretive but chronological.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]
- **How much did the federal government make the West?** Whether the West is best explained by federal law, subsidy, and coercion, or by environment and local initiative, remains debated.[^ch25-white-misfortune][^ch25-plains-powell]
- **How much power did Indigenous nations hold?** Hämäläinen and others argue that nations such as the Lakota and the Comanche built empires and shaped the colonial order, rather than merely resisting it; the chapter should test that claim against the wars and treaties it narrates.[^ch25-hamalainen-lakota]
- **How limiting was the environment?** Worster treats the arid West as a “hydraulic empire” built on federal water projects; whether aridity is a constraint or was overcome by capital and the state is a central disagreement.[^ch25-worster-rivers]
- **When should the era be said to end?** The chapter’s 1900 endpoint is a planning choice; the 1890 Census, Wounded Knee, and the 1890 Oklahoma runs offer competing markers, and the chapter must label anything after 1900 as consequence.[^ch25-check-dates][^ch25-check-scope]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the West was made at once by treaty and its breach, by corporate and federal extraction, by the labor of Mexicans, Chinese, and Exodusters, and by Indigenous nations who shaped and resisted the colonial order; no single explanation accounts for it, and the chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch25-white-misfortune][^ch25-hamalainen-lakota][^ch25-check-sources]
- **The Turner position.** Turner argued that the existence of free land and its recession explained American development, and that the frontier produced individualism and democracy; the thesis made the West a process and gave it a national meaning.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-yawp-turner]
- **The conquest position.** Limerick rejected the process and its supposed end, treated the West as a real place full of different peoples, and compared the legacy of conquest to the legacy of slavery.[^ch25-archive-limerick][^ch25-ushistoryscene-limerick]
- **The political-economy position.** Richard White treats the West as a region organized by federal power, corporate capital, and the labor of diverse peoples, and his survey is the standard synthesis of that approach. It was consulted only through descriptions for this outline.[^ch25-white-misfortune][^ch25-check-sources]
- **The environmental and Indigenous positions.** Worster reads western development through water and federal power, and Hämäläinen restores Indigenous nations to the center as imperial powers in their own right. Both were consulted only through reviews or descriptions for this outline.[^ch25-worster-rivers][^ch25-hamalainen-lakota][^ch25-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The West was conquered and also made: by treaties and their breach, by capital and federal law, by migrant and coerced labor, and by the Indigenous nations who persisted. The chapter should state each mechanism with its evidence.[^ch25-white-misfortune][^ch25-hamalainen-lakota][^ch25-check-table]

## Legacies

The instruments of conquest built in these decades outlasted them. The reservations, the railroad grants, the mining law of 1872, and the allotment policy of 1887 fixed the legal geography of the West, and the boarding schools left a legacy of family separation and cultural loss that later generations would document and litigate.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-shpo-comstock]

The chapter’s land question did not close in 1890. The Black Hills were taken from the Lakota by an agreement whose legality remains disputed, and in the twentieth century the Lakota brought the claim to the courts and refused the money awarded in compensation. The exact holding and the Lakota refusal must be cited to the court records before the chapter states them.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-check-sources]

The chapter’s environmental question remains open as well. The aridity Powell described still governs the West, and the water systems later built along the Colorado and other rivers made possible a growth that the region’s rainfall could not have sustained. The frontier story that Cody’s shows and Turner’s essay fixed continued to be invoked in American politics long after the frontier line was declared gone.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-worster-rivers][^ch25-ushistoryscene-limerick]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The United States was not the only settler society to dispossess Indigenous nations through treaties, reservations, and schools; Canada, Australia, and Argentina ran parallel programs in the same decades. The chapter should place the American case beside those cases without claiming that they were identical or causally linked.[^ch25-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Treaty records, military reports, agency correspondence, and school photographs survive in abundance, while the words of the dispossessed survive unevenly, and the Chinese railroad workers left no known letters at all. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-dickinson-torlino][^ch25-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 17 owns earlier removal; Chapter 23 owns Reconstruction’s western connection; Chapter 26 owns the southern racial order; Chapter 29 owns conservation and Progressive reform. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch25-check-ch17][^ch25-check-ch23][^ch25-check-ch26]

## Summary

- On August 2, 1874, miners on Custer’s Black Hills expedition reported finding gold, Custer published the news, and the report — doubted even within the expedition — helped open a treaty-protected Lakota homeland to a rush.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]
- The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 recognized the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, and restricted further cessions; the United States stopped enforcing it in 1875, and the Great Sioux War followed.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]
- About 10,000 Chinese laborers built the western transcontinental railroad, paid on average about 30 percent less than white workers; their June 1867 strike was the largest U.S. labor strike of its time, and more than 1,000 are estimated to have died.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]
- The Comstock Lode made Virginia City boom after 1859; the open range collapsed in the winters of 1885–86; and Powell’s 1878 report warned that the arid West could not be settled on the 160-acre model.[^ch25-shpo-comstock][^ch25-tsha-big-die-up][^ch25-plains-powell]
- The Plains and far-western wars included the 1868 settlement of Red Cloud’s War, the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, the Nez Perce flight of 1877, Geronimo’s surrender in 1886, and the massacre at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-nps-nez-perce][^ch25-nps-chiricahua][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]
- The Carlisle school opened in 1879 under Pratt’s “kill the Indian … and save the man” policy, and the Dawes Act of 1887 allotted reservation land and opened the surplus to non-Native buyers.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes]
- Between an estimated 30 and 60 million bison in the early 1800s, fewer than 1,000 remained by 1900, and the Homestead Act and the 1889 Oklahoma land run converted the public domain into private farms.[^ch25-nps-bison][^ch25-archives-homestead][^ch25-okhistory-land-run]
- Exodusters brought about 6,000 Black southerners to Kansas in 1879, and Kansas’s Black population rose from about 16,250 in 1870 to 43,110 in 1880.[^ch25-nps-exodusters]
- The Census of 1890 reported no frontier line, and Turner’s 1893 address made that report the basis of a national frontier thesis that Limerick’s *Legacy of Conquest* later rejected.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]

> *A closing note.* The decades after the Civil War took the West from the nations that held it by treaty and turned it into a reserve of land, minerals, water, and myths. The treaties were signed and broken; the railroads and mines were built by Mexican and Chinese and Black labor as well as White; the Plains nations lost the bison and then the land; the schools took the children; and the census declared the frontier gone while the conquest continued. The chapter’s question — who held title, and how was it enforced — was answered in land, in law, and in violence, and it was never answered once.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Mississippi in 1890, where a state convention wrote a new constitution that stripped Black men of the vote. The instruments of exclusion that had organized the West through treaty, allotment, and schooling were being matched in the South by instruments of racial rule; Chapter 26 takes up the South, race, and the new order.[^ch25-check-ch26][^ch25-check-bridge]
