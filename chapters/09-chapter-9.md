## Epigraphs

> “Why does not this imaginary representation extend to America, as well as over the whole island of Great-Britain? If it can travel three hundred miles, why not three thousand? if it can jump over rivers and mountains, why cannot it sail over the ocean?”[^ch09-jenyns-objections]

> “That it is inseparably essential to the freedom of a people, and the undoubted rights of Englishmen, that no taxes should be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.”[^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]

> “Opposition thought, in the form it acquired at the turn of the seventeenth century and in the early eighteenth century, was devoured by the colonists. From the earliest years of the century it nourished their political thought and sensibilities.”[^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer]

- **Why these three.** Soame Jenyns, a member of Parliament and of the Board of Trade, restates the theory of virtual representation: the colonists are represented in Parliament as the unrepresented towns of England are, so Parliament may tax them. The Stamp Act Congress answers that consent must be given personally or by representatives the taxed choose. Bailyn steps back and argues that the colonists argued from a long-absorbed opposition ideology, not from sudden grievance. The three disagree about where lawmaking authority lay, whether the colonies could be represented at a distance, and whether the crisis was about interests or about ideas.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Jenyns is quoted from a course transcription of the 1765 pamphlet; it must be collated against a printed or digitized edition of the original. The Declaration of Rights is quoted from Encyclopedia Virginia’s transcription of Hezekiah Niles’s 1822 compilation, not from the congress’s own journal; the wording must be checked against the journal or a scholarly edition. Bailyn is quoted from the Pulitzer Prize Board’s spotlight, which reproduces his words; the sentence must be located in *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* before it is set in type.[^ch09-jenyns-objections][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration][^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer][^ch09-check-epigraphs]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the colonists were unrepresented in any legal sense, that virtual representation was a mere fiction, or that ideology by itself caused the Revolution. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The elm and the effigies.** On the morning of August 14, 1765, a crowd hung one or more effigies from a great elm near Boston’s South End, at the corner of what are now Washington and Essex Streets. The tree became known as the Liberty Tree. The effigies represented Andrew Oliver, the colony’s designated stamp distributor and a brother-in-law of Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson. Reports disagree about whether a second or third figure — a boot, a devil, or an unpopular minister — hung beside it, and the scene must say so rather than settle it.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson][^ch09-check-scene]
- **The day.** Hutchinson, who was also chief justice, sent the sheriff to cut the effigies down, but the crowd was too large and the sheriff withdrew. Oliver’s brother-in-law and others understood the notice attached to the figure as a warning, and Oliver began to fear for his house and family.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]
- **The night.** By nightfall a large crowd took the effigies down and paraded through the town, carrying them past the Town House and the province house. The crowd moved to Oliver’s dock, demolished a newly built brick building rumored to be intended as a stamp office, and then went to Oliver’s house, where it burned the effigies. Oliver’s family escaped shortly before. Within days Oliver resigned the distributorship.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]
- **The witnesses.** John Adams, then 30, wrote in his diary on August 15 that the destruction was “a very attrocious [sic] Violation of the Peace and of dangerous Tendency and Consequence,” even as he criticized the Oliver family’s concentration of offices. A Boston newspaper later distinguished the assembly of August 14 from the “unbridled Licentiousness” of the second riot on August 26, when Hutchinson’s own house was destroyed.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]
- **The setting and weather.** The scene’s documented furniture is the elm, the notice, the dock, the unfinished brick building, the crowd, and Oliver’s house. The sources do not describe the weather that day, and the scene must not invent it.[^ch09-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A documented protest, not a tableau.** The scene should open at the elm in the morning and end at Oliver’s house that night, holding the two moments apart so the reader sees a crowd hanging a figure and then a crowd burning it. Every action and place named should be one the record supports.
- **The crowd had names and a structure.** A shoemaker, Ebenezer Mackintosh, led a South End gang that joined the protest, and better-placed organizers later known as the Loyal Nine helped prepare it. The scene should not reduce the crowd to an anonymous “mob” or pretend that its leadership is fully documented; Peter Oliver’s later claim that Mackintosh commanded a “Mob of 2000 men” is a Loyalist accusation, not a neutral count.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-check-names]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: How did imperial reform become a crisis of authority?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that the colonists rejected all British authority in 1765. Oliver resigned and the crowd dispersed; most colonists still claimed the rights of English subjects and the protection of the crown. Reform collided with colonial political practice and with ideas of representation, and the collision damaged the authority of both Parliament and the governor.
- **The widening.** The scene sits in Boston in August 1765. The framing paragraph follows and sends the reader to London, to the colonial assemblies from Virginia to Massachusetts, to the ports, and to the newspapers that carried the news.[^ch09-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- No invented weather, streetscape, or dialogue, and no interior thoughts. The record names the elm, the notice, the dock, the unfinished building, and Oliver’s house.[^ch09-check-scene]
- No settled count of the effigies or the crowd. Sources disagree on how many figures hung from the tree and on how many people joined the night’s procession.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-check-scene]
- No claim that Oliver was personally corrupt, that every protester opposed all British rule, or that the protest was simply an artisan uprising or simply elite orchestration. Both popular anger and elite organization are visible, and the scene should hold them together.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-check-names]
- No claim that the August 26 attack on Hutchinson’s house was planned as part of the August 14 protest; the sources treat them as distinct episodes with different motives.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]

## 1. Debt and reform

### 1.1 The war debt and the case for colonial revenue

- **The claim.** The crisis began as a fiscal problem. Victory in the Seven Years’ War left Britain with an enlarged empire and a debt that made colonial revenue attractive, and the peacetime army the empire kept in America gave the argument a standing rationale.
- **The debt.** Britain’s national debt roughly doubled across the war. The compilations give the debt stock at about £70–75 million in 1756 and about £133–140 million in 1763, with interest consuming several million pounds a year. These are estimates from different series, and the chapter must present them as a range with sources, not as a single exact figure.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-check-revenue]
- **The peacetime establishment.** Grenville’s predecessor had recommended keeping about 10,000 soldiers in America at an estimated £200,000 a year, and Pontiac’s War made the case for a garrison seem urgent. The colonies, by contrast, paid low taxes relative to Britons at home, and the Board of Trade estimated heavy smuggling. The chapter should present the British case as its proponents made it: the colonists benefited from the war and should help pay for its consequences.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-jyf-stamp]
- **The consequence.** Reform was not invented as a plot against liberty. It was a solution to a real problem, and that is what made it politically dangerous: the measures came from a government that believed it was acting reasonably.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

### 1.2 The Grenville program, 1764–1765

- **The claim.** Between 1764 and 1765 Grenville’s ministry moved from trade regulation toward direct taxation, and the shift, not any single duty, produced the crisis.
- **The Sugar Act.** The American Revenue Act received royal assent on April 5, 1764, and took effect in September. It cut the duty on foreign molasses from sixpence to threepence a gallon, retained high duties on foreign sugar, prohibited foreign rum, and added duties on wine, coffee, and other goods. More than half its text concerned enforcement: bonds, affidavits, Royal Navy searches, and trial in a vice-admiralty court at Halifax rather than by a local jury.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- **The Currency Act.** Passed on April 19, 1764, and effective September 1, it banned colonial paper currency as legal tender, requiring the new duties to be paid in hard money. Together with the Sugar Act, it tightened credit in a postwar slump.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- **The Quartering Act.** Passed in 1765, it required colonial authorities to provide quarters and supplies for British troops. It became a grievance in New York and later in Boston.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should treat the Grenville measures as a program with several parts, and should show which parts angered which colonies. New England resented the molasses duty and the currency restriction; the southern and middle colonies felt the trade provisions differently.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-jyf-stamp]

### 1.3 In Focus: the crisis in figures

**In Focus.** Figure 1 plots two quantities that contemporaries argued about but rarely compared directly: the stock of British public debt and the annual revenue Parliament hoped to raise in the colonies. Plotted together, they show why a small colonial revenue mattered politically out of proportion to its size. The debt was a stock that had roughly doubled in seven years; the colonial duties were flows that, even by the ministry’s optimistic estimates, were small. The Townshend duties of 1767 were expected to raise little more than £40,000 a year, and their author treated them as a beginning rather than a solution. Read carefully, Figure 1 is not a graph of colonial burden; it is a graph of British ambition. It shows the scale of the problem the ministry was trying to solve and the modesty of the instruments it chose, which is exactly the mismatch that made the taxes feel like a matter of principle rather than of money. Every series requires verification, and the chapter must label debt stocks and annual flows separately rather than merging them.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-our-rev-townshend][^ch09-check-figure]

## 2. The Stamp Act

### 2.1 The statute and the schedule

- **The claim.** The Stamp Act of 1765 was the first general internal tax Parliament laid on the colonies, and its long schedule of duties is the best evidence of what it actually did.
- **The statute.** Parliament passed the act on March 22, 1765, for “granting and applying certain stamp duties, and other duties, in the British colonies and plantations in America, towards further defraying the expences of defending, protecting, and securing the same.” Its duties took effect on November 1, 1765.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- **The schedule.** The act taxed court papers, deeds, bonds, probates, licenses, land grants, apprentices’ indentures, pamphlets, newspapers, advertisements, almanacs, playing cards, and dice. It charged three pence on a court pleading, two pence on a bond up to ten pounds, a halfpenny on a half-sheet newspaper and a penny on a full-sheet newspaper, two shillings on an advertisement, a shilling on a pack of cards, and ten shillings on a pair of dice. Documents in a language other than English paid double.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]
- **The enforcement.** Unstamped matter could not be “pleaded or given in evidence” in court, and using unstamped paper carried a ten-pound forfeiture. The design was to make the stamp unavoidable in ordinary legal and commercial life, which is why it touched people who never saw a customs officer.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]
- **The consequence.** The schedule explains the breadth of the protest. The tax fell on printers, lawyers, merchants, tavern-keepers, and card players at once, and it made the newspaper press both a target and a mouthpiece.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]

### 2.2 The assemblies answer: Virginia and the Stamp Act Congress

- **The claim.** The colonial response came first from the assemblies, in the language of English rights, and it produced the first coordinated intercolonial meeting of the era.
- **The Virginia Resolves.** On May 29, 1765, Patrick Henry introduced resolves in the House of Burgesses. They claimed for Virginians the privileges of English subjects born in the realm and concluded that the General Assembly of Virginia “have the only and sole exclusive Right & Power to lay Taxes & Impositions upon the Inhabitants of this Colony.” The House softened the resolves the next day, but the full set had already been printed and circulated.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-jyf-stamp]
- **The congress.** Delegates from nine colonies met at the Stamp Act Congress in New York from October 7 to 25, 1765. On October 19 they adopted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances. It affirmed allegiance to the crown and due subordination to Parliament, then held that “no taxes should be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives,” and that the colonists could not be represented in the House of Commons.[^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration][^ch09-check-names]
- **The merchants and the streets.** In the same weeks, crowds and committees pressed stamp distributors to resign, and merchants adopted non-importation agreements. On October 31, 1765, New York merchants resolved to countermand orders for British goods “unless the Stamp Act be repealed,” and retailers pledged not to buy goods shipped after January 1 unless the act fell.[^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]
- **The consequence.** By the end of 1765 the colonists had produced three instruments at once: assembly resolves, a congress declaration, and market pressure. The chapter should show that these were different tools used by different people, not a single movement.[^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration][^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]

### 2.3 In Focus: a stamp, the paper, and the statute

**In Focus.** Few stamped papers survive from the American colonies, because the act was never fully enforced and the stamps were withdrawn after repeal. What survives is often a printer’s proof or a loose stamp rather than a document that circulated. The Museum of the American Revolution holds a paper stamp of about 1765, printed in Britain and meant to be attached to taxed documents, and the National Postal Museum holds a proof sheet marked with the red imprint of Saint Edward’s Crown, struck before production began. Set beside the proof, the statute’s schedule explains what the mark meant: a halfpenny on a half-sheet newspaper, a penny on a full sheet, two pence on a single-sheet almanac, a shilling on a pack of cards. Read closely, the objects and the statute tell one story. The stamp was not merely a receipt; it was a condition of legality, since unstamped matter could not be used in court. And the proof sheet shows a tax being manufactured in London to be sold in America, which is why colonists called it a tax imposed without their consent. The chapter should name the objects and their holding institutions and avoid presenting any image as the frame unless it is separately registered.[^ch09-amrev-stamp-object][^ch09-ev-stamp-proof][^ch09-stamp-act-avalon][^ch09-check-focus]

### 2.4 The crisis of authority in the streets

- **The claim.** Between August 1765 and the spring of 1766, crowds went beyond petition and resolution, and the chapter must show what that shift did to the authority of governors and Parliament.
- **The two August riots.** The August 14 protest hung and burned Oliver in effigy and ended with his resignation. On August 26 a crowd attacked the houses of customs officials and then destroyed Thomas Hutchinson’s mansion, carrying off furniture, silver, and about £900 in cash; Hutchinson put his loss above £3,000 sterling. He called it the “most barbarous outrage that ever was committed in America.”[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]
- **The debate over the mob.** Patriots and printers argued about whether the violence helped or harmed the cause. A Boston newspaper conceded that “the Cause of Liberty requires an extraordinary Spirit to support it” yet insisted that “pulling down Houses and robbing Persons of their substance” was “utterly inconsistent with the first Principles of Government.” The chapter should quote both sides.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]
- **The consequence.** The crowds made the stamp impracticable and cost the governor the initiative. After August 1765, enforcing the act in Massachusetts required the army, not the sheriffs, which set up the occupation of 1768.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-check-names]

## 3. Repeal and the Declaratory Act

### 3.1 The repeal of 1766

- **The claim.** Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in March 1766 because of pressure from British merchants and manufacturers, not because it conceded the colonists’ constitutional argument.
- **The repeal.** An act repealing the stamp duties received assent on March 18, 1766, and voided the 1765 statute from May 1, 1766. Its preamble gave the reason: the continuance of the act “would be attended with many inconveniencies, and may be productive of consequences greatly detrimental to the commercial interests of these kingdoms.”[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon]
- **The motive.** The repeal followed a campaign by merchants whose American debts and orders were at risk. The chapter should present the repeal as the product of commercial interest and colonial disorder at once.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-morgan-commonplace]
- **The consequence.** Repeal calmed the ports but settled nothing. Parliament gave up the tax and kept the principle.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon]

### 3.2 The Declaratory Act

- **The claim.** On the same day it repealed the Stamp Act, Parliament declared its authority to bind the colonies “in all cases whatsoever,” and that claim became the central constitutional question of the decade.
- **The declaration.** The Declaratory Act of March 18, 1766, declared that the colonies “have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate unto, and dependent upon the imperial crown and parliament of Great Britain,” and that Parliament had “full power and authority to make laws and statutes of sufficient force and validity to bind the colonies and people of America … in all cases whatsoever.”[^ch09-declaratory-avalon]
- **The wording.** The Avalon transcription has evident OCR faults (“had. bath, and of right ought to have” for “hath”), so the chapter must collate the quoted clause against a printed edition of the statute.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-check-dating]
- **The consequence.** The repeal satisfied the colonists’ practical grievance and the Declaratory Act preserved Parliament’s theoretical claim. The next crisis would test whether the two could be separated, and the answer, for the Townshend ministry, was that they could not.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-check-dating]

### 3.3 What repeal settled and what it left open

- **The claim.** The chapter should state plainly that 1766 produced a truce, not a settlement, and should not let the calm of 1766–67 obscure the unresolved question.
- **The truce.** Non-importation lapsed, the stamps were withdrawn, and colonial assemblies returned to ordinary business. But the assemblies’ resolves of 1765 had claimed for themselves the exclusive power to tax, and that claim was incompatible with the Declaratory Act.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]
- **The terms.** The quarrel was framed as “internal” versus “external” taxation, a distinction colonists such as Benjamin Franklin and John Dickinson drew and the ministry declined to accept. The chapter should define the distinction at first use and explain why it could not hold.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend]
- **The consequence.** The unresolved question — whether Parliament could tax the colonies at all — returned in 1767 with the Townshend duties, which were deliberately designed to look external.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend]

## 4. Townshend measures

### 4.1 The Revenue Act of 1767

- **The claim.** The Townshend Revenue Act of 1767 revived the fiscal project by dressing it as external trade duties, and it created the machinery of enforcement that made it more effective and more resented than the Stamp Act.
- **The act.** The act received assent on June 29, 1767, and took effect November 20. It laid duties on glass, red and white lead, painters’ colors, paper, pasteboard, china, and tea imported into the colonies from Great Britain, and it was accompanied by measures establishing an American Board of Customs Commissioners in Boston and tightening the vice-admiralty courts.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
- **The purpose.** The preamble named the objects: “the administration of justice, and the support of civil government” and “defending, protecting, and securing the said dominions.” Townshend intended the duties to pay governors and judges directly, freeing them from dependence on colonial assemblies, which aimed at the assemblies’ power as much as at their purses.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
- **The scale.** The duties were expected to raise little more than £40,000 a year. They were meant as a beginning, and their modest yield is part of the story: the issue was authority, not money.[^ch09-our-rev-townshend]
- **The consequence.** By funding officials directly and stationing the customs board in Boston, the Townshend program concentrated the conflict in Massachusetts.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

### 4.2 The Circular Letter and colonial coordination

- **The claim.** Massachusetts turned the new duties into a coordinated constitutional protest, and the ministry answered by threatening the assembly’s charter.
- **The letter.** On February 11, 1768, the Massachusetts House adopted a circular letter to the other colonial legislatures, drafted by Samuel Adams and James Otis. It conceded that Parliament was “the supreme legislative power over the whole empire,” but argued that the constitution limited that power, and that acts “imposing duties on the people of this province, with the sole and express purpose of raising a revenue, are infringements of their natural and constitutional rights.”[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon]
- **The response.** The letter asked the other assemblies to harmonize their measures, and several replied. The ministry demanded that Massachusetts rescind it; the House refused by a large majority, and the governor dissolved the assembly.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon][^ch09-check-names]
- **The consequence.** The circular letter was a constitutional argument made in the form of an intercolonial message, and it produced both coordination among the assemblies and a direct confrontation over the charter. The chapter should distinguish this assembly-led resistance from the crowd actions of 1765.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon]

### 4.3 Non-importation and the occupation of Boston

- **The claim.** When the duties took effect, merchants and artisans revived non-importation, and the ministry answered with troops, which brought imperial authority and the town face to face.
- **The boycott.** Colonial merchants again agreed not to import British goods, and town committees enforced the agreements by naming violators. Non-importation worked because it turned private consumption into a public test of loyalty.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
- **The occupation.** With the customs board in Boston and customs officers demanding naval protection, the ministry stationed British regiments in the town beginning in 1768. Troops quartered in a town of about 15,000, with about 4,000 soldiers present by 1770, made daily friction routine.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-gilder-revere]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should show that the occupation was a policy choice, not an accident, and that it made the enforcement of customs law a matter of street-level conflict.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]

## 5. Occupation and the Boston Massacre

### 5.1 The confrontation of March 5, 1770

- **The claim.** On the night of March 5, 1770, a crowd and a squad of soldiers met outside the Custom House on King Street, and the soldiers fired, killing five civilians and wounding several more.
- **The setting.** The evening was cold, with light snow on the ground. Private Hugh White stood sentry at the Custom House; a crowd taunted him and threw objects, and White called for help. Captain Thomas Preston led a small party to his aid, and the crowd surrounded the soldiers. A shot was fired, then several more.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]
- **The dead and wounded.** Five men died: Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray, James Caldwell, Samuel Maverick, and, days later, Patrick Carr. Six others were wounded, two of them fatally. Attucks, a sailor of African and Indigenous ancestry, was described in testimony as “mulatto” or “Indian”; he was the first to fall and became a martyr.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-nps-attucks]
- **The public response.** Samuel Adams led a funeral procession on March 8 that drew a reported 10,000 people, about two-thirds of the town. The victims were buried in a common plot at the Granary Burying Ground, and the day was commemorated for years afterward.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-masshist-massacre]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the shooting as the outcome of a specific confrontation, not as a planned massacre, while showing why colonists experienced it as one.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-check-dates]

### 5.2 The trials and the rule of law

- **The claim.** The colony tried the soldiers in civil courts, and John Adams helped defend them, winning acquittals that became a claim about law rather than loyalty.
- **The trials.** Captain Preston was tried first and acquitted. The eight soldiers were tried together in the fall of 1770; John Adams and Josiah Quincy appeared for the defense, while Robert Treat Paine and Samuel Quincy represented the deceased. Six soldiers were acquitted and two were convicted of manslaughter and branded on the hand.[^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-nps-boston-massacre]
- **Adams’s argument.** Adams argued that the soldiers had been attacked by a mob and had fired in self-defense. His willingness to defend them, and the acquittals, became evidence that the colonists wanted law and not vengeance. The chapter should quote the case accurately and not overstate it.[^ch09-masshist-massacre]
- **The consequence.** The trials separated the cause of liberty from the demand for retaliation, which strengthened the patriots’ constitutional claim even as it disappointed those who wanted convictions.[^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-check-names]

### 5.3 The prints and the memory

- **The claim.** The Boston Massacre became a national image because Paul Revere and others engraved it, and those prints shaped what later generations believed happened.
- **The engraving.** Paul Revere issued “The Bloody Massacre” in 1770, probably based on an engraving by Henry Pelham, which he got to market first. It shows an orderly line of soldiers firing into a defenseless crowd, with a poem and a legend; the scene did not match the testimony, which described a confused struggle, and the print omits or alters details such as the weather.[^ch09-masshist-massacre-visual][^ch09-gilder-revere]
- **The prints compared.** Several versions appeared in Massachusetts and London within two years, each shaped to express outrage and build support. Comparing them shows how quickly an event became an argument.[^ch09-masshist-massacre-visual]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should use the prints as evidence of how the Massacre was remembered, not as evidence of what happened on King Street. The distinction is the chapter’s method in miniature.[^ch09-gilder-revere][^ch09-check-names]

### 5.4 The partial repeal and the uneasy calm

- **The claim.** Parliament removed the Townshend duties in April 1770 but kept the duty on tea, and the colonists’ non-importation collapsed, leaving the constitutional question open.
- **The partial repeal.** On March 5, 1770, Lord North moved to repeal all the Townshend duties except the tax on tea. George Grenville argued for repealing the tea duty as well, calling the duties bad for trade, but his amendment failed. The repeal received Royal Assent on April 12, 1770.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal][^ch09-our-rev-townshend]
- **The reason for keeping tea.** North kept the tea duty to avoid the appearance of surrendering to colonial protest, which preserved the principle at the cost of the revenue. The compromise divided colonial merchants, who wanted trade to resume, from artisans, who wanted non-importation to continue while any duty stood.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
- **The consequence.** The years 1770–73 were quieter but not settled. The tea duty remained as a standing provocation, and it set up the crisis of 1773 that Chapter 10 takes up.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal][^ch09-check-ch10]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Parliament’s measures were a fiscal response to the war debt and the peacetime establishment.** The Grenville and Townshend programs sought colonial revenue and tighter enforcement, and their designers understood them as reasonable and necessary.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp][^ch09-our-rev-townshend]
- **The colonists denied that Parliament could tax them without their consent.** Assembly resolves, the Stamp Act Congress, and the Circular Letter all made the same claim in different forms.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration][^ch09-circular-letter-avalon]
- **Colonial resistance was both popular and organized.** Crowds, committees, merchants, and assemblies acted at once and sometimes in tension.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest][^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]
- **Repeal in 1766 settled the practice and not the principle.** The Declaratory Act preserved Parliament’s claim to bind the colonies in all cases whatsoever.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-declaratory-avalon]
- **The crisis did not make independence inevitable.** Through 1770 the dominant colonial claim was to English rights and the protection of the crown, not to separation.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon][^ch09-check-ch10]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Whether ideas or interests drove resistance.** Did colonists act from an absorbed ideology of liberty, from material grievance, from fear of slavery and corruption, or from some combination?[^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer][^ch09-nash-retrospective]
- **How far the crowd acted on its own.** Were the August riots elite-orchestrated, or did artisans and laborers press leaders into more radical action? The evidence is partial and mostly official.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]
- **Whether the colonists were genuinely unrepresented.** Virtual representation was a real constitutional theory in Britain, not merely a cynical dodge, and the chapter should present it fairly before the colonists’ answer.[^ch09-jenyns-objections]
- **What the numbers were.** The debt, the expected yield of the duties, and the size of the crowds and the losses vary across sources and must be given as estimates with attributions.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-our-rev-townshend][^ch09-check-revenue]
- **What the archives cannot show.** The crowds, the enslaved people they involved, and the Indigenous nations on the frontier appear mainly through the writings of officials and printers, and the chapter should name those mediations.[^ch09-check-names]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which structural pressures, competing legal theories, and contingent crowd actions all mattered, and in which no single cause explains the crisis.
- **Bailyn and ideology.** Bernard Bailyn’s *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (1967) argued that a radical-Whig or Commonwealth ideology, absorbed from English opposition writers, gave the colonists a coherent way to interpret the measures as a conspiracy against liberty. He denied that social and economic conflict by itself explained the Revolution.[^ch09-bailyn-pulitzer][^ch09-amrev-bailyn]
- **Morgan and constitutional principle.** Edmund S. and Helen M. Morgan’s *The Stamp Act Crisis* (1953) read the resistance as a principled defense of English liberties and constitutional government, and it remains the starting point for the crisis.[^ch09-morgan-commonplace][^ch09-morgan-jstor]
- **Nash and class.** Gary B. Nash’s *The Urban Crucible* (1979) located the origins of the Revolution in the economic and social transformation of the port cities, where the growth of poverty and inequality produced popular politics and class tension. Nash’s account explains why Boston was the most radical of the towns.[^ch09-nash-retrospective]
- **Hutchinson and parliamentary sovereignty.** Thomas Hutchinson insisted that the colonies must acknowledge the supremacy of Parliament, and he treated the assembly’s denial of it as a dangerous innovation; his position, like Jenyns’s, stated the imperial case from within America.[^ch09-hutchinson-speeches]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, the positions say that the crisis was at once a contest over ideas, a conflict of interests, and a collision between imperial administration and colonial practice. Later scholarship has questioned a hard dichotomy between ideas and interests, and the chapter should present the debate as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch09-bailyn-wiki][^ch09-check-sources]

## Legacies

The crisis left a constitutional question that no act had answered. Parliament gave up the Stamp Act in 1766 and the Townshend duties in 1770, but it kept the Declaratory Act and the tea duty, so the claim to bind the colonies “in all cases whatsoever” outlasted the taxes that had provoked the argument.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

It also left a repertoire. Resolves and declarations, a continental congress, non-importation, committees, and crowd action would be reused in every crisis from 1773 onward, and the memory of the Boston Massacre was institutionalized in annual orations at a time when the shooting itself had become a print.[^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon][^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-check-ch10]

And it left a town under arms. The troops who had occupied Boston after 1768 were withdrawn to Castle William after the Massacre, but the customs board remained, the tea duty remained, and in 1773 the same issues returned at Griffin’s Wharf. The crisis of 1765–70 did not end in independence; it ended in a truce that everyone understood to be temporary.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-check-ch10]

## Summary

- Britain’s debt roughly doubled in the Seven Years’ War, and the peacetime garrison gave the ministry a reason to seek colonial revenue.[^ch09-ari-debt][^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- The Grenville program of 1764–65 moved from trade regulation to direct taxation through the Sugar Act, the Currency Act, the Quartering Act, and the Stamp Act.[^ch09-nps-sugar-stamp]
- The Stamp Act of March 22, 1765, taxed a long schedule of legal papers, printed matter, cards, and dice, and made the stamp a condition of legality.[^ch09-stamp-act-avalon]
- The Virginia Resolves and the Stamp Act Congress claimed that the colonies could be taxed only with their own consent, given by representatives they chose.[^ch09-virginia-resolves][^ch09-stamp-congress-declaration]
- On August 14, 1765, a Boston crowd hung effigies of Andrew Oliver from the Liberty Tree and burned them outside his house that night; he resigned.[^ch09-rodwin-stamp-protest]
- On August 26, 1765, a crowd destroyed Thomas Hutchinson’s mansion, and Hutchinson estimated his loss above £3,000 sterling.[^ch09-colonialsociety-hutchinson]
- Parliament repealed the Stamp Act on March 18, 1766, for commercial reasons, and on the same day passed the Declaratory Act claiming authority to bind the colonies in all cases whatsoever.[^ch09-stamp-repeal-avalon][^ch09-declaratory-avalon]
- The Townshend Revenue Act of 1767 laid external duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea, funded royal officials directly, and placed a customs board in Boston.[^ch09-townshend-avalon][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
- The Massachusetts Circular Letter of 1768 made a constitutional argument to the other assemblies, and non-importation became the colonists’ principal economic weapon.[^ch09-circular-letter-avalon][^ch09-ny-nonimportation-avalon]
- British troops occupied Boston from 1768, and on March 5, 1770, soldiers fired on a crowd, killing five, including Crispus Attucks.[^ch09-nps-boston-massacre][^ch09-nps-attucks]
- John Adams helped defend the soldiers, and the trials ended in acquittals and two manslaughter convictions; Paul Revere’s engraving made the event an enduring image.[^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-masshist-massacre-visual]
- Parliament repealed the Townshend duties in April 1770 except the duty on tea, leaving the constitutional question open on the eve of the tea crisis.[^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]

> *A closing note.* The years from 1763 to 1770 turned a fiscal problem into a crisis of authority. A government that had won a world war asked its colonies to help pay for the peace, and it did so through duties that touched nearly every printed and legal page of colonial life. The colonists answered with resolves, a congress, non-importation, and crowds, and they framed their answer in the language of English rights. Parliament gave up the taxes but kept the principle, first in the Declaratory Act and then in the tea duty that survived 1770; the colonists tasted the power of coordinated resistance and kept the memory of the Massacre. Neither side had surrendered its understanding of who could make law for America, and that unresolved question, not any single tax, is what the chapter leaves standing.[^ch09-declaratory-avalon][^ch09-masshist-massacre][^ch09-history-townshend-repeal]
