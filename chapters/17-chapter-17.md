# Chapter 17. Removal, Indian Country, and the Mexican North (1820–1848)

> “It gives me pleasure to announce to Congress that the benevolent policy of the Government, steadily pursued for nearly thirty years, in relation to the removal of the Indians beyond the white settlements is approaching to a happy consummation.”[^ch17-jackson-removal-message]

> — Andrew Jackson, Second Annual Message to Congress, December 6, 1830[^ch17-jackson-removal-message]

> “We are denationalized; we are disfranchised. We are deprived of membership in the human family! We have neither land nor home, nor resting place that can be called our own.”[^ch17-nps-ross-protest]
>
> — John Ross, “To the Senate and House of Representatives,” Red Clay Council Ground, Cherokee Nation, September 28, 1836[^ch17-nps-ross-protest]

> “The Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community, occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force.”[^ch17-worcester]
>
> — Chief Justice John Marshall, *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832)[^ch17-worcester]

> “‘Indian Removal’ … was an unusual construction that left unstated who was removing whom.”[^ch17-saunt-review]
>
> — Claudio Saunt, *Unworthy Republic* (2020), p. xiii, quoted in a scholarly review[^ch17-saunt-review]

Four voices, four answers. A president told Congress that dispossession was benevolence near its completion. A principal chief answered that the same policy had stripped his people of every attribute of law and membership. A chief justice held that the Cherokee were a distinct community beyond Georgia’s reach. A modern historian argued that the phrase “Indian Removal” hid the agent and the deed. They disagree about whether removal was humane or coercive, lawful or lawless, inevitable or chosen. The question is not which voice was right. It is how sovereignty survived when federal and state power rejected it.[^ch17-check-epigraphs]

New Echota sat at the headwaters of the Oostanaula River, in the Cherokee Nation, on land the state of Georgia claimed as its own. The national legislature had made it the capital in 1825. By December 1835 the town held a council house, a court house, a print shop, and the home of the missionary Samuel Worcester. The *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper, had been printed there in English and Cherokee from 1828 until 1834. New Echota was a seat of government, not a village waiting to be moved.[^ch17-gastateparks-newechota][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix]

On December 21, 1835, a council met there at the summons of two United States commissioners, William Carroll and John F. Schermerhorn. The commissioners had given notice that those who did not come would be understood to assent to whatever the council did. On December 29, they produced a treaty. Its first article ceded all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for five million dollars and a new country west of the river. Its sixteenth article gave the Cherokee two years from ratification to remove. The testimonium said the signers acted “being duly authorized by the people in general council assembled.”[^ch17-new-echota-treaty]

The signers included Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, Stand Watie, Andrew Ross, James Starr, and George Chambers. The National Park Service records that only 300 to 500 Cherokees were present, that none were elected officials of the nation, and that 20 signed. The elected government had not authorized them. A petition from the Aquohee and Taquoee districts, bearing 3,352 names, called the instrument the work of “a few individuals” and “so utterly repugnant to reason and justice and every dictate of humanity” that the Senate should reject it. Ross’s letter of September 1836 said the treaty “is not the act of our Nation.” The Senate ratified it on May 23, 1836, by one vote.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-docsteach-petition][^ch17-nps-ross-protest]

No account consulted here describes the weather at New Echota that week, the room where the signatures were made, or what was said. The record preserves the instrument, the notice, the names, and the protests. It does not preserve the scene’s air. What it preserves is a contest over who could speak for a nation. How did sovereignty survive when federal and state power rejected it?[^ch17-check-scene]

This chapter covers the making of Indian Country and the borderlands to its south and west between the 1820s and 1848. It draws on the federal removal act and statutes, the texts of the removal treaties, the reported opinions of the Supreme Court, the records and publications of Indigenous nations, congressional and Senate records, missionary and traveler accounts, and the scholarship on removal and borderlands history. It treats “removal,” “emigration,” “Indian Country,” and “the frontier” as the policy’s own vocabulary, not neutral description. “Removal” was the government’s word for a process that ranged from treaty negotiation to armed roundup; it left unstated who was removing whom. “Indian Country” was a legal and geographic term for lands west of the Mississippi set aside for displaced nations, and it was never empty. The Mexican North — the northern provinces of Mexico, including Texas, New Mexico, and California — was a region of Indigenous power as well as Mexican governance and American ambition. The chapter stops in 1848, when the war with Mexico ended and the Oregon question had been settled; the war itself belongs to Chapter 18. The chapter names the people the policy displaced and the nations it failed to erase. The Cherokee and other nations built governments, schools, and newspapers, and removal killed thousands. A story that reports only the dispossession, or only the survival, will be incomplete.[^ch17-check-scope][^ch17-check-removal-language][^ch17-check-race][^ch17-check-names][^ch17-check-dates]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 follows Cherokee nation-building and the courts, from Sequoyah’s syllabary to *Worcester v. Georgia*. §2 takes up the Indian Removal Act, the treaties, the routes west, and the Trail of Tears. §3 turns to the Old Northwest, the Southern Plains, and Comanchería, where Black Hawk, the Potawatomi, and the Comanche show that removal was neither the only story nor a completed conquest. §4 examines Texas and the Mexican North, where Anglo colonization and the republic’s founding entangled land, slavery, and independence. §5 follows the Oregon and Mormon migrations as new settlement projects. §6 asks how historians explain removal, sovereignty, and the borderlands. The chapter begins at New Echota in December 1835, where a minority signed a treaty that most Cherokees had refused.[^ch17-check-scope]

## 1. Cherokee government and the courts

### 1.1 Nation-building: Sequoyah’s syllabary, the constitution, and the *Cherokee Phoenix*

By the 1820s the Cherokee had built the institutions of a modern nation-state to defend a sovereignty the surrounding states denied. Sequoyah, a Cherokee silversmith, developed a syllabary for the Cherokee language in the early 1820s. The nation adopted it about 1821. It gave the Cherokee a written language, and with it literacy and printed government. In 1827 the Cherokee Nation adopted a written constitution modeled on that of the United States, with a principal chief, a bicameral council, and courts. The nation had been led by a principal chief regularly elected by chiefs from Cherokee towns, and the constitution was its answer to Georgia’s claim of jurisdiction.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-gastateparks-newechota]

In 1828 the General Council established the *Cherokee Phoenix* at New Echota, with Elias Boudinot as editor. It was the first Native American newspaper and printed in parallel English and Cherokee columns. The paper announced the nation’s laws and documents and carried the removal crisis to subscribers across the United States and Europe. It was an instrument of the national leadership, not a free-speech forum. Boudinot was forced to resign in August 1832 after he came to support voluntary removal.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix]

The Cherokee were not a people waiting to disappear. They were a nation with a constitution, a capital, a newspaper, and a legal strategy — and that is precisely why the states moved to abolish their government by law. The nation was not of one mind about removal, and the argument inside it would become as serious as the argument with Georgia. A minority came to believe that survival required a treaty and a new home in the West; the majority, under Ross, refused to treat removal as inevitable. The signers of the Treaty of New Echota became known as the Treaty Party, and their opponents as the National Party.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-smithsonian-resisting-removal]

### 1.2 Georgia’s extension laws and the Cherokee response

Georgia answered Cherokee statehood with statutes that purported to extend state jurisdiction over Cherokee land and to abolish the Cherokee government, courts, and laws. In 1827 the Cherokee adopted their constitution. The next year Georgia declared the Cherokee government abolished and its citizens subject to Georgia law, annexed Cherokee lands, and set up a process for distributing that land to White citizens. The state treated Cherokee landholding as subject to state regulation and prohibited Cherokee testimony against Whites, making abuse of Cherokee people difficult to remedy.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]

In 1830 gold was discovered on Cherokee land. Georgia held lotteries to distribute Cherokee land and gold rights to Whites and barred Cherokee people from conducting tribal business, contracting, testifying against Whites, or mining gold. The statutes and the gold rush together made the Cherokee a nation under legal siege.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-check-race]

Georgia also enacted a law prohibiting “white persons” from residing within the Cherokee Nation without a state license. It was aimed at the missionaries who advised the Cherokee leadership and gave them until March 1, 1831, to obtain a license or leave. The law forced the Cherokee to choose between submission and a legal challenge to the states’ power over them — the challenge that produced the Cherokee cases.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

### 1.3 The Cherokee cases and In Focus: *Worcester v. Georgia*

In 1831 the Cherokee sued Georgia in the Supreme Court. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, the Court held that it lacked jurisdiction to hear the suit because the Cherokee were not a foreign state. Chief Justice John Marshall called them “domestic dependent nations” under the guardianship of the United States. The phrase would shape Indian law into the twenty-first century.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-case][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

The Cherokee then supported the case of Samuel Worcester, a missionary who had served as federal postmaster at New Echota. Georgia arrested Worcester and several other missionaries in March 1831, released him when his lawyers argued that his federal post made him exempt, and arrested him again in July. After he returned to New Echota to care for his sick wife and daughter, the Georgia Guard arrested him a third time. In September the missionaries were tried, convicted, and sentenced to four years at hard labor. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832), the Court struck down Georgia’s extension laws.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

**In Focus.** The reported opinion in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), reads the treaty relationship as a wall around Cherokee territory. Marshall wrote that “the treaties and laws of the United States contemplate the Indian territory as completely separated from that of the States.” He described “the Indian nations” as “distinct, independent political communities retaining their original natural rights as undisputed possessors of the soil.” From those premises he concluded that “the Cherokee nation … is a distinct community, occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force,” and that “the act of the State of Georgia under which the plaintiff in error was prosecuted is consequently void, and the judgment a nullity.”[^ch17-worcester]

The reasoning is expansive, but it is bounded. Marshall rested on the treaties and on the federal government’s exclusive power over intercourse with the tribes, not on a general theory that every Indigenous nation was a foreign state. A year earlier, the same Court had called the Cherokee “domestic dependent nations.” The opinion’s force lies in its reading of the treaty relationship; its weakness lies in the remedy. The Court could declare a state judgment void. It depended on the executive to enforce the declaration, and the executive would not.[^ch17-worcester][^ch17-check-focus]

### 1.4 The limits of law: non-enforcement and the treaty

Georgia ignored the ruling and refused to release the missionaries. President Andrew Jackson did not enforce it and instead urged the Cherokee to relocate. The New Georgia Encyclopedia records Jackson’s words to General John Coffee: “The decision of the supreme court has fell still born.” The popular line attributed to him — “John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it” — is not what the record shows he wrote.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

The missionaries accepted a pardon. Georgia repealed the law used to convict them, and Worcester and the others were released in January 1833. The case that defined tribal sovereignty ended for the plaintiffs in a state pardon.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

With enforcement unavailable, the federal government pressed a removal treaty on a faction willing to sign. The choice at New Echota, and the protest against it, is the subject of §2. *Worcester* was a real victory and a real defeat.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-worcester]

## 2. Removal and its routes

### 2.1 The Indian Removal Act and the close votes

The Indian Removal Act of 1830 made removal a national project and passed by narrow margins after a sectional fight. Congress passed the act on May 28, 1830. It authorized the president to set aside districts west of the Mississippi for tribes that agreed to exchange their homelands, to guarantee the new country “forever,” and to pay removal and subsistence costs. Section 8 appropriated five hundred thousand dollars.[^ch17-removal-act]

The Senate voted 28 to 19 and the House 102 to 97. Northern Members were almost universally opposed and southern Members demanded passage. Opponents tried quorum calls and substitute amendments to stall the bill, and Speaker Andrew Stevenson voted three times to keep it alive. The margin was narrow enough that a few votes either way would have stopped it.[^ch17-loc-removal-guide][^ch17-house-removal]

Jackson called removal a “benevolent policy” approaching “a happy consummation.” He said it would “incalculably strengthen the southwestern frontier” and argued that separating the tribes from the states would “retard the progress of decay, which is lessening their numbers.” The act converted a long-running pressure into a funded federal program and set the terms — exchange, treaty, removal — by which the southeastern nations would be dispossessed.[^ch17-jackson-removal-message][^ch17-check-ch16]

### 2.2 The southeastern nations and the routes west

Removal fell unevenly across the southeastern and midwestern nations, and the treaties, not the act alone, did the dispossessing. Between 1816 and 1840 the tribes between the original states and the Mississippi signed more than 40 treaties ceding land. Between 1830 and 1850, the National Park Service estimates, about 100,000 Native people moved west. Other accounts give different totals. The Office of the Historian counts nearly 50,000 during Jackson’s presidency; Claudio Saunt writes of “nearly eighty thousand” deportations in the 1830s. The differences reflect dates, definitions, and counting. These are estimates, not a single figure.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-smithsonian-removal-overview][^ch17-history-state-removal][^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-check-numbers]

The Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, Cherokee, and Seminole of the Southeast and nations of the Old Northwest were all affected. Each negotiated or resisted under different circumstances. The Cherokee case is not the whole of removal. The Creek removal was deadly: about 3,500 Creeks died in Alabama and on the westward journey, some transported in chains. The Seminole fought the longest war. The Treaty of Payne’s Landing in 1832 required them to give up their Florida lands within three years. When the army arrived in 1835, the Second Seminole War began. Fewer than 3,000 warriors under Micanopy, Alligator, Jumper, and Osceola fought more than 30,000 troops and four generals. The war cost the United States more than $20 million and left more than 1,500 soldiers dead. Osceola was captured under a truce and died in prison in 1838. Most Seminoles were removed by 1842, and no peace treaty ended the war.[^ch17-nps-liri-trail-of-tears][^ch17-florida-seminole-wars]

The policy set aside an Indian Territory and promised a “permanent Indian frontier” of military posts to keep White intruders out. It was a guarantee the same expansionist forces would soon breach. Removal was a federal land program with a legal form and a military backstop. Its routes and destinations — not only its causes — are what Map 13 must show.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west][^ch17-check-map]

### 2.3 The Treaty of New Echota and the Trail of Tears

The Treaty of New Echota, signed by a minority and ratified by one vote, produced the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838–39. The treaty ceded all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for five million dollars and lands west, and required removal within two years of ratification. It was concluded December 29, 1835, and ratified May 23, 1836, by a single vote. Most Cherokee, under Ross, refused to treat it as binding.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-agency][^ch17-nps-ross-protest]

In May 1838 federal troops under General Winfield Scott and state militias began gathering Cherokee people into stockades. Families were separated. The elderly and ill were forced out at gunpoint; people were given only moments to collect possessions; White looters followed. Three groups left in the summer by rail, boat, and wagon, but river levels were too low. By November, 12 groups of 1,000 each were trudging 800 miles overland; the last party, including Ross, went by water. Heavy autumn rains and hundreds of wagons made roads impassable. Two-thirds of the ill-equipped Cherokee were trapped between the ice-bound Ohio and Mississippi Rivers in January.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]

The dead are counted differently. Elizur Butler, the missionary doctor who accompanied the Cherokee, estimated that more than 4,000 died, which the National Park Service calls nearly a fifth of the Cherokee population. The Cherokee Nation says some 4,000 died, about one-fourth of the population. The Office of the Historian says between three and four thousand of the 15,000 to 16,000 removed. The Gilder Lehrman essay gives a lower figure of two thousand. The range, with its sources, is the honest record.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-history-state-removal][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west][^ch17-check-numbers]

The survivors rebuilt the nation in Indian Territory. They adopted a new constitution in 1839 and made Tahlequah their capital. About 1,000 Cherokee in North Carolina escaped the roundup and became the Eastern Band. The Trail of Tears is the chapter’s central documented event of coerced movement, but it is not the end of the Cherokee Nation.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]

## 3. The Old Northwest, Plains, and Comanchería

### 3.1 Black Hawk’s war and the Sauk and Meskwaki

In the Old Northwest, removal took the form of war, defeat, and treaty. The Sauk and Meskwaki case shows how a contested treaty could become a forced cession. At St. Louis in 1804, William Henry Harrison negotiated a treaty with two Sauk representatives who, the Sauk chiefs held, had no authority to speak for the nation. The government believed it had bought all Sauk land east of the Mississippi for a small sum — $2,500. Black Hawk did not accept the treaty as valid.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk]

In 1830 the United States required the Sauk to move to Iowa. In April 1832 Black Hawk led about 1,000 Sauk and Meskwaki people back across the Mississippi into Illinois. He hoped to forge an alliance with the Ho-Chunk (Winnebago) and other tribes and to plant corn on ancestral land. Illinois settlers organized a militia. Black Hawk reconsidered and decided to surrender, but an undisciplined militia ignored a peace flag and attacked. The Sauk returned fire and retreated north.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk]

The war ended at the Bad Axe River on August 2. United States soldiers attacked the band as it tried to ford the Mississippi. Ignoring a truce flag, troops aboard a steamboat fired cannons and rifles, killing hundreds, including many children. Only 150 of the 1,000 survived. Black Hawk surrendered at Fort Crawford. The Treaty of 1832 ceded a large tract and required the Sauk and Meskwaki to remove from it by June 1, 1833, while reserving a tract in Iowa. The war showed that resistance in the Old Northwest would be met with overwhelming force.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-sauk-treaty-1832][^ch17-check-race]

### 3.2 The Potawatomi and removals across the Old Northwest

Removal reached the Great Lakes nations through roundups and forced marches, not only through negotiated exchange. On September 4, 1838, General John Tipton assembled 859 Potawatomi at Menominee’s village near Twin Lakes, Indiana, with their leaders shackled in a wagon. The militia burned their houses and fields behind them and forced a 660-mile march to a reserve in present-day Kansas. More than 40 people died on what the Potawatomi call the Trail of Death.[^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]

The Potawatomi march, the Black Hawk War, and the southeastern treaties together show that “removal” covered a range of methods — treaty, war, and direct militia coercion. The Old Northwest nations experienced it as forcibly as the Southeast did. The Cherokee case is not the only story. Removal was national in scope and varied in form, and the Potawatomi case belongs on Map 13 alongside the southern routes.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death][^ch17-check-map]

### 3.3 Comanchería and Indigenous power on the Southern Plains

On the Southern Plains, the Comanche built a power that shaped the borderlands and that neither Mexico nor the United States could ignore. Mounted and mobile, they dominated a vast *Comanchería* across much of Texas and the Southern Plains from the eighteenth century. They controlled the commerce of the region by trade as well as raid. By 1836, when Texas won independence, they were “still in absolute control of the Texas plains.”[^ch17-tsha-comanche]

Comanche and allied raids on Texas and northern Mexico continued through the 1830s and 1840s. In May 1836 a Comanche and Kiowa force attacked Fort Parker, killed several settlers, and took five hostages, including nine-year-old Cynthia Ann Parker. She lived with the Comanche for 24 years and became the mother of Quanah Parker. In 1840 the Council House Fight in San Antonio killed 35 Comanches, including 12 chiefs, and seven Texans. The retaliatory Linnville raid by more than 500 warriors killed 25 Texans.[^ch17-tsha-comanche]

Historians Pekka Hämäläinen and Brian DeLay argue that Native power in the Mexican North shaped the fate of the region and even the U.S.-Mexican War. They make the borderlands a story of Indigenous sovereignty rather than only of American conquest. The Handbook of Texas account, written earlier, still acknowledges Comanche dominance in the period. The Southern Plains was a place where removal was not the only force and where Indigenous nations remained powerful actors into the 1840s.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-sources][^ch17-saunt-review]

### 3.4 The “permanent Indian frontier” and its fragility

The Indian Country promised by the 1830 act was contested from the start and was already being undermined by the same expansion it was meant to contain. Elliott West explains that the act created an Indian Territory and planned a “permanent Indian frontier” of military posts along its eastern boundary to keep White settlers out. The removed nations helped carry the institutions of the society that expelled them into the West.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

The same decades brought trader pressure, resource depletion, and intertribal conflict. West notes that the fur trade shifted from beaver to bison robes by the 1840s, and that by the mid-1840s about a hundred thousand robes from the plains passed through St. Louis annually. Removed nations fought with nations already in the West. The “permanent” frontier was a promise measured in years, not generations.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

Indian Country was real and consequential. Nations rebuilt governments and communities there. But it was never a secure refuge from the expansion the chapter elsewhere describes.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

## 4. Texas and the Mexican North

### 4.1 Empresarios, Anglo colonization, and Mexico’s abolition of slavery

Anglo colonization of the Mexican province of Texas after 1821 created a settler society whose labor system collided with Mexico’s antislavery law. Moses Austin obtained a Spanish commission to bring 300 families. After his death in 1821 his son Stephen F. Austin fulfilled the grant and became the first *empresario*. The Mexican Constitution of 1824 joined Coahuila and Texas, and the state’s Colonization Act of 1825 offered generous terms that set off a “Texas land rush.” Most immigrants arrived from the southern United States, “many with their slaves in tow.”[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

Mexico moved against slavery in the 1820s and abolished it in 1829 by decree of President Vicente Guerrero. Enslaved people from Texas and Louisiana fled toward Mexico, where the border offered freedom. The journey was long and dangerous, and no reliable figures exist for how many made it. The Law of April 6, 1830, forbade Anglo-American immigration and further introduction of slaves. It angered the colonists but “only slowed immigration and the Peculiar Institution, but did not curb them.”[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

By the early 1830s Texas held a growing Anglo population and an enslaved population whose status depended on resisting Mexican law. That contradiction was central to the coming revolution.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]

### 4.2 The revolution of 1836 and the protection of slavery

The Texas revolution began as a federalist revolt against Santa Anna’s centralism and ended as an independence movement whose new republic protected slavery. After Santa Anna abandoned federalism and imposed the centralist Siete Leyes, revolts spread across Mexican states, including Texas. The first clash came at Gonzales in October 1835. The Alamo fell in March 1836. The convention at Washington-on-the-Brazos declared independence on March 2, 1836. Houston’s army won at San Jacinto on April 21, 1836, and captured Santa Anna.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

The convention adopted a constitution on March 17 that “guaranteed protection of slavery.” The revolution’s leaders took care to preserve enslavement even as they invoked liberty.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]

The causes are contested. The Handbook of Texas sets out the competing explanations: economic interest in cheap land, a clash of cultures, Texan violations of Mexican terms, Santa Anna’s centralism, “slaveowners’ concern with protecting their peculiar institution,” and a supposed United States conspiracy, for which it finds “scant evidence.” It concludes that the revolution “resulted from a complex set of preconditions and ‘a spark that ignited them.’” The revolution was overdetermined. Land, federalism, and slavery each mattered, and the outcome protected slavery whether or not it was the sole cause.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-check-race]

### 4.3 The Republic of Texas and the annexation controversy

Independent Texas kept the slavery and annexation questions open, and its republic was itself contested ground among Mexicans, Tejanos, Anglos, and Native nations. The new republic faced Mexicans who never accepted its independence, Tejanos who had their own claims on the revolution’s meaning, and Native nations — Comanche, Cherokee, and others — who controlled much of the territory. Native groups numbered about 20,000 in Texas and “far outnumbered the Anglo settlers and Tejanos.”[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

Annexation to the United States became a live national issue before Polk took office, entangled with slavery’s extension and with the risk of war with Mexico. The 1845 decision and its consequences belong to Chapter 18. Texas was a republic built on land taken from Mexico and Native nations and on the labor of enslaved people. That combination made annexation a national crisis rather than a simple territorial addition.[^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

### 4.4 The Mexican North and flight to freedom

The international border between Texas and Mexico made the Mexican North a destination for enslaved people seeking freedom. It gave the borderlands a meaning that American expansion threatened. The NEH essay on the southern Underground Railroad records that escape routes to Mexico “were known among slaves in Texas,” that Mexico had abolished slavery in 1829, and that a nineteenth-century Texas Ranger estimated 4,000 escapes, though “quantifying this is never going to happen.”[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]

The essay notes complicity by Tejanos and some German settlers. It also records a widely told story, which the historian Sean Kelley treats as significant whether or not it is literally true, of a man who floated to freedom on a bale of cotton. Texas’s annexation would extend slavery’s border and turn a border of freedom into one of danger for enslaved people. That argument belongs alongside the expansionist and the slaveholding-strategy readings of annexation.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom][^ch17-check-race]

## 5. Oregon and the Mormon migration

### 5.1 The Oregon Trail, missions, and the Whitmans

Overland settlement to Oregon joined religious mission, national rivalry, and settler sovereignty into a single movement. After the Anglo-American Convention of 1818 opened the Oregon Country to both powers’ citizens, the United States needed settlers to make good its claim against a British population anchored by the Hudson’s Bay Company. The common Eastern view treated the route as a “howling wilderness,” and experts said that respectable women and families would never make it to Oregon or live there.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]

Narcissa Prentiss Whitman and her husband Marcus joined a caravan to Oregon in 1836. Her letters and journal entries, circulated and published in eastern newspapers, reassured readers that rivers were fordable, wagon travel was practical, and the so-called hostiles were “generally friendly.” They helped open the Oregon Trail to families. By the mid-1850s some 400,000 had made the journey, with perhaps 30,000 dying en route, mostly of disease.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]

In 1847 the Whitmans were killed at their Walla Walla mission by Cayuse people who resented the mission’s proselytizing, its trading practices, and the measles epidemic that devastated them. The Oregon migration was at once a religious mission and a claim of sovereignty, and it rested on the same displacement of Native nations the chapter describes elsewhere.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-check-race]

### 5.2 The Mormon exodus from Nauvoo

The Mormon migration was a religiously driven settlement project that sought a place apart, under its own governance, beyond the reach of its persecutors. After Joseph Smith was killed by a mob at Carthage, Illinois, on June 27, 1844, and anti-Mormon violence spread, Brigham Young planned an evacuation of some 15,000 people to the Great Basin. The first wagons crossed the Mississippi from Nauvoo on February 4, 1846.[^ch17-nps-mormon-history]

The National Park Service records that the 1846 trek ran from Nauvoo to the Missouri River near present-day Omaha, and the 1847 journey continued to the Great Salt Lake Valley. The first 260 miles tested the vanguard most severely: freezing nights, steady rain, axle-deep mud, and flooding streams. The first death came March 18, 1846. Young reorganized the camp into companies of 100 families and established way stations at Garden Grove and Mount Pisgah to feed later emigrants. More than 700 Mormon people died on the prairie during the winter and spring of 1846–47.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]

The Mormons sought a sovereign community of their own in the Great Basin. Their migration intensified the question, central to the chapter, of who could claim land and self-government in the expanding republic.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]

### 5.3 Migration, religion, and settler sovereignty

The Oregon and Mormon migrations show that expansion was not only a federal policy but a movement of settler communities claiming land and self-rule. Both migrations crossed Native homelands. Both framed their journeys as escapes or callings. Both sought to establish communities with their own laws. Neither could be separated from the displacement of Native nations on the routes and at the destinations. Expansion created new sovereignties for some and destroyed or threatened those of others.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-nps-mormon-1846][^ch17-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The evidence supports several conclusions. Removal was a federal program. Congress passed the Indian Removal Act in 1830 by close votes and funded it. The Jackson administration negotiated some 70 removal treaties, and the army and state militias carried removal out.[^ch17-loc-removal-guide][^ch17-removal-act][^ch17-history-state-removal]

The Cherokee resisted through law and lost to force. Sequoyah’s syllabary, the 1827 constitution, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, and *Worcester v. Georgia* are documented. So are the non-enforcement of the ruling and the coerced removal of 1838–39.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-worcester][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]

Removal was national in scope. It reached the Old Northwest as well as the Southeast, and it took the form of war and militia coercion as well as treaty. The nations survived. The Cherokee rebuilt a government in Indian Territory, some remained in the East, and the Eastern Band and the Cherokee Nation continue to exercise sovereignty today.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]

Texas and the borderlands were contested. The Texas revolution, the republic, and annexation entangled land, slavery, and Native power. Mexico’s antislavery law made the border a route to freedom.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]

### 6.2 What is open

Was removal inevitable? Claudio Saunt argues emphatically that it was not. Individual and institutional choices produced it. Older accounts treated it as the unavoidable result of a superior civilization displacing a weaker one. The question is how much contingency the record will bear against the structural pressure of land hunger, slavery, and state power.[^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-jackson-removal-message]

How many were removed and how many died? Estimates range from about 50,000 during Jackson’s presidency to about 100,000 between 1830 and 1850. Cherokee deaths are estimated at about 2,000 to more than 4,000. The differences reflect dates, definitions, and sources. The evidence supports ranges, not one number.[^ch17-history-state-removal][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west][^ch17-check-numbers]

What was the weight of slavery in the Texas revolution? Historians range from arguing that it was a leading cause to treating it as one among several. The Handbook of Texas sets out the positions and warns against monocausal answers.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-check-race]

How should borderlands sovereignty be told? Hämäläinen and DeLay center Indigenous power in the Mexican North. Other scholars caution against overstating a Comanche “empire.” Both the power and its limits need evidence.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-sources]

Whose voices survive? Government records, treaties, and court opinions document the policy and the signers. Cherokee petitions, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, and nation histories document resistance. Many removed people left no direct testimony. The silence should be named, not filled.[^ch17-docsteach-petition][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix][^ch17-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which removal was a deliberate federal project, contested by Indigenous nations who built modern institutions to resist it, and fatal in its consequences without ever extinguishing the nations it displaced. The borderlands to the south and west were shaped by Indigenous power as much as by American expansion.

The benevolent-necessity tradition treated removal as humane and inevitable, a way to save a people believed to be disappearing. The policy’s own defenders, from Jackson’s message to the commissioners of Indian affairs, framed it that way. Their claim is a claim to be tested against the record, not a description.[^ch17-jackson-removal-message][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

The deportation and dispossession synthesis reframes removal as state-sponsored deportation and expulsion. Claudio Saunt’s *Unworthy Republic* ties it to capitalism extending land and slavery and frames it as a model others would follow. He insists that it was contingent.[^ch17-saunt-review]

The Indigenous-centered and sovereignty accounts center Cherokee nation-building, legal resistance, and the survival of sovereignty. Theda Perdue and Michael D. Green, the Cherokee Nation’s institutional history, and legal historians of *Worcester* read removal from inside the nation as well as from Washington.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-check-sources]

The borderlands turn places Comanche and other Indigenous power at the center of the Mexican North. Pekka Hämäläinen and Brian DeLay argue that it shaped the region’s history and the U.S.-Mexican conflict.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-sources]

Read together, these positions show that removal and survival, conquest and sovereignty, were parts of one history. The debate is best read as overlapping positions, not teams, and each can be tested against the 1830 votes, the treaty texts, the routes, and the persistence of the nations.[^ch17-check-table][^ch17-check-sources]

## Legacies

Removal did not erase the nations it displaced. The Cherokee rebuilt a government at Tahlequah under a new constitution in 1839, and about 1,000 Cherokee who escaped the roundup in North Carolina became the Eastern Band. Both nations continue to exercise treaty rights and sovereignty today. The same is true of the Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, and the Great Lakes nations whose removals this chapter records.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]

The central question has a double answer. Sovereignty survived because nations carried it with them: they wrote constitutions, printed newspapers, filed suits, and rebuilt governments. But survival came at a cost the policy intended to be total — thousands dead, homelands lost, and a century of further dispossession ahead.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

The borderlands left a different inheritance. Texas entered the Union as a slave state in 1845, and the Mexican North’s border, once a route to freedom, became a line that American slavery pressed against and eventually crossed. The unresolved questions of Texas annexation, slavery’s extension, and the status of Indian Country carried forward into the war and crisis the next chapters describe.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom][^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The United States’ removal policy and its borderlands were entangled with Mexico, Britain, and an Atlantic economy in land and cotton. Saunt argues that the American handling of the “Indian question” became a model for other nations expelling populations, and the Texas question drew in British and Mexican diplomacy.[^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **Archives and silences.** Treaties, statutes, court opinions, and removal rolls record the policy and its agents most fully. Cherokee petitions, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, and nation histories preserve an Indigenous counter-archive, while most removed people left no direct testimony. That unevenness should be named, and the displaced sought in petitions, rolls, and missionary and traveler records without speaking for them.[^ch17-docsteach-petition][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix][^ch17-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** The market revolution that made land and cotton valuable belongs to Chapter 15. The political history of the 1830 removal vote belongs to Chapter 16. The Mexican-American War and the Guadalupe Hidalgo settlement belong to Chapter 18, and the later Plains conquest belongs to Chapter 25.[^ch17-check-ch16][^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-check-scope]

## Summary

- By the 1820s the Cherokee had a written language, a constitution, a national newspaper, and a government that Georgia moved to abolish.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix]
- In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) the Marshall Court called the Cherokee “domestic dependent nations”; in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) it held that Georgia’s laws “can have no force” in Cherokee territory — a ruling Georgia and the president declined to enforce.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-case][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-worcester]
- The Indian Removal Act of 1830 passed the Senate 28–19 and the House 102–97 and funded a federal removal program that Jackson called a “benevolent policy.”[^ch17-loc-removal-guide][^ch17-jackson-removal-message]
- The Treaty of New Echota (December 1835), signed by a minority of Cherokees and ratified by one Senate vote, led to the forced removal of 1838–39; sources estimate the Cherokee dead at about 2,000 to more than 4,000 of roughly 16,000.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-check-numbers]
- Removal reached the Old Northwest through war and militia coercion, including Black Hawk’s defeat in 1832 and the Potawatomi Trail of Death of 1838.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]
- On the Southern Plains, Comanche power defined Comanchería and shaped the Mexican North into the 1840s.[^ch17-tsha-comanche]
- Anglo colonization of Mexican Texas collided with Mexico’s 1829 abolition of slavery; the 1836 revolution produced a republic whose constitution protected slavery, and annexation became a national controversy.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- The Oregon and Mormon migrations carried settler communities across Native homelands and made new claims to land and self-government.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- The debate turns on whether removal was humane necessity, federal deportation, a contest Indigenous nations survived, or a borderlands history of Indigenous power, and the honest answer holds several of these together.[^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-check-table]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The United States removed tens of thousands of Native people to make room for a republic that called itself free, and it did so through statutes, treaties, courts, and soldiers. The Cherokee answered with a constitution, a newspaper, and a lawsuit, and the Supreme Court agreed with them; the state and the president did not. What this history records is not the disappearance of nations but the attempt to make them disappear, and the survival of peoples who carried their sovereignty into Indian Country and beyond. The question is how that survival was possible, and what it cost.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-worcester][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-saunt-review]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at Rancho de Carricitos on April 25, 1846, where Captain Seth Thornton’s patrol was surrounded and captured on ground both the United States and Mexico claimed. This chapter has followed Texas from Anglo colonization through revolution to the unresolved annexation controversy. Chapter 18 takes up the annexation decision’s international consequences and the Mexican-American War.[^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
