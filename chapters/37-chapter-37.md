> “I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]

— Harry S. Truman, address to a joint session of Congress, March 12, 1947.[^ch37-truman-doctrine]

- **Why these three.** A British statesman described a Europe already divided. An American president announced a global commitment to resist subjugation. A historian charged that the deeper engine of American policy was expansion itself. The three disagree about what the confrontation was: a Soviet threat met by a defensive West, a universal principle of freedom, or an American empire that called its expansion freedom.[^ch37-churchill-iron-curtain][^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-williams-tragedy]
- **The attribution rule.** Churchill’s sentence is quoted from the full text of the speech as reproduced by America’s National Churchill Museum and must be collated against a published edition and the *Congressional Record*. Williams’s sentence is quoted from *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* as reproduced in David S. Brown’s fiftieth-anniversary essay and must be collated against the 1959 or 1962 edition with a page number. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On February 22, 1946, George F. Kennan, then the chargé d’affaires at the United States embassy in Moscow, sent a long telegram to the secretary of state. The cable is numbered 511 and answers a State Department query of February 3; it is headed “SECRET” and timed 9 p.m. Moscow time.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]
- **The shape of the argument.** Kennan said the questions were “so intricate, so delicate, so strange to our form of thought” that he would answer in five parts: the Soviet outlook, its background, its official and unofficial projections, and “practical deductions from standpoint of US policy.” He apologized for “burdening of telegraphic channel.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]
- **Kennan’s diagnosis.** Kennan traced Soviet policy to a “traditional and instinctive Russian sense of insecurity,” not to an objective reading of the world, and said the Kremlin’s view of foreign affairs was “neurotic.” He wrote that Soviet power was “neither schematic nor adventuristic. It does not work by fixed plans. It does not take unnecessary risks,” and that it was “Impervious to logic of reason, and it is highly sensitive to logic of force.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]
- **The prescription.** Kennan urged a calm, long-term, and firm response and warned that “the greatest danger that can befall us in coping with this problem of Soviet communism, is that we shall allow ourselves to become like those with whom we are coping.” He said the problem was “within our power to solve — and that without recourse to any general military conflict.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]
- **The length.** The Wilson Center describes the telegram as 5,540 words; the Council on Foreign Relations describes it as roughly 8,000 words. The discrepancy is a counting convention, and the chapter should give Kennan’s own phrase — a message he could not “compress … into single brief message” — rather than a contested word count.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-check-scene]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the sender, the recipient, the text, and its argument. It cannot show what Secretary of State James F. Byrnes or President Truman made of it, how quickly it circulated, or that it alone caused containment. The chapter should treat the telegram as an influential formulation, not a cause that can be measured by its text alone.[^ch37-check-scene][^ch37-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In February 1946 no one knew that the alliance would harden into a forty-year confrontation, that the Soviet Union would test an atomic bomb in 1949, that China would become a communist state, or that the United States would fight in Korea. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch37-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A telegram opens a problem rather than settling it.** The scene’s claim is not that the Long Telegram created the Cold War but that it named a way of seeing the Soviet Union that then became policy: a patient, firm, and watchful containment of an expansionist power that responded to force but not to reason.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-kennan-x-article]
- **The stakes.** Kennan’s insistence that the Soviet system could be contained “without recourse to any general military conflict” is the tension the chapter follows, because the institutions built after 1947 — the CIA, NATO, NSC-68, and the Korean deployment — increasingly rested on military power.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-nsc68]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did a wartime alliance become a permanent security confrontation?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from a Moscow cable to the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, to the Berlin airlift and NATO, to the loyalty boards and the blacklist, to the first Soviet test and the People’s Republic of China, and to the battlefields of Korea.[^ch37-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The date, sender, text, and argument are documented. The scene may not stage the embassy office, the typing of the cable, or Kennan’s private thoughts beyond what the record states.[^ch37-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of NATO, the Soviet bomb, the fall of China, or the Korean War.[^ch37-check-scene]
- **No lone-author framing.** The scene must not reduce the origins of containment to one diplomat’s cable. The Truman Doctrine, NSC-68, and the institutional choices of 1947–50 did the governing work.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-silences]

## 1. Postwar disagreements

### 1.1 The claim: an alliance of expedience

- **The claim.** The wartime Grand Alliance was a coalition of necessity against a common enemy, and victory removed the thing that had held it together while leaving unresolved the questions of Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic bomb. The Cold War grew from that vacuum and from the choices each side made within it.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-check-yalta]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the breakdown as a sequence of decisions and misreadings, not a single act of aggression, because the evidence supports competing accounts of responsibility (§6).[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-postrevisionist-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show conferences, occupation arrangements, and communications. It cannot show that either side intended the conflict from the start, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch37-check-yalta][^ch37-check-silences]

### 1.2 Yalta, Potsdam, and the occupation of Europe

- **The claim.** The Yalta Conference of February 1945 and the Potsdam Conference of July–August 1945 fixed occupation zones, reparations arrangements, and loose promises about free elections in liberated Europe, but left the meaning of those promises and the future of Germany in dispute.[^ch37-check-yalta]
- **The instruments.** The occupation of Germany and Berlin in zones, the Declaration on Liberated Europe, and the reparations negotiations shaped the first confrontations. The chapter should name the documents and place them before the reader rather than relying on the conference names alone.[^ch37-check-yalta]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the texts and the disputes over them. It cannot settle whether the agreements were read in good faith, which is the interpretive question of §6.[^ch37-check-yalta][^ch37-check-debate-works]
- **Research status.** The Yalta and Potsdam records in *Foreign Relations of the United States* and the State Department’s conference milestones are **discovered — not yet consulted**; they must be read before the section is drafted.[^ch37-check-yalta]

### 1.3 The atomic monopoly and the Baruch Plan

- **The claim.** The American atomic monopoly and the failed attempt to place atomic energy under international control poisoned the first postwar negotiations, because the bomb was simultaneously a secret, a bargaining chip, and a threat.[^ch37-check-atomic][^ch37-nsc68]
- **The episode.** The United States proposed the Baruch Plan for international control of atomic energy in June 1946, and the Soviet Union rejected it; the failure left the world without an agreed nuclear regime. The chapter should name the plan and the rejection without claiming that a different outcome was available.[^ch37-check-atomic]
- **Ownership.** Chapter 36 owns the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Manhattan Project. This chapter names them only as the starting condition of the postwar atomic problem.[^ch37-check-ch36][^ch37-check-atomic]

### 1.4 Iran, Turkey, and the Eastern European sphere

- **The claim.** Crises over northern Iran in 1946 and Soviet pressure on Turkey over the Straits, alongside the consolidation of communist governments in Eastern Europe, convinced American officials that Soviet power was expanding by pressure and subversion.[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- **The evidence.** The State Department’s account of the Truman Doctrine records the four 1946 setbacks: the failure to withdraw Soviet troops from northern Iran, pressure for Iranian oil concessions, pressure on Turkey for base and transit rights, and the Soviet rejection of the Baruch Plan.[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- **What the record can show.** It can show American perceptions and the events they cited. It cannot show that Stalin intended the expansion American officials feared, and the chapter should attribute that claim to those who make it. The milestone itself notes that Stalin refrained from supporting the Greek communists.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 The Long Telegram and the invention of containment

- **The claim.** Kennan’s Long Telegram of February 1946 and his anonymous “X” article, “The Sources of Soviet Conduct,” of July 1947 gave American policy a name and a strategy: “a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies.”[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram][^ch37-kennan-x-article]
- **The text.** The “X” article argued that Soviet pressure “can be contained by the adroit and vigilant application of counter-force at a series of constantly shifting geographical and political points,” and that Soviet society could be expected “to bear within itself the germs of its own decay.”[^ch37-kennan-x-article]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the words and their influence. It cannot show that containment meant one thing: Treasury officials, the military services, and Kennan himself later disagreed about whether containment was primarily political or military. The chapter should present containment as a contested program, not a settled doctrine.[^ch37-kennan-x-article][^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-debate-works]

## 2. Containment, the Marshall Plan, Berlin, NATO, and security agencies

### 2.1 The claim: building institutions

- **The claim.** Between 1947 and 1949 the United States converted a diagnosis of Soviet expansion into a set of permanent institutions — economic aid, a military alliance, a reorganized defense establishment, and intelligence and planning bodies — that outlasted the crisis that produced them.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present institution-building as a cumulative and partly unintended process, because each step answered a specific crisis while enlarging the American commitment.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-berlin-airlift]
- **What the record can show.** It can show statutes, treaties, programs, and organizations. It cannot show that officials foresaw the enduring structure they were building.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-check-silences]

### 2.2 The Truman Doctrine, March 12, 1947

- **The claim.** With the British withdrawal from Greece, Truman asked Congress on March 12, 1947, for $400 million in aid to Greece and Turkey and stated the principle that the United States would support “free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- **The argument.** Truman framed the world as divided between “alternative ways of life,” one based on the will of the majority and one “based upon the will of a minority forcibly imposed upon the majority,” and said the United States must “assist free peoples to work out their own destinies in their own way.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the request, the sum, and the argument. It cannot show that Greece and Turkey were the real object of the policy rather than its occasion, and the chapter should present that as an interpretation.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 The Marshall Plan, April 3, 1948

- **The claim.** Secretary of State George C. Marshall proposed on June 5, 1947, that Europeans design their own recovery and that the United States finance it; Congress passed the Economic Cooperation Act, signed on April 3, 1948, and over four years appropriated $13.3 billion for European recovery.[^ch37-marshall-plan]
- **The terms.** The aid provided capital and materials for reconstruction, opened markets for American goods, and aimed to support “stable democratic governments in Western Europe.” Soviet refusal to participate or to allow its satellites to accept the aid hardened the division of Europe.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the program, the sum, and the stated aims. It cannot show the counterfactual of European recovery without it, and the chapter should give the figure with its period and its nominal dollars.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-check-numbers]

### 2.4 The Berlin blockade and airlift, 1948–1949

- **The claim.** When the Western powers introduced a new currency into their zones and West Berlin in June 1948, Soviet forces blockaded land access to the western sectors of Berlin; the United States and Britain answered with an airlift that supplied the city for nearly a year.[^ch37-berlin-airlift]
- **The episode.** The blockade began on June 24, 1948. The United States launched “Operation Vittles” on June 26, and Britain followed with “Operation Plainfare.” At the height of the campaign “one plane landed every 45 seconds at Tempelhof Airport,” and in September 1948 some 300,000 West Berliners gathered at the Reichstag against Soviet domination. Moscow lifted the blockade on May 11–12, 1949.[^ch37-berlin-airlift]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the dates, the operations, and the outcome. It cannot show that the airlift alone saved the city’s Western status, since the Western counter-blockade and Soviet calculations also mattered.[^ch37-berlin-airlift][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 2.5 NATO, April 4, 1949

- **The claim.** On April 4, 1949, twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty, agreeing that an attack against one would be considered an attack against all; it was the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered outside the Western Hemisphere.[^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **The instrument and its limits.** The founding members were the United States, Canada, Belgium, Denmark, France, Iceland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, and the United Kingdom. The collective-defense commitment formally applied only to attacks in Europe or North America and did not cover colonial conflicts, and the treaty entered into force on August 24, 1949.[^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the treaty text, the members, and the commitments. It cannot show that Article 5 would have been honored, because it was never invoked during the period; the chapter should present deterrence as a claim, not a demonstrated result.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 2.6 The security agencies and Map 28

- **The claim.** The National Security Act of 1947 reorganized the American defense establishment, creating the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency, and the Department of Defense, and the new machinery made covert action and global planning permanent instruments of policy.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-check-map28]
- **Figure and map.** The registered visual `ch37-map-01` (Map 28, “Containment’s geography, 1945–1952”) belongs to §§1–2. Its caption and source line must state the observation date, distinguish bases, alliance commitments, and changing memberships, and use the 1952 snapshot it is planned for rather than implying coverage of later events. Its evidence targets are *Foreign Relations of the United States*, NATO accession records, and deployment records, and its production record is `repo-map28`. The map must distinguish commitments the United States made from territories it occupied or controlled.[^ch37-check-map28][^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **What the record can show.** It can show organizations and deployments. It cannot show causation from a map or organization chart, and the chapter should keep policy in the prose.[^ch37-check-map28]
- **Research status.** The National Security Act’s text and the agency histories are **discovered — not yet consulted**; they must be read before the section is drafted.[^ch37-check-map28]

## 3. Loyalty investigations and anticommunism

### 3.1 The claim: containment comes home

- **The claim.** Containment was not only a foreign policy; it became a domestic program of loyalty investigations, blacklists, and prosecutions that treated political belief and association as security risks.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-truman-loyalty]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should show the federal program and the private and congressional campaigns together, because the loyalty order, HUAC, the Smith Act prosecutions, and McCarthy’s accusations reinforced one another.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-hollywood-ten]
- **What the record can show.** It can show orders, prosecutions, and dismissal figures. It cannot measure the chill the program produced, and the chapter should present “chilling effect” as a claim with evidence rather than a count.[^ch37-truman-loyalty][^ch37-check-silences]

### 3.2 Executive Order 9835 and the In Focus

- **The claim.** On March 21, 1947, Truman signed Executive Order 9835, creating the first general loyalty program for federal employees, with investigations of applicants and employees, loyalty boards, a central master index, and an appeal to a Civil Service Commission Loyalty Review Board.[^ch37-eo9835]
- **The standard.** Part V set the standard: employment could be refused or removed where “on all the evidence, reasonable grounds exist for belief that the person involved is disloyal to the Government of the United States.” The listed grounds included “membership in, affiliation with or sympathetic association with” any organization the attorney general designated as totalitarian, fascist, communist, or subversive.[^ch37-eo9835]
- **The later revision.** Executive Order 10241 of April 28, 1951, amended that standard to a harsher one: removal where “there is a reasonable doubt as to the loyalty of the person involved.” The In Focus box should read the 1947 standard closely and compare the 1951 revision outside the single-text box.[^ch37-eo10241]

**In Focus.** The In Focus reads a single sentence of Executive Order 9835 as a document of the security state: the Part V standard that “on all the evidence, reasonable grounds exist for belief that the person involved is disloyal to the Government of the United States.” The sentence rewards a close reading because it defines loyalty negatively and probabilistically. It does not require proof of disloyalty, an overt act, or a crime; it asks whether “reasonable grounds” for “belief” exist, and it locates the judgment in an administrative board rather than a court. The order’s grounds for disloyalty include “membership in, affiliation with or sympathetic association with” organizations the attorney general designated as “totalitarian, fascist, communist, or subversive” — a definition that reaches association and sympathy as well as action. The order also created procedure: written notice of charges, a hearing before a three-member loyalty board, counsel, and an appeal to the Civil Service Commission’s Loyalty Review Board. The box should read both halves together — the broad standard and the procedural protections — because the order’s defenders cited the procedures and its critics cited the standard and the reliance on confidential informants, whose identities the order permitted investigators to withhold. In 1951 Executive Order 10241 replaced “reasonable grounds for belief” with “reasonable doubt,” tightening the standard. The box should not treat the later revision as if it were part of the 1947 text. Of the more than five million federal workers screened between 1947 and 1956, the program produced an estimated 2,700 dismissals and 12,000 resignations; the box should give those figures with their source and note that they understate the program’s reach.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-eo10241][^ch37-truman-loyalty]

- **The registered status.** No image is assigned to this chapter, so the section is planned as a textual close reading of the executive order. Reproducing a facsimile of the order would require a new register entry, with its own caption, credit, rights, and alt text, before it could be added.[^ch37-check-focus][^ch37-check-image]
- **The exact text.** The Part V standard and the list of grounds are quoted from the full text of the order as reproduced by the American Presidency Project and must be collated against the *Federal Register*, 12 FR 1935 (March 25, 1947), and the enrolled original.[^ch37-eo9835]
- **What must not be done.** The box must not treat every investigation as persecution, must not treat the procedural protections as dispositive, and must not let one sentence stand in for the whole experience of the investigated, which §3.3 and §3.5 present in their own terms.[^ch37-check-focus][^ch37-check-silences]

### 3.3 HUAC, the Hollywood Ten, and the blacklist

- **The claim.** The House Committee on Un-American Activities’s October 1947 hearings on communism in Hollywood produced the first systematic political blacklist in the film industry, after ten witnesses refused to answer questions about their affiliations.[^ch37-hollywood-ten]
- **The episode.** The ten producers, directors, and screenwriters — Alvah Bessie, Herbert Biberman, Lester Cole, Edward Dmytryk, Ring Lardner Jr., John Howard Lawson, Albert Maltz, Samuel Ornitz, Adrian Scott, and Dalton Trumbo — were voted in contempt of Congress on November 24, 1947, convicted the next year, and sentenced to six months to one year in prison; most were then blacklisted.[^ch37-hollywood-ten]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the hearings, the contempt vote, the sentences, and the blacklist. It cannot show that all the witnesses were communists or that the blacklist ended their employment entirely, since some wrote under pseudonyms.[^ch37-hollywood-ten][^ch37-check-silences]

### 3.4 The Smith Act, Dennis, and the Rosenbergs

- **The claim.** The federal government also prosecuted communist leadership criminally: the Smith Act prosecutions of 1948–51 and the espionage case of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg made the courtroom a central arena of anticommunism.[^ch37-dennis][^ch37-rosenberg]
- **The case.** In 1948 eleven Communist Party leaders were convicted under the Smith Act of conspiring to advocate the overthrow of the government, and in *Dennis v. United States* (1951) the Supreme Court upheld the convictions 6–2, with Justices Hugo L. Black and William O. Douglas dissenting against what Black called “a virulent form of prior censorship of speech and press.” The Rosenbergs were executed at Sing Sing on June 19, 1953, after Eisenhower declined clemency.[^ch37-dennis][^ch37-rosenberg]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the indictments, convictions, and sentences. It cannot show that the Rosenbergs’ guilt or the fairness of the trials is settled; the chapter should present the case’s contested evidence and the later archival findings as separate claims.[^ch37-rosenberg][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 McCarthy and the politics of accusation

- **The claim.** On February 9, 1950, Senator Joseph R. McCarthy of Wisconsin told a Republican women’s club in Wheeling, West Virginia, that he held “a list of 205” State Department employees known to the secretary of state as members of the Communist Party, launching the most visible phase of the second Red Scare.[^ch37-mccarthy][^ch37-check-names]
- **The dispute.** The precise contents of the Wheeling speech quickly became contested, and McCarthy’s numbers changed from 205 to 57 as he repeated the charge. The chapter should present the speech as a political performance whose factual claims were unstable, not as a report.[^ch37-mccarthy]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the speech’s occasion and its reported words. It cannot show that the list existed as described, and the chapter should attribute the claim to McCarthy rather than treating it as evidence.[^ch37-mccarthy][^ch37-check-debate-works]

## 4. China and the atomic shock of 1949

### 4.1 The claim: a cascade of reversals

- **The claim.** In the space of a few months in 1949 the communist victory in China and the Soviet atomic test shattered the assumption that containment was working, and 1950 brought a policy response, NSC-68, that militarized containment.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-nsc68]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the shocks in sequence and show how each was read through the others, rather than treating them as isolated events.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-nsc68]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the events and the policy documents. It cannot show that either event caused NSC-68, and the chapter should present the timing as context, not proof.[^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.2 The “loss” of China

- **The claim.** On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the People’s Republic of China, ending the civil war against the Nationalists, who retreated to Taiwan; the United States suspended diplomatic ties with the new state for decades.[^ch37-chinese-rev]
- **The politics of blame.** The Truman administration published the “China White Paper” in August 1949, arguing that only Chinese forces could determine the civil war’s outcome, but the step failed to protect it from the charge that it had “lost” China, a charge that the unresolved Nationalist government on Taiwan and the outbreak of the Korean War intensified.[^ch37-chinese-rev]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the victory, the retreat, and the American response. It cannot show that American aid could have reversed the outcome, and the chapter should present the counterfactual as an argument.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 The Soviet atomic test, August 29, 1949

- **The claim.** The Soviet Union detonated its first atomic device at the Semipalatinsk test site in Kazakhstan on August 29, 1949, ending the American atomic monopoly and forcing a re-examination of American strategy.[^ch37-soviet-bomb]
- **The evidence.** The device, known in the West as “Joe-1,” was detected by American monitoring in September 1949, and the detection was announced without explaining how the United States had found it. The chapter should date the test and the announcement separately.[^ch37-soviet-bomb]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the test and the detection. It cannot show how quickly the American atomic advantage had actually narrowed, and the chapter should avoid treating the test as an immediate strategic parity.[^ch37-soviet-bomb][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 NSC-68, April 14, 1950

- **The claim.** NSC-68, presented to Truman on April 14, 1950, argued that the Soviet Union sought to impose its absolute authority over the rest of the world and that the United States had to mobilize to ensure the survival of “civilization itself.”[^ch37-nsc68]
- **The argument.** The report described a world polarized between freedom and slavery, said the “assault on free institutions is world-wide,” and concluded that the United States had “no choice but to demonstrate the superiority of the idea of freedom by its constructive application” while using military power to deter attack and “hasten the decay of the Soviet system.”[^ch37-nsc68]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the document and its recommendations. It cannot show that it caused the military buildup by itself, because the Korean War supplied both the occasion and the budget.[^ch37-nsc68][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The China lobby, Taiwan, and the politics of blame

- **The claim.** The “loss of China” became a domestic political weapon that tied foreign policy to loyalty, and the defense of Taiwan became a test of anticommunist resolve.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-eo9835]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should connect the fall of China to the domestic investigations of §3, because the charge that officials had betrayed China overlapped with the charge that they were disloyal.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-truman-loyalty]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the rhetoric and the policy. It cannot show that the “China lobby” alone directed policy, and the chapter should present its influence as a contested claim.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-check-debate-works]

## 5. Korea through the 1953 armistice

### 5.1 The claim: containment becomes a shooting war

- **The claim.** When North Korean forces attacked South Korea in June 1950, the United States led a United Nations command that fought a three-year war, making Korea the first major battle waged in the name of containment.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-map28]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the war as both a superpower confrontation and a Korean civil and national conflict, because the Korean actors had their own aims and the war’s human cost fell heavily on Koreans.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-newhistory-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the invasion, the command, and the armistice. It cannot show that Moscow ordered the attack or that Beijing’s intervention was inevitable, and the chapter should attribute those claims to their evidence.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The invasion, June 25, 1950, and the United Nations

- **The claim.** On June 25, 1950, North Korean troops attacked south across the 38th parallel toward Seoul; the United Nations Security Council condemned the invasion as a “breach of the peace,” and the United States and other member nations sent forces to support South Korea.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]
- **The division.** After the Second World War the United States and the Soviet Union divided Korea at the 38th parallel to oversee the removal of Japanese forces; the Soviets backed Kim Il Sung in the north and the United States backed Syngman Rhee in the south, and American forces withdrew from the south in the late 1940s.[^ch37-korea-milestone]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the attack and the international response. It cannot show that American officials had excluded Korea from the defense perimeter in a way that invited the attack, and the chapter should present that as an interpretation.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 Inchon, the push north, and Chinese intervention

- **The claim.** The American-led force pushed the North Korean army back above the 38th parallel and beyond, and after the People’s Republic of China entered the war in late 1950, the fighting settled into a costly stalemate.[^ch37-korea-milestone]
- **The sequence.** The State Department’s account records the return of American forces, the United Nations authorization, the drive back above the parallel, and Chinese entry in late 1950, after which the government worked to isolate Beijing and hold its coalition together.[^ch37-korea-milestone]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the campaigns and the intervention. It cannot show that the advance to the Yalu was the cause of Chinese entry rather than one condition among several, and the chapter should present that as an open question.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.4 Stalemate and the armistice of July 27, 1953

- **The claim.** After two years of truce talks, the Korean Armistice Agreement was signed at Panmunjom on July 27, 1953, ending active combat without a peace treaty and leaving the peninsula divided near the original line.[^ch37-armistice]
- **The instrument.** The armistice was signed by Lieutenant General William K. Harrison Jr. for the United Nations Command and General Nam Il for the Korean People’s Army and the Chinese People’s Volunteers, at 10 a.m.; the truce took effect at 10 p.m. It suspended hostilities, created a demilitarized zone about 4,000 meters wide, and was signed by no nation, only by military commanders.[^ch37-armistice]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the terms and the outcome. It cannot show that the armistice was inevitable or that it settled the war’s political questions, since no peace treaty followed. The chapter must not present the armistice as a settlement.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 The costs, the alliance, and Map 28

- **The claim.** The war killed millions of soldiers and civilians, deepened the American commitment to a permanent military presence in Northeast Asia, and left a divided Korea that persists.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]
- **The statistics.** The National Archives records that the three-year conflict “claimed the lives of millions of soldiers and civilians.” United States in-theater deaths are commonly given as 36,574 in the Defense Casualty Analysis System, while older totals near 54,000 circulate; the chapter should give the count, its source, its cutoff, and the difference between total in-theater deaths and battle deaths, and should not present one figure as settled. Korean and Chinese military and civilian losses are estimates whose methods must be named. These figures resolve to App. B, Table 4.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-check-numbers][^ch37-korea-casualties]
- **The alliance.** In 1953 the United States and South Korea signed a mutual security treaty to protect the South, and the American security commitment to Japan and Korea became a lasting part of the containment structure.[^ch37-korea-milestone]
- **Map 28 and the expanded commitment.** Map 28’s 1952 snapshot must include the Korean theater and the Northeast Asian commitments without implying that the map covers the 1953 armistice or later events.[^ch37-check-map28][^ch37-korea-milestone]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The wartime alliance ended and was replaced by open confrontation.** The disputes over Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic bomb, and the institution-building of 1947–49, are documented.[^ch37-truman-doctrine][^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **Containment was institutionalized.** The Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, the Berlin airlift, NATO, and the National Security Act created durable structures.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-nato-milestone]
- **The domestic loyalty program existed and reached millions.** Executive Order 9835 created a federal loyalty program that screened more than five million workers between 1947 and 1956.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-truman-loyalty]
- **China became a communist state and the Soviet Union tested an atomic device in 1949.** The dates and the policy responses are documented.[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-soviet-bomb]
- **The Korean War ended in an armistice, not a peace.** The agreement, its terms, and its unresolved political questions are documented.[^ch37-armistice]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Who began the Cold War?** Orthodox accounts emphasize Soviet expansion and broken agreements; revisionist accounts emphasize American power and economic expansion; post-revisionist accounts emphasize security dilemmas and mutual misperception. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch37-orthodox-works][^ch37-revisionist-works][^ch37-postrevisionist-works]
- **Was the atomic bomb a diplomatic instrument?** One position holds that the bomb was used and the monopoly held as leverage against the Soviets; another treats the atomic decision as primarily military and the postwar program as a response to Soviet power. The chapter should present the strongest version of each.[^ch37-revisionist-works][^ch37-check-atomic]
- **Was the Korean War a superpower proxy or a Korean civil war?** A superpower frame reads it as a test of containment directed from Moscow; Bruce Cumings and others center Korean politics and society. The chapter should weigh both against the sources.[^ch37-korea-milestone][^ch37-newhistory-works]
- **How costly was the domestic loyalty program?** Defenders cite the procedural protections and the small number of dismissals; critics cite the standard, the informants, and the “chilling effect” on a far larger number of employees. The chapter should present both with evidence.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-domestic-works]
- **How many died in Korea?** Estimates vary widely by method, population, and cutoff. The chapter should give ranges, name the methods, and refuse to present one number as settled.[^ch37-check-numbers][^ch37-korea-casualties]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which American power, Soviet power, ideology, security dilemmas, and human choices all mattered, and in which the domestic costs of containment were as real as its foreign commitments. Neither a story of Soviet aggression met by innocence nor a story of American empire alone explains the record.[^ch37-postrevisionist-works][^ch37-domestic-works]
- **Contingency.** The Marshall Plan, NATO, NSC-68, the loyalty program, and the Korean intervention were choices taken by people who could have chosen otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome.[^ch37-marshall-plan][^ch37-nsc68]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold structural pressures — geography, the power vacuum, the atomic monopoly, and domestic politics — together with the contingent choices made within them.[^ch37-postrevisionist-works][^ch37-check-debate-works]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that the United States built a permanent security state abroad and at home between 1945 and 1953, that it did so in response to real Soviet power and to its own political needs, and that the institutions it created outlasted the crises that produced them.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-eo9835]

## Legacies

The years from 1945 to 1953 left the United States a permanent national security state. The National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency, the Department of Defense, and the alliance system built in these years outlasted the crises that produced them, and the military budget that followed NSC-68 and the Korean War reshaped the federal government.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-nsc68]

The domestic loyalty program left a shorter but sharper legacy. The dismissals and resignations were a small fraction of those screened, but the program’s reach and its reliance on informants and broad standards of association became a cautionary precedent for later debates over security and civil liberty, and the Supreme Court’s later narrowing of the Smith Act prosecutions did not restore the careers the investigations had ended.[^ch37-truman-loyalty][^ch37-dennis]

The Korean War left an armistice that is not a peace, a demilitarized zone that remains, and a continuing American military presence in South Korea. The war’s unresolved politics and its heavy Korean and Chinese death tolls are part of the chapter’s argument that containment’s costs fell unequally on the people of the places where it was fought.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]

## Summary

- In February 1946 Kennan’s Long Telegram described the Soviet Union as insecure, expansionist, and responsive to force, and urged a patient, firm containment without general war.[^ch37-kennan-long-telegram]
- The wartime alliance broke down over Germany, Eastern Europe, and the atomic monopoly, and the Baruch Plan for international control failed when the Soviet Union rejected it in 1946.[^ch37-check-atomic][^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- On March 12, 1947, Truman asked Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey and announced the policy of supporting “free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation.”[^ch37-truman-doctrine]
- The Marshall Plan, signed on April 3, 1948, appropriated $13.3 billion over four years for European recovery and aimed to build stable Western European democracies.[^ch37-marshall-plan]
- The Soviet blockade of Berlin from June 1948 to May 1949 was answered by the Berlin airlift, and on April 4, 1949, twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty.[^ch37-berlin-airlift][^ch37-nato-milestone]
- The National Security Act of 1947 created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency, and the Department of Defense.[^ch37-nato-milestone]
- Executive Order 9835 of March 21, 1947, created the federal loyalty program; its standard was tightened by Executive Order 10241 in 1951, and more than five million workers were screened between 1947 and 1956.[^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-eo10241][^ch37-truman-loyalty]
- The Hollywood Ten were cited for contempt of Congress on November 24, 1947, and eleven Communist Party leaders were convicted under the Smith Act, upheld in *Dennis v. United States* in 1951.[^ch37-hollywood-ten][^ch37-dennis]
- On February 9, 1950, McCarthy claimed to hold “a list of 205” State Department employees known as communists, launching the most visible phase of the second Red Scare.[^ch37-mccarthy]
- The People’s Republic of China was proclaimed on October 1, 1949, and the Soviet Union tested its first atomic device on August 29, 1949; NSC-68 of April 14, 1950, called for a military buildup to ensure the survival of “civilization itself.”[^ch37-chinese-rev][^ch37-soviet-bomb][^ch37-nsc68]
- North Korean forces attacked south on June 25, 1950; after Chinese intervention in late 1950 the war stalemated, and the armistice of July 27, 1953, ended active combat without a peace treaty.[^ch37-armistice]
- The war killed millions of soldiers and civilians and left the United States with permanent commitments in Northeast Asia and an unresolved division of Korea.[^ch37-armistice][^ch37-korea-milestone]

> *A closing note.* Between 1945 and 1953 the United States answered a broken wartime alliance with a permanent security state abroad and at home. It built the institutions of containment — the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, NATO, the National Security Council, and the Central Intelligence Agency — and it screened, investigated, and blacklisted its own citizens in the name of loyalty. The chapter’s question — how did a wartime alliance become a permanent security confrontation? — is answered by that double record of commitment and coercion, and by a Korean armistice that ended the fighting without ending the war.[^ch37-nato-milestone][^ch37-eo9835][^ch37-armistice]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in 1950 in Levittown, New York, where developers were building the first mass-produced suburbs for a growing and largely White middle class. Chapter 38 takes up the affluent society of the 1950s and asks for whom postwar abundance was secure. The security state that Chapter 37 describes did not recede as the fighting in Korea ended; it became the backdrop for the consumer culture and the conformity that the next chapter examines.[^ch37-check-bridge][^ch37-check-ch38]
