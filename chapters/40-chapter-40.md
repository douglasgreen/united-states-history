## Epigraphs

> “The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time.”[^ch40-great-society-speech]

— Lyndon B. Johnson, remarks at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, May 22, 1964.[^ch40-great-society-speech]

> “Just as the 1960s forgot the failures of the 1930s, we today forget the failures of the 1960s.”[^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

— Amity Shlaes, *Great Society: A New History* (New York: Harper, 2019), as quoted in Fred Siegel’s review.[^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

> “It’s a myth to say the Great Society failed, just as it’s a myth to portray it as a radical left-wing, big-government project.”[^ch40-zeitz]

— Joshua Zeitz, *Building the Great Society: Inside Lyndon Johnson’s White House* (New York: Viking, 2018), adapted in *Politico Magazine*, January 28, 2018.[^ch40-zeitz]

- **Why these three.** Johnson stated the ambition in the period’s own words: the Great Society would end poverty and racial injustice, not merely administer relief. Shlaes argues that the programs’ failures were structural and were forgotten in their own time. Zeitz argues that the same programs were moderate in design, effective in important respects, and misremembered by both friends and critics. The three disagree about the program’s aims, its results, and how it should be remembered.[^ch40-great-society-speech][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-zeitz]

Zeitz’s sentence is quoted from the *Politico Magazine* adaptation of the book and must be collated against the published text. House style requires a period voice and a historian; Johnson supplies the period voice, and Shlaes and Zeitz supply the historians who disagree.[^ch40-check-epigraphs][^ch40-great-society-speech][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-zeitz]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On July 30, 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson traveled to the Harry S. Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, and signed the Social Security Amendments of 1965 — the Medicare and Medicaid Act — into law. Johnson chose the Truman Library to honor the former president, who had first proposed a national health insurance plan twenty years earlier.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-medicare-senate][^ch40-medicare-nara]
- **Who attended.** The LBJ Presidential Library records that those present included Johnson; former President Harry S. Truman; Truman’s wife, Bess; Lady Bird Johnson; and Vice President Hubert H. Humphrey. Johnson invited Senator Clinton Anderson of New Mexico, who had managed the bill.[^ch40-medicare-ssa][^ch40-medicare-senate]
- **The enrollment.** At the ceremony Johnson enrolled Truman as the first Medicare beneficiary and presented him with the first Medicare card; Truman and Bess received the first two cards. Johnson signed Truman’s application for optional Part B medical coverage as a witness. The photographs and the appointment log for the day are held by the Truman and LBJ libraries.[^ch40-medicare-ssa][^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-medicare-cards]
- **The law.** The act established Medicare as Title XVIII of the Social Security Act — hospital insurance plus a voluntary supplementary plan covering physicians’ services — and Medicaid as Title XIX, a federal-state program of medical assistance for people with limited income. It covered up to 90 days of hospital care, 100 days of nursing home care, and 100 home health visits per year; enrollees over 65 could pay $3 a month for Part B, which covered 80 percent of doctor bills and related services.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-medicare-nara]
- **The scale.** Nearly 20 million beneficiaries enrolled in the program’s first three years; budget funding for Medicare and Medicaid in fiscal year 1966, the program’s first full year, was about $2.2 billion, and more than 2.5 million Americans received hospital care covered by Medicare in the first six months.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-medicare-lbj]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the place, the participants, the provisions, the enrollment of the Trumans, and the implementation figures. It cannot show what Truman privately thought that morning, what Johnson’s political calculation weighed, or that the ceremony itself produced the statute. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch40-check-scene][^ch40-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** On July 30, 1965, no one knew how the program would grow, that Medicaid would become the largest source of federal health coverage for low-income people, or that Medicare’s hospital-insurance trust fund would become a recurring fiscal controversy. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch40-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A ceremony opens a question.** The scene’s claim is not that a bill signing created the welfare state but that the ceremony made visible the transfer of a twenty-year-old reform proposal into an operating federal program, and it placed that transfer under the gaze of a former president who had been defeated on the same issue.[^ch40-medicare-senate][^ch40-medicare-lbj]
- **The stakes.** The act joined health security to the New Deal’s social insurance framework, and it did so through a “three-layer cake” of hospital insurance, physicians’ insurance, and expanded federal assistance to the states — a design that later mattered as much as the ceremony.[^ch40-medicare-senate]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: what could federal rights and social programs achieve, and what constrained them?[^ch40-check-scope]
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the signing table to the War on Poverty, the 89th Congress, the education and immigration statutes, the organizing of women, consumers, environmentalists, and gay and lesbian activists, the cities and their budgets, and the backlash of 1966–68.[^ch40-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented dialogue or interior thought.** The ceremony, the participants, and the enrollment are documented. The scene may not stage a fabricated exchange, an unrecorded remark by Truman, or a private thought attributed to Johnson.[^ch40-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the program’s later growth, the Medicaid expansion, the fiscal crises of the 1970s, or the 1968 election.[^ch40-check-scene]
- **No single-hero framing.** The scene must not reduce the statute to Johnson alone; Anderson’s management, the Democratic majorities, the 1964 landslide, and the movement pressure of the preceding decade were all part of the same achievement.[^ch40-medicare-senate][^ch40-check-silences]

## 1. The New Frontier and Kennedy’s death

### 1.1 The claim: a stalled program and a sudden inheritance

- **The claim.** Between 1961 and November 1963 Kennedy’s New Frontier proposed a broad domestic agenda but won only part of it from a Congress whose southern Democrats controlled the key committees; Johnson inherited both the unfinished program and the political opening created by Kennedy’s assassination.[^ch40-jfk-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the Great Society as an extension of proposals that predated Johnson — aid to education, medical care for the elderly, urban aid, regional development, and a tax cut — and should show that the difference after 1963 lay in a landslide majority, a skilled legislator, and the movement pressure of the civil rights years, not in a wholly new set of ideas.[^ch40-jfk-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the proposals, the votes, and the electoral results. It cannot show that any single cause produced the 1964–65 legislative burst, and the chapter should present the sequence as the interaction of inheritance and opportunity.[^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 The New Frontier and its limits

- **The claim.** Kennedy took office during the fourth postwar recession, with business bankruptcies at their highest level since the 1930s and 5.5 million people looking for work, and he pursued tax cuts, a higher minimum wage, and new social programs, but southern committee chairmen and the parochial-school dispute blocked much of the agenda.[^ch40-jfk-miller]
- **The evidence.** Kennedy proposed federal aid to education, medical care for the elderly, urban mass transit, a Department of Urban Affairs, and regional development in Appalachia. He won an increase in the minimum wage and a housing bill, but the medical program for the elderly went nowhere, southern Democrats killed the Department of Urban Affairs on the assumption that Kennedy would appoint an African American secretary, and the education bill foundered on the question of aid to parochial schools.[^ch40-jfk-miller]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the proposals and their fates. It cannot show that Kennedy would have succeeded after 1964, and the chapter should mark that counterfactual as speculation.[^ch40-jfk-miller][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 1.3 The assassination and the 1964 mandate

- **The claim.** Kennedy’s assassination on November 22, 1963, gave Johnson both the presidency and a moral mandate to finish Kennedy’s program; Johnson signed the Revenue Act of 1964 in February and the Civil Rights Act of 1964 in July, then won a landslide over Barry Goldwater in November with large Democratic majorities.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]
- **The evidence.** On the evening of the assassination Johnson told aides that every issue on his desk had been on it when he entered Congress in 1937 — civil rights, health insurance for the elderly and the poor, aid to education, higher-education support, and antipoverty and nutrition programs. In his first State of the Union address he declared an “unconditional war on poverty.” The 1964 election returned 68 Democratic senators and 295 representatives, giving Johnson working, often veto-proof majorities.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the dates, the statutes, and the election result. It cannot show that the landslide alone produced the 89th Congress’s output, and the chapter should hold the mandate and the organizing together.[^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The “Great Society” and the Great Society speech

- **The claim.** Johnson first used the phrase “Great Society” in a speech at Ohio University on May 7, 1964, and developed it in his May 22, 1964, commencement address at the University of Michigan, which named cities, the countryside, and the classrooms as the three places to begin.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-great-society-speech]
- **The text.** At Michigan Johnson said the Great Society “rests on abundance and liberty for all” and “demands an end to poverty and racial injustice,” and he described “a creative federalism, between the National Capital and the leaders of local communities.” The chapter should quote only what the argument requires.[^ch40-great-society-speech]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the speech and its framing. It cannot show that the speech committed the government to specific programs, and the chapter should present it as a statement of purpose whose content came later.[^ch40-great-society-speech][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 Consequences and the map

- **The claim.** By the end of 1964 Johnson had converted an inherited agenda into an electoral mandate, and the 89th Congress was poised to pass the largest body of social legislation since the New Deal.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-shlaes-siegel]
- **The map.** The registered visual `ch40-map-01` (Map 31) belongs to §2; it should show the geography of poverty and of Great Society program activity, distinguishing where need was measured from where programs were placed. Its caption and source line must identify the retrospective poverty definition and threshold used, name the observation years and geographic units, and cite Appendix B, Table 7 for economic context; the underlying series must come from the Census poverty reconstruction and Office of Economic Opportunity and Appalachian Regional Commission records.[^ch40-check-map31]
- **The limit.** The map can show the coincidence of need and provision but not causation; the chapter should keep the causal argument in the prose.[^ch40-check-map31]

## 2. The 89th Congress: poverty, health, education, and immigration

### 2.1 The claim: a legislative system, not a single program

- **The claim.** The 89th Congress of 1965–67 assembled a linked system of antipoverty, health, education, and immigration programs, and the system’s reach depended on how each program was designed, funded, and administered, not on the breadth of the president’s rhetoric.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the statutes as a portfolio with different mechanisms — cash-adjacent services for the poor, social insurance for the elderly, grants-in-aid for the states, and a new immigration preference system — and should show that each had a different constituency and a different fate.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-immigration-house]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the appropriations, and the enrollment figures. It cannot show that the programs formed a coherent design, and the chapter should say that the coherence was partly retrospective.[^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 2.2 The Economic Opportunity Act and the War on Poverty

- **The claim.** The Economic Opportunity Act, signed on August 20, 1964, created the Office of Economic Opportunity under R. Sargent Shriver, launched the Job Corps, VISTA, and the Community Action Program, and made poverty a permanent subject of federal administration.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-eoa-lbj][^ch40-great-society-history]
- **The evidence.** Johnson declared an “unconditional war on poverty” in January 1964. The Community Action Program required each local Community Action Agency to have “maximum feasible participation” by residents of the communities served, and this requirement produced conflict between community activists and city officials who had expected to control the agencies. By the end of Johnson’s presidency more than 1,000 Community Action Agencies were in operation; by 1967 Congress had given local governments the option to take them over.[^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **The programs and the numbers.** Project Head Start began as an eight-week summer program for about 500,000 children ages three to five, and other initiatives included legal services and neighborhood health centers. Federal expenditures targeted at the poor doubled from about $6 billion in 1965 to about $12 billion in 1968, and the poverty rate fell from about 20 percent to about 12 percent between 1964 and 1974, though a booming economy did part of that work.[^ch40-earnings-history][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the agencies, the funding, and the poverty series. It cannot show that any single program caused the decline in poverty, and the chapter should present the “maximum feasible participation” conflict as a design choice with political consequences.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 Medicare and Medicaid

- **The claim.** The Social Security Amendments of 1965 created Medicare, a social insurance program for the elderly, and Medicaid, a federal-state program of medical assistance for people with limited income, ending two decades of failed attempts to enact national health insurance for the aged.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-medicare-senate]
- **The evidence.** Medicare was Title XVIII of the Social Security Act and Medicaid was Title XIX. Senate Finance Committee chairman Harry Byrd of Virginia and the American Medical Association had defeated earlier bills, including a narrow loss in 1962, and the 1964 landslide changed the congressional arithmetic. The final bill was a “three-layer cake” of hospital insurance for the aged, physicians’ insurance for the elderly, and expanded federal assistance to state medical payments for the poor.[^ch40-medicare-senate][^ch40-medicare-nara]
- **The limit and the link.** The act required a Communist-organization oath carried over from the existing Social Security Act, and its implementation used Title VI of the Civil Rights Act to require hospitals to integrate as a condition of reimbursement. The chapter should present Medicare as both a social insurance achievement and a program whose administration enforced the civil rights settlement.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-ch39]

### 2.4 Education: the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and the Higher Education Act

- **The claim.** The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 broke the long deadlock over federal aid to schools by directing funds to school districts with concentrations of low-income students, and the Higher Education Act of 1965 expanded scholarships, work-study, and low-interest loans for college students.[^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **The evidence.** ESEA’s Title I, which accounted for five-sixths of the act’s authorized funds, sent money to schools and districts with high percentages of low-income students; Title II supported libraries and textbooks; later titles supported special education and adult education. Johnson signed the act in April 1965 at the one-room schoolhouse near Stonewall, Texas, that he had attended as a child, with his former teacher Kate Deadrich Looney present. Sources give the signing date as April 9, 11, or 12, and the chapter must collate the date before publication.[^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **The link to civil rights.** Weeks after the act passed, the Johnson administration used Title VI of the Civil Rights Act to threaten the withholding of federal school funds from segregated districts, and between 1965 and 1968 the share of Black students in the South attending majority-white schools rose from roughly 2.3 percent to almost 23.4 percent. The chapter should present the aid and the enforcement as one system.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-ch39]

### 2.5 Immigration: the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965

- **The claim.** Signed at the base of the Statue of Liberty on October 3, 1965, the Immigration and Nationality Act — the Hart–Celler Act — abolished the national-origins quota system and replaced it with preference categories that favored family reunification and needed skills.[^ch40-immigration-house]
- **The evidence.** The bill was introduced by House Judiciary Committee chairman Emanuel Celler and managed in the Senate by Philip A. Hart; it passed the House 318 to 95. It capped annual visas at 290,000, with a restriction of 20,000 visas per country per year, and its family-reunification provisions admitted far more immigrants than the framers had expected: annual immigration rose to nearly half a million, and only about 20 percent came from Europe.[^ch40-immigration-house]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, the vote, the quotas, and the flow. It cannot show that Congress foresaw the demographic result, and the chapter should distinguish the law’s text from its later consequences.[^ch40-immigration-house][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 2.6 Map 31: need and provision

## 3. Women, consumer, environmental, and early gay and lesbian organizing

### 3.1 The claim: rights organizing beyond the civil rights statutes

- **The claim.** The same years that produced the Great Society’s statutes also produced new organizing among women, consumers, environmentalists, and gay and lesbian people, and these groups used the federal government’s language of rights and its new administrative agencies as instruments of their own demands.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-dob-loc]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the organizing as both a response to and a use of the liberal state: Title VII’s ban on sex discrimination, the new consumer agencies, the conservation statutes, and the federal security apparatus that policed gay and lesbian employees all provided targets and leverage.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-nader-fhwa]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the organizations, their demands, and their actions. It cannot show that these movements were unified or that they shared a single agenda, and the chapter should present their internal differences.[^ch40-check-silences][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 3.2 Women: *The Feminine Mystique* and the National Organization for Women

- **The claim.** Betty Friedan’s *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) named a widespread dissatisfaction among suburban white middle-class women, and in 1966 Friedan and others founded the National Organization for Women, which demanded that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforce Title VII’s guarantee of equality in employment.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm]
- **The evidence.** Friedan’s book was published in 1963 and became a best-seller that identified “the problem that has no name.” NOW had 28 founders and elected Friedan its first president; its first action was to demand that the EEOC end sex-segregated help-wanted advertising. The chapter should note that NOW’s early leadership drew mainly on white, middle-class, educated women and that later critics faulted it for that focus.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the book, the organization, and the demands. It cannot show that the book caused the organizing, and the chapter should present it as a catalyst among several and give the women’s movement’s internal diversity.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-check-silences]

### 3.3 Consumers: Ralph Nader and auto safety

- **The claim.** Ralph Nader’s *Unsafe at Any Speed* (1965) attacked the auto industry for putting style and cost ahead of safety, and the ensuing Senate hearings and public pressure produced the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act and the Highway Safety Act in 1966.[^ch40-nader-fhwa]
- **The evidence.** Nader, then a young lawyer, had written on auto safety and been hired as a consultant; his book, published in November 1965, focused on the General Motors Corvair’s rear suspension. Senator Abraham Ribicoff’s subcommittee held hearings in 1965, and Johnson signed the two safety laws on September 9, 1966. The chapter should also name the era’s modest consumer statutes, including the packaging and labeling law.[^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the book, the hearings, and the statutes. It cannot show that the book alone produced the laws, and the chapter should present Nader as one force among several, including the industry’s own scandals.[^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 Environment: *Silent Spring* and the conservation statutes

- **The claim.** Rachel Carson’s *Silent Spring* (1962) brought the hazards of DDT and other pesticides to a mass audience, and in the same years Congress passed the first federal air- and water-quality laws and the Wilderness Act.[^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-cleanair-epa][^ch40-wilderness]
- **The evidence.** Carson’s book was published on September 27, 1962, and drew fierce attacks from the chemical industry. The Clean Air Act of 1963 was the first federal legislation to authorize control of air pollution; the Water Quality Act of 1965 set national water quality standards; and the Wilderness Act, signed on September 3, 1964, created the National Wilderness Preservation System and placed 54 areas totaling 9.1 million acres into it. Howard Zahniser of the Wilderness Society wrote 66 drafts of the act over eight years, and died four months before Johnson signed it.[^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-cleanair-epa][^ch40-wilderness]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the books, the statutes, and the acreage. It cannot show that *Silent Spring* alone produced the laws, and the chapter should distinguish public alarm from the administrative machinery of enforcement.[^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Gay and lesbian organizing before Stonewall

- **The claim.** The early 1960s brought the first sustained public organizing for gay and lesbian rights, from the Mattachine Society and the Daughters of Bilitis to Frank Kameny’s pickets and the 1966 “sip-in” at Julius’ bar in New York.[^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-julius-gvshp]
- **The evidence.** Kameny was dismissed from federal employment in 1957 because he was gay, petitioned the Supreme Court for reinstatement, and co-founded the Mattachine Society of Washington; in 1965 he led small groups in the first public protests for gay rights at the White House and in Philadelphia. The Daughters of Bilitis, founded in San Francisco on September 21, 1955, by Rosalie “Rose” Bamberger, Del Martin, Phyllis Lyon, and others, began as a private social club and grew into a national organization; from 1956 it published *The Ladder*, the first nationally distributed lesbian magazine, and its member Ernestine Eckstein joined the 1965 pickets. On April 21, 1966, members of the Mattachine Society staged a “sip-in” at Julius’ bar in Greenwich Village, identifying themselves as homosexuals and asking to be served; the action helped end New York State’s practice of prohibiting the serving of alcohol to anyone known to be gay.[^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-dob-smithsonian][^ch40-julius-gvshp]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the organizations, the protests, and the licensing change. It cannot show that these groups represented all gay and lesbian people, and the chapter should name the price of visibility: known members were watched by local police and by the FBI and the CIA.[^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** Before the 1969 Stonewall uprising — which belongs to Chapter 42 — no participant knew it would become the movement’s emblem, and the chapter must not read the later narrative back into 1965–66.[^ch40-check-ch42-43][^ch40-check-scene]

## 4. Cities and fiscal pressures

### 4.1 The claim: urban policy and the cost of guns and butter

- **The claim.** The Great Society expanded federal urban policy through the new Department of Housing and Urban Development and the Model Cities program, but by 1966–68 the cost of the Vietnam War, rising inflation, and the political price of open housing constrained what the programs could do.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-zeitz]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the cities as the place where the Great Society’s promise and its fiscal limits met, and should show that the constraint was both budgetary and electoral.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the departments, the appropriations, and the surtax. It cannot show that the war alone caused the programs’ limits, and the chapter should give the fiscal and political causes together.[^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 4.2 HUD and Robert C. Weaver

- **The claim.** The Department of Housing and Urban Development was created in September 1965, and in January 1966 Robert C. Weaver became its first secretary and the first African American to serve in a presidential cabinet; HUD coordinated slum clearance, public housing, and urban redevelopment.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **The evidence.** Weaver had administered the Housing and Home Finance Agency under Kennedy and helped draft the administration’s housing and urban renewal programs, including a $7.8 billion housing bill in 1965 that expanded public housing and created a rent-supplement program for low-income families. As HUD secretary he promoted the 1966 Metropolitan Development Act and the Demonstration Cities (Model Cities) program. Critics including Senators Abraham Ribicoff and Robert F. Kennedy charged in 1966 that federal urban renewal had failed to stop urban decay.[^ch40-weaver-miller]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the department, the programs, and the criticism. It cannot show that HUD reduced urban poverty, and the chapter should present the criticism as a live dispute rather than a settled verdict.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 The guns-and-butter squeeze

- **The claim.** Johnson insisted the country could afford both the Great Society and the Vietnam War, and for several years rapid growth sustained that claim; by 1968 inflation and the war’s costs forced a temporary income tax surcharge, and the fiscal room for new domestic programs closed.[^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **The evidence.** Johnson signed the Revenue and Expenditure Control Act of 1968 on June 28, 1968, which enacted a temporary 10 percent income tax surcharge as Public Law 90-364, 82 Stat. 251. The chapter should present the surcharge as the moment the guns-and-butter bargain was acknowledged to be over, and should keep the total costs and the inflation series with their sources and base years.[^ch40-surcharge-state]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute and the surcharge. It cannot isolate the war’s fiscal effect from the era’s broader inflation, and the chapter should mark that attribution as an interpretation.[^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 In Focus: an early Medicare card

**In Focus.** The In Focus reads one identified early Medicare card as a document of the new social insurance program, using the card issued to Harry S. Truman as the reading copy. The card is a small object with large claims embedded in it. Its printed fields — name, claim number, hospital-insurance entitlement, and the Part B election — record a design decision: Medicare was built as a contribution-based social insurance program administered through the Social Security system, not as a means-tested welfare benefit, and the enrollment of a former president at the signing made that social-insurance identity visible on the first day. The box should distinguish four things the ceremony and the card tend to merge: the signing of the law on July 30, 1965; Truman’s enrollment and the issuance of the first cards; eligibility under the law; and the program’s actual start of coverage, which came later. It should also read the card’s exclusions and conditions — the Part B premium, the deductibles, and the Communist-organization oath carried over from the existing Social Security Act — as evidence that the program was both universal in aspiration and bounded in design. ## 5. Backlash and the political transition

### 5.1 The claim: reform and reaction together

- **The claim.** The Great Society and the civil rights statutes produced a white political backlash that reshaped the parties, and by 1966 the Johnson legislative momentum had ended; the result was not the program’s repeal but the beginning of its containment and of a durable Republican resurgence.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-1966-retroreport]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the backlash as a political response to real changes in law and neighborhood life, not as a simple verdict on individual voters’ motives, and it should keep the movement demands and the reaction in the same frame.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-1966-retroreport]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the votes, the election results, and the defeated bills. It cannot show that any one issue caused the shift, and the chapter should not treat an election map as proof of motive.[^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 Open housing and the white backlash

- **The claim.** When the administration proposed a fair-housing provision in 1966, it met a backlash among white voters, particularly in northern communities that usually supported Democrats, and the bill died in Congress.[^ch40-zeitz]
- **The evidence.** Johnson had pressed for a bill to bar racial discrimination in housing sales and rentals; a *Time* correspondent told a White House aide that the “backlash” issue had overtaken Vietnam as the top concern for white voters in Indiana and Ohio; the bill failed. The chapter should distinguish the 1966 defeat from the Fair Housing Act that passed in 1968 after King’s assassination, which belongs to Chapter 39.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-ch39]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the proposal, the defeat, and the reporting. It cannot show how individual voters weighed neighborhood and race, and the chapter should present the backlash as a political fact with contested causes.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The 1966 midterms and the Republican resurgence

- **The claim.** In the 1966 midterms the Republicans gained 47 House seats, three Senate seats, eight governorships, and 557 state legislative seats, won a majority of the aggregated national Senate vote, and made deep inroads in the South for the first time since Reconstruction.[^ch40-midterm-ashbrook]
- **The evidence.** The party’s gains halted Johnson’s legislative momentum and advanced a generation of Republican leaders: Ronald Reagan was elected governor of California in 1966 and became a national conservative figure, and Richard Nixon, who campaigned hard for Republican candidates, later called 1966 a crucial step toward the White House.[^ch40-midterm-ashbrook]
- **The realignment.** The 1966 elections accelerated a regional realignment that had begun with white southern defections from the Democrats in 1964; the chapter should present the realignment as a process over several elections, and Chapter 42 owns the 1968 election that completed it.[^ch40-1966-retroreport][^ch40-check-ch42-43]

### 5.4 The long hot summers and the crises of 1968

- **The claim.** The urban uprisings of 1965–68 made urban poverty and policing a national crisis, the Kerner Commission blamed white institutions and white racism, and the resulting political pressure, combined with the stalemate in Vietnam, led Johnson to withdraw from the 1968 race and set the stage for the Nixon administration.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-check-ch39]
- **The evidence.** Federal troops were deployed in Los Angeles, Detroit, and Washington, D.C., between 1964 and 1968; Johnson appointed the Kerner Commission in July 1967, and it reported in March 1968 that the nation was “moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal,” a finding Johnson largely rejected. By 1968, with his attention on Vietnam, Johnson’s efforts to extend the Great Society had ended; he told Doris Kearns that he had sacrificed “the woman I really loved — the Great Society” to the war.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-check-ch39]
- **The limit and the hand-off.** The chapter should present the uprisings and their consequences without re-narrating their movement history, which belongs to Chapter 39, and it should stop at the transition to the Nixon administration rather than narrating the 1968 campaign, which belongs to Chapter 42.[^ch40-check-ch39][^ch40-check-ch42-43]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The 89th Congress enacted a large and durable body of social legislation.** The Economic Opportunity Act, Medicare and Medicaid, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, the Higher Education Act, and the Immigration and Nationality Act are documented.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-immigration-house]
- **Medicare and Medicaid became lasting parts of American health care.** The statutes, the enrollment of nearly 20 million beneficiaries in the first three years, and the Civil Rights Act’s use in hospital desegregation are documented.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-zeitz]
- **The Great Society reduced measured poverty while leaving it intact.** Federal expenditures targeted at the poor doubled between 1965 and 1968, and the poverty rate fell from about 20 percent to about 12 percent between 1964 and 1974, but poverty persisted and became more concentrated.[^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **New organizing among women, consumers, environmentalists, and gay and lesbian people emerged in the same years.** *The Feminine Mystique*, NOW, *Unsafe at Any Speed*, *Silent Spring*, the Wilderness Act, and the Mattachine and Daughters of Bilitis actions are documented.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm][^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-wilderness][^ch40-dob-loc]
- **The Great Society produced a political backlash that reshaped the parties.** The defeat of open housing in 1966, the Republican gains of 1966, and the fiscal squeeze of 1968 are documented.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-midterm-ashbrook][^ch40-surcharge-state]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Did the Great Society complete the New Deal or fall short of it?** Zeitz and Zelizer present the programs as a moderate, largely effective completion of New Deal liberalism; Quadagno, Matusow, and Sugrue emphasize the racial and structural limits that kept the welfare state two-tiered. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-zelizer][^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-matusow][^ch40-sugrue]
- **Was the main problem design or politics?** Moynihan argued that the antipoverty programs failed because social-science theory was imposed without clear goals, and Marris and Rein traced the dilemmas of community-action reform; other accounts stress that the programs were undermined by underfunding, federalism, and the war. Moynihan’s 1965 Labor Department report *The Negro Family* had already framed the debate over race, family structure, and poverty, and was itself attacked for locating the problem in family structure rather than in jobs and discrimination. The chapter should distinguish design from politics and treat the report as a contested period document.[^ch40-moynihan-max][^ch40-moynihan-report][^ch40-quadagno]
- **How much did pressure from below shape the legislation?** Zelizer’s account emphasizes the interaction of Johnson, Congress, and the civil rights movement; a president-centered account emphasizes Johnson’s skill and the 1964 landslide. The chapter should present the balance as contested.[^ch40-zelizer][^ch40-shlaes-siegel]
- **Did the backlash explain the program’s limits, or did the program’s limits produce the backlash?** Accounts of white backlash emphasize northern neighborhood politics and the open-housing fight; fiscal and administrative accounts emphasize inflation, the war, and the two-tier design. The chapter should hold both without collapsing one into the other.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-surcharge-state]
- **What should be counted as the Great Society’s measure?** Supporters point to the poverty decline, the reduction of racial exclusion in southern schools and hospitals, and the durability of Medicare and Medicaid; critics point to the persistence of poverty, the concentration of urban disadvantage, and the fiscal and political costs. The chapter should say what each measure can and cannot establish.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-shlaes-siegel][^ch40-lbj-domestic]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the Great Society delivered real and durable security — health coverage, school aid, and legal equality in federally funded institutions — while leaving poverty, racial hierarchy, and urban disadvantage intact, and in which the reform’s limits came from design, federalism, race, and war together rather than from any one cause.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-quadagno]
- **Contingency.** The open-housing defeat of 1966, the surcharge of 1968, and the “maximum feasible participation” conflict were decisions people made and could have made otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-surcharge-state][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold structural conditions — the racial order, federalism, the postwar boom, and the Cold War — together with the contingent choices made within them.[^ch40-check-scope][^ch40-zelizer]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that between 1961 and 1968 the federal government built the broadest social provision since the New Deal and used it to enforce legal equality in federally funded institutions, and that the same years revealed how much of poverty, racial hierarchy, and urban disadvantage lay beyond the reach of the programs and how quickly the war and the backlash consumed the political room to extend them.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]

## Legacies

The years from 1961 to 1968 left the country a larger and more durable federal role in health, education, and income security. Medicare and Medicaid became permanent, school aid and federal enforcement of desegregation reshaped southern education, and the immigration act of 1965 remade the sources of American immigration; those changes outlived the political coalition that produced them.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-immigration-house]

The years also left a two-tier welfare state and a politics of backlash. Quadagno argues that racism divided social provision into a universal social insurance tier and a stigmatized means-tested tier; Matusow and Sugrue connect liberal reform and urban racial conflict to the backlash that ended the reform era. The result was a welfare state that grew and a coalition that fractured at the same time.[^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-matusow][^ch40-sugrue]

The years left a memory and a dispute. The Great Society became, for its defenders, proof that government could expand opportunity and, for its critics, proof that it could not; the chapter should present both verdicts as arguments to be tested against the record rather than as settled conclusions.[^ch40-zeitz][^ch40-shlaes-siegel]

## Summary

- On July 30, 1965, Johnson signed the Social Security Amendments of 1965 at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, creating Medicare as Title XVIII and Medicaid as Title XIX; he enrolled Harry S. Truman as the first Medicare beneficiary and gave the Trumans the first two cards.[^ch40-medicare-lbj][^ch40-medicare-ssa][^ch40-medicare-nara]
- Kennedy’s New Frontier proposed aid to education, medical care for the elderly, urban aid, and regional development but won only part of it; southern committee chairmen and the parochial-school dispute blocked the rest.[^ch40-jfk-miller]
- Johnson signed the Revenue Act of 1964 and the Civil Rights Act of 1964, declared an “unconditional war on poverty” in January 1964, and won a landslide over Barry Goldwater that brought 68 Democratic senators and 295 representatives.[^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-zeitz]

- The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 sent Title I funds to districts with concentrations of low-income students, and the Higher Education Act of 1965 expanded scholarships, work-study, and loans; by 1968 the share of Black students in southern majority-white schools had risen from about 2.3 percent to almost 23.4 percent.[^ch40-esea-vcu][^ch40-zeitz]
- The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, signed on October 3, abolished the national-origins quota system and set family reunification and skills as priorities; it passed the House 318 to 95 and was followed by a jump in annual immigration to nearly half a million.[^ch40-immigration-house]
- The Department of Housing and Urban Development was created in September 1965, and Robert C. Weaver became its first secretary and the first African American cabinet member in January 1966.[^ch40-weaver-miller][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
- Betty Friedan’s *The Feminine Mystique* appeared in 1963, and Friedan and others founded the National Organization for Women in 1966, whose first action was to demand that the EEOC enforce Title VII.[^ch40-friedan-nwhm]
- Ralph Nader’s *Unsafe at Any Speed* appeared in November 1965 and helped produce the 1966 auto-safety laws; Rachel Carson’s *Silent Spring* appeared in 1962, the Clean Air Act of 1963 and the Water Quality Act of 1965 expanded federal pollution control, and the Wilderness Act of 1963–64 protected 9.1 million acres.[^ch40-nader-fhwa][^ch40-carson-nrdc][^ch40-cleanair-epa][^ch40-wilderness]
- The Mattachine Society and the Daughters of Bilitis built the first sustained public gay and lesbian organizing; Kameny led the 1965 White House pickets, and the April 21, 1966, sip-in at Julius’ bar helped end New York’s ban on serving gay patrons.[^ch40-dob-loc][^ch40-kameny-loc][^ch40-julius-gvshp]

> *A closing note.* Between 1961 and 1968 the federal government built the broadest system of social provision since the New Deal and used it to enforce legal equality in federally funded schools and hospitals. The system was real, and so were its limits: poverty persisted, the welfare state was divided by race, urban disadvantage deepened, and the war and the backlash consumed the political room to extend it. The chapter’s question — what could federal rights and social programs achieve, and what constrained them? — is answered by that double record of durability and constraint.[^ch40-medicare-nara][^ch40-lbj-domestic][^ch40-quadagno][^ch40-zeitz]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on August 4, 1964, with the naval reports from the Gulf of Tonkin and the resolution that followed, and takes up the Vietnam War and the global Cold War. Chapter 41 follows containment into the client state, the escalation, the draft, and the crises of 1968, and it shows how the same years that produced the Great Society also produced the war that helped constrain it. The domestic liberal state and the global garrison state grew together, and the next chapter turns to the second of them.[^ch40-check-ch41][^ch40-check-bridge][^ch40-lbj-domestic]
