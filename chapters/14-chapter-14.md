## Epigraphs

> “Peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations, entangling alliances with none.”[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]

— Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1801.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]

> “The General Government has no powers but such as the Constitution gives it. … It has not given it power of holding foreign territory, and still less of incorporating it into the Union. An amendment of the Constitution seems necessary for this.”[^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

— Thomas Jefferson to John Dickinson, 1803, as quoted by the National Constitution Center.[^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

> “Sell a country! Why not sell the air, the great sea, as well as the earth? Did not the Great Spirit make them all for the use of his children?”[^ch14-tecumseh-speech]

— Tecumseh, as reported by the American Battlefield Trust.[^ch14-tecumseh-speech]

> “Although most Americans pretended they had won the war — even calling it a ‘Second War of Independence’ — they could point to few concrete gains — certainly none in the peace treaty — to sustain this claim.”[^ch14-hickey-pbs]

— Donald R. Hickey, “An American Perspective on the War of 1812,” PBS LearningMedia.[^ch14-hickey-pbs]

- **Why these four.** A president promised honest friendship with all nations. The same president privately concluded that acquiring Louisiana lay beyond the Constitution. An Indigenous statesman refused the premise that land could be sold. A modern historian answered that the war fought to vindicate American rights produced few concrete gains. The four disagree about whether the era’s republicanism was principled restraint, constitutional evasion, theft, or a triumph of memory over substance.[^ch14-check-epigraphs]
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Jefferson’s First Inaugural is quoted from the Avalon Project’s edition and must be collated against the manuscript and *The Papers of Thomas Jefferson*, vol. 33. The Dickinson passage is quoted as the National Constitution Center reproduces it and must be collated against Founders Online or the printed *Papers*. Tecumseh’s speech survives only in a later reported rendering; it carries its own limitation note and should not be set as a verbatim utterance until a scholarly edition is consulted. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The morning.** On Wednesday, March 4, 1801, Thomas Jefferson emerged from Conrad and McMunn’s boarding house at New Jersey Avenue and C Street, where he had lived for several months, and walked the short distance to the unfinished Capitol. He was accompanied by Virginia militia officers, District of Columbia marshals, and a group of congressmen.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]
- **The dress.** Demonstrating what the Thomas Jefferson Encyclopedia calls his desire for “republican simplicity,” Jefferson broke with the precedent of Washington and Adams, who had worn elegant suits and swords and arrived in liveried coaches. The *Alexandria Times* reported that he wore the clothes “of a plain citizen without any distinctive badge of office.”[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]
- **The chamber and the absence.** Jefferson found the Senate Chamber “so crowded,” one observer noted, that “not another creature could enter—there was near a thousand persons within the walls.” John Adams, the outgoing president, had left town in the middle of the night and did not attend.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]
- **The oath and the address.** Chief Justice John Marshall, Jefferson’s distant cousin and political opponent, administered the oath. Jefferson then read an address that one observer said was “delivered in so low a tone” as to be barely audible; he had given an advance copy to a printer that morning so it could be distributed the same day.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]
- **The words.** The address called for “peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations, entangling alliances with none”; promised “a wise and frugal Government, which shall restrain men from injuring one another, shall leave them otherwise free to regulate their own pursuits of industry and improvement, and shall not take from the mouth of labor the bread it has earned”; and declared, “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists.”[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]
- **The witness.** Margaret Bayard Smith wrote that she had “this morning witnessed one of the most interesting scenes, a free people can ever witness,” and that changes of administration elsewhere had “most generally been epochs of confusion, villainy and bloodshed,” while in this country they took place “without any species of distraction, or disorder.”[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]

### The scene’s argument

- **A contested transfer becomes a ceremony of reconciliation.** The scene should open with the plain-dressed president walking through the new capital and let the reader watch a partisan crisis dissolve into a ritual. The point is not that the ceremony was sacred; it is that a transfer of party power, which Federalists had feared as a coup, was performed in public as ordinary.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]
- **The reconciliation and its limits.** “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists” sat beside the exclusions the new government would defend: enslaved people, Native nations, and women took no part in choosing it, and the Federalists in Congress had worked to block the transfer only weeks earlier.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-check-names]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: could a republic of limited government expand without enlarging the coercive power it criticized?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that Jefferson’s frugality made the government weak. Within four years the administration would buy Louisiana on doubtful constitutional authority, fight a war in the Mediterranean, and enforce an embargo with the army and navy it had been reducing. The instruments of restraint would become the instruments of expansion.[^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary]
- **The widening.** The scene stands on a March morning in 1801. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader through the purchase, the embargo, the western war, and the peace of 1815.[^ch14-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No horse.** Some accounts claim that Jefferson rode a horse to the Capitol; the Thomas Jefferson Encyclopedia states plainly that this is incorrect and cites a thorough rebuttal. The scene follows the documented walk and names the story as unsupported. The Chapter 13 bridge’s wording about Jefferson “walked to the Capitol” should be confirmed against this scene before the bridges are matched.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration][^ch14-check-horse]
- **No invented dialogue, weather, or interior thought.** The walk, the dress, the crowded chamber, Adams’s absence, the oath, and the address are documented; the crowd’s feelings and the day’s weather are not.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration][^ch14-check-scene]
- **No claim that the audience heard the address.** Observers noted how quietly Jefferson spoke; the address reached the country through print, not through the chamber.[^ch14-monticello-inauguration]
- **No claim that the transfer was inevitable or unanimously accepted.** The scene should keep the recent electoral crisis and the exclusions in view.[^ch14-check-ch13][^ch14-check-names]

## 1. Jefferson’s program and Marbury

### 1.1 The promise of a frugal government

- **The claim.** Jefferson’s program was a deliberate program of restraint: economy in government, the repeal of internal taxes, the reduction of the army and navy, and the payment of the public debt.
- **The stated principles.** The First Inaugural promised “a wise and frugal Government,” “economy in the public expense, that labor may be lightly burthened,” “the honest payment of our debts and sacred preservation of the public faith,” and “encouragement of agriculture, and of commerce as its handmaid.”[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]
- **The measures.** Peter Onuf’s account for the Miller Center states that Jefferson reduced the number of employees subject to presidential appointment, cut the Army to two regiments (about 3,500 men) with “similar reductions in the Navy,” pressed Congress to abolish the direct tax of 1798, and pardoned the ten people still imprisoned under the Alien and Sedition Acts. Onuf adds that, even after paying $15 million in cash for Louisiana, the national debt fell from about $80 million to about $57 million during Jefferson’s two terms.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic]
- **The cost to be examined.** The program reduced taxes and military spending while the government was simultaneously acquiring territory, fighting Tripoli, and later enforcing an embargo. §2–§3 test whether restraint and expansion were compatible. The program should be presented as a real choice, not as hypocrisy from the start.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-monticello-embargo]

### 1.2 The judiciary, the midnight judges, and Marbury

- **The claim.** Jefferson’s program faced a Federalist judiciary that the Republicans could not remove by appointment, and the resulting conflict produced *Marbury v. Madison* and the principle of judicial review.
- **The setting.** The lame-duck Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801 on February 13 of that year. It created sixteen new circuit judgeships, relieved Supreme Court justices of circuit riding, and reorganized the federal courts. Adams appointed the new judges in his final weeks; the Federal Judicial Center notes that Secretary of State John Marshall raced to deliver the commissions and failed to distribute some on Adams’s last day.[^ch14-fjc-midnight][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]
- **The repeal.** The Republican Congress repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801, the repeal signed January 22, 1802, and passed the Judiciary Act of 1802, which abolished the new courts and restored circuit riding. Divisions over the repeal survived in *Stuart v. Laird* (1803), in which the Court held that the Constitution did not restrain Congress’s power to reorganize the courts.[^ch14-capitol-repeal][^ch14-fjc-midnight]
- **The case.** William Marbury, Dennis Ramsay, Robert Townsend Hooe, and William Harper moved in December 1801 for a writ of mandamus compelling Secretary of State James Madison to deliver their commissions as justices of the peace. In February Term 1803, Chief Justice Marshall held that Marbury had a right to the commission and that the laws afforded a remedy, but that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 granting the Court power to issue such writs to officers was unconstitutional, so the Court could not issue it.[^ch14-marbury-text]
- **The words.** Marshall wrote that “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is,” and that “a law repugnant to the constitution is void.” The National Archives calls the decision the moment the Court first declared an act of Congress unconstitutional.[^ch14-marbury-text][^ch14-marbury-na]
- **The consequence.** Jefferson won the immediate fight—the commission was never delivered and the 1801 act was repealed—but the decision strengthened the branch he could not remove. Restraint in the executive coincided with the enlargement of judicial power. This is the chapter’s first example of how a limited government’s conflicts produced lasting state capacity.[^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-fjc-midnight]

### 1.3 The Twelfth Amendment and the rules of party

- **The claim.** The tie of 1800 produced a constitutional repair—separate electoral votes for president and vice president—that made partisan succession routine.
- **The measure.** Congress passed the Twelfth Amendment on December 9, 1803, and the states ratified it on June 15, 1804. Electors thereafter named their choices for president and vice president on distinct ballots, and the Vice President’s tie-breaking role in the Senate was narrowed. Chapter 13 owns the crisis that produced it; this chapter notes the repair.[^ch14-twelfth-amendment][^ch14-check-ch13]
- **The consequence.** The machinery of the transfer was fixed, but the chapter’s central question—how a limited government would expand—was not. That question moves to the West in §2.[^ch14-twelfth-amendment][^ch14-check-scope]

## 2. Louisiana and the expeditions

### 2.1 The purchase and the constitutional doubt

- **The claim.** The Louisiana Purchase doubled the republic’s claimed territory and forced Jefferson to choose between the strict construction he had preached and the expansion he wanted.
- **The transaction.** The treaty with France was signed April 30, 1803. The National Archives records that the United States purchased 828,000 square miles west of the Mississippi for $15 million, “for roughly 4 cents an acre.” Livingston and Monroe had been authorized to pay up to $10 million for New Orleans and the Floridas; they were offered the entire territory and agreed to a price beyond their instructions.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-senate-louisiana]
- **The ratification.** The Senate approved the treaty for ratification by 24 votes to 7 on October 20, 1803, with the treaty signed on October 31. All seven opponents were Federalists who objected that the acquisition lacked specific constitutional authorization. The Senate’s own history records that the Haitian revolution and the prospect of renewed war with Britain had pushed Napoleon to sell.[^ch14-senate-louisiana][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]
- **The doubt.** Jefferson, a strict constructionist, wrote to John Dickinson that “the General Government has no powers but such as the Constitution gives it,” that it had “not given it power of holding foreign territory, and still less of incorporating it into the Union,” and that “an amendment of the Constitution seems necessary for this.” He nevertheless concluded that “we must ratify and pay our money, as we have treated, for a thing beyond the Constitution, and rely on the nation to sanction an act done for its great good, without its previous authority.”[^ch14-ncc-louisiana]
- **The abandoned amendment.** Jefferson drafted an amendment incorporating the province and confirming to the “Indian inhabitants” their rights of occupancy and self-government. His cabinet disagreed about the need for it, and the deadline forced the treaty through without one. The draft is evidence of what Jefferson thought the Constitution required and of what he was willing to do without.[^ch14-jefferson-draft-amendment][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]
- **The consequence.** The purchase passed without an amendment, and the constitutional question was never resolved in court. The episode is the chapter’s clearest case of expansion outrunning the limits of republican doctrine.[^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-na-louisiana]

### 2.2 The expeditions and the reach of the state

- **The claim.** The western expeditions were instruments of commerce, diplomacy, and knowledge, funded quietly and aimed at Native nations as much as at geography.
- **The message.** In a confidential message of January 18, 1803, Jefferson asked Congress for $2,500 “for the purpose of extending the external commerce of the United States,” a phrase he expected to “cover the undertaking from notice.” He described a policy of encouraging Native nations to abandon hunting and to multiply trading houses: “to provide an extension of territory which the rapid increase of our numbers will call for, two measures are deemed expedient,” he wrote, the first to encourage agriculture and domestic manufacture, the second to multiply trading houses among them.[^ch14-secret-message]
- **The instructions.** Jefferson’s instructions to Meriwether Lewis, dated June 20, 1803, appointed him to carry the objects of the confidential message into execution, provided instruments for celestial observations, and directed the expedition toward the Missouri River and the water communication to the Pacific. The Corps of Discovery departed in 1804 and returned in 1806. The National Park Service notes that it depended on more than fifty Native nations and “set forth untold loss” for the tribes who helped it survive.[^ch14-lewis-instructions][^ch14-nps-lecl]
- **The map.** Map 11 (`ch14-map-01`) plots the Louisiana Purchase’s claimed boundaries against the routes actually traveled by the Lewis and Clark and Pike expeditions between 1803 and 1806. It must distinguish the treaty’s claims from the explored and documented routes, and it must state its date. Zebulon Pike’s expedition is part of the map’s scope; a source for its route and dates is an open task.[^ch14-check-map][^ch14-check-pike]
- **The consequence.** The expedition was state policy as well as exploration. The medals, trade goods, and instructions carried west were the instruments of a diplomacy that treated Native nations as sovereigns to be courted and as obstacles to be displaced at once. That double purpose is the subject of the In Focus box.[^ch14-secret-message][^ch14-monticello-medals]

### 2.3 In Focus: a Jefferson peace medal

- **In Focus: a Jefferson peace medal.** **In Focus.** The Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History holds a Jefferson peace medal, object ID 1990.0466.0001, from accession 1990.0466. It is silver, hollow, and made of two thin embossed plates joined by a ring around the circumference, about 10.15 centimeters across, because the United States Mint lacked a press strong enough to strike heavy solid medals. One face bears the bust of Thomas Jefferson facing left; the other bears clasped hands beneath a crossed peace pipe and hatchet, with the words “PEACE AND FRIENDSHIP.” The Smithsonian lists Robert Scot as the engraver of both faces; Monticello and Encyclopedia Virginia credit the design to John Reich, a German immigrant who later became an assistant engraver at the Mint—a discrepancy the chapter must resolve before publication. This medal, or one of its type, accompanied the Lewis and Clark expedition, which distributed about eighty-seven of them to Indigenous leaders. Read closely, the medal is a diplomatic instrument, not a decoration. Jefferson described the practice in 1793 as an “antient Custom,” writing that the medals “confer no power” but serve as “marks of friendship.” The two faces carry the chapter’s contradiction in miniature: the clasped hands of friendship on one side, and the crossed pipe and hatchet—peace and war—on the other, offered by a republic that was also preparing to take the land. The box should read the object as evidence of how expansion addressed Native sovereignty, not as a curiosity.[^ch14-si-medal][^ch14-monticello-medals][^ch14-encyclopediava-medal][^ch14-check-medal-engraver]

## 3. Barbary conflict, embargo, and Atlantic war

### 3.1 The Barbary War

- **The claim.** The first overseas war under the “frugal” program was a naval war with Tripoli, fought to end tribute and protect Mediterranean commerce.
- **The war.** In May 1801 the Pasha of Tripoli, Yusuf Karamanli, declared war on the United States after the consul informed him that the United States would not meet his latest tribute demand. Congress authorized Jefferson to send the Navy in February 1802. On October 31, 1803, the frigate USS *Philadelphia*, commanded by William Bainbridge, ran aground on a reef in Tripoli harbor; Bainbridge and his crew were captured and held about two years.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary]
- **The turn.** In February 1804 Lieutenant Stephen Decatur led a party aboard a disguised ketch into Tripoli harbor and burned the *Philadelphia* rather than let Tripoli refit it. A land operation under William Eaton, with a small Marine contingent and the exiled Hamet Karamanli, marched from Alexandria and took Derna. A peace treaty in June 1805 ended the war; it retained Yusuf Karamanli in power and required a ransom for the prisoners, though the Office of the Historian records that it provided for no future tribute.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-state-barbary]
- **The consequence.** The small-navy policy did not prevent war; the administration fought when it judged the interest great enough. The Barbary War is the chapter’s first instance of the republican government projecting force overseas while promising economy at home.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]

### 3.2 The embargo and the turn to coercion

- **The claim.** After the *Chesapeake*–*Leopard* affair, Jefferson chose an economic embargo, and the enforcement of that embargo enlarged the very government it was meant to restrain.
- **The affair.** On June 22, 1807, the British warship *Leopard* fired on the American frigate *Chesapeake* after its captain refused a search for deserters. The British took four men; three American seamen were killed and others wounded. Early in 1808 Secretary of State James Madison reported that 4,228 American seamen had been impressed into British service since the war’s start, 936 discharged, leaving 3,292 in service.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **The policy.** On December 17, 1807, Jefferson recommended “an inhibition of the departure of our vessels from the ports of the United States.” Congress passed the Embargo Act four days later. Supplementary, additional, and enforcement acts followed in 1807 and 1808; the enforcement act allowed officials to seize cargo on suspicion and empowered the President to use the Army or Navy to enforce the embargo.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **The failure.** The embargo did not force Britain to relent, damaged the American economy, and was repealed in March 1809, replaced by the Non-Intercourse Act. Jefferson later wrote that “the affair of the Chesapeak put war into my hand. I had only to open it, and let havoc loose.”[^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **The consequence.** The embargo is the chapter’s clearest illustration of the central question. A policy chosen to avoid war and to avoid a standing military required domestic coercion—seizure, bonding, and the use of armed force against American merchants—and it failed on its own terms. The chapter should show the enforcement machinery as the bridge between restraint and war.[^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]

### 3.3 The road to war: neutral rights and the West

- **The claim.** The declaration of war in 1812 rested on maritime grievances and on the western and southern demand to secure the frontier against Native resistance and to expand.
- **The grievances.** The two leading causes were the British Orders in Council, which restricted American trade with the European continent, and impressment, the Royal Navy’s removal of seamen from American vessels. Congress had already passed non-intercourse measures; the Monroe–Pinkney Treaty of 1806 failed because it did not address impressment, and Jefferson did not send it to the Senate.[^ch14-ussconstitution-1812][^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **The politics.** The “War Hawks,” led by Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun, pressed the case for war as a defense of American honor and as a second war of independence; the Federalists opposed it, and New England and the maritime states feared its effect on trade. Madison asked for a declaration on June 1, 1812; Congress voted on June 18, 1812, and no Federalist voted for war.[^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-na-ghent]
- **The timing.** Hickey notes that Britain was preparing to suspend the Orders in Council even as Congress voted, but the news could not cross the Atlantic in time. The declaration was made, in part, against a policy already being abandoned.[^ch14-hickey-loa]
- **The consequence.** A war fought for “free trade and sailors’ rights” was also a war to secure the western frontier and open Native land to settlement. The western war and the maritime war were one conflict, and §4 turns to its Native center.[^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-check-slavery]

## 4. Tecumseh, Tenskwatawa, and Prophetstown

### 4.1 The confederacy and the Prophet’s town

- **The claim.** Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa built a pan-Indigenous movement against unilateral land cessions, and the American response helped drive the country to war.
- **The setting.** The Treaty of Fort Wayne of 1809, which required Indiana nations to sell about three million acres, helped provoke the resistance. Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa founded Prophetstown in 1808 as the center of a new confederacy; Tenskwatawa led a nativist “Purification” movement that rejected Anglo-American ways, and Tecumseh recruited across nations and languages, including among the Muscogee (Creek), whose Red Stick movement he encouraged.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh]
- **The argument.** Tecumseh’s reported reply to the land sales—that no one could sell a country any more than the air or the great sea—should be presented as a reported rendering rather than a verbatim quotation. Its substance, that Native nations held land in common and could not alienate it by treaty with the United States, was the confederacy’s central political claim.[^ch14-tecumseh-speech][^ch14-check-tecumseh]
- **The consequence.** The confederacy disputed the premise of American expansion: that land could be bought and that title rested on the buyer and seller alone. Its rise turned a western land question into a continental conflict.[^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-check-slavery]

### 4.2 Tippecanoe and the destruction of Prophetstown

- **The claim.** William Henry Harrison’s attack at Tippecanoe in November 1811 destroyed Prophetstown and deepened the war’s western dimension.
- **The battle.** Harrison led about 1,000 soldiers and militia toward Prophetstown. On the evening of November 6, 1811, he was met with a white flag and a request for a parley; he camped about a mile from the town. Before dawn on November 7, Tenskwatawa’s forces attacked the camp. Harrison’s line held, and the Americans then burned Prophetstown. Tecumseh was absent, recruiting in the South.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe]
- **The numbers.** The American Battlefield Trust estimates American casualties at about 189 and Native casualties at about 120. These totals differ across sources and must be given as ranges with their definitions.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe][^ch14-check-numbers]
- **The consequence.** Tippecanoe intensified calls for war and narrowed the confederacy’s options. Tecumseh and the remnant of his forces allied with Britain when the war began, and Native nations became “the greatest losers” of the conflict that followed.[^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-hickey-loa]

## 5. The War of 1812 and peace

### 5.1 The war on the frontiers

- **The claim.** The early campaigns failed, and the war exposed the military weakness of a republic that had chosen a small army and navy.
- **The campaigns.** The American invasion of Canada failed; American forces had earlier burned public buildings in Canada, and the British retaliated. On August 24–25, 1814, the British army burned the Capitol, the President’s house, and other public buildings in Washington. In September the British failed to take Baltimore; the defense of Fort McHenry produced Francis Scott Key’s “Defence of Fort M’Henry.” American victories at Lake Champlain and elsewhere undermined British bargaining power.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa]
- **The White House.** As the British approached Washington, President Madison asked his wife, Dolley Madison, to stay behind and gather important documents and the state portrait of George Washington. The popular legend has her tearing the portrait from its frame and carrying it out; state and federal historians caution that the enslaved members of the household most likely carried it out under her orders. The chapter should present the documented order, name the legend, and identify the enslaved people whose labor the legend erases. Paul Jennings’s later recollection is the source to consult; asserting his account requires reading it.[^ch14-dncr-dolley][^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-check-dolley]
- **The consequence.** The war demonstrated that the republic’s military weakness had real costs. It also showed that the government’s response to invasion could bypass the restraints the program had promised, from enforced militia service to the burning of the capital itself.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-slavery]

### 5.2 Ghent, Hartford, and New Orleans

- **The claim.** The peace restored the prewar borders, settled none of the war’s declared causes, and arrived after a ruinous final battle.
- **The treaty.** The Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, restored relations to the status quo ante bellum. Article I provided for the restoration of territory and possessions and for the return of “any Slaves or other private property.” Article IX provided that the United States would end hostilities with the tribes and restore to them the “possessions, rights, and privileges” they had enjoyed in 1811. Article X recorded that both parties would use their best endeavors to abolish the slave trade. The treaty said nothing about impressment or neutral rights.[^ch14-na-ghent]
- **The ratification.** The Senate approved the treaty unanimously on February 16, 1815, the day Madison sent it. News of the treaty took about six weeks to cross the Atlantic, so the final battle was fought in ignorance of it.[^ch14-senate-ghent]
- **The Hartford Convention.** Twenty-six Federalists from New England met at Hartford in December 1814 and January 1815 to protest a war they regarded as ruinous to their region. The convention took a moderate, states’-rights course, proposing constitutional amendments rather than voting secession; its report arrived in Washington alongside news of the victory and the peace, and it discredited the Federalist party.[^ch14-battlefields-hartford][^ch14-hickey-loa]
- **New Orleans.** On January 8, 1815, Andrew Jackson’s force, about 5,700 engaged, defeated a numerically superior British army of about 8,000 at the Chalmette plantation below New Orleans. American casualties were about 71; British casualties about 2,034, including the death of Major General Sir Edward Pakenham. Jackson’s army included Tennessee and Kentucky militia, Louisiana militia, Free Men of Color, Choctaw, and Jean Lafitte’s privateers. News of the victory and of the treaty arrived almost together.[^ch14-battlefields-neworleans][^ch14-senate-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa]
- **The consequence.** The convergence of the victory, the peace, and the Hartford report produced a surge of nationalism and forever discredited the Federalists, even though the treaty conceded none of the war’s stated aims. The war also broke the Native confederacy’s power and ended its ability to count on a European ally. The chapter’s point is that the republic expanded and secured itself by the coercive means it had criticized: war, embargo enforcement, and the dispossession it ratified at Ghent.[^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-na-ghent]
- **The numbers.** Casualty figures, the size of the engaged forces, and the death toll including disease differ across sources. Hickey estimates American battle deaths at about 2,300 but total deaths from all causes, including disease, at as many as 20,000. Every figure must carry its denominator, date, and source, and estimates must be given as ranges.[^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-check-numbers]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Retrenchment was real.** Jefferson cut internal taxes, reduced the army and navy, and paid down the debt while in office. The program was a genuine attempt to shrink the federal government’s domestic footprint.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-jefferson-inaugural]
- ***Marbury* established judicial review.** The Court for the first time held an act of Congress unconstitutional, and the decision strengthened the judiciary against the administration that had tried to dismantle the 1801 court system.[^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-marbury-text]
- **Louisiana was acquired on doubtful authority.** Jefferson himself believed an amendment was necessary, drafted a proposed one, and then ratified the treaty without it.[^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-jefferson-draft-amendment]
- **The expeditions were state policy.** Congress funded them quietly for commerce and diplomacy, and they carried American power, gifts, and claims into Native homelands.[^ch14-secret-message][^ch14-nps-lecl]
- **The embargo was coercive and failed.** It required seizure, bonding, and armed enforcement, damaged the American economy, and did not move Britain.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **The war ended in a negotiated status quo, and the Native confederacy lost most.** The treaty settled nothing about impressment and left the tribes worse off than in 1811.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-hickey-loa][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the restraint sincere or already compromised?** Did Jefferson mean to shrink the state and simply meet emergencies, or was restraint a political language used to legitimate expansion?[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]
- **Did expansion strengthen or endanger the republic?** Louisiana and the war enlarged the country and deepened the sectional and slavery questions that would produce the Missouri crisis and, eventually, civil war. The chapter should hold both outcomes open.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-check-slavery]
- **What was the War of 1812 about?** Maritime rights, national honor, western expansion, or Native dispossession? The chapter should weigh the evidence rather than choose one cause.[^ch14-battlefields-warhawks][^ch14-hickey-pbs]
- **How much was contingency?** The Orders in Council were being suspended as Congress voted; the peace and the victory arrived together; Napoleon’s defeat changed the war’s conditions. The chapter should show how much depended on timing and accident.[^ch14-hickey-loa]
- **What do the archives show about the people the expansion displaced and enslaved?** Enslaved people and Native nations appear in the treaties and letters mainly as property and as obstacles, and the chapter must name that limit rather than let the documents speak for everyone.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-slavery][^ch14-check-dolley]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which Jeffersonian restraint and continental expansion were not opposites but partners: the frugal republic expanded by borrowing the fiscal, military, and diplomatic instruments it had criticized, and it paid for the expansion in the dispossession and enslavement of others.
- **The administration and its defenders.** The sympathetic tradition represented by Dumas Malone and Merrill D. Peterson treats Jefferson’s program as a principled attempt to keep the republic small and to fund expansion without a standing military or heavy taxes. It takes the language of restraint seriously.[^ch14-check-sources]
- **The empire of liberty.** Gordon S. Wood and Peter S. Onuf read the era as the construction of an “empire of liberty,” in which the extension of republicanism across the continent was the central project rather than a departure from it. Onuf’s Miller Center essay is the essayist’s own version of that reading, and it should be cited as an interpretation, not as neutral ground.[^ch14-millercenter-domestic][^ch14-check-sources]
- **The war as a second war of independence.** Popular memory and much older scholarship framed the War of 1812 as a vindication of American sovereignty; Hickey, writing in the nationalist tradition, still calls it the country’s “most obscure war” and questions the concrete gains.[^ch14-hickey-pbs][^ch14-hickey-loa]
- **The war as expansion.** Histories of the War Hawks and of the West, including work by Reginald Horsman and Alan Taylor, treat the conflict as a war for land and for the destruction of Native power, not only for sailors’ rights.[^ch14-check-sources][^ch14-battlefields-warhawks]
- **The war as failure.** The Federalist critique, and Hickey’s verdict, notes that the treaty conceded nothing and that the military record was largely a story of bungling; the apparent victory was constructed after the fact from the news of New Orleans.[^ch14-hickey-pbs][^ch14-battlefields-hartford]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions say that a government pledged to restraint bought an empire, fought wars it was unprepared for, and ended with a more capable and more expansive state than it began. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping, not as teams.[^ch14-check-sources][^ch14-check-table]

## Legacies

The era left the republic larger and more capable than it found it. The Louisiana Purchase opened the West and with it the question of slavery’s extension, which returned in the Missouri crisis and would not be settled until the Civil War. *Marbury v. Madison* made judicial review a lasting feature of the government Jefferson had tried to shrink.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-senate-louisiana]

The war’s end broke the Native confederacy’s power and ended the possibility of a European ally for Indigenous resistance in the eastern woodlands, while the Treaty of Ghent’s clauses on Native rights went unenforced. The dispossession that Ch. 17 calls removal was already underway in the treaties and the war.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh][^ch14-check-slavery]

And the chapter’s central irony endured: a government pledged to economy and to peace expanded through a war it could barely fund, an embargo it could barely enforce, and a purchase whose constitutionality its own author doubted. The “empire of liberty” was built with the fiscal and military instruments the Republicans had spent the 1790s denouncing. Chapter 15 takes up the markets and infrastructure that would knit that empire together.[^ch14-check-ch15][^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-ncc-louisiana]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The Napoleonic wars, the revolution in Haiti, and the Barbary states shaped American policy at every turn. Napoleon sold Louisiana because Haiti had slipped and Britain threatened; Britain’s Orders in Council drove the embargo; Tripoli’s war made the small navy a fighting force.[^ch14-senate-louisiana][^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- **Archives and silences.** Native diplomacy reaches the record mainly through American treaties, instructions, and medals, and the enslaved appear in the expansion’s documents as property—including the Treaty of Ghent’s provision for returning “any Slaves or other private property.” The chapter should read those clauses as evidence of who counted and who did not, and should name the enslaved rescuers behind the Dolley Madison legend.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-dolley][^ch14-check-slavery]
- **Distant connections.** Louisiana opened the slavery question that returned in Missouri (Ch. 16); the confederacy’s defeat prepared removal (Ch. 17); the war’s fiscal and naval legacy fed the Market Revolution (Ch. 15) and later overseas empire (Ch. 27).[^ch14-check-ch15][^ch14-check-slavery]

## Summary

- Jefferson’s program promised a “wise and frugal Government,” cut internal taxes, reduced the army and navy, and paid down the debt.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-millercenter-domestic]
- The conflict with the Federalist judiciary produced *Marbury v. Madison*, which established judicial review even as Jefferson repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801.[^ch14-marbury-na][^ch14-fjc-midnight][^ch14-capitol-repeal]
- The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, gave the parties a constitutional rule for succession.[^ch14-twelfth-amendment]
- The Louisiana Purchase doubled the republic’s claimed territory for $15 million, and Jefferson acquired it without the constitutional amendment he thought necessary.[^ch14-na-louisiana][^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-jefferson-draft-amendment]
- The Lewis and Clark expedition carried American commerce, diplomacy, and claims into Native homelands, and distributed peace medals whose imagery addressed Native leaders as sovereigns.[^ch14-secret-message][^ch14-nps-lecl][^ch14-monticello-medals]
- The Barbary War showed that the frugal republic would still project force overseas to protect commerce.[^ch14-ussconstitution-barbary][^ch14-state-barbary]
- The *Chesapeake*–*Leopard* affair led to an embargo that required seizure, bonding, and armed enforcement, failed to move Britain, and was repealed in 1809.[^ch14-monticello-embargo]
- Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa’s confederacy resisted land sales; Harrison’s attack at Tippecanoe in 1811 destroyed Prophetstown and deepened the western war.[^ch14-battlefields-tippecanoe][^ch14-battlefields-tecumseh]
- The War of 1812 brought the burning of Washington, the defense of Fort McHenry, and the negotiated peace at Ghent, which restored the prewar borders and said nothing about impressment.[^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-senate-ghent]
- Jackson’s victory at New Orleans, fought after the peace was signed, arrived with the treaty and discredited the Federalists, even as the Native confederacy lost its power and its ally.[^ch14-battlefields-neworleans][^ch14-hickey-loa]

> *A closing note.* The era that began with a plainly dressed president walking to the Capitol ended with the capital in ashes and a peace that settled none of the issues that had caused the war. In between, the republic bought an empire, fought a war in the Mediterranean, enforced an embargo with the army and navy it had been reducing, broke an Indigenous confederacy, and doubled its territory. Jefferson had promised “a wise and frugal Government” and warned against entangling alliances; he acquired Louisiana on authority he doubted and met British impressment with a coercive economic policy that failed. The “empire of liberty” was not a departure from republican restraint. It was what restraint became when a republic with continental ambitions met a world at war, and the people who paid most were those the documents counted as property and as obstacles.[^ch14-jefferson-inaugural][^ch14-ncc-louisiana][^ch14-monticello-embargo][^ch14-na-ghent][^ch14-check-slavery]
