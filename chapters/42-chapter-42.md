## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the dates.** The Democratic National Convention met in Chicago from August 26 to August 29, 1968, and nominated Vice President Hubert H. Humphrey. Thousands of demonstrators, organized in part by the National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam and the Youth International Party, protested the war and the party’s nomination process in the parks and streets around the convention hall.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-dnc-primary]
- **The documented provocation and response.** The Chicago Study Team’s report to the National Commission on the Causes and Prevention of Violence, chaired by Daniel Walker and publicly released on December 1, 1968, found that “the Chicago police were the targets of mounting provocation by both word and act,” including “obscene epithets, and of rocks, sticks, bathroom tiles, and even human feces.” It concluded that “the nature of the response was unrestrained and indiscriminate police violence on many occasions, particularly at night,” often “inflicted upon persons who had broken no law, disobeyed no order, made no threat,” including “peaceful demonstrators, onlookers, and large numbers of residents who were simply passing through.”[^ch42-walker-summary]
- **The scale of the inquiry.** The Walker Report was based on the Chicago Study Team’s review of over 20,000 pages of statements from 3,437 eyewitnesses and participants, 180 hours of film, and more than 12,000 still photographs; the report’s summary states that the violence “reached its culmination on Wednesday night,” August 28, 1968.[^ch42-walker-summary]
- **The phrase and the contest.** The report’s finding was widely summarized as the judgment that the events were “a police riot”; the report also noted that Mayor Richard J. Daley bore partial responsibility for the “unrestrained and indiscriminate police violence.” Eight protest leaders were later indicted for conspiring to violate the 1968 Anti-Riot Act in the trial of the “Chicago Eight,” and the convictions were reversed on appeal.[^ch42-dnc-primary]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the setting, the organizers, the dates, the documented provocations, the documented police violence, the report’s scale and conclusions, the nomination, and the later prosecutions. It cannot show what every participant intended, whether the violence was centrally ordered, or what the crowd as a whole felt. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-check-scene][^ch42-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In August 1968, no one in the streets or the hall knew that Humphrey would lose narrowly in November, that Nixon would resign six years later, or that the convention’s violence would become the emblem of the decade. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch42-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A convention opens a question.** The scene’s claim is not that the police “caused” the 1968 election or the realignment, but that a party that had carried an unpopular war met a movement it could not absorb, and that the resulting image of disorder shaped the politics of order.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-electoral-1968]
- **The stakes.** The conflict between a politics of liberation and a politics of order is the chapter’s central tension: the same years produced the rights and liberation movements of §4 and the law-and-order coalition of §1, and the chapter must hold both together.[^ch42-check-scope][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did demands for liberation coexist with a politics of order?[^ch42-check-scope]
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from Chicago back to the 1968 race, forward to the Nixon domestic program, the end of the war, the liberation movements, and Watergate.[^ch42-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented dialogue or interior thought.** The organizers, the dates, the report’s findings, and the prosecutions are documented. The scene may not stage a fabricated exchange between demonstrators, an unrecorded remark by Daley, or a private thought attributed to a protester.[^ch42-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the 1969 Stonewall uprising, the 1970 Kent State shootings, the 1972 election, or Nixon’s resignation, and it must not present the Walker Report’s December findings as knowledge available in August.[^ch42-check-scene][^ch42-check-dates-appD]
- **No single-hero or single-villain framing.** The scene must not reduce the week to Daley alone or treat the demonstrators as a single undifferentiated body; the report itself distinguishes provocation from response and notes that both occurred.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-check-silences]
- **No ventriloquizing the crowd.** The record over-represents police, officials, journalists, and the trial defendants; where the voices of ordinary protesters, residents, and bystanders do not survive, the chapter should say so rather than inventing them.[^ch42-check-silences]

### Drafting target

Three hundred to four hundred words. Open on the week of August 26–29, 1968, with the documented facts — the convention, the organizers, the provocations, the police response, and the Walker Report — and quote sparingly. Mark the limits of what the record can show about motive and about causation, and end with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows and supplies the years, scope, and limits.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-check-scene]

## 1. Chicago and the 1968 election (1968)

### 1.1 The claim: a politics of order meets a politics of liberation

- **The claim.** The election of 1968 was not a single verdict on the war or on the movements but a narrow, three-way contest in which Richard M. Nixon assembled a coalition out of the politics of order and the resentments of the late 1960s; the result ended the Democratic coalition’s dominance without producing a stable new majority, and the meaning of the “realignment” is still disputed.[^ch42-electoral-1968][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-perlstein-nixonland]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Chicago convention in August 1968 through the election of November 5, 1968, the Wallace vote, and the first year of the Nixon presidency, including the November 1969 “silent majority” address and the October and November 1969 Moratorium demonstrations.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

### 1.2 Convention and conflict

- **The convention.** The Democratic Party nominated Hubert H. Humphrey in Chicago in late August 1968 amid protests and police violence documented by the Walker Report.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-dnc-primary]
- **The evidentiary caution.** The report documents both provocation and an “unrestrained and indiscriminate” police response; the chapter should present the week as a conflict with responsibility on more than one side and avoid the temptation to let the report’s phrase “police riot” stand in for a full account.[^ch42-walker-summary]

### 1.3 The election of 1968: three coalitions: Map 33

- **The result.** Nixon won 301 electoral votes to Humphrey’s 191; George C. Wallace of the American Independent Party won 46. In the popular vote Nixon received 31,785,480 votes (43.4 percent), Humphrey 31,275,166 (42.7 percent), and Wallace 9,906,473 (13.5 percent), out of 73,211,875 total votes cast; the popular margin between Nixon and Humphrey was about half a million votes, or seven-tenths of a percentage point.[^ch42-electoral-1968][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular]
- **The map.** Map 33, “Elections of 1968 and 1972,” is the registered visual for this section. It should show the 1968 three-way race and the 1972 landslide side by side, so that the reader can compare the coalitions rather than assume that one produced the other.[^ch42-check-map33]
- **The map’s limits.** The map cannot prove that the voters who chose Wallace in 1968 became Nixon voters in 1972, nor that any single group’s motives explain the national result; the README specifically warns that Map 33 cannot prove that all Wallace voters became Nixon voters. Elections are aggregates of individual choices taken for many reasons, and the chapter should present the map as a description of outcomes, not as evidence of motive.[^ch42-check-map33][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **The data source.** The presidential-election returns are the province of Appendix B, Table 2; the chapter cites those returns and states the units, the totals, and the fact that popular votes and electoral votes are different measures.[^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-check-map33]

### 1.4 Law and order, the “silent majority,” and the Moratorium

- **The appeal.** In a televised address on November 3, 1969, Nixon asked for the support of “the great silent majority of my fellow Americans,” presenting the war and the protests as matters of order and patience.[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]
- **The movements.** The Moratorium demonstrations of October 15, 1969, and the Mobilization march of November 15, 1969, brought hundreds of thousands into the streets; the chapter should present the “silent majority” and the mass movements as claims about the same nation, each advanced by people who believed they spoke for its center.[^ch42-miller-vietnamization][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The chapter should not treat the election returns or the “silent majority” language as proof of what most Americans thought; the polls and returns each measure something narrower, and the terms are the period’s own.[^ch42-check-numbers][^ch42-check-names]

## 2. New Federalism, regulation, and the vote (1969–1972)

### 2.1 The claim: a conservative president expanded the state

- **The claim.** Nixon governed as a conservative in rhetoric and an expansionist in administration: he proposed to shift power to states and localities through revenue sharing while signing a burst of environmental, safety, and welfare legislation, and the contradiction is central to the debate over liberal-state continuity.[^ch42-dol-nixonford][^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-hoff-nixon]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the New Federalism and welfare proposals of 1969–71 through the environmental legislation of 1970–73, the creation of OSHA, the 26th Amendment of 1971, and the busing conflict of 1971–72.[^ch42-dol-nixonford][^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-26th-nara]

### 2.2 New Federalism and the welfare state

- **The program.** The Nixon and Ford administrations pursued a “new working partnership between the Federal Government and the States and localities,” termed New Federalism, whose unifying theme was revenue sharing and whose instruments were block grants rather than categorical programs, on the principle that “decisions are better made when they are made by those directly concerned.”[^ch42-dol-nixonford]
- **The welfare proposals.** Nixon proposed a Family Assistance Plan built around a negative income tax, and Congress rejected it; part of his welfare reform did pass, in the form of Supplemental Security Income, providing a guaranteed income for elderly and disabled citizens, while Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid benefits increased. The chapter should present the proposal and the outcome separately, and note the evidence that Nixon’s support for the Family Assistance Plan was partly tactical.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-nara-homefront]

### 2.3 The environmental and labor-regulatory burst

- **The legislation.** Nixon signed the National Environmental Policy Act, proposed and then established the Environmental Protection Agency, signed the Clean Air Act of 1970, and signed the Endangered Species Act and the Marine Mammal Protection Act; the Miller Center notes that “probably more new regulation was imposed on the economy … than in any other presidency since the New Deal,” and that Nixon also impounded funds Congress had authorized for clean-air enforcement.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-nixonfoundation-environment][^ch42-nara-homefront]
- **The Clean Air Act date.** The Environmental Protection Agency dates the signing to December 31, 1970, while a Nixon Library page dates it to December 30; the discrepancy must be resolved against the *Statutes at Large* or the *Public Papers* before a date is asserted.[^ch42-epa-caa]
- **The labor side.** Nixon signed into law in 1970 the bill creating the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, extending federal regulation into the workplace.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-dol-nixonford]
- **The context.** The burst responded to a mass environmental and consumer politics the chapter names but does not re-narrate; the environmental movement’s first Earth Day was held in April 1970. The exact date and scale of Earth Day should be sourced before use.[^ch42-nixonlibrary-earthday][^ch42-check-numbers]

### 2.4 The 26th Amendment and the changing electorate

- **The amendment.** The 26th Amendment, lowering the voting age to 18, was passed by Congress on March 23, 1971, and ratified on July 1, 1971, after the Supreme Court held in *Oregon v. Mitchell* that Congress could lower the voting age for federal but not state elections. It was the fastest ratification of any constitutional amendment.[^ch42-26th-nara][^ch42-26th-reagan]
- **The consequence.** The amendment tied the chapter’s threads together: the draft that sent 18-year-olds to fight had made “old enough to fight, old enough to vote” a national demand, and the new voters entered the electorate in time for the 1972 election.[^ch42-26th-reagan][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

### 2.5 The Southern strategy and the busing fight

- **The strategy.** The chapter should examine how the administration’s electoral strategy and its opposition to court-ordered busing shaped the coalition Nixon assembled, without treating the strategy as the sole cause of the realignment.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The busing conflict and the “Southern strategy” are contested terms and contested causal claims; the chapter should present them at their strongest and test them against the returns rather than the rhetoric, and it must not attribute to all White voters a single motive.[^ch42-check-map33][^ch42-check-debate-works]

## 3. Vietnamization, Cambodia, and the opening to China (1969–1973)

### 3.1 The claim: a war ended on terms the settlement could not hold

- **The claim.** Nixon and Henry Kissinger ended direct American combat in Vietnam by a combination of troop withdrawals, expanded bombing, an opening to China, and détente with the Soviet Union, and they signed a settlement in January 1973 that neither Vietnamese party honored; the war’s ending became a source of the era’s distrust of government.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-state-salt][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the “Menu” bombings and first withdrawals of 1969 through the Cambodian incursion and Kent State in 1970, the opening to China and the SALT I agreements in 1972, the Easter Offensive and the Christmas bombing, and the Paris settlement of January 1973.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-kent-state][^ch42-state-salt]

### 3.2 Vietnamization and the widening war

- **The policy.** Nixon began withdrawing forces in June 1969 while building South Vietnam’s capacity in a program Secretary of Defense Melvin Laird called “Vietnamization”; the administration simultaneously expanded the air war, including secret B-52 “Menu” bombings of North Vietnamese base camps in Cambodia.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]
- **The speech.** In the November 3, 1969, address Nixon said, “In the previous administration, we Americanized the war in Vietnam. In this administration, we are Vietnamizing the search for peace,” and asked the “great silent majority” for support.[^ch42-nixon-silent-quote][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]

### 3.3 Cambodia, Kent State, and the campus strike

- **The incursion.** After the fall of Prince Norodom Sihanouk in March 1970, Nixon ordered a United States–South Vietnamese “incursion” into Cambodia, limited to a 30-kilometer strip along the border and to the end of June; the State Department’s account dates the order to April 30, while a Miller Center account dates the authorization to April 28, a discrepancy to reconcile.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-miller-vietnamization]
- **The shootings.** On May 4, 1970, members of the Ohio National Guard fired into a crowd of demonstrators at Kent State University, killing four and wounding nine; 28 of more than 70 Guardsmen fired, discharging between 61 and 67 shots in about 13 seconds. The Scranton Commission concluded that “the indiscriminate firing of rifles into a crowd of students and the deaths that followed were unnecessary, unwarranted, and inexcusable.”[^ch42-kent-state]
- **The aftermath.** The shootings triggered a nationwide student strike that closed hundreds of campuses, and ten days later, on May 14–15, 1970, state police killed two students at Jackson State College in Mississippi; the chapter should connect the two without conflating them.[^ch42-kent-state][^ch42-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 China, Moscow, and SALT I

- **The opening.** Kissinger’s secret trip to Beijing in July 1971 was followed by Nixon’s visit to the People’s Republic of China in February 1972, the first by an American president, and by a summit in Moscow in May 1972; the strategy was to “play China against the Soviet Union, the Soviet Union against China, and both against North Vietnam.”[^ch42-miller-china][^ch42-state-ending-vietnam]
- **SALT I.** After formal talks opened in Helsinki on November 17, 1969, Nixon and Soviet General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev signed the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty and the interim SALT agreement in Moscow on May 26, 1972; the ABM Treaty limited strategic missile defenses to 200 interceptors each and two sites. It was the first Cold War agreement to limit nuclear arsenals.[^ch42-state-salt]

### 3.5 Paris and after

- **The offensive and the bombing.** The North Vietnamese Easter Offensive opened in late March 1972; Nixon mined North Vietnam’s harbors on May 4 and began sustained air operations (Linebacker). After talks stalled, he ordered the December “Christmas bombing” of the North Vietnamese heartland.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam]
- **The settlement.** The United States and North Vietnam initialed an agreement on January 23, 1973, and signed it on January 27; President Nguyen Van Thieu accepted it reluctantly after American pressure. Neither Vietnamese party abided by the settlement, and the war continued until the North Vietnamese offensive of 1975 and the fall of Saigon on April 30, 1975 — events that belong to the chapter’s retrospective frame and to later chapters, with their dates labeled.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-check-dates-appD]
- **The Pentagon Papers and the War Powers Resolution.** The Supreme Court’s decision in *New York Times Co. v. United States*, 403 U.S. 713 (1971), refused to enjoin publication of the classified Pentagon Papers, holding that the government had not met the heavy burden required for a prior restraint; Congress later passed the War Powers Resolution of 1973 over Nixon’s veto, requiring the president to report and to terminate hostilities absent congressional authorization. The chapter names these as the legal and constitutional accompaniments of the war’s ending, cross-referencing Chapter 43, and confirms the resolution’s enactment date before asserting it.[^ch42-nyt-v-us][^ch42-warpowers][^ch42-check-dates-appD]

## 4. Liberation movements and rights litigation (1969–1973)

### 4.1 The claim: rights expanded while the coalition that would define them narrowed

- **The claim.** The same years that produced the politics of order produced a widening of rights — for women, gay people, Native nations, and Chicanos — through organizing, litigation, and legislation; these movements did not fit neatly into either party, and their gains and the backlash they provoked are two parts of one story.[^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-justia-roe][^ch42-skrentny-rights]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Women’s Strike for Equality and Stonewall through Title IX and the ERA, the Alcatraz occupation and Wounded Knee, the Chicano Moratorium, and the Supreme Court decisions in *Furman* and *Roe*.[^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-usmarshals-wounded-knee][^ch42-loc-chicano][^ch42-justia-furman]

### 4.2 Women’s liberation, Title IX, and the ERA

- **The strike.** On August 26, 1970, the fiftieth anniversary of the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment, the National Organization for Women organized a nationwide Women’s Strike for Equality; the largest event was a march down Fifth Avenue in New York City that witnesses estimated at more than 50,000 women, after which NOW’s membership increased by about half within a few months.[^ch42-nyhistory-strike]
- **Title IX.** Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, signed June 23, 1972, provided that “no person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Its later application to athletics and its enforcement are matters for later chapters.[^ch42-justice-titleix][^ch42-check-ch43]
- **The ERA.** Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment in March 1972 and sent it to the states; the ratification struggle belongs to Chapter 43. The chapter should name the 1972 passage and state that ratification was not completed within the deadline then set.[^ch42-check-ch43][^ch42-check-dates-appD]

### 4.3 Gay liberation after Stonewall

- **The uprising.** On June 28, 1969, police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York City’s Greenwich Village, and patrons and community members fought back in events that stretched over six days; veterans of the events prefer “uprising” or “rebellion” to “riot.” The chapter should note that the events were not the first act of LGBTQ+ resistance but became the symbolic beginning of gay liberation and the annual Pride traditions.[^ch42-loc-stonewall]
- **The caution.** The record of the uprising is largely retrospective and contested; the chapter should attribute statements to the witnesses who made them, date later recollections, and note that the movement’s history includes people of color and gender-nonconforming people whose roles the early account often minimized.[^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-check-silences]

### 4.4 Native and Chicano activism

- **Alcatraz.** On November 20, 1969, Native activists began the occupation of Alcatraz Island, which lasted until June 1971; the occupation asserted Native claims to the island and brought the slogan of reclaimed land and self-determination to national attention.[^ch42-nps-alcatraz][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **Wounded Knee.** On February 27, 1973, followers of the American Indian Movement seized the town of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, in a 71-day occupation that ended on May 8, 1973; two Indians were killed and a United States marshal was seriously wounded.[^ch42-usmarshals-wounded-knee]
- **The Chicano Moratorium.** On August 29, 1970, between 20,000 and 30,000 demonstrators marched through East Los Angeles in the National Chicano Moratorium against the Vietnam War; the march ended in violence after the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department arrived, three people were killed including the journalist Rubén Salazar, and the park where the rally was held was later renamed for him. The chapter should give the organizers, the scale, the violence, and the deaths accurately, and note the organizers’ claim about the disproportionate draft and casualty rates of Mexican Americans.[^ch42-loc-chicano]
- **The framing.** The chapter should connect Native and Chicano activism to the broader rights revolution without merging distinct nations, organizations, and demands into one movement.[^ch42-skrentny-rights][^ch42-check-silences]

### 4.5 Rights litigation: *Furman* and *Roe*

- **Furman.** In *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, decided June 29, 1972, the Supreme Court held, per curiam, that the imposition and carrying out of the death penalty in the cases before it constituted cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments; the decision invalidated the death penalty laws then in force as applied, and it was followed by a wave of new state statutes that the Court addressed in *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976), which belongs to Chapter 43.[^ch42-justia-furman][^ch42-check-roe-furman]
- **Roe.** In *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U.S. 113, decided January 22, 1973, the Court held, in an opinion by Justice Harry A. Blackmun, that the constitutional right of privacy protected a woman’s decision to have an abortion, subject to the state’s interests in maternal health and fetal life as pregnancy advanced.[^ch42-justia-roe]
- **The caution.** The chapter should present the two decisions as legal events with specific holdings and a contested aftermath, not as a single “rights revolution” and not as settled public consensus; the README assigns *Furman* and *Roe* here and *Gregg* to Chapter 43.[^ch42-check-roe-furman][^ch42-check-ch43]

## 5. Watergate, resignation, pardon, and the oil shock (1972–1974)

### 5.1 The claim: the politics of order consumed the president who had promised it

- **The claim.** Watergate began as a burglary and became a constitutional crisis because the president and his aides used the powers of the office to conceal it; the tapes made the concealment provable, the courts and Congress forced disclosure, and Nixon resigned in August 1974, leaving his successor to pardon him and the country to absorb the oil shock that struck the same months.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-smoking-gun][^ch42-proclamation-4311][^ch42-frbsf-oil]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the break-in of June 17, 1972, through the cover-up, the Senate hearings, the Saturday Night Massacre, the release of the tapes, the Supreme Court’s subpoena decision, the impeachment articles, the resignation, the pardon, and the oil embargo of October 1973–March 1974.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-frbsf-oil]

### 5.2 The break-in and the cover-up

- **The crime.** On June 17, 1972, five men were arrested at the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate building; the bugging equipment had been installed on May 28, and the burglars were connected to the White House through E. Howard Hunt and G. Gordon Liddy, who had earlier broken into the office of Daniel Ellsberg’s psychiatrist as part of the “Plumbers” operation.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-domestic]
- **The cover-up.** The administration denied White House involvement and sought to contain the investigation; on June 23, 1972, Nixon and H. R. Haldeman discussed having the CIA tell the FBI to “stay the hell out of this,” a conversation that became the “smoking gun” when it was released in 1974.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-miller-smoking-gun]

### 5.3 The investigations and the tapes

- **The sequence.** The Senate created the Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities on February 7, 1973, and began public hearings on May 17; on July 16, Alexander Butterfield revealed the existence of a White House taping system; Archibald Cox was sworn in as special prosecutor on May 25, 1973.[^ch42-nara-watergate]
- **The Saturday Night Massacre.** On October 20, 1973, after Cox refused a compromise on the tapes, Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire him; Richardson resigned, Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus refused and resigned, and Acting Attorney General Robert Bork fired Cox.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-domestic]
- **The missing minutes.** On November 21, 1973, the Senate committee announced an 18½-minute gap in a tape of a Nixon–Haldeman conversation of June 20, 1972; the gap was never fully explained.[^ch42-nara-watergate]
- **The endgame.** The House authorized an impeachment inquiry on February 6, 1974; the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the special prosecutor’s subpoena for the tapes on July 24, 1974; and the House Judiciary Committee adopted three articles of impeachment on July 27–30, 1974.[^ch42-nara-watergate]

### 5.4 In Focus: the June 23, 1972, Oval Office recording

- **The object.** The In Focus reads one recorded conversation: the June 23, 1972, meeting in the Oval Office between Nixon and Haldeman, identified by the Nixon Library as the “smoking gun” among the Watergate trial tapes and given the tape number 741-002 by the Miller Center.[^ch42-miller-smoking-gun][^ch42-nixon-library-tapes]
- **The close reading.** The box should quote from the transcript (the conversation ran from about 10:04 to 11:39 a.m.) and read the crucial exchange, in which Nixon and Haldeman discuss directing the CIA to ask the FBI to curtail its investigation, invoking “the whole Bay of Pigs thing.” The point is that the recording contradicted the president’s public claims of noninterference and gave the House a documented basis for obstruction of justice.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-miller-smoking-gun][^ch42-pbs-smoking-gun]
- **The distinctions the box must make.** It must distinguish the recording from the Watergate break-in itself, from the later cover-up, and from the report of the conversation; it must note that the transcript was released in August 1974 under court order, and it must not treat one conversation as the whole case.[^ch42-miller-smoking-gun][^ch42-nara-watergate]
- **The rights and register record.** The National Archives holds the tapes and the transcripts. The README lists no image for this chapter, so the box is a textual and audio close reading unless a reproduction or a transcript facsimile is registered with an exact object locator, caption, source line, and rights record. Do not assume that a host’s public-domain statement covers every object it displays.[^ch42-check-focus][^ch42-check-rights]

### 5.5 Resignation, succession, and the pardon

- **The resignation.** On August 8, 1974, Nixon addressed the nation: “Therefore, I shall resign the Presidency effective at noon tomorrow.” He resigned on August 9, 1974, and Gerald R. Ford was sworn in as president.[^ch42-miller-resignation][^ch42-nara-watergate]
- **The pardon.** On September 8, 1974, Ford issued Proclamation 4311, granting “a full, free, and absolute pardon unto Richard Nixon for all offenses against the United States which he, Richard Nixon, has committed or may have committed or taken part in during the period from January 20, 1969 through August 9, 1974.”[^ch42-proclamation-4311]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the resignation and the pardon as a single unraveling of the claim that the executive had acted within the law, and should note that the pardon’s political cost fell on Ford; it must not present the pardon as an established legal conclusion about Nixon’s guilt.[^ch42-proclamation-4311][^ch42-check-debate-works]

### 5.6 The oil shock

- **The embargo.** On October 19, 1973, immediately after Nixon asked Congress for $2.2 billion in emergency aid to Israel during the Yom Kippur War, the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries imposed an oil embargo on the United States, ceased imports from participating states, and began production cuts.[^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-state-oil]
- **The price.** The production cuts nearly quadrupled the price of oil, from $2.90 a barrel before the embargo to $11.65 a barrel in January 1974; the embargo was lifted in March 1974, but the higher prices remained. The Nixon administration announced “Project Independence” on November 7, 1973, and the crisis prompted the creation of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, a national 55-mile-per-hour speed limit, and, later, fuel-economy standards.[^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-state-oil]
- **The overlap with Chapter 43.** The chapter owns the embargo’s onset and its immediate politics; the longer stagflation, deindustrialization, and energy policy of the 1970s belong to Chapter 43. The chapter should state this division rather than repeat Chapter 43’s material.[^ch42-check-ch43][^ch42-check-scope]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Nixon won narrowly in 1968 and overwhelmingly in 1972.** The returns establish a three-way race with a popular margin of about half a million votes in 1968 and a landslide of 520 electoral votes to 17 in 1972.[^ch42-electoral-1968][^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-electoral-1972]
- **The Chicago convention week produced documented police violence.** The Walker Report documents provocation and an “unrestrained and indiscriminate” police response based on an extensive inquiry.[^ch42-walker-summary]
- **The Nixon administration expanded federal regulation and the welfare state even as it preached restraint.** The environmental legislation, OSHA, and the New Federalism revenue-sharing program are documented.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-dol-nixonford]
- **The United States ended direct combat in Vietnam in January 1973.** The Paris settlement was signed on January 27, 1973, after the Christmas bombing and American pressure on Thieu.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam]
- **Watergate was a documented abuse of presidential power.** The break-in, the cover-up, the taping system, the Saturday Night Massacre, the Supreme Court decision, and the impeachment articles are documented, and Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974.[^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-miller-domestic]
- **The oil embargo struck in October 1973 and lifted in March 1974.** The price increase is documented by the Federal Reserve’s history and the State Department’s account.[^ch42-frbsf-oil][^ch42-state-oil]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was 1968 a realignment or a dealignment?** Perlstein and the backlash school argue that Nixon assembled a durable new majority out of the politics of order; accounts centered on party dealignment emphasize that the party system became less stable, turnout fell, and control of institutions see-sawed rather than settling. The chapter should state each at its strongest and test the claims against the returns.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-burnham-dealignment][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **Did Nixon expand the liberal state or begin to dismantle it?** Joan Hoff and others argue that Nixon preserved and even extended the New Deal order; the “end of the New Deal order” school emphasizes that the economic and political foundations of that order were already cracking. The chapter should separate the administrative record from the long-term fiscal and ideological story.[^ch42-hoff-nixon][^ch42-fraser-gerstle]
- **Were the movements’ gains a cause of the backlash or independent of it?** The grassroots-rights school treats the “rights revolution” as a real expansion that reshaped American life; the backlash school treats the same expansion as the trigger for a conservative realignment. The chapter should hold both and avoid a single causal arrow.[^ch42-skrentny-rights][^ch42-edsall-chain]
- **What is the right unit for the politics of the “silent majority”?** Lassiter and Kruse locate the era’s decisive politics in the suburbs and metropolitan growth, where race, taxation, and neighborhood were fused; national accounts locate it in presidential coalitions. The chapter should say which level each explanation is answering.[^ch42-lassiter-silent][^ch42-kruse-whiteflight]
- **How much did Watergate change policy?** One position treats Watergate as the decisive wound that paralyzed the Nixon presidency and shaped the Ford and Carter years; another treats it as a scandal that confirmed existing distrust without redirecting policy on its own. The chapter should present both and distinguish the scandal’s political costs from its policy effects.[^ch42-miller-domestic][^ch42-check-debate-works]
- **What does an election map measure?** Electoral geography describes aggregated outcomes; it cannot establish individual motive, and the README warns that Map 33 cannot prove that all Wallace voters became Nixon voters. The chapter should say what the map can and cannot establish rather than reading a coalition off the returns.[^ch42-check-map33][^ch42-check-numbers]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the politics of liberation and the politics of order grew from the same soil — a war, a rights revolution, and an economic order under strain — and in which the era’s outcome was decided by contingent choices: a narrow election, a set of administrative decisions, an opening to China, a break-in, and an embargo that no one controlled.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-skrentny-rights][^ch42-miller-domestic]
- **Contingency.** The 1968 election, the Cambodian incursion, the China opening, the Watergate cover-up, and the oil embargo were decisions and events that could have gone otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome. The chapter should identify the alternatives that were actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-frbsf-oil]
- **Structure and choice together.** The synthesis should hold structural conditions — the end of the postwar boom, the changing party system, the rights revolution, and the Cold War — together with the contingent choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation over the other.[^ch42-fraser-gerstle][^ch42-check-scope]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that between 1968 and 1974 the United States ended one war and unmade one presidency, widened rights for many Americans while assembling a conservative coalition against the movements that had won them, and discovered that neither the politics of order nor the promise of liberation could by itself hold the center together.[^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nara-watergate]

## Legacies

The years from 1968 to 1974 left a politics of distrust that outlasted the individuals. The Pentagon Papers and Watergate taught Americans to expect their government to conceal what it knew; the resignation of a president and the pardon of his successor attached that lesson to the highest office; and the War Powers Resolution tried to write it into law. The chapter should connect these developments to the present without turning them into a score.[^ch42-nyt-v-us][^ch42-nara-watergate][^ch42-warpowers][^ch42-proclamation-4311]

The era also left a changed party system and a changed map of belonging. The coalitions that contested 1968 and 1972 are the ancestors of the coalitions that contest the present; Map 33, by placing the two elections side by side, lets the reader see how the same electorate produced a narrow three-way result and a landslide in four years, without pretending that the map explains why.[^ch42-electoral-1968-popular][^ch42-electoral-1972][^ch42-check-map33]

And it left the movements that had demanded recognition. Women, gay people, Native nations, and Chicanos won rights and visibility in these years that reshaped American life, and the backlash those gains provoked became a lasting feature of the politics that followed. The chapter should present the gains and the backlash as the same history, and the questions they raised about who belongs to the nation as the question the book keeps asking.[^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-usmarshals-wounded-knee][^ch42-justia-roe]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The era’s politics were made in Beijing, Moscow, Paris, Cairo, and Riyadh as well as Washington; the opening to China, the SALT agreements, the Paris settlement, and the oil embargo all show how international decisions bounded American choices. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate the diplomatic history.[^ch42-state-salt][^ch42-state-ending-vietnam][^ch42-state-oil]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, officials, prosecutors, and journalists and under-represents the poor, the young, people of color, and the organizers who built the movements; the Walker Report itself rests on the statements of eyewitnesses who came forward, and the taping system favored the president’s own voice. The chapter should name these asymmetries rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch42-check-silences][^ch42-walker-summary]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 41 owns the war through 1968; Chapter 43 owns stagflation, the Ford and Carter presidencies, the later ERA struggle, and *Gregg*; Chapter 39 owns the earlier civil rights movement. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch42-check-ch41][^ch42-check-ch43][^ch42-check-roe-furman]

### Rights and liberation

- **Women and gay liberation.** The New-York Historical Society’s account of the Women’s Strike for Equality and the Library of Congress’s guide on Stonewall were read for the strike and the uprising; consult the movement records and the oral histories for the participants’ own words.[^ch42-nyhistory-strike][^ch42-loc-stonewall]

> *A closing note.* Between 1968 and 1974 the United States ended one war and unmade one presidency, widened rights for many of its people while assembling a coalition against the movements that had won them, and discovered that neither the politics of order nor the promise of liberation could hold the center together. The chapter’s question — how did demands for liberation coexist with a politics of order? — is answered by a decade in which both were real, both were contested, and neither could claim the whole nation.[^ch42-walker-summary][^ch42-perlstein-nixonland][^ch42-loc-stonewall][^ch42-nara-watergate]
