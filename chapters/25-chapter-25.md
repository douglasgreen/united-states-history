# Chapter 25. The West: Conquest, Extraction, and the End of the Frontier (1865–1900)

> “I have seen nothing that the white man has … which is as good as our right to roam and live on the open plains as we choose.”[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull]
>
> — Sitting Bull (Tatanka-Iyotanka), Hunkpapa Lakota leader, statement recorded without a date, quoted by the National Park Service.[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull]

> “And now, four centuries from the discovery of America, at the end of a hundred years of life under the Constitution, the frontier has gone, and with its going has closed the first period of American history.”[^ch25-tah-turner]
>
> — Frederick Jackson Turner, “The Significance of the Frontier in American History,” address to the American Historical Association, Chicago, 1893.[^ch25-tah-turner]

> “Deemphasize the frontier and its supposed end, conceive of the West as a place and not a process …”[^ch25-archive-limerick]
>
> — Patricia Nelson Limerick, *The Legacy of Conquest: The Unbroken Past of the American West*, 1987.[^ch25-archive-limerick]

A Lakota leader treated the plains as a right to live upon, not an empty area to be crossed. A historian declared the frontier closed and made its closing the end of a period. A second historian refused both the process and its end, treating the West as a place whose conquest continued. The three disagree about whether the West was a home, a stage of development, or an unfinished conquest. This chapter asks who held title to the West, and how it was enforced.[^ch25-check-epigraphs]

On August 2, 1874, Horatio N. Ross and William T. McKay panned for gold in a valley below Harney Peak in the Black Hills. The two miners had been working the gravel since July 27. Their camp lay in the southern hills, where the expedition would remain for five days. That day George Armstrong Custer, the former Union general commanding the 7th Cavalry, reported a discovery “right from the grass roots.” The *New York Times* printed his account on August 22: gold was “distributed throughout an extensive area within the Black Hills.” Reporters with the party sent the news across the country.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline]

The land was not empty and it was not unclaimed. The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 had set the Great Sioux Reservation apart for “the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation” of the Lakota and their allies, and the Black Hills lay inside its boundary. The treaty forbade unauthorized persons to “pass over, settle upon, or reside in” the reservation. The expedition’s scientific corps could be described as official business. Its four reporters and two civilian miners could not.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]

Doubt traveled with the report. Newton H. Winchell, the Minnesota state geologist, later said he had not seen gold himself and doubted its quantity. Lieutenant Colonel Frederick Grant, the expedition’s second in command, wrote that the miners “showed the same pieces every day,” worth about two dollars, and concluded, “I don’t believe that any gold was found at all.” No member of the expedition left to prospect. The rich deposits that would make Deadwood and Lead lay in the northern hills, which the expedition never visited. The Lakota called the wagon track the “Thieves Road.”[^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]

The report did not need to be true to work. By 1875 some 15,000 miners had come to the Dakota Territory, and some entered the Hills in violation of the treaty. On November 3, 1875, President Ulysses S. Grant and his cabinet decided that the Army would no longer stop them. In December the Commissioner of Indian Affairs ordered the Lakota living outside the reservation to return by January 31, 1876, and declared those who refused hostile. The deadline passed, and the Great Sioux War began. Custer himself had warned, in a letter to the *New York Times*, that the expedition was “goading the Indians to madness by invading their hallowed grounds.”[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-kirkby-black-hills][^ch25-check-scene]

Who held title to the West, and how was it enforced?

This chapter covers the thirty-five years from the end of the Civil War to 1900. It asks how treaties, federal law, corporate capital, and organized violence converted Indigenous homelands into reservations, railroad grants, mines, ranches, farms, and a national myth. It draws on treaties and statutes, the reports of the Commissioner of Indian Affairs and the Army, census and land-office records, the records and photographs of the boarding schools, and the accounts of miners and railroad workers. Where the record preserves them, it also draws on the testimony and memoirs of Indigenous people. It treats “frontier,” “settler,” “Indian,” “savage,” “civilization,” “conquest,” and “manifest destiny” as contested period terms that require definition, not as neutral descriptions. “Frontier” here means the margin of settlement as the Census defined it, not the edge of the world. “Settler” means a migrant who took up land under federal law, not a moral category. The archive over-represents the conquerors and under-represents the people they dispossessed, and the chapter says so where the imbalance bears on the argument.[^ch25-check-scope][^ch25-check-names][^ch25-check-race]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the treaty system and the railroads. §2 follows mining, cattle, irrigation, and the Mexican and Chinese labor that built the extractive economy. §3 examines the Plains and far-western wars. §4 turns to the boarding schools and the Dawes Act. §5 asks what the destruction of the bison and the settlement of the land made of the frontier story. §6 asks how historians have explained the West. The chapter begins in the Black Hills in the summer of 1874, when a treaty was tested by a gold report.

## 1. Treaties and railroads

The West was not simply taken. It was surveyed, negotiated, reserved, granted, and then taken again. The treaty system supplied the legal grammar for the first half of that sequence, and the railroads supplied the machinery for the second.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie]

### 1.1 The treaty system and the Great Plains

The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 was the second of the two great Laramie treaties. The first, in 1851, had recognized territories beyond the later reservation; the second set the reservation that mattered in the Black Hills. The treaty’s “Sioux Nation” was a collective name for the Sioux bands and their allies, not a single government. It opened with a pledge: “From this day forward all war between the parties to this agreement shall for ever cease.” It then described the Great Sioux Reservation: the Missouri River on the east, the northern line of Nebraska on the south, the 46th parallel on the north, and the 104th meridian on the west. The Black Hills lay inside that boundary. The United States promised that no unauthorized person would “ever be permitted to pass over, settle upon, or reside in” the reservation.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-sioux-nation]

Two further articles made the treaty a document the United States would later break openly. Article XI reserved the country north of the North Platte River and east of the summits of the Big Horn Mountains as unceded Indian territory, where no White person could settle or pass without consent. Article XII provided that no cession of reservation land held in common would have “any validity or force as against the said Indians” unless it was “executed and signed by at least three-fourths of all the adult male Indians occupying or interested in the same.” The treaty also promised schools and teachers and bound the signatories to send their children to school. It was a document about land, labor, education, and sovereignty at once.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-sioux-nation]

Red Cloud signed at Fort Laramie on November 6, 1868, months after the treaty had been concluded. Sitting Bull refused to sign, and he and his followers felt no obligation to its terms. The treaty system recognized Indigenous title and reduced it in the same instrument. It gave the United States a legal vocabulary for both keeping and extinguishing a claim. That is why the later taking of the Black Hills was not a simple matter of conquest: the United States had promised to obtain consent, and it did not.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-nps-sitting-bull][^ch25-sioux-nation]

The treaty was also a bargain about the future. The Lakota agreed to withdraw opposition to railroads that did not cross the reservation, and to permit roads and military posts that did not violate earlier treaties. In exchange, the United States promised rations, schools, and the services of farmers and blacksmiths. The document assumed that the Lakota would become farmers and that the Plains would fill with lines of steel. It did not assume that the Lakota would disappear.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie]

### 1.2 The railroads and the land grants

The railroad was the second instrument of title. The Pacific Railroad Acts of 1862 and 1864 chartered the Union Pacific and authorized federal land grants and bonds for the line; the Central Pacific was chartered in 1862 to build the western portion. The two lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869. The route crossed the Plains on corridors that the treaties had already begun to divide.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]

The grants were public land, but the land was not unowned. Railroad companies received alternating sections along the right of way, and the federal government retained the rest. The result was a checkerboard of private and public claims laid over Indigenous homelands. The line carried the mines, ranches, and farms of the interior to eastern and world markets. It also carried soldiers, federal agents, and settlers into the reservation country. The railroad did not merely pass through the West; it reorganized the terms on which the West could be owned.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-map]

Chapter 24 follows the corporation, the trust, and the national market. This chapter treats the railroad as an instrument of conquest: its grants carved up the public domain, its corridors divided hunt and range, and its schedule made the reservation system administrable. The distinction is a division of labor between chapters, not a claim that the railroad was only one thing.[^ch25-check-ch24]

### 1.3 Chinese labor on the iron road

Chinese immigrants built much of the western line. The Central Pacific hired its first 50 Chinese workers in 1864 and, after they disproved the managers’ assumptions about their capacity, hired thousands more. By the end of construction about 10,000 Chinese laborers had worked on the railroad. They were paid on average about 30 percent less than White workers, were segregated in camps, and had to pay for their own lodging, food, supplies, and equipment.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]

On June 24, 1867, all Chinese railroad workers from Cisco to Truckee, California, stopped work. It was, at the time, the largest organized labor strike in the United States. The workers demanded higher wages, a ten-hour day, and shorter shifts in the tunnels. The company cut off food and supplies and broke the strike within a week. No accurate count of the dead exists, but estimates exceed 1,000; rockslides, explosions, exposure, violence, and avalanches killed workers in the Sierra Nevada.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]

Some historians call the Chinese workers “Silent Spikes,” because none of their correspondence or diaries are known to survive and the company’s records systemically anonymized them. The silence is part of the evidence. It does not mean that the workers left no trace; it means that the archive preserved the company’s account and not theirs. The workers who built the line belonged to a people whom the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 would later bar from entering the country, a policy that Chapter 24 treats as national labor and immigration law.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-ch24]

Map 20 plots the competing land regimes of the northern and central West: reservation boundaries as established by treaty and executive order, railroad lines and land-grant corridors, and the cattle range as of the 1880s. The map is a test of the chapter’s argument. The same acre appears on it as Lakota treaty territory, as a federal reservation, as a railroad grant, and as open range. The map must also show what it cannot settle. A boundary drawn by treaty is not the same as a boundary enforced on the ground, and a land grant is not the same as a title confirmed by a court. Its caption and source line must name the treaties, agency reports, and railroad maps behind it.[^ch25-check-map]

## 2. Mining, cattle, irrigation, and labor

Treaties supplied the legal grammar; capital and labor did the extracting. The mining camp, the cattle range, and the irrigated field each turned a legal claim into a commodity, and each depended on workers whose names the record often omits.[^ch25-shpo-comstock][^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican]

### 2.1 Mining and the mining law

Silver was discovered near Virginia City, Nevada, in 1859. The Comstock Lode, a deposit about two and a half miles long, made Virginia City a boomtown for two decades, helped bring Nevada into the Union in 1864, and helped finance San Francisco. The production totals are reported inconsistently, and this account gives the boom’s dimensions and dates rather than a single figure.[^ch25-shpo-comstock]

The General Mining Act of 1872 governed hardrock claims on federal land. It allowed United States citizens to explore for, discover, and purchase valuable mineral deposits on federal land open to mineral entry, and it set five elements: discovery, location, recordation, annual maintenance, and patent. It remained the basic mining statute long after 1900.[^ch25-blm-mining-law]

The law made a private property right out of a public mineral deposit. It asked the claimant to discover a valuable deposit, mark the claim, record it, and keep up the annual work or fee. Its five elements did not include a royalty on what the claim produced. That absence would shape western politics for more than a century.[^ch25-blm-mining-law]

The rushes of the 1860s were not the industrial mining of the 1880s and 1890s. Early placer mining gave way to underground shafts, stamp mills, timbering, pumps, and capital-intensive companies. Mexican and Chinese miners worked the claims, and the mines occupied Indigenous homelands. The chapter names them because the sources often do not.[^ch25-shpo-comstock][^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican][^ch25-check-race]

### 2.2 The cattle range and its collapse

The open-range cattle boom was a speculative enterprise that destroyed itself against weather and barbed wire. By the early 1880s the ranges of the southern Plains were overstocked and the grasses depleted. Ranchers built barbed-wire drift fences to keep cattle from drifting south in winter; by 1885 fences stretched across the northern Texas Panhandle.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]

Beginning in late December 1885, a series of blizzards struck the southern Plains. Cattle drifting south were stalled against the drift fences and froze or smothered in bunches some 400 yards across. When the storms ended in January 1886, thousands of dead cattle were piled against the fences, and entire herds were almost wiped out. The open range never fully recovered.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]

The Big Die-Up was not simply a natural disaster. Overstocking, speculation, and fencing were human decisions that made the herds vulnerable. The weather was the occasion; the organization of the industry turned it into a catastrophe. The same pattern would recur in the dry years that followed: a market built on a fragile environment, and a failure that looked like weather but was also a business model.[^ch25-tsha-big-die-up]

### 2.3 Aridity and the irrigation question

Much of the West could not be farmed like the East. In 1878 John Wesley Powell, the explorer and geologist who had run the Colorado River, published his *Report on the Lands of the Arid Region of the United States*. He argued that beyond the 100th meridian rainfall averages less than 20 inches a year, too little for most crops, and that the 160-acre homestead was badly adapted to aridity. He recommended smaller farms organized around communal irrigation and, more broadly, a pastoral economy of sheep and cattle ranches. He predicted repeated, disastrous droughts.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-loc-powell]

Federal policy proceeded largely without his advice. The Homestead Act kept offering quarter-sections, and settlers kept taking them. Irrigation development came later, through the states and the Reclamation Act of 1902. The act belongs to the chapter’s consequences, not to its pre-1900 narrative, but it confirms Powell’s point: the West would be settled on terms that required collective investment in water.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-check-dates]

Aridity is the environmental limit that every later section must respect. Settlement, mining, and ranching all depended on water the region did not reliably supply. The West could be conquered, but it could not be made to rain.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-worster-rivers]

### 2.4 Mexican and Chinese labor

The mining, railroad, and agricultural economy of the Southwest and the Pacific coast depended on Mexican and Chinese labor. Mexican-born workers filled commercial agriculture, mining, smelting, and railroad jobs. They lived in segregated rural *colonias* and urban *barrios*, and they were paid less than workers of other groups similarly employed. A 1912 survey reported Mexican section hands on the Santa Fe earning $1.00 to $1.25 a day. The Mexican-born population of the border states rose from 66,312 in 1880 to 99,969 in 1900.[^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican]

Chinese immigrants worked in the gold mines, in agriculture, and in the garment industry as well as on the railroads, and they faced organized hostility as their numbers grew. The chapter connects Chinese labor in the West to the exclusion policy that Chapter 24 treats as a national labor and immigration law. The connection is not an aside: the same economy that recruited Chinese workers organized to exclude them.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-check-ch24]

The 1912 survey is a reform document that describes discrimination while reproducing its own racial assumptions. It is evidence of a period argument as well as of wages and population. Read that way, its numbers are usable, its framing is purposeful, and the voices of the workers it describes are mostly missing. The chapter does not invent them.[^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican][^ch25-check-race]

## 3. Plains and other western wars

The treaty system did not prevent war. It defined what the wars were about. Each campaign began with a claim about land, a demand about movement, or a refusal to accept the reservation as the end of a nation’s jurisdiction.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-nps-bozeman]

### 3.1 Sand Creek and the terms of war

On the morning of November 29, 1864, about 750 Cheyenne and Arapaho people were encamped along Big Sandy Creek some 40 miles north of Fort Lyon. The leaders included Black Kettle and White Antelope. The camp had been directed there by the Army while peace negotiations continued, and its people were waiting to move to Fort Lyon for supplies and safety. A Colorado volunteer force under Colonel John M. Chivington attacked it. The National Park Service puts the dead at around 230, mostly women, children, and the elderly; other accounts give 150 or more.[^ch25-nps-sand-creek]

Sand Creek lies outside this chapter’s 1865–1900 frame, but it set the terms of the wars that followed. A peaceful camp under federal protection had been attacked by volunteer troops, and the survivors carried the memory into the reservation era. The massacre also showed that the treaty system’s promises of protection depended on the willingness of local officials and volunteer soldiers to honor them. That willingness was never reliable.[^ch25-nps-sand-creek][^ch25-check-dates]

### 3.2 Red Cloud’s War and the 1868 settlement

The Bozeman Trail cut through the Powder River country, the hunting ground that the Lakota and their allies claimed and used. In 1866 the Army built three forts along the trail: Reno, Phil Kearny, and C. F. Smith. The Oglala Lakota leader Red Cloud and his allies attacked soldiers, civilians, and supply trains. On December 21, 1866, a detachment of 79 soldiers and two civilians under Captain William Fetterman was lured from Fort Phil Kearny and destroyed. The Hayfield Fight followed on August 1, 1867, and the Wagon Box Fight soon after.[^ch25-nps-bozeman]

The United States abandoned the forts and signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. Red Cloud signed at Fort Laramie on November 6, 1868. The National Park Service calls the outcome the first war the United States had lost. The sequence — trail, forts, war, treaty, reservation — is the chapter’s basic grammar. It shows that Indigenous nations could force the United States to negotiate, and that the negotiation could produce a reservation rather than a victory.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-nps-bozeman]

Sitting Bull refused the treaty and felt no obligation to it. He and his followers remained outside the reservation, hunted in the unceded territory, and treated the treaty chiefs as representatives of themselves alone. The division between treaty and non-treaty Lakota was not a division between peace and war. It was a division over whether the reservation could be a base for a continuing Lakota life.[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull][^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie]

### 3.3 The Great Sioux War and the Little Bighorn

The federal decision to stop enforcing the 1868 treaty in the Black Hills produced the Great Sioux War. After the November 3, 1875, cabinet decision and the December order to return by January 31, 1876, the deadline passed and the war began. On June 25, 1876, Custer and the 7th Cavalry were overwhelmingly defeated at the Battle of the Little Bighorn by Lakota and Cheyenne forces led by Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse. The village was large, the victory was real, and the news shocked the country.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-nps-sitting-bull]

The victory did not change the political outcome. Sitting Bull fled to Canada in 1877; Crazy Horse surrendered at Fort Robinson in Nebraska the same year. Sitting Bull surrendered at Fort Buford in 1881. In 1877 Congress passed the act that implemented the Black Hills “agreement,” abrogated the Fort Laramie Treaty, and legitimized the settlers’ invasion of the Hills. The Lakota had won the battle and lost the war.[^ch25-nps-sitting-bull][^ch25-sioux-nation]

The distinction matters for the chapter’s argument. A military history that stops at the Little Bighorn makes the battle the end of the story. A political history asks what happened to the treaty, the land, and the people after the troops rode away. The answer is the 1877 Act, the reservation, the ration, and the school.[^ch25-sioux-nation][^ch25-check-dates]

### 3.4 The Nez Perce and Apache wars

Beyond the northern Plains, the wars of the 1870s and 1880s were fought over reservation boundaries and forced removal. The Treaty of 1855 designated a Nez Perce reservation; the Treaty of 1863 reduced it by 90 percent, and even those who refused to sign were evicted. In 1877 the non-treaty Nez Perce, led by Chief Joseph, made a 126-day, 1,170-mile flight across four states. United States troops surprised a camp at the Big Hole on August 9, 1877, killing 60 to 90 Nez Perce men, women, and children. The final battle came at Bear Paw, 40 miles south of the Canadian border, from September 30 to October 5, 1877. The people were separated afterward; some were sent to Indian Territory.[^ch25-nps-nez-perce]

In the Southwest, Geronimo’s final surrender came in Skeleton Canyon, Arizona, in the summer of 1886, after the Army had committed thousands of troops to the pursuit over years. The Chiricahua Apache were held as prisoners of war rather than tried, taken to Fort Bowie and then by train to Florida, and kept apart from their families for years. The surrender ended a military campaign, not Apache life.[^ch25-nps-chiricahua]

The two wars had different geographies and different enemies, but the same logic. A reservation boundary drawn in Washington could be reduced or ignored in the West; a people who refused the new boundary could be pursued across a continent; and a surrender could be followed by imprisonment without trial. The wars were not episodes of “savagery” met by “civilization.” They were conflicts over jurisdiction, and the United States usually wrote the jurisdiction it wanted.[^ch25-nps-nez-perce][^ch25-nps-chiricahua][^ch25-check-names]

### 3.5 Wounded Knee, 1890

By 1890 the reservations occupied poor land with little game, the bison herds were destroyed, and the Ghost Dance — a revival blending Indigenous and millenarian teachings — spread across the Plains. Officials read the dance as insurrection. On November 13, 1890, President Benjamin Harrison ordered the Army into the Sioux reservations, and one-third of the Army eventually deployed.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

On December 15, Lakota police sent to arrest Sitting Bull shot and killed him. On December 28 a band of Ghost Dancers who had fled the Cheyenne River Reservation surrendered to Colonel James Forsyth’s 7th Cavalry at Wounded Knee Creek. The next morning, while troops searched for weapons, a shot was fired and the soldiers opened fire with rifles and four Hotchkiss guns on the mostly unarmed camp. Thirty troopers died. The burial party counted the bodies of 84 men, 44 women, and 18 children, and more died whose bodies were never counted.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

This was a massacre, and the chapter calls it one. The historian Louis S. Warren argues that Wounded Knee suppressed not a “naive” uprising but pragmatic people seeking a way forward under industrial capitalism and a growing federal bureaucracy. That interpretation frames the killing as policy, not accident: a people who had been confined, starved, and told that their religion was a threat were then shot while soldiers searched their camp.[^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

## 4. Boarding schools and the Dawes Act

The wars were one instrument of conquest. The schools and the allotment statute were another, aimed at the land and the children at once. If the reservation separated a nation from its territory, the school separated a child from the language, kinship, and memory that made the nation a nation.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-archives-dawes]

### 4.1 Carlisle and the boarding-school policy

The Carlisle Indian Industrial School opened in 1879 in Carlisle, Pennsylvania. It was the first federally run, non-reservation boarding school for Indigenous children, and it operated until 1918. Its founder and first superintendent, Captain Richard Henry Pratt, popularized the phrase “Kill the Indian in him, and save the man” in a speech delivered in 1892 to the National Conference of Charities and Correction in Denver.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-dickinson-pratt]

The phrase is a period slogan, quoted here because it states the policy’s aim without euphemism. The schools separated children from their families and homelands, taught in English, and framed citizenship as the reward for cultural surrender. This was a deliberate federal policy, not the work of one man. Pratt’s phrase described a method; the method outlived him.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-dickinson-pratt]

### 4.2 The Dawes Act of 1887

The General Allotment Act, approved on February 8, 1887, broke up reservation land held in common into individual plots. It authorized the President to allot a quarter-section to each head of a family, an eighth-section to each single person over 18 and to each orphan child under 18, and a sixteenth-section to each other single person under 18. It held the allotted land in trust for 25 years, after which the United States would convey it in fee. It declared allottees citizens of the United States. It separately declared citizens any Indigenous person who voluntarily took up residence apart from any tribe and adopted “the habits of civilized life.”[^ch25-archives-dawes]

The second category is the tell. Citizenship was offered as a reward for leaving the tribe and adopting a prescribed way of life. The first category was no less consequential: an allottee received a parcel of land that had been held in common, and the parcel could be sold after the trust period. The statute assumed that individual ownership would make the allottee a farmer and a citizen. It did not ask whether the land could support a farm or whether the allottee wanted to farm.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes]

The policy stripped more than 90 million acres of tribal land, and the “surplus” land was sold to non-Native buyers. Many allotments were unsuitable for farming, and the acreages were comparable to those of the Homestead Act even though the Indigenous nations already controlled the land being divided. The contrast is exact: the Homestead Act gave public land to settlers; the Dawes Act gave land that was already owned, in common, to individuals, and then sold what was left.[^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-archives-homestead]

The act exempted several nations and did not apply uniformly. Allotment was not a single nationwide event with a single date. Indigenous nations resisted, negotiated, and litigated, and the policy’s effects varied by nation, region, and decade. The chapter does not describe allotment as a single blow. It describes a long process with many hands.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-check-treaty]

### 4.3 In Focus: Choate’s paired portraits of Tom Torlino

**In Focus.** John N. Choate’s paired portraits of Tom Torlino, a Diné (Navajo) student at Carlisle, were taken in 1882 and 1885. The caption printed with the pair reads: “TOM TORLINO – NAVAJO. AS HE ENTERED THE SCHOOL IN 1882. AS HE APPEARED THREE YEARS LATER.” The image appears in Choate’s *Souvenir of the Carlisle Indian School* (Carlisle, Pa.: J. N. Choate, 1902), held by the Dickinson College Archives and Special Collections as item CIS-I-0039.[^ch25-dickinson-torlino][^ch25-check-image]

The pair is one composite, staged to manufacture a before and an after. The art historian María Beatriz H. Carrión argues that the “before” pictures deliberately intensified the students’ cultural and racial markers, while the “after” photographs de-emphasized otherness by showing sitters in gendered uniforms and hairstyles. The school commissioned the photographs to promote its mission and raise funds. The pair is institutional evidence, not a transparent record of one person’s transformation.[^ch25-carrion-torlino]

The photographs can show what the institution wanted viewers to see. They cannot show how Torlino understood the change, what he lost, or what he kept. The surviving descriptions are the school’s own, and no account by Torlino of the change is preserved in the record this chapter consulted. The silence is not an absence of experience. It is an absence of record.[^ch25-dickinson-torlino][^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-check-focus]

### 4.4 The consequences of allotment and schooling

Allotment, schooling, and the destruction of the bison worked together. With the herds gone and the reservations allotted, many families could not support themselves on the land they received, and poverty and dependency compounded the effects of dispossession. The boarding schools belonged to the land question, not beside it: a child removed from a family and a family removed from its land were parts of one policy.[^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee][^ch25-carrion-torlino]

The policy did not go unchallenged. Indigenous nations continued to litigate, organize, and hold land and culture through the allotment era. Resistance did not reverse the policy, but it did not cease, and it kept Indigenous claims alive through the decades when the statute was meant to extinguish them. The chapter states the policy and the resistance together, because a history that records only the first is not a history of the West.[^ch25-check-sources][^ch25-check-treaty]

## 5. Bison destruction, settlement, and the frontier story

The conquest was not complete when the shooting stopped. It continued in the herd, the survey, the land office, and the show. The bison disappeared, the public domain filled, the census declared the frontier closed, and the West became a story that Americans told about themselves.[^ch25-nps-bison][^ch25-okhistory-land-run]

### 5.1 The bison and the Plains economy

In the early 1800s an estimated 30 to 60 million bison roamed North America. By 1900 fewer than 1,000 remained. The railroad transformed the Plains by bringing large-scale commercial hunting and new markets for bison hides, meat, and bones to eastern cities. Hunting had effectively ceased by the early 1880s, and the only survivors lived in Yellowstone National Park, on a few private ranches, in Canada, or in zoos and Wild West shows.[^ch25-nps-bison]

The estimates vary widely, and the chapter gives them as estimates. What is not uncertain is the direction. A species that had fed and clothed Plains nations for generations became a curiosity in a park and a specimen in a show.[^ch25-nps-bison][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

The destruction had a military consequence even where it was not a military order. A nation that could not hunt could not move freely, and the ration that replaced the hunt was distributed at the agency.[^ch25-nps-bison][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

### 5.2 Settlement: the Homestead Act and the land runs

The Homestead Act, passed on May 20, 1862, granted adult heads of families 160 acres of surveyed public land for a minimal filing fee and five years of continuous residence and improvement. It converted the public domain into private farms, and it became the legal model for the settlement of the West.[^ch25-archives-homestead]

The land runs were the dramatic version of the same policy. On April 22, 1889, the government opened the two-million-acre Unassigned Lands of Indian Territory to settlement. An estimated 50,000 people surrounded the district; at noon, signals from troops, rifles, and cannon started the rush. An estimated 11,000 agricultural homesteads were claimed. Those who entered early — the “sooners” — generated hundreds of contests, one of which reached the Supreme Court in *Smith v. Townsend* (1892). The land had been set aside for Indigenous nations and was being opened over their claims.[^ch25-okhistory-land-run][^ch25-check-numbers]

Black southerners also sought the western land. The 1879 Great Exodus brought about 6,000 African Americans from Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas to Kansas, joining earlier migrants. Kansas’s Black population rose from about 16,250 in 1870 to 43,110 in 1880, and the town of Nicodemus became the best-known Black settlement. The Exodusters claimed the same land laws on their own terms. The West was not a White story.[^ch25-nps-exodusters][^ch25-check-race]

### 5.3 The Census of 1890 and the “closing” of the frontier

The Census of 1890 reported that there was no longer a frontier line of settlement. Turner opened his 1893 address by quoting the Superintendent of the Census: “Up to and including 1880 the country had a frontier of settlement, but at present the unsettled area has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line.” Turner called this “the closing of a great historic movement.”[^ch25-yawp-turner][^ch25-tah-turner]

The census line measured the density of settlement, not the end of Indigenous presence or of conflict. The Census defined the frontier as the margin of settlement at a density of two or more persons per square mile; a line drawn by that definition could “close” while Indigenous nations still lived, worked, and governed west of it. The “closing” was a claim about a definition of settlement, exactly the kind of claim the debate section tests.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]

### 5.4 Buffalo Bill and the frontier story

William F. Cody (“Buffalo Bill”) built a Wild West exhibition that toured the United States. In 1885 Sitting Bull joined the show for one season. He rode in the opening procession, earned $50 a week, and met President Grover Cleveland. When the season ended, the Standing Rock agent refused to let him return. “The wigwam is a better place for the red man,” Sitting Bull said, using the period’s language for Indigenous people.[^ch25-pbs-sitting-bull][^ch25-nps-sitting-bull]

The show turned the West into entertainment and fixed a myth of conquest as adventure. It gave the conquest a heroic form at the very moment the census declared the frontier closed. The myth is evidence of how the conquest was remembered, not a description of what happened. Cody’s show and Turner’s essay did different kinds of work, but they agreed on one thing: the West was a story the nation told about its own becoming.[^ch25-archive-limerick][^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-ushistoryscene-limerick]

## 6. The Debate

The argument over how the West should be explained began while the West was being taken. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open. The debate is not a quarrel about whether conquest happened. It is a quarrel about what conquest was, who made it, and when it ended.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]

### 6.1 What is settled

The treaty system and its breach are documented. The United States signed treaties that recognized Indigenous title, including the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty, and then took land, including the Black Hills, without the consent the treaties required. The 1876 agreement was signed by about 10 percent of adult male Sioux, and Congress implemented it by statute in 1877.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-sioux-nation]

The federal and corporate machinery of extraction is documented. The railroads received public land and built across Indigenous homelands, and mining, ranching, and irrigation reorganized the western economy around distant markets. The 1872 mining law and the Dawes Act fixed legal regimes that outlasted the century.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-blm-mining-law][^ch25-archives-dawes]

The violence is documented. Sand Creek, the Great Sioux War, the Nez Perce and Apache wars, and Wounded Knee were campaigns and massacres with reported casualty counts that can be cited. The counts vary, and the variation is part of the record.[^ch25-nps-sand-creek][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-nps-nez-perce][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

The land loss is documented. The Dawes Act allotted reservation land and opened the surplus, and the allotment policy stripped more than 90 million acres from Indigenous nations. The bison were destroyed, and Plains nations lost the economic base of their independence.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes][^ch25-nps-bison]

The racial order is documented. Chinese workers were excluded, Mexican workers were segregated and underpaid, and Black settlers claimed the same land laws while facing a national racial hierarchy. The West was not a refuge from the South’s racial order; it was another theater of it.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-digitalhistory-mexican][^ch25-nps-exodusters]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the West a process, a place, or a conquest? Turner treated the frontier as a process that ended in 1890; Limerick and the New Western historians treated the West as a place whose conquest continued. The difference is not only interpretive but chronological.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]

How much did the federal government make the West? Whether the West is best explained by federal law, subsidy, and coercion, or by environment and local initiative, remains debated. White puts the state and capital at the center; Powell and Worster put aridity at the center.[^ch25-white-misfortune][^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-worster-rivers]

How much power did Indigenous nations hold? Hämäläinen and others argue that nations such as the Lakota and the Comanche built empires and shaped the colonial order, rather than merely resisting it. The claim is powerful and contested, and the chapter tests it against the wars and treaties it narrates.[^ch25-hamalainen-lakota]

How limiting was the environment? Worster treats the arid West as a “hydraulic empire” built on federal water projects; whether aridity is a constraint or was overcome by capital and the state is a central disagreement. The chapter’s answer is that both are true: the environment set limits, and capital and the state organized the response.[^ch25-worster-rivers][^ch25-plains-powell]

When should the era be said to end? The 1900 endpoint is a planning choice. The 1890 Census, Wounded Knee, and the 1889 Oklahoma land run offer competing markers, and the chapter labels anything after 1900 as consequence.[^ch25-check-dates][^ch25-check-scope]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

Turner argued that the existence of free land and its recession explained American development, and that the frontier produced individualism and democracy. The thesis made the West a process and gave it a national meaning. Its power is that it connected settlement to the shape of American institutions. Its weakness is that it treated Indigenous nations as obstacles to civilization, took “free land” as empty, and declared the frontier closed as if conflict had ended.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-yawp-turner]

Limerick rejected the process and its supposed end. She treated the West as a real place full of different peoples, and she compared the legacy of conquest to the legacy of slavery. The conquest framework restores Indigenous, Mexican, Chinese, and Black people to the story and treats conquest as a subject as serious as slavery. Its risk is that a broad synthesis can underplay the weight of federal policy and environmental constraint, and that “conquest” can blur the different positions of non-Indigenous settlers.[^ch25-archive-limerick][^ch25-ushistoryscene-limerick]

Richard White treats the West as a region organized by federal power, corporate capital, and the labor of diverse peoples. His synthesis is a standard political-economy account of the field. Its strength is that it puts law, subsidy, and the state at the center and connects the West to national industrial development. Its risk is that a survey can make the state seem more coherent than it was.[^ch25-white-misfortune]

Worster reads western development through water and federal power, and Hämäläinen restores Indigenous nations to the center as imperial powers in their own right. Both were consulted only through reviews or descriptions for this chapter, and their rows in Table 1 are marked accordingly. Their arguments matter even where the chapter cannot yet verify them: aridity and Indigenous power are not decorative alternatives to the conquest story. They are part of it.[^ch25-worster-rivers][^ch25-hamalainen-lakota][^ch25-check-sources]

The synthesis for this chapter is that the West was conquered and also made: by treaties and their breach, by capital and federal law, by migrant and coerced labor, and by the Indigenous nations who persisted. No single explanation accounts for it. The positions overlap more than they compete, and the table is a map of the arguments rather than a set of teams.[^ch25-check-table][^ch25-white-misfortune][^ch25-hamalainen-lakota]

## Legacies

The instruments of conquest outlasted the generation that built them. The reservations, the railroad grants, the mining law of 1872, and the allotment policy of 1887 fixed the legal geography of the West. The boarding schools left a legacy of family separation and cultural loss that later generations would document and litigate.[^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-blm-mining-law]

The land question did not close in 1890. The Black Hills were taken by the 1877 Act, which the Supreme Court later held had effected a taking. In *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians* (1980), the Court affirmed an award of $17.1 million plus interest from 1877. The Lakota have refused the payment, which has grown to more than $1 billion, insisting that the land was never for sale. The refusal is the point: a price can be assessed, but consent cannot be manufactured after the fact.[^ch25-sioux-nation][^ch25-csm-black-hills]

The environmental question remains open. The aridity Powell described still governs the West, and the water systems built along the Colorado and other rivers made possible a growth the rainfall could not have sustained. The frontier story that Cody’s shows and Turner’s essay fixed continued to be invoked in American politics long after the frontier line was declared gone.[^ch25-plains-powell][^ch25-worster-rivers][^ch25-ushistoryscene-limerick]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** A transnational history would place the American case beside Canada, Australia, and Argentina; the comparison would ask how settler states used treaties, reserves, and schools, without assuming that the cases were identical or causally linked.[^ch25-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Treaty records, military reports, agency correspondence, and school photographs survive in abundance, while the words of the dispossessed survive unevenly, and the Chinese railroad workers left no known letters at all. The chapter names that asymmetry rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor][^ch25-dickinson-torlino][^ch25-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 17 owns earlier removal; Chapter 23 owns Reconstruction’s western connection; Chapter 26 owns the southern racial order; Chapter 29 owns conservation and Progressive reform. The chapter cross-references these rather than re-narrating them.[^ch25-check-ch17][^ch25-check-ch23][^ch25-check-ch26]

## Summary

- On August 2, 1874, miners on Custer’s Black Hills expedition reported gold, Custer published the news, and the report — doubted even within the expedition — helped open a treaty-protected Lakota homeland to a rush.[^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-kirkby-black-hills]
- The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 recognized the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, and required three-fourths of adult male consent for any cession; the United States stopped enforcing it in 1875, and the Great Sioux War followed.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-sioux-nation]
- About 10,000 Chinese laborers worked on the western transcontinental railroad, paid on average about 30 percent less than White workers; their June 1867 strike was the largest United States labor strike of its time, and more than 1,000 are estimated to have died.[^ch25-nps-chinese-labor]
- The Comstock Lode made Virginia City boom after 1859; the open range collapsed in the winter of 1885–86; and Powell’s 1878 report warned that the arid West could not be settled on the 160-acre model.[^ch25-shpo-comstock][^ch25-tsha-big-die-up][^ch25-plains-powell]
- The Plains and far-western wars included the 1868 settlement of Red Cloud’s War, the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, the Nez Perce flight of 1877, Geronimo’s surrender in 1886, and the massacre at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890.[^ch25-nps-bozeman][^ch25-pbs-custer-timeline][^ch25-nps-nez-perce][^ch25-nps-chiricahua][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]
- The Carlisle school opened in 1879 under Pratt’s “Kill the Indian … and save the man” policy, and the Dawes Act of 1887 allotted reservation land and opened the surplus to non-Native buyers.[^ch25-carrion-torlino][^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-nps-dawes]
- From an estimated 30 to 60 million bison in the early 1800s, the herds fell to fewer than 1,000 by 1900, and the Homestead Act and the 1889 Oklahoma land run converted the public domain into private farms.[^ch25-nps-bison][^ch25-archives-homestead][^ch25-okhistory-land-run]
- Exodusters brought about 6,000 Black southerners to Kansas in 1879, and Kansas’s Black population rose from about 16,250 in 1870 to 43,110 in 1880.[^ch25-nps-exodusters]
- The Census of 1890 reported no frontier line, and Turner’s 1893 address made that report the basis of a national frontier thesis that Limerick’s *Legacy of Conquest* later rejected.[^ch25-tah-turner][^ch25-archive-limerick]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The decades after the Civil War took the West from the nations that held it by treaty and turned it into a reserve of land, minerals, water, and myths. The treaties were signed and broken; the railroads and mines were built by Mexican, Chinese, and White labor, and Black settlers claimed the land laws on their own terms; the Plains nations lost the bison and then the land; the schools took the children; and the census declared the frontier gone while the conquest continued. The chapter’s question — who held title, and how was it enforced — was answered in land, in law, and in violence, and it was never answered once.[^ch25-avalon-ft-laramie][^ch25-archives-dawes][^ch25-pbs-wounded-knee]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Jackson, Mississippi, in 1890, where a state convention wrote a new constitution that stripped Black men of the vote. The instruments of exclusion that had organized the West through treaty, allotment, and schooling were being matched in the South by instruments of racial rule. Chapter 26 takes up the South, race, and the new order.[^ch25-check-ch26][^ch25-check-bridge]
