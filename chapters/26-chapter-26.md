## Epigraphs

> “There is no use to equivocate or lie about the matter. … Mississippi’s constitutional convention of 1890 was held for no other purpose than to eliminate the negro from politics.”[^ch26-ms-plan-1890]

— James K. Vardaman, delegate and later governor and U.S. senator from Mississippi, statement on the 1890 convention, as quoted by *Mississippi Today*.[^ch26-ms-plan-1890]

> “The right to vote. Civic equality. The education of youth according to ability.”[^ch26-dubois-souls]

— W. E. B. Du Bois, “Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others,” in *The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903.[^ch26-dubois-souls]

> “One of the strangest things about the career of Jim Crow was that the system was born in the North and reached an advanced age before moving South in force.”[^ch26-woodward-summary]

— C. Vann Woodward, *The Strange Career of Jim Crow*, 1955.[^ch26-woodward-summary]

- **Why these three.** A Mississippi leader said plainly that a state constitution existed to remove Black men from politics. A Black scholar answered with the three things the new order denied. A historian argued that the order those two described was not ancient custom but a recent invention, and a northern one first. The three disagree about whether the new order was frank purpose, contestable demand, or contingent construction.[^ch26-woodward-summary][^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-check-epigraphs]
- **The attribution rule.** The Vardaman declaration is quoted in a journalism feature; some secondary accounts attribute the same sentence to the convention president, Solomon S. Calhoon, so the epigraph must resolve the attribution against the convention journal or Vardaman’s own papers before publication. Du Bois’s three demands are transcribed from an excerpt of the 1903 first edition. Woodward’s sentence is transcribed from an unofficial study summary that prints a page number (p. 17) but is not the book; it must be collated character by character against the 1955 first edition (Oxford University Press) or the 2002 commemorative edition.[^ch26-ms-plan-1890][^ch26-dubois-souls][^ch26-woodward-summary][^ch26-check-attribution][^ch26-check-epigraphs]
- **The historian’s voice.** Woodward supplies the required historian’s voice and frames the chapter’s central disagreement: whether the new order is best explained by an elite decision in the 1890s, by class conflict among Whites, by the mechanics of state law, or by the violence and gendered culture that enforced it.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-check-epigraphs]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The call and the composition.** In early 1890, alarmed in part by the possibility of federal supervision of elections, Mississippi officials acted; Gov. John Stone approved a legislative call for an August constitutional convention. Of the 137 delegates, Democrats held 134 seats. The lone Black delegate was Isaiah T. Montgomery, of Bolivar County, a co-founder of the all-Black town of Mound Bayou and a man once enslaved by Jefferson Davis.[^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890][^ch26-ms-plan-1890]
- **The purpose was stated.** Vardaman’s declaration is the most quoted admission that the convention existed to remove Black voters from politics, and the convention’s work matched the admission. Delegates did not propose a race-based restriction that the Fifteenth Amendment plainly forbade; they wrote facially neutral qualifications that registrars could apply by race.[^ch26-ms-plan-1890][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **The franchise article.** Article 12 set the qualifications. It imposed a two-dollar poll tax on male inhabitants (Sec. 243), a criminal disqualification, a registration oath, and, at Sec. 244, the requirement that after January 1, 1892, every elector “shall, in addition to the foregoing qualifications, be able to read any section of the constitution of this State; or he shall be able to understand the same when read to him, or give a reasonable interpretation thereof.” A new registration was required before the next election after that date.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise]
- **The adoption.** The convention sat for about ten weeks and approved the constitution by a vote of 129 to 8. Unlike Mississippi’s previous constitutions, this one was not submitted to the voters for ratification.[^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890][^ch26-ms-plan-1890]
- **Montgomery’s choice.** Montgomery spoke in support of the Franchise Committee report — the speech was printed as “What answer?” — and voted for the constitution, reportedly hoping that formal disfranchisement might end the violence against Black Mississippians. It did not.[^ch26-ms-plan-1890][^ch26-check-scene]
- **The measured effect.** Within a decade, the number of registered Black voters in Mississippi fell from more than 130,000 to fewer than 1,300. The exact figures must be collated against registration records, but the direction is not in dispute.[^ch26-ms-plan-1890][^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-numbers]
- **What the scene can show.** It can show documented dates, a documented delegate count, the text of the franchise article, a documented vote, and a documented claim about the convention’s purpose. It cannot show the weather in Jackson, the floor debate, or Montgomery’s private thoughts.[^ch26-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A state constitution, not a mob, built the order.** The scene’s claim is that the “new order” was not only the work of night riders. It was written into a state’s fundamental law by a convention that met in daylight, kept a journal, and published its text. The distance between the frank admission of purpose and the neutral wording of Sec. 244 is the scene’s point.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-plan-1890]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question: how did racial domination become an institutional order after Reconstruction? The answer begins with law, but it runs through credit, employment, punishment, and violence as well.[^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890][^ch26-check-scope]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did racial domination become an institutional order after Reconstruction?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the franchise article to the courts, the fields, the mines, the mills, the ballot box, and the lynch rope.

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The call, the delegate count, the text of the article, the vote, and the non-ratification are documented. The mood of the chamber, the speeches not recorded, and the private reasoning of Montgomery or Vardaman must not be staged beyond what the record states.[^ch26-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** In 1890 no one knew how far other states would copy Mississippi, that Plessy would follow in six years, or that the new order would stand for more than half a century. The scene must preserve the uncertainty of the moment.[^ch26-check-scene][^ch26-check-dates]
- **No claim that disfranchisement was complete on adoption.** Sec. 244 took effect on January 1, 1892, and enforcement varied by county and registrar; the scene should present the rule and its machinery, not a finished result.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-check-numbers]

## 1. Redemption and the Court’s retreat

### 1.1 Redemption by violence and fraud

- **The claim.** The new order began with the forcible end of Reconstruction government, achieved through organized violence, intimidation, and fraud rather than through a change in the law, and it was consolidated when the federal government stopped enforcing the Reconstruction amendments.[^ch26-ms-plan-1875][^ch26-house-demise]
- **The Mississippi Plan of 1875.** After Republicans carried the state in 1874, White leagues used widespread violence and fraud to recapture it. Over several days in September 1875, about 50 Black Mississippians were killed along with White supporters, including a teacher who worked with the Black community at Clinton. The governor asked President Ulysses S. Grant to intervene; he declined, and the violence and fraud continued. Other states copied the method, which became known as the Mississippi Plan.[^ch26-ms-plan-1875]
- **The federal retreat.** Through 1874 and 1875, Black and Republican voters across the South faced stolen ballot boxes, fraudulent counts, armed intimidation, and violence. A federal elections bill passed the House in 1875 but was abandoned in the Senate, and federal enforcement collapsed before the 1876 election.[^ch26-house-demise]
- **The settlement of 1877.** The disputed presidential election of 1876 was resolved by an informal understanding in which Southern Democrats ended their resistance to Rutherford B. Hayes in exchange for the withdrawal of the last federal troops from the South and a policy of leniency. When Hayes took office he withdrew the troops, which historians largely treat as the end of Reconstruction. The chapter should say that no written text of the bargain has been found and that the chapter’s account of it must rest on a scholarly source, not on the popular name alone.[^ch26-wikipedia-1877][^ch26-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** Redemption did not merely change officeholders. It transferred the protection of Black political and civil rights from the federal government to state governments that were committed to ending them, which is the structural fact the rest of the chapter explains.[^ch26-house-demise][^ch26-civil-rights-cases]

### 1.2 The Supreme Court’s narrowing of the amendments

- **The claim.** Between 1873 and 1898 the Supreme Court read the Reconstruction amendments narrowly, denying Congress the power to reach private discrimination and upholding state measures that were neutral on their face but racial in operation. The Court’s retreat made the new order legally survivable.[^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **The Civil Rights Cases (1883).** In a single decision covering five prosecutions, the Court held that the first two sections of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 were unconstitutional as applied to the states. The Fourteenth Amendment, the Court reasoned, is “prohibitory upon the States only,” and the Thirteenth Amendment reaches only slavery “and its incidents”; the denial of equal accommodations, the Court said, “imposes no badge of slavery or involuntary servitude.” The decision, eight to one, was the constitutional threshold the chapter opens on.[^ch26-civil-rights-cases]
- **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).** The Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring “equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races” on railroads, establishing the “separate but equal” doctrine that governed public accommodations for the next half century. The chapter should present Plessy as the validation of a practice the states had already begun after the 1887 Florida railroad-segregation law, not as its origin.[^ch26-archives-plessy]
- **Williams v. Mississippi (1898).** The Court upheld Mississippi’s poll tax, literacy tests, and disfranchisement clauses on the ground that the constitution did not discriminate on the face and that “the actual administration was not shown to be evil: only that evil was possible under them.” The ruling licensed the spread of the Mississippi model.[^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **The limit.** The chapter should name the dissents — most importantly Justice John Marshall Harlan’s — and should note that the Court’s reasoning left the door open for later litigation, which is why the chapter’s Legacies returns to the line running from these cases to *Brown v. Board of Education*.[^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-archives-plessy]

## 2. Crop liens, sharecropping, and convict leasing

### 2.1 Land, labor, and the failure of redistribution

- **The claim.** Emancipation gave Black Southerners their labor but not the land to live from, and the new order in the countryside rested on contracts that converted free labor into debt and dependence.[^ch26-duke-sharecropping][^ch26-pbs-sharecropping]
- **The lost land.** At a Savannah meeting in 1865, Black leaders asked the government for land. Their spokesman, the Baptist minister Garrison Frazier, said that “the way we can best take care of ourselves, is to have land, and turn it and till it by our own labor.” General William T. Sherman’s Special Field Order No. 15 set aside coastal land in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida, but President Andrew Johnson rescinded the order in late 1865, and the promise of forty acres was never fulfilled.[^ch26-duke-sharecropping]
- **The sharecropping contract.** On January 1, 1867, in Marshall County, Mississippi, two formerly enslaved men, Cooper Hughes and Charles Roberts, contracted to work 40 acres of corn and 20 acres of cotton on I. G. Bailey’s land for one-third of the corn and one-half of the cotton, with stipends of meat, a mule, a garden plot, and cabins. The chapter should read the contract as evidence of both the freedpeople’s bargaining and the narrowness of their options.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping]
- **The crop-lien cycle.** Under the crop-lien system, a merchant or planter advanced supplies against the coming crop at interest and took first legal claim to the harvest. As cotton prices fell, families who could not pay could not leave, because the law and the debt bound them to the land. The chapter should show the mechanism — advance, lien, crop, debt — rather than treat “sharecropping” as a single institution.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-check-numbers]

### 2.2 Convict leasing and the criminal-exception economy

- **The claim.** The Thirteenth Amendment’s exception for criminal punishment became the legal doorway for a system that leased Black prisoners to private employers, and convict leasing returned the South’s labor problem to a punishment system that racialized crime.[^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing]
- **The mechanism.** Southern states leased prisoners to railways, mines, lumber yards, brick yards, factories, and large plantations. The state, county, or local government collected leasing fees; the prisoner earned no pay and faced dangerous, often deadly conditions. Black Codes criminalized vagrancy, loitering, and failure to prove employment, and professional “crime hunters” were paid per arrest, with apprehensions rising when labor demand rose. Even people found innocent could be placed in the system if they could not pay their court fees.[^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing]
- **The Alabama case.** The Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company was among the largest users of prisoner labor, most of it Black men convicted of petty offenses, and convict labor became central to the iron and steel industry around Birmingham. The chapter should connect leasing to the industrial story in §3 rather than treat them separately.[^ch26-loc-convict-leasing][^ch26-eoa-birmingham]
- **The duration and the silence.** The system persisted into the 1930s and in places until after the Second World War, and it left behind a thin record and a large silence. The chapter should name that silence rather than invent prisoners’ voices.[^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing][^ch26-check-race]

### 2.3 The cost of dependence

- **The claim.** Debt, immobility, and the convict lease worked with disfranchisement to strip Black Southerners of the economic and legal standing that free citizenship implied, even as they remained the majority labor force in much of the region.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing]
- **The consequence.** A laborer who could not leave the land, could not vote, and could be arrested on a vagrancy charge and leased out had lost the instruments of self-defense that freedom had promised. The chapter should state this as an argument built on the mechanisms already shown, not as a summary assertion.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-duke-sharecropping]
- **The connection.** This section supplies the material base that §4’s disfranchisement and §5’s violence presuppose: the new order was not only a set of legal rules but a distribution of land, credit, and punishment.[^ch26-check-scope]

## 3. New South industry

### 3.1 Grady’s program and the promise of reunion

- **The claim.** The “New South” was a promotional program for northern investment and southern industrial growth, and its leading spokesman sold it as reunion while promoting White supremacy.[^ch26-yawp-grady][^ch26-nge-grady]
- **The speech.** Henry W. Grady, managing editor of the *Atlanta Constitution*, won national recognition for his December 21, 1886, speech to the New England Society in New York City. Quoting Benjamin H. Hill, he declared: “There was a South of slavery and secession — that South is dead. There is a South of union and freedom — that South, thank God, is living, breathing, growing every hour.” The new South, he said, offered “a hundred farms for every plantation” and “a diversified industry that meets the complex needs of this complex age,” and “the South has nothing to take back.”[^ch26-yawp-grady]
- **The politics.** Grady used the *Constitution* to promote northern investment, industrial growth, diversified farming — and White supremacy. The chapter should present the two together rather than treat the economic program as separable from the racial order it depended on.[^ch26-nge-grady]
- **The limit.** The chapter should not adopt Grady’s description as analysis. “New South” was a slogan, and the chapter should test it against wages, hours, land tenure, and who received the investment.[^ch26-nge-grady][^ch26-check-names]

### 3.2 Textiles, iron, and steel

- **The claim.** The region’s industrial growth rested on cheap labor — including the labor of children and of leased prisoners — and on the raw materials and transport that tied the South to national markets.[^ch26-eoa-birmingham][^ch26-bls-child-labor]
- **Birmingham.** Central Alabama’s Jones Valley held closely associated deposits of iron ore, coal, limestone, and dolomite, giving the Birmingham District the lowest raw-material assembly costs in the country. Iron makers built vertically integrated companies, and the district grew as rapidly as Pittsburgh and Chicago in the last two decades of the nineteenth century. The labor force was drawn from impoverished freedmen and poor Whites who migrated from tenant farming and sharecropping.[^ch26-eoa-birmingham]
- **The mills.** The textile industry grew rapidly after the Civil War, and by 1900 about 25,000 of the nearly 100,000 textile workers in the South were children under 16; the chapter should state the definition of “child” used and note that the figure is a single-year snapshot.[^ch26-bls-child-labor][^ch26-check-numbers]
- **The condition of labor.** The chapter should connect the industrial expansion to the crop-lien and leasing sections: the New South’s growth did not lift the people whose labor made it possible, and its leading enterprises used the same racialized labor controls that governed the countryside.[^ch26-eoa-birmingham][^ch26-loc-convict-leasing]

### 3.3 Who benefited

- **The claim.** The New South enriched a narrow class of planters, merchants, and industrialists and left most farmers and workers in place, which is why the region’s politics after 1890 ran toward agrarian revolt.[^ch26-nge-grady][^ch26-nge-watson]
- **The turn to Populism.** Thomas E. Watson of Georgia began as a voice for poor farmers and sharecroppers of both races and depended on Black votes in his 1892 campaign; the chapter should note that dependence and leave the movement’s history to Chapter 27.[^ch26-nge-watson]
- **The connection.** The chapter should close §3 by handing the agrarian revolt to Chapter 27 and keeping its own focus on the racial order that the New South’s leaders served.[^ch26-check-ch27]

## 4. Disfranchisement

### 4.1 The Mississippi model

- **The claim.** Mississippi’s 1890 constitution supplied the template for disfranchisement because its restrictions were neutral in text and discriminatory in administration. The chapter should distinguish the written rule from its enforcement, exactly as the README requires.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-encyclopedia-1890]
- **The devices.** The poll tax of two dollars (Sec. 243); the criminal disqualification for offenses that Black men were most likely to be charged with; the registration oath; and, at Sec. 244, the literacy test with its “understand the same when read to him, or give a reasonable interpretation thereof” alternative. A new registration and the discretion of the registrar were what turned neutral words into exclusion.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise]
- **In Focus: Section 244.** **In Focus.** The box reads the text of Section 244 as a legal instrument. The section says that after January 1, 1892, every elector must, “in addition to the foregoing qualifications, be able to read any section of the constitution of this State; or he shall be able to understand the same when read to him, or give a reasonable interpretation thereof.” The decisive words are “understand” and “reasonable interpretation,” because the registrar decided both, and the section required a fresh registration. The box should note that the section was later repealed and replaced by a registration-qualification provision, and should read the original text against that change.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise]
- **How to read it.** Read the section as a machine for discretion rather than as an education standard. Its face is a reading requirement; its function was to place the power to admit or refuse a voter in the hands of an official chosen by the party that wrote the section. Say what the text cannot show: how each registrar used it, and how many people were refused without a record.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-focus]

### 4.2 The spread, 1890–1910

- **The claim.** Between 1890 and 1910 most of the former Confederate states rewrote their constitutions or passed amendments that used the same neutral devices, and the Supreme Court’s Williams decision removed the last real federal check.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **The devices in combination.** The poll tax, the literacy and understanding test, the grandfather clause, the property qualification, the registration form filled out “without assistance,” the white primary, and felony disfranchisement. Georgia’s cumulative poll tax, adopted in 1877, cut Black turnout by about half before the 1890 wave even began.[^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **Louisiana, 1898.** Louisiana held a convention to write a “disfranchisement constitution.” Before the calling referendum, registration had already been driven down: total registered voters fell from 294,432 in 1897 to 87,240 in 1898; White registrants fell from 164,088 to 74,133, and Black registrants from 130,344 to 12,902. The new constitution added an annual poll tax, a literacy test administered at the registrar’s discretion, a complicated “without assistance” registration form, a $300 property qualification, and a grandfather clause; the legislature put it into effect without submitting it to the voters. By 1900 the number of Black registrants had fallen to 5,320, and by 1922 to 598.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana]
- **The national scale.** By 1910, according to figures attributed to the historian Donald G. Nieman, Black registration had fallen to about 15 percent in Virginia and under 2 percent in both Alabama and Mississippi. The chapter should give these as estimates with their year and source, and should note that the same devices also removed many poor White voters.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-numbers]

### 4.3 Figure 5 and the geography of disfranchisement

- **The claim.** The disfranchising constitutions followed a discernible sequence across the South, and a single dated timeline makes the pattern visible without suggesting a uniform result.[^ch26-check-figure]
- **The measure.** **Figure 5 plan.** The registered visual `ch26-figure-01` (Figure 5, “Disfranchising constitutions, 1890–1910”) belongs in §4.3. It should show, for each state that adopted a disfranchising constitution or amendment, the year of adoption and the specific mechanism or mechanisms (poll tax, literacy or understanding test, grandfather clause, property qualification, white primary, other). It must distinguish the adoption of a rule from its observed effect, must not invent registration numbers that the sources do not supply, and must carry a caption, source line, credit if any, alt text, and accessible description. It must reconcile with the register and with Appendix C.[^ch26-check-figure][^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-64parishes-louisiana]
- **The dates to verify.** The widely cited sequence includes Mississippi (1890), South Carolina (1895), Louisiana (1898), North Carolina (1900), Alabama (1901), Virginia (1902), Georgia (1908), and Oklahoma (1910). Each date and each mechanism must be collated against the state’s constitution or convention journal before the figure is produced.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-my-note-figure-dates][^ch26-check-figure]

### 4.4 The consequences of exclusion

- **The claim.** Disfranchisement did more than remove Black voters. It produced a one-party South, shifted power toward White rural counties through apportionment on total population, and forced the region’s politics into a single racial channel.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **The mechanism.** Because the disfranchised Black population was still counted for apportionment, White voters in majority-Black districts gained disproportionate power; primaries became the only meaningful election and were restricted to White voters; and candidates competed not on policy but on the intensity of their racial appeals. John B. Knox, an Alabama delegate at the 1901 convention, said the convention’s “goal is to establish white supremacy in the State, within the limits imposed by the Federal Constitution.”[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **Who was also excluded.** The devices fell on poor White men as well, which is why the order required the grandfather clause and the property qualification to protect them. The chapter should show that the new order sorted by race but controlled by class and party as well.[^ch26-history-disfranchise]

## 5. Segregation, lynching, and Black response

### 5.1 Segregation as law and custom

- **The claim.** Segregation after 1890 moved from custom and scattered statute to a system of state law that separated the races in transport, schools, and public life, and Plessy supplied its constitutional cover.[^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]
- **The course.** Beginning with an 1887 Florida railroad law, states required separate accommodations, and the practice spread to schools and most public and semi-public facilities. Railway companies disliked the expense and Black citizens saw each new law as a further repudiation of the Reconstruction amendments; when Louisiana proposed its separate-car bill in 1890, the Black community of New Orleans protested.[^ch26-archives-plessy]
- **The debate over timing.** The chapter should not assume segregation was ancient. Woodward’s argument that rigid segregation took shape only in the 1890s, and that earlier decades had seen more fluid race relations and more White dissent, is the position §6 tests rather than adopts.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-woodward-summary]

### 5.2 Lynching and the “national crime”

- **The claim.** Lynching was the enforcement arm of the new order, and Ida B. Wells produced the first sustained statistical and moral counter-record of it.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-wells-southern-horrors]
- **Wells and the Free Speech.** In May 1892 Wells published an editorial in her Memphis paper, the *Free Speech*, after eight Black men were lynched: “Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread bare lie that Negro men rape white women.” A White mob destroyed the *Free Speech* office, and Wells, then in the North, could not return.[^ch26-wells-southern-horrors][^ch26-durocher-wells]
- **Southern Horrors (1892).** Wells delivered the lecture that became *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* in New York in October 1892; the pamphlet named the pattern of hanging followed by shooting the body and challenged the rape charge that justified it.[^ch26-wells-southern-horrors]
- **A Red Record (1895).** In *A Red Record*, Wells argued that “more than ten thousand Negroes have been killed in cold blood” since emancipation, that only three White men had been tried, convicted, and executed for those murders, and that in 1894 there were 132 executions by due process of law against 197 killings by mobs. She built the record from the *Chicago Tribune*’s compilations so that the evidence could not be dismissed as a Black source.[^ch26-wells-red-record]
- **The three excuses.** Wells showed that the justification for lynching shifted over time — from alleged race riots, to “Negro domination,” to the charge of assaulting White women — as each earlier claim failed. The chapter should use her analysis of the shifting excuse as the section’s argument about how violence was legitimated.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-check-race]

### 5.3 Wilmington, 1898

- **The claim.** In Wilmington, North Carolina, White supremacists destroyed a functioning Fusion government by massacre and coup, the most complete violent seizure of a city government in the period.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-eji-wilmington]
- **The city.** Wilmington was a port of about 20,000 whose Black community had made itself a political force; the votes of Black men helped a biracial Fusion alliance elect candidates of both races, and three of the ten aldermen were Black, along with the coroner, jailer, treasurer, and register of deeds.[^ch26-eji-wilmington]
- **The trigger and the coup.** White leaders targeted the Fusion officeholders and the Black-owned *Daily Record*. After a sexually charged editorial by its editor, Alex Manly, was reprinted across the state, about 500 White men assembled at the Wilmington Light Infantry armory on November 10, 1898, and, led by the former congressman Alfred Moore Waddell, moved on the *Record* office. Waddell had earlier urged that White citizens, if necessary, “choke the Cape Fear with carcasses.”[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington]
- **The casualty problem.** The number of people killed is disputed: the coroner reported fourteen, the 2006 state report settled on “as many as sixty,” and other accounts claim scores or hundreds; all reported victims were Black. The chapter should give the range with its sources and explain why the record is uncertain.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-check-numbers]

### 5.4 Washington’s accommodation

- **The claim.** Booker T. Washington’s 1895 Atlanta address made industrial education and social separation the price of White tolerance, and it became the most influential statement of Black strategy between 1895 and 1915.[^ch26-washington-atlanta][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]
- **The address.** Speaking at the Cotton States and International Exposition in Atlanta on September 18, 1895, Washington told the story of the ship answered “Cast down your bucket where you are,” urged Blacks to “dignify and glorify common labour,” and said the races could “be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.” He argued that agitation for social equality was folly and that progress came through “constant struggle rather than of artificial forcing.”[^ch26-washington-atlanta][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]
- **The reception.** The address won a standing ovation, was telegraphed across the country, and cemented Washington’s influence with White leaders and presidents alike. The chapter should present his control over Black school funding and political patronage as part of the strategy, not as an aside.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]

### 5.5 Du Bois and the challenge

- **The claim.** W. E. B. Du Bois’s 1903 critique broke the assumption that accommodation was the only Black strategy and demanded the vote, civic equality, and education as matters of right.[^ch26-dubois-souls][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]
- **The critique.** In “Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others,” Du Bois wrote that Washington represented “the old attitude of adjustment and submission,” that his program “practically accepts the alleged inferiority of the Negro races,” and that it asked Black people to give up “political power,” “insistence on civil rights,” and “higher education of Negro youth.” Against that, he set three demands: “The right to vote. Civic equality. The education of youth according to ability.”[^ch26-dubois-souls]
- **The Talented Tenth.** Du Bois called for the higher education of a “talented tenth” to lead the struggle, and *The Souls of Black Folk* rallied opposition to Washington in Black intellectual circles. The chapter should present the Washington–Du Bois disagreement as a debate about strategy and power, not as a contest of personalities.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise][^ch26-dubois-souls]

### 5.6 The organizations, 1905 and 1909

- **The claim.** The Niagara Movement and the NAACP converted the argument over strategy into permanent organizations, and Black women and men built them even as the new order closed the ballot box.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise][^ch26-durocher-wells]
- **The Niagara Movement.** Du Bois and other Black leaders met at Niagara Falls in 1905 and founded a movement demanding civil and political rights and offering an alternative to Washington’s accommodation; it was the immediate precursor of the NAACP.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]
- **The NAACP.** The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People was founded in 1909. Wells was among its forty founders, though its white coordinators first removed her name from the founding committee before she forced it back on. The association launched *The Crisis* and chose Du Bois, not Wells, as its editor — a decision the chapter can use to show how the new organizations reproduced some of the inequalities they opposed.[^ch26-durocher-wells]
- **The connection.** The 1905 and 1909 organizations belong to this chapter even though they are also milestones of the Progressive Era; Chapter 29 should refer back to them rather than retell their founding.[^ch26-check-ch29]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The order was made by law as well as violence.** Mississippi’s 1890 constitution, the 1883 Civil Rights Cases, Plessy, and Williams v. Mississippi together gave the new order a legal architecture, and the devices of disfranchisement were written as neutral rules applied by racial discretion.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **The economic order was coercive.** The crop-lien system bound laborers to the land through debt, and convict leasing leased Black prisoners to private employers under conditions that killed them.[^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing]
- **Lynching was enforced and documented.** Wells’s record and the Wilmington casualty reports establish both the scale of the violence and the inadequacy of the surviving count.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington]
- **Black Southerners never stopped organizing.** From Wells’s anti-lynching campaign to the Niagara Movement and the NAACP, Black men and women built institutions and arguments against the order throughout its making.[^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise]
- **The order was regional and national at once.** The South wrote the rules, but the Supreme Court, the national press, and northern investment made them durable.[^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-yawp-grady]

### 6.2 What is open

- **When did segregation begin?** Woodward argued that rigid, statutory segregation took shape only in the 1890s, after a period of fluid and experimental race relations; his critics found segregation already present in the antebellum North, the antebellum urban South, and Reconstruction South Carolina. The question is both interpretive and chronological.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-woodward-summary]
- **Why did the order arrive when it did?** Woodward emphasized the collapse of three restraining forces — northern liberalism, southern conservatism, and southern radicalism — and the turning of Populists against their Black allies. Later historians stress urbanization, gender and sexual anxiety, and the ambitions of a White urban middle class. The chapter should state both mechanisms and test them against its evidence.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]
- **Who made disfranchisement?** The “elite manipulation” account treats the disfranchising conventions as a project of planters and industrialists to remove a political threat; the “agrarian” account treats them as a response to the Populist revolt. Whether the decisive actor was the elite, the party, or the county registrar remains open.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- **How much agency did Black Southerners hold?** Woodward’s early editions treated Black Southerners as “objects” rather than “subjects” of the segregation story, a charge his own later edition answered in part. The chapter should test the charge against Wells, Du Bois, and the organizers.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-durocher-wells]
- **When should the era end?** The chapter’s 1910 endpoint is a planning choice; 1896, 1898, 1901, and 1906 are competing markers, and the chapter must label anything after 1910 as consequence.[^ch26-check-dates][^ch26-check-scope]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the new order was made at once by elite decision, by class conflict among Whites, by the mechanics of state law, by gendered and sexualized anxiety, and by the violence that enforced it — and in which Black Southerners shaped and resisted it throughout. No single explanation accounts for it, and the chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-sources]
- **The Woodward position.** Woodward made segregation a problem with a history and a timeline, and gave the 1890s a central place in it.[^ch26-woodward-summary][^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]
- **The state-building position.** Work in the tradition of J. Morgan Kousser treats disfranchisement as a rational project of political actors who chose the device that removed the most opponents at the least cost to their own supporters.[^ch26-history-disfranchise][^ch26-check-sources]
- **The culture and gender position.** Glenda Elizabeth Gilmore and Grace Elizabeth Hale show that the making of segregation ran through ideas about White womanhood, Black masculinity, and urban space, not through law alone.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward]
- **The violence and resistance positions.** Michael J. Pfeifer and W. Fitzhugh Brundage treat lynching as a form of rough justice tied to local order, and the scholarship on Black resistance restores Wells, Du Bois, and the organizers to the center. The synthesis for this chapter: the new order was built by law, capital, and violence and was contested at every step.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-table]

## Legacies

The order built between 1877 and 1910 outlasted the people who made it. The disfranchising constitutions and amendments stood until the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and the segregation upheld in Plessy survived until *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954 and the civil rights laws that followed. The convict-lease system was abolished state by state before the Second World War, but its logic persisted in the prison labor and mass incarceration that followed.[^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing][^ch26-history-disfranchise]

The order also shaped where people could live. Disfranchisement and violence helped drive the Great Migration of the twentieth century, as Black Southerners left for northern and western cities; the chapter should state the migration as a consequence the evidence supports and not as a single response to any one law.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-check-sources]

The chapter’s central problem — how an order of racial domination was made and maintained — did not end in 1910. Its devices returned in new forms, and its memory was contested: Wilmington was called a race riot for a century before a state commission called it a coup, and the disfranchising conventions were described in their own time as reforms. The chapter should present that contested memory as part of the history, with the evidence on each side.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-check-race]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The American South was not the only society to build legal segregation in these decades; South Africa’s segregationist order and its later apartheid ran on parallel ideas, and Woodward’s book itself compared the South to South Africa. The chapter should place the comparison in a note rather than claim the two systems were identical or that one caused the other.[^ch26-pfeifer-woodward][^ch26-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Constitutions, court reports, registrars’ records, and newspaper accounts survive in abundance, while the words of the disfranchised and the lynched survive unevenly. Wells built her record from White compilations precisely because Black testimony was disbelieved. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-durocher-wells][^ch26-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 23 owns Reconstruction, Chapter 24 owns industrial transformation, Chapter 25 owns western conquest, Chapter 27 owns Populism and the 1898 empire, and Chapter 29 owns Progressive reform. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch26-check-ch23][^ch26-check-ch25][^ch26-check-ch27][^ch26-check-ch29]

## Summary

- In 1875 the “Mississippi Plan” used violence and fraud to restore White Democratic rule, about 50 Black Mississippians were killed, and Grant declined to intervene; the 1877 settlement withdrew the last federal troops.[^ch26-ms-plan-1875][^ch26-wikipedia-1877]
- The Supreme Court narrowed the Reconstruction amendments in the Civil Rights Cases (1883), upheld “separate but equal” in Plessy (1896), and validated Mississippi’s disfranchisement in Williams (1898).[^ch26-civil-rights-cases][^ch26-archives-plessy][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- Emancipation left most Black Southerners without land; sharecropping and the crop-lien system bound them to the land through debt, and convict leasing leased Black prisoners to private employers under deadly conditions.[^ch26-duke-sharecropping][^ch26-pbs-sharecropping][^ch26-eji-convict-leasing]
- The “New South” promised northern investment and industrial growth; Birmingham became a major iron and steel center, but the region’s mills and furnaces rested on cheap child and convict labor.[^ch26-yawp-grady][^ch26-eoa-birmingham][^ch26-bls-child-labor]
- Mississippi’s 1890 constitution used a poll tax and Sec. 244’s literacy and understanding test to remove Black voters; within a decade registered Black voters fell from more than 130,000 to fewer than 1,300.[^ch26-ms-1890-franchise][^ch26-ms-plan-1890]
- Other states followed, combining the poll tax, the literacy and understanding test, the grandfather clause, the property qualification, and the white primary; Louisiana’s Black registration fell from 130,344 in 1897 to 12,902 in 1898 and to 5,320 by 1900.[^ch26-64parishes-louisiana][^ch26-history-disfranchise]
- Ida B. Wells documented lynching in *Southern Horrors* (1892) and *A Red Record* (1895), arguing that more than 10,000 Black people had been killed since emancipation and that mob deaths in 1894 outnumbered legal executions.[^ch26-wells-southern-horrors][^ch26-wells-red-record]
- In Wilmington in 1898 a White-supremacy campaign destroyed a Fusion government by massacre and coup; the death toll is disputed, from the coroner’s fourteen to “as many as sixty” in the 2006 state report, all victims Black.[^ch26-ncdncr-wilmington][^ch26-eji-wilmington]
- Booker T. Washington’s 1895 Atlanta address made accommodation and industrial education the price of White tolerance; W. E. B. Du Bois answered in 1903 with the demand for “The right to vote. Civic equality. The education of youth according to ability.”[^ch26-washington-atlanta][^ch26-dubois-souls]
- The Niagara Movement (1905) and the NAACP (1909) turned the argument into organizations, with Wells among the NAACP’s forty founders and Du Bois editing *The Crisis*.[^ch26-nge-atlanta-compromise][^ch26-durocher-wells]

> *A closing note.* Between 1877 and 1910 the South’s leaders turned emancipation’s promise inside out. They redeemed the state by violence, leased the prisoners, kept the land, wrote exclusion into constitutions, upheld it in the courts, and enforced it with the rope. Yet the same decades produced Ida B. Wells’s record of the lynchings, Du Bois’s demand for the vote, and the organizations that carried the struggle into the next century. The chapter’s question — how racial domination became an institutional order — is answered by law, capital, violence, and the choices of people who refused to accept it.[^ch26-ms-plan-1890][^ch26-wells-red-record][^ch26-durocher-wells]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Chicago on July 9, 1896, where William Jennings Bryan told the Democratic National Convention that “you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.” The agrarian revolt that Bryan spoke for had once promised a politics that crossed the color line; Chapter 27 takes up what became of it, and of the republic’s turn to empire.[^ch26-check-ch27][^ch26-check-bridge]
