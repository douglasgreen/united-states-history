# Chapter 20. The Election of 1860 and Secession (1860–1861)

> “The issue before the country is the extinction of slavery. … The Southern States are now in the crisis of their fate; and, if we read aright the signs of the times, nothing is needed for our deliverance, but that the ball of revolution be set in motion.”[^ch20-nps-sc-secession]
>
> — *Charleston Mercury*, November 3, 1860, quoted in the National Park Service, “South Carolina Secession.”[^ch20-nps-sc-secession]

> “In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war. The Government will not assail you. You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors.”[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
>
> — Abraham Lincoln, “First Inaugural Address,” Washington, D.C., March 4, 1861.[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

> “In his account of the aftermath of Lincoln’s election, Freehling overturns the conventional picture of a state rushing headlong into disunion.”[^ch20-nytimes-freehling-review]
>
> — Eric Foner, review of William W. Freehling, *The Road to Disunion*, vol. 2: *Secessionists Triumphant, 1854–1861*, *The New York Times*, April 8, 2007.[^ch20-nytimes-freehling-review]

A secessionist editor called for revolution to save slavery. A president-elect told the seceded states that they, not he, would choose war. A historian said the conventional picture of the South rushing headlong out of the Union was wrong. The three disagree about whether secession was a revolutionary necessity, a choice the North could avoid provoking, or a contested and contingent decision made by divided communities. They also disagree about who decided: the slaveholders, the voters, or the politicians. The chapter’s question is how an election became a war.[^ch20-check-epigraphs]

On April 23, 1860, the Democratic National Convention opened in South Carolina Institute Hall in Charleston. The party that had managed the slavery question by postponing it for a generation met to write a platform and nominate a candidate. President James Buchanan, a Pennsylvania Democrat, was not seeking a second term. Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, the leading advocate of popular sovereignty, was the favorite. But the Deep South came to Charleston with a demand that Douglas could not meet: a federal slave code for the territories.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]

Alabama’s Democratic Party had instructed its delegates in January to “insist” on a plank protecting slavery in the territories and to withdraw if it were rejected. In February, Senator Jefferson Davis of Mississippi introduced resolutions calling for a federal code, and the Senate Democratic caucus passed them. When the platform committee reported, it offered two documents: a majority report that protected slavery in the territories and a minority report that endorsed popular sovereignty.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-essential-cwc-election]

William Lowndes Yancey of Alabama spoke for the majority. He invoked John Brown’s raid of October 1859 as proof that the North meant to dominate the South and emancipate its enslaved people. Douglas’s supporters had nearly passed their platform by the third day. On April 30, 50 Southern delegates walked out.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-aha-chronology]

The convention could not repair the breach. After 57 ballots, Douglas remained 50 votes short of the two-thirds majority, and the convention adjourned on May 3 without a nominee. The delegates who had walked out met at Richmond on June 11 and failed to nominate anyone. When the Democrats reconvened at Baltimore on June 18, Douglas won the rump convention; a separate convention nominated John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky. A party that had spent a decade telling the country that slavery could be kept off the national agenda now had two candidates and one question.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-check-scene]

How did an election become a war?

This chapter covers the year between the Democratic convention at Charleston in April 1860 and the secession of the Upper South in the weeks after Fort Sumter in April 1861. It asks how a presidential election became a war. It follows the four nomination contests and the election; the secession of the Lower South and the founding of the Confederacy; the failed winter compromises; the Sumter decision and bombardment; and the first mobilization. It does not re-narrate the 1850 settlement, Kansas, *Dred Scott*, or Harpers Ferry, which belong to Chapter 19, or the war’s campaigns and emancipation, which belong to Chapter 21.[^ch20-check-scope][^ch20-check-ch19][^ch20-check-ch21]

It draws on the party platforms and convention records, the state ordinances and declarations of causes, the census and election returns, the congressional compromise proposals, the federal and Confederate founding documents, and the scholarship on secession. It treats “secession,” “the Confederacy,” “the border states,” and what contemporaries called the Slave Power — the political influence they attributed to slaveholders — as terms that must be defined by the states and dates they name, not as synonyms. Enslaved people were the central subjects of a crisis that was about them. They had no vote in it, and within months they would begin to act on their own.[^ch20-check-scope][^ch20-check-race]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 follows the four conventions and four tickets and the election that Lincoln won without a single slave state. §2 turns to the secession of the Lower South, the declarations of causes, and the founding of the Confederacy at Montgomery. §3 examines the winter compromises and why they failed. §4 follows Lincoln’s inaugural and the Fort Sumter decision and bombardment, and the secession of the Upper South. §5 compares the two sides’ resources at the war’s outset and states their first war aims. §6 asks how historians explain secession and the coming of the war. The chapter begins in Charleston in April 1860, where a party split over whether to protect slavery in the territories.[^ch20-check-scope]

## 1. Four tickets

### 1.1 The Democratic split: Charleston and Baltimore

The Democratic Party broke in two over a federal slave code for the territories, and the split produced two sectional tickets that together could not stop Lincoln. The demand was the direct answer to Douglas’s Freeport Doctrine: if a territorial legislature could exclude slavery by withholding the police regulations that sustained it, slaveholders wanted the federal government to protect the institution instead. Chapter 19 traced that answer; here its consequence arrived at Charleston.[^ch20-encva-election][^ch20-nps-freeport-note]

The walkout did not itself cause secession, but it made the Democratic Party incapable of offering a single national candidate who could defeat Lincoln and reassure the South at once. The delegates who left Charleston met at Richmond on June 11 and accomplished nothing. The main convention reconvened at Baltimore on June 18 and nominated Douglas with Herschel V. Johnson of Georgia. A rival Baltimore convention nominated Breckinridge with Joseph Lane of Oregon. Each candidate claimed the party’s name and its history; neither could speak for the whole.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]

For the first time, the country’s oldest party could not present a national choice. The rupture made a Republican plurality in the electoral college more likely, because it divided the anti-Republican vote in the North and gave the Lower South a candidate of its own. The Democrats had spent a generation arguing that the slavery question could be managed by leaving it to the states and territories. At Charleston they discovered that the question could not be left alone inside their own party.[^ch20-harpweek-1860][^ch20-aha-chronology]

### 1.2 The Republicans at Chicago and the Constitutional Unionists at Baltimore

The Republicans nominated a moderate who could hold the free states together, and a new Constitutional Union Party nominated a border-state conservative who refused to take a position on slavery in the territories. The two conventions completed the four-way field the Democrats had begun to divide.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]

The Republican convention opened at Chicago on May 16, 1860, with William H. Seward the early favorite. Lincoln, seen as a moderate with fewer enemies, was nominated on the third ballot. The platform opposed slavery’s extension into the territories and called for a higher tariff, federal internal improvements, and a homestead act. It did not call for abolition in the states where slavery existed.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-harpweek-1860]

The Constitutional Union Party, made up mainly of conservative former Whigs and Know-Nothings, met at Baltimore on May 9, 1860, and nominated John Bell of Tennessee with Edward Everett. The delegates refused to adopt a platform and pledged themselves only to “the Union and the Constitution.” Their silence on slavery was the platform.[^ch20-aha-chronology]

The four tickets mapped the sections: Lincoln for the free states, Douglas for northern Democrats, Breckinridge for the Lower South, and Bell for the border and Upper South. No ticket except Douglas’s even tried to compete nationally. The election would be less a single national contest than four overlapping regional ones.[^ch20-harpweek-1860][^ch20-essential-cwc-election]

### 1.3 Table 1: the election of 1860

Lincoln won a decisive electoral majority while receiving well under half the popular vote, because his support was concentrated in the free states and split among three opponents. The result was sectional in a way that no previous presidential election had been.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]

Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and 1,867,198 reported popular votes, 39.8 percent. Douglas won 12 electoral votes and 1,379,434 votes, 29.4 percent. Breckinridge won 72 electoral votes and 854,248 votes, 18.2 percent. Bell won 39 electoral votes and 591,658 votes, 12.6 percent. The electoral college total was 303, with a majority of 152.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]

Table 1 shows the four-way result that made Lincoln president without a single slave state. The popular-vote total was 4,692,538. Lincoln carried 17 of the 33 states outright and four of New Jersey’s seven electors, and he won no state in which slavery was legal. Douglas carried Missouri and three of New Jersey’s electors. Breckinridge carried the seven Lower South states, Arkansas, North Carolina, Delaware, and Maryland. Bell carried Virginia, Kentucky, and Tennessee.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860][^ch20-app-election-1860]

### 1.4 The meaning of a sectional victory

Lincoln’s victory was a majority of the electoral college and a minority of the popular vote, won entirely in the free states — and the South read it as the capture of the federal government by an antislavery party. The campaign had made the stakes visible.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-encva-election]

The Republicans staged theatrical “Wide-Awake” torchlight parades, most spectacularly in New York City on October 5, 1860. Douglas campaigned in person, telling a Norfolk crowd on August 25 that Lincoln’s election would not justify secession and that the federal government had the right to use force to preserve the Union. Southern leaders had said openly that a Republican victory would mean disunion. The election did not cause secession by itself; it supplied the event the Lower South’s secessionists had said they would treat as the point of no return.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-constitution-center-secession]

On November 6, 1860, the republic elected a president who had won no state in which slavery was legal — the states where nearly four million enslaved people lived. Enslaved people and free Black Americans had no vote in the election, but they had a stake in its outcome that the candidates did not share. The question of whether the slave states would remain became the question of whether the Union could be held without war.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-check-race]

It separates the states Lincoln carried from the states that seceded before Fort Sumter and the states that seceded after the call for troops, and it distinguishes the slave states that remained in the Union. The dates matter: a map that shows “the Confederacy” as a single fact conceals the sequence by which it was made.[^ch20-check-map][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]

## 2. Lower South secession

### 2.1 South Carolina’s ordinance and declaration

South Carolina seceded first, on December 20, 1860, and published a declaration of causes that named the North’s hostility to slavery as the reason. The state’s convention opened at Columbia on December 17. On December 20, its 169 delegates, meeting in Charleston, voted 169 to 0 to dissolve the union between South Carolina and the other states. One hundred and fifty-three of the 169 delegates held slaves in 1860. The vote was unanimous in the convention; it was not a popular referendum.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-battlefields-secession-acts][^ch20-nps-sc-secession]

Four days later, the convention adopted a “Declaration of the Immediate Causes which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina from the Federal Union.” The declaration argued that the free states had “denounced as sinful the institution of slavery” and had “encouraged and assisted thousands of our slaves to leave their homes; and those who remain, have been incited by emissaries, books and pictures to servile insurrection.” It then named the election: “A geographical line has been drawn across the Union, and all the States north of that line have united in the election of a man to the high office of President of the United States, whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery.”[^ch20-avalon-sc-declaration][^ch20-constitution-center-secession]

The declaration made the case in the language of a broken compact. The free states had failed to return fugitives, had nullified the Fugitive Slave Law, and had elected a sectional president. South Carolina claimed the right to resume the powers it had delegated. The argument was legal in form and proslavery in substance; the compact it described was one for the security of slavery.[^ch20-avalon-sc-declaration][^ch20-check-race]

South Carolina’s action made disunion a fact and emboldened secessionists in the other Lower South states. Federal property in Charleston Harbor, including Fort Sumter, became the immediate point of confrontation. The state’s declaration and ordinance were the first of the secession winter’s public arguments, and they set the terms for the others.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sc-secession]

### 2.2 In Focus: Mississippi’s declaration of causes

**In Focus.** Mississippi’s convention adopted “A Declaration of the Immediate Causes which Induce and Justify the Secession of the State of Mississippi from the Federal Union” on January 9, 1861, the day the state seceded. The document does not begin with an abstract theory of state sovereignty. It begins with property: “Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery,” it says, calling slavery “the greatest material interest of the world.” A blow at slavery, the convention warned, “is a blow at commerce and civilization.” The choice was submission or “the loss of property worth four billions of money.”[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration]

The declaration then lists the grievances that had brought Mississippi to this point: the Ordinance of 1787, the Missouri Compromise, the refusal to protect slavery in the territories, the nullification of the Fugitive Slave Law, and the election of a president “hostile to slavery.” Its racial language is explicit. It says that “none but the black race can bear exposure to the tropical sun” and treats “negro equality” as an attack on the South. The compact theory is there, but the compact it describes is one for the security of slavery. The declaration is a public argument, not a transcript of every White Mississippian’s motives; it cannot prove that secession was universally popular. It can prove what the state’s leaders told the world they were doing.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-check-focus][^ch20-check-race]

### 2.3 The Lower South secedes

Between January 9 and February 1, 1861, six more Lower South states followed South Carolina out of the Union, each through a convention and an ordinance of secession. Mississippi seceded on January 9, Florida on January 10, Alabama on January 11, Georgia on January 19, Louisiana on January 26, and Texas on February 1. Georgia published a declaration of causes that accused the “non-slave-holding confederate States” of hostility to “African slavery.”[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-aha-chronology]

Secession proceeded through state conventions and votes, not a single plebiscite. The conventions’ declarations and the secession commissioners’ speeches made the protection of slavery and racial subordination the leading public argument. The commissioners carried the message into the Upper South and border states, where the decision was still open. Their language was explicit about race as well as property.[^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-nytimes-dew-review]

By February 1, seven states had left the Union and controlled most federal property within their borders. The remaining slave states of the Upper South and border had not yet decided, and their decision would depend on events in Charleston Harbor. The Lower South had acted before Lincoln took office; the Upper South would act after he called for troops.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]

The seven states that seceded first were the states of the Lower South. Their conventions did not wait for a compromise or for an overt act; they treated the election itself as the act. That timing is the chapter’s basic chronology, and it is also an argument about how the secessionists understood the election.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-check-scope]

### 2.4 The Confederate States of America at Montgomery

The seceded states built a new nation at Montgomery, Alabama, with a constitution that protected slavery more explicitly than the United States Constitution had, and elected Jefferson Davis and Alexander H. Stephens to lead it. Delegates from the seven states opened their convention at Montgomery on February 4, 1861. They adopted a provisional constitution on February 8, elected Davis provisional president and Stephens vice-president on February 9, and inaugurated Davis on February 18. A permanent constitution followed on March 11.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-davis-first-inaugural]

The Confederate Constitution denied Congress the power to pass any law “denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves.” It required the Confederate government and the territorial governments to recognize and protect slavery in any territory. It guaranteed slaveholders the right of transit and sojourn in any Confederate state “with their slaves and other property,” and it retained the Three-Fifths Clause for representation. The protections were chiefly limits on Congress and guarantees in the territories; the Constitution did not expressly bar a state from abolishing slavery.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution]

The preamble announced the theory: “We, the people of the Confederate States, each State acting in its sovereign and independent character.” Davis’s inaugural the month before had cast secession as a defense of the consent of the governed and the states’ sovereignty. He insisted that the act was not a revolution: “it is by abuse of language that their act has been denominated a revolution.”[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-davis-first-inaugural]

Stephens made the foundation explicit in Savannah on March 21, 1861. “Our new government is founded upon exactly the opposite idea,” he said. “Its foundations are laid, its corner-stone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery subordination to the superior race is his natural and normal condition.” The speech described the new nation as the first government in history based on that “great physical, philosophical, and moral truth.”[^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]

The Confederate founding is the chapter’s clearest evidence for slaveholding state-building. The new nation wrote slavery’s protection into its fundamental law and described racial subordination as its foundation. It also claimed the language of consent and self-government while denying both to the enslaved people within its borders. That contradiction was not incidental to the Confederate project; it was the project.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-check-race]

### 2.5 Federal property, the *Star of the West*, and the commissioners

Secession immediately became a contest over federal property and a diplomatic offensive aimed at the still-undecided Upper South. Major Robert Anderson moved his small garrison from Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter on December 26, 1860, which South Carolinians treated as a violation of the understanding with President Buchanan. On January 9, 1861, Southern guns fired on the *Star of the West* as it tried to resupply the fort; the ship withdrew. State forces seized federal forts, arsenals, and property across the Lower South. By the end of December 1860, Fort Sumter was the last federal property in Charleston Harbor still held by the United States.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

Five Deep South states sent commissioners into the Upper South and border states to argue for disunion. Their message, as Charles B. Dew reconstructed it, warned of “servile insurrection” and defended slavery as “ordained by God and sanctioned by humanity.” They described the Confederacy as a republic whose “chief cornerstone” was “African inequality and subordination, and the equality of white men.”[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review]

The commissioners’ words and the state declarations supply the evidence for the chapter’s debate. The secessionists argued their cause in terms of slavery and race, not in the abstract language their descendants would later prefer. That does not make every White Southerner a secessionist or every secessionist a spokesman for a unanimous South. It does establish what the movement’s public voice said.[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-check-race]

The contest over the forts made the constitutional crisis physical. A state could declare itself out of the Union; it still had to decide whether to fire on a federal garrison. The seceded states had begun to answer that question before Lincoln took office. The remaining question was what the federal government would do.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

## 3. Failed compromise

### 3.1 Buchanan’s paralysis and the secession of the government

President Buchanan condemned secession but denied that he had the power to coerce a state, so the federal government drifted while its own officials resigned and the seceded states took its property. In his State of the Union message of December 4, 1860, Buchanan blamed the crisis on “the intemperate interference of the Northern people with the question of slavery,” condemned secession, and signaled that he would defend federal forts if attacked. The message pleased neither side.[^ch20-aha-chronology]

Secretary of the Treasury Howell Cobb of Georgia resigned on December 8, Secretary of State Lewis Cass of Michigan on December 12 over Buchanan’s refusal to reinforce Charleston’s forts, and Secretary of War John B. Floyd of Virginia on December 29 over Anderson’s move to Fort Sumter. The cabinet was breaking apart along with the party.[^ch20-aha-chronology]

With the executive unwilling to act, the winter’s decisions fell to Congress — which also could not agree — and to the seceded states, which acted. The federal government’s paralysis was not neutrality. It left the initiative to the states that had already left.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-senate-crittenden]

### 3.2 The Crittenden Compromise

Senator John J. Crittenden of Kentucky proposed constitutional amendments restoring the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific, and the proposal failed because the Republicans would not extend slavery or abandon the election’s verdict. Crittenden submitted his plan on December 18, 1860. Its central article would have prohibited slavery north of 36°30′ and recognized and protected it south of that line. Other articles would have barred Congress from abolishing slavery in places under its exclusive jurisdiction and in the District of Columbia, and from interfering with the interstate slave trade.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-battlefields-crittenden]

Referred to a special committee, the plan won support from some powerful senators and from members of the public who petitioned Congress, but Republicans such as Senator James Grimes of Iowa rejected it. It died in committee, and the Senate defeated it on January 16, 1861. The plan’s supporters believed that another territorial line might do what the line of 1820 had done: postpone the conflict by dividing the map.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-aha-chronology]

Crittenden’s plan would have reopened to slavery the territories the Republicans had just won an election to keep free. Its failure showed that the election’s result, not Senate maneuvering, was the boundary of what the North would concede. The Republicans were not abolitionists, but they had promised to stop slavery’s expansion, and they treated that promise as the meaning of their victory.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-check-ch19]

The strongest case for the plan was that it might have preserved the Union without war, as the settlements of 1820 and 1850 had preserved it for a time. The strongest case against it was that it asked the North to surrender the election’s central issue before the new president took office. The Senate chose the second course.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-battlefields-crittenden]

### 3.3 The Washington Peace Conference and the Corwin amendment

The last winter compromise efforts, a national peace conference and a proposed constitutional amendment protecting slavery in the states where it existed, failed to halt secession. On Virginia’s call, delegations from 21 states — 14 free and seven slaveholding — gathered in Washington beginning February 4, 1861, with former president John Tyler among them. The conference proposed a set of constitutional amendments on February 27, including the 36°30′ line and a bar on acquiring territory without slave-state consent. Congress took no action.[^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-ahc-peace-conference][^ch20-aha-chronology]

In a separate effort, Representative Thomas Corwin of Ohio proposed an amendment barring Congress from interfering with slavery in any state. The House passed it on February 28, 1861, with President-elect Lincoln’s support. It was ratified by only a few states and never took effect.[^ch20-capital-corwin][^ch20-aha-chronology]

Even the one Republican concession that could pass, a guarantee of slavery where it already existed, addressed the wrong grievance. The secessionists sought new guarantees for slavery’s expansion and protection, which the North would not give. The Corwin amendment would have protected slavery in the states; it would not have opened the territories.[^ch20-capital-corwin][^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-check-scope]

The peace conference and the Corwin amendment were the last attempts to settle the crisis by constitutional text. They failed because the text could not answer the question the election had decided. The seceded states were not waiting for another amendment; they were building a government.[^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-aha-chronology]

### 3.4 Why compromise failed

The winter compromises failed because they tried to settle by amendment what the election had settled by votes: whether slavery could expand into the territories. The Republicans would not extend slavery; the secessionists would not accept its containment. A compromise that gave the South the territories would have repealed the election’s result. A compromise that gave the North the territories would have left the secessionists where they began.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-check-ch19]

The strongest case for compromise was that a union saved on Crittenden’s or the peace conference’s terms might have preserved the peace for a time. Crittenden argued that the alternative was the destruction of the Union, and his plan had the backing of some senators and much of the public. The strongest case against was that the Republicans had just won an election on the promise to keep slavery out of the territories, and to concede the point would have been to surrender the election’s meaning and to admit that the secessionists could veto a lawful result by threatening to leave.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-avalon-peace-conference]

By the time Lincoln was inaugurated on March 4, 1861, seven states had seceded and armed themselves, and the only remaining question was what would happen at the two federal forts still in Southern waters: Sumter and Pickens. The compromises had failed, but the war had not begun. The next decision belonged to the new president and to the Confederate government at Montgomery.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

## 4. Sumter and the Upper South

### 4.1 Lincoln’s inauguration and the Sumter decision

Lincoln’s inaugural offered the South no new provocation but refused to recognize secession or surrender federal property. On March 4, 1861, he said he had “no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists,” but he declared secession illegal and the Union perpetual. He promised to “hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government,” and he told the seceded states, “In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war.”[^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

On March 5, Lincoln learned from Major Anderson that Fort Sumter must be re-supplied or abandoned within weeks. General Winfield Scott and most of the cabinet counseled evacuation, but Lincoln concluded that surrendering the fort would mean conceding federal sovereignty. On March 29 he ordered a relief expedition. On April 4 he sent word to Anderson, and on April 6 he informed South Carolina’s governor, Francis Pickens, that the government would send provisions and no troops if the ships were allowed to land.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

Lincoln’s choice shifted the decision to Jefferson Davis and the Confederate government at Montgomery. They could allow the resupply and accept a continuing federal presence, or fire on the fort and begin the war. The choice was theirs, but Lincoln had made it unavoidable. He had refused to surrender the fort without forcing them to take the first shot.[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

The inaugural and the relief decision together defined Lincoln’s position. He would not interfere with slavery where it existed, and he would not abandon a federal installation to a government he did not recognize. That position was conciliatory on slavery and firm on sovereignty. It left the secessionists with the choice between peace and war.[^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

### 4.2 The bombardment of Fort Sumter

On April 12–13, 1861, Confederate batteries under General P. G. T. Beauregard bombarded Fort Sumter, and Major Anderson surrendered after 34 hours of fighting. On April 10, Confederate Secretary of War LeRoy Walker authorized Beauregard to use force if the federal government tried to resupply the fort. On April 11 Anderson refused a demand to surrender. A final demand came in the early morning hours of April 12, and the bombardment began at 4:30 a.m.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-aha-chronology]

The fort was unable to reply effectively. Anderson surrendered at 2:30 p.m. on April 13 and evacuated the garrison the next day. The bombardment was the opening engagement of the Civil War. It gave Lincoln the provocation he needed to treat the secessionists as the aggressors, and it forced the Upper South and border states to choose.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

The first shot was not an accident of local command. It followed weeks of preparation, a formal demand, and a decision by the Confederate government to treat the resupply of a federal fort as an act of war. The Confederacy chose to fire. Lincoln chose to hold the fort. Both choices made the war’s beginning a matter of policy, not drift.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]

### 4.3 The call for volunteers and the Upper South

Lincoln’s call for 75,000 militia on April 15, 1861, turned the secession crisis into a war effort and drove four more Upper South states out of the Union. The proclamation called forth “the militia of the several States of the Union to the aggregate number of 75,000 in order to suppress said combinations and to cause the laws to be duly executed.” It named the seven seceded states and said the first service of the forces would probably be to “repossess the forts, places, and property which have been seized from the Union.” It also summoned Congress to meet on July 4, 1861.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80]

Virginia’s convention voted 88 to 55 for secession on April 17, 1861, and a popular referendum ratified it on May 23 by 132,201 to 37,451. Arkansas seceded on May 6, North Carolina on May 20, and Tennessee on June 8. Unionists in western Virginia began a separate movement that would become West Virginia. The Upper South had refused to leave before Sumter; the call for troops made the choice unavoidable.[^ch20-encva-virginia-ordinance][^ch20-lva-virginia][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-archives-west-virginia]

Lincoln’s call forced the Upper South to choose between furnishing troops against the Lower South and joining it, and most of the Upper South chose secession. In May 1861 the Confederate Congress voted to move the capital from Montgomery to Richmond, and the war’s geography took shape. The four border slave states — Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri — remained in the Union, but their loyalty was not guaranteed.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-nps-richmond-capital]

The secession of the Upper South transformed the war’s map. The Confederacy now stretched from Virginia to Texas, and the Union’s capital stood across the Potomac from a Confederate state. The first mobilization had produced the geography of the war that Chapter 21 will follow.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-check-ch21]

### 4.4 The border states and the shape of the coming war

Four slave states — Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri — remained in the Union, and holding them was central to any Union strategy. Maryland’s position around Washington, D.C., made it decisive. Kentucky and Missouri controlled the Ohio and Mississippi valleys. Their retention was never guaranteed and required both political and military effort.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-check-ch21]

The border states were not simply loyal. They were slave states with their own sectional conflicts, and their governments included both Unionists and secessionists. Lincoln’s early policy toward them was designed to keep them in the Union without provoking them into leaving. That policy belongs to the war’s political history, which Chapter 21 takes up.[^ch20-check-ch21][^ch20-check-race]

This chapter stops at the first mobilization. The campaigns, the border-state fighting, and the transformation of the war’s aims belong to Chapter 21. Here the story stops at the moment the war began.[^ch20-check-ch21][^ch20-check-scope]

## 5. Resources and initial war aims

### 5.1 Table 2: resources at the war’s outset

The two sides entered the war with sharply unequal populations, industrial capacity, and railroad networks, but the categories “Union” and “Confederacy” must be defined by the states counted rather than by an unexplained North–South shorthand. The 1860 census counted 31,443,321 people in the United States, including 3,953,760 enslaved people. The National Park Service gives approximately 23 million people in the states that stayed in the Union and approximately 9 million in the states of the Confederacy. It estimates about 3.5 million military-age males in the Union against about one million in the South.[^ch20-census-1860][^ch20-nps-industry-economy]

By 1860, 90 percent of the nation’s manufacturing output came from northern states. The South had about 29 percent of the railroad tracks and only 13 percent of the nation’s banks. About 84 percent of its population was still engaged in agriculture, against about 40 percent in the North. The North produced far more textiles, leather goods, pig iron, and firearms, and its farms were more mechanized.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy]

### 5.2 The first war aims

Lincoln’s first war aim was to suppress a rebellion and restore the Union, not to end slavery; the Confederacy’s aim was independence and the protection of slavery. The April 15 proclamation framed the conflict as the suppression of unlawful “combinations” obstructing federal law and the repossession of seized property. It promised to “avoid any devastation” and to interfere as little as possible with peaceful citizens. Preserving the Union, not emancipation, was its stated object.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80]

The Confederacy’s aim was independence and the security of the slaveholders’ republic. The Confederate Constitution protected slavery, and Stephens’s “Cornerstone Speech” made the new nation’s foundation the inequality of the races. For the Confederacy, the war was for independence and for the social order the new constitution had written into law.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]

The war’s aims were not fixed. Enslaved people began fleeing to Union lines, and the question of emancipation would transform the conflict within two years. That transformation belongs to Chapter 21, but its opening conditions belong here: a war for the Union, begun by a government that had promised not to touch slavery where it existed, and a Confederacy that had made slavery its cornerstone.[^ch20-check-ch21][^ch20-upenn-mccurry]

### 5.3 The measure: asymmetry and its limits

The Union’s advantages in population, industry, and railroads did not determine the war’s length. The Confederacy fought on the defensive, expected European help from cotton, and began with better military leadership. The North could field and supply far larger armies and replace losses more readily; its factories, banks, and rail network gave it a margin that grew with the war.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy]

On the eve of the war, cotton prices were at an all-time high, and Confederate leaders were confident that Britain and France, dependent on Southern cotton, would intervene or at least recognize the new nation. That confidence was a strategic bet, not a certainty. It rested on the belief that cotton was king and that the European powers would act accordingly.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy]

The Confederacy’s foundation rested on an enslaved population that it excluded from its politics and depended on for its economy. Stephanie McCurry argues that the slaveholders built “a modern proslavery and antidemocratic state dedicated to the proposition that all men were not created equal,” and that the enslaved majority and White women would become political actors the Confederacy had not reckoned with. That argument connects the battlefield to the household and the plantation.[^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-mccurry-book]

Table 2 measures power at the outset, not the outcome. The numbers are conditions, not a prediction. The Confederacy’s advantages in leadership, interior lines, and defensive war, and the Union’s need to conquer and occupy a vast territory, kept the contest open longer than the raw totals suggested. The war would be decided by politics, will, and the actions of the enslaved people whose labor the Confederacy depended on.[^ch20-nps-industry-economy][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The evidence supports several conclusions. Secession was undertaken to protect slavery. South Carolina, Mississippi, Georgia, and Texas published declarations of causes that named slavery and the election of Lincoln, and the secession commissioners preached the same message. The Confederate Constitution protected slavery, and Stephens called racial subordination its cornerstone.[^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]

The election was sectional. Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and no slave state. The three other tickets divided the vote in ways that made a Republican electoral majority possible. The result was not a national mandate for abolition; it was a majority in the free states and a rejection in the slave states.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]

The Confederate founding protected slavery. The Confederate Constitution denied Congress the power to impair “the right of property in negro slaves,” required the protection of slavery in the territories, and guaranteed transit with slaves. Stephens described racial inequality as the new government’s “corner-stone.”[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone]

The sequence is documented. Seven states seceded before Sumter and four after Lincoln’s call. The bombardment of April 12–13, 1861, was the first engagement. The Upper South’s decisions followed the call for troops, not the election alone.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

The costs fell unequally. The enslaved people whose labor produced the wealth of the slaveholding republic had no voice in the decision, and they and ordinary soldiers would bear the war’s heaviest costs. The chapter keeps that asymmetry central.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-check-race]

### 6.2 What is open

Was secession a revolution, a counterrevolution, or slaveholding state-building? The secessionists called it a revolution. It also functioned as a conservative counterrevolution to protect an unequal order, and it built a new slaveholders’ state. The chapter weighs all three without collapsing them.[^ch20-nps-sc-secession][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution]

Was secession popular? Lower South conventions moved quickly, but Freehling argues that disunion was contested and that the Upper South left only after Sumter. The conventions’ unanimity was not a referendum. The chapter distinguishes the secessionists’ confident words from the evidence about support.[^ch20-nytimes-freehling-review][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]

What started the war: the election, the failed compromise, or Sumter? Lincoln’s relief decision and Davis’s order to fire each shaped the timing. The chapter presents the choices of both governments, not only one.[^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook][^ch20-aha-chronology]

Who acted? Enslaved people and Unionists in the Upper South shaped events alongside the commissioners and cabinet officers. A story that reports only the politicians will misstate who decided what. The enslaved people who fled to Union lines in 1861 were already acting on the war’s meaning before emancipation became policy.[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-check-ch21]

Whose voices survive? Conventions, ordinances, platforms, and speeches document the politicians. The enslaved majority left few direct records, and no enslaved person voted or sat in the conventions. The chapter names that silence without speaking for the silenced.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which secession and the coming of the war were contingent in timing but structured by slavery. The secessionists’ own words name slavery as the cause. The contest over disunion in the Upper South and the sequence of choices at Sumter show that the outcome was not automatic.[^ch20-check-tables][^ch20-jhu-foner-forum]

The secessionists’ own argument is the strongest evidence for slavery’s centrality. The declarations of causes and the commissioners’ speeches make the slaveholders’ purpose explicit. The postwar “states’ rights” and “constitutional government” explanations were, as Dew writes, a way not “to come to grips with our own history at a time when honesty might have carried the day.”[^ch20-nytimes-dew-review][^ch20-battlefields-declarations]

The contingency and division position restores the South’s internal conflicts. Freehling’s account of the aftermath of Lincoln’s election “overturns the conventional picture of a state rushing headlong into disunion,” stressing the Lower South’s lead and the Upper South’s hesitation. That position restores contingency and internal division without denying slavery’s centrality. The state-building position connects secession to the Confederate state and to the enslaved as political actors. McCurry treats the Confederacy as a proslavery, antidemocratic project whose exclusion of the enslaved majority and White women produced a wartime reckoning.[^ch20-nytimes-freehling-review][^ch20-upenn-mccurry][^ch20-mccurry-book]

The synthesis for this chapter holds slavery’s centrality together with the contingency of the choices at Charleston, Montgomery, and Sumter. The modern consensus holds slavery central while debating popular support and contingency. The chapter presents the debate as overlapping positions, not teams, and tests each against the declarations, the ordinances, the votes, and the Sumter correspondence.[^ch20-jhu-foner-forum][^ch20-check-sources]

## Legacies

The election of 1860 did not make a war, but it made secession possible and secession made a war likely. A four-way race produced a president who received no electoral votes from the slave states, and the Lower South answered with ordinances that cited slavery and the election as their cause. The winter’s compromises failed because they offered the South either less than it demanded or more than the North would give, and the Confederate attack on Fort Sumter turned a constitutional crisis into a shooting war.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-battlefields-declarations][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

The Confederate republic wrote slavery into its founding law and called racial inequality its cornerstone. The war it began would destroy that republic, and the enslaved people whose labor it relied on would help decide the outcome. What began in 1860 as a question about the territories became, within two years, a question about whether the nation could endure half slave and half free at all.[^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution][^ch20-battlefields-cornerstone][^ch20-check-ch21]

The chapter’s lasting legacy is a question about legitimacy. A party of Union broke rather than accept a platform; a section left the Union rather than accept an election; a president-elect told the seceded states that the choice of war was theirs. The republic had decided that majorities governed, and a minority had decided it would not be governed against its central interest. The war settled the immediate question by force; the argument over the terms of the settlement is the subject of the chapters that follow.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-check-race]

## Summary

- The Democratic National Convention at Charleston split in April 1860 over a federal slave code for the territories; 50 Southern delegates walked out, and the party ended with two tickets, Douglas’s and Breckinridge’s.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-encva-election]
- The Republicans nominated Lincoln at Chicago in May, and the Constitutional Union Party nominated Bell at Baltimore in May; the four tickets mapped the sections.[^ch20-aha-chronology]
- On November 6, 1860, Lincoln won 180 electoral votes and 1,867,198 reported popular votes, about 39.8 percent, and won no slave state; Douglas won 12 electoral votes, Breckinridge 72, and Bell 39.[^ch20-app-election-1860][^ch20-archives-electoral-1860]
- South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, and six more Lower South states followed by February 1, 1861; the published declarations of causes named slavery and the election of Lincoln.[^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession][^ch20-battlefields-declarations]
- Mississippi’s declaration of causes of January 9, 1861, stated that the state’s “position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery,” “the greatest material interest of the world.”[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration]
- The seceded states formed the Confederate States of America at Montgomery in February 1861, with a constitution that protected slavery and with Jefferson Davis and Alexander H. Stephens as its leaders.[^ch20-aha-chronology][^ch20-avalon-csa-constitution]
- The Crittenden Compromise, the Washington Peace Conference, and the Corwin amendment all failed to stop secession; the Republicans would not extend slavery past the election’s verdict.[^ch20-senate-crittenden][^ch20-avalon-peace-conference][^ch20-capital-corwin]
- Lincoln’s inaugural refused to recognize secession; on March 29, 1861, he ordered a relief expedition to Fort Sumter, shifting the choice of war to the Confederacy.[^ch20-avalon-lincoln-first-inaugural][^ch20-nps-sumter-handbook]
- Confederate forces bombarded Fort Sumter on April 12–13, 1861; Major Anderson surrendered after 34 hours, and the Civil War had begun.[^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]
- Lincoln’s call for 75,000 militia on April 15, 1861, drove Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee out of the Union, while Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri stayed.[^ch20-app-proclamation-80][^ch20-nps-kennesaw-secession]
- The debate turns on whether secession was a revolution, a counterrevolution, slaveholding state-building, or a contingent political failure, and the honest answer holds slavery’s centrality together with the contingency of the choices at Charleston, Montgomery, and Sumter.[^ch20-check-tables][^ch20-jhu-foner-forum]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The year between Charleston and Sumter is the record of a republic discovering that it could not decide by election what it had avoided deciding for a generation. A party broke rather than agree on whether to nationalize slavery’s protection; a section left rather than accept a lawful result; a president-elect refused to surrender a fort and thereby forced his opponents to choose between peace and the first shot. The secessionists said plainly what they were doing, and their own declarations are the chapter’s central evidence. The war that followed would settle the immediate question by force and leave the meaning of the settlement to be fought over again.[^ch20-avalon-mississippi-declaration][^ch20-app-proclamation-80][^ch20-archives-fort-sumter]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at First Bull Run on July 21, 1861, where spectators rode out from Washington expecting a short and decided battle and a Confederate victory scattered the Union army back toward the capital. The chapter takes up the war’s campaigns, the enslaved people who fled to Union lines, and the transformation of a war for Union into a war against slavery. Chapter 21 takes up the Civil War, 1861–1863.[^ch20-check-ch21][^ch20-check-bridge]
