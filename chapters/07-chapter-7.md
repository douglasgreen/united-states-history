# Chapter 7. Colonial Society and Culture on the Eve of Empire (1692–1750)

> “The God that holds you over the Pit of Hell, much as one holds a Spider, or some loathsome Insect, over the Fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked…”
>
> — Jonathan Edwards, “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God,” sermon preached at Enfield, July 8, 1741 (Boston: S. Kneeland and T. Green, 1741)[^ch07-edwards-sinners]

> “It is a Principle among Printers that when Truth has fair Play, it will always prevail over Falsehood.”
>
> — Benjamin Franklin, *The Pennsylvania Gazette*, July 24, 1740[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

> “What historians call ‘the first Great Awakening’ can best be described as a revitalization of religious piety that swept through the American colonies between the 1730s and the 1770s.”
>
> — Christine Leigh Heyrman, “The First Great Awakening,” *Divining America* (National Humanities Center)[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

The three quotations disagree about how minds are changed. Edwards speaks of a God who holds the sinner over the fire. Franklin speaks as a printer, confident that truth will prevail when it is allowed fair play in public argument. Heyrman steps back to describe a movement that historians have named, dated, and argued over. Fear, persuasion, and the retrospective label are three different ways of explaining the same decades. Together they frame the chapter’s question: what connected these increasingly different colonies?[^ch07-check-epigraphs]

On July 8, 1741, Jonathan Edwards preached at Enfield, a town on the Connecticut River that Massachusetts then governed and Connecticut would annex in 1749.[^ch07-enfield-wiki] Edwards was minister at Northampton; his text at Enfield was Deuteronomy 32:35: “Their Foot shall slide in due Time.” The printed sermon that S. Kneeland and T. Green issued in Boston that year preserved the argument. God, Edwards said, held every unconverted person over the pit of hell “much as one holds a Spider, or some loathsome Insect, over the Fire.” Nothing in the sinner’s own power kept him out. “There is nothing that keeps wicked Men at any one Moment, out of Hell, but the meer Pleasure of GOD.” The sermon’s force lay in that reduction: the sinner hangs by a slender thread, and the flames are already flashing about it.[^ch07-edwards-sinners]

The printed title page described the occasion as “a Time of great Awakenings; at attended with remarkable Impressions on many of the Hearers.” That phrase was the printers’ claim, not a transcript of the congregation’s response. The familiar story that Edwards’s hearers groaned, wept, or cried out comes from later accounts; the chapter does not treat it as a record of the delivery. What survives is the printed sermon, and the printing is part of the history. Edwards had preached the sermon at Northampton before, and the Enfield occasion belonged to a revival already under way in the Connecticut Valley. The pamphlet carried the words to readers who never heard him speak, into the same market that carried newspapers, almanacs, and advertisements.[^ch07-sinners-wiki][^ch07-check-scene]

The question the chapter follows is not whether the sermon was terrifying. It is what connected these increasingly different colonies.

This chapter covers the colonial societies of British North America between 1692 and 1750: their staple economies, migrations, slave systems, revivals, households, and social strain. It draws on newspapers and printed sermons, merchant and court records, statutes, probate and church records, and the documents of the Salem trials. It treats “credit,” “household,” “revival,” and “deference” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter must establish. It uses historical scholarship for what the records cannot show, names the silences left by the archives of the poor, the enslaved, and women, and does not present the colonies as either one egalitarian society or a set of wholly separate worlds. Interdependence did not mean equality. The connections that made the colonies one Atlantic economy also made some colonists rich and others property.[^ch07-check-names][^ch07-nhc-awakening]

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 examines the staple economies, the Navigation system, and the credit chain that linked the colonies. §2 turns to population and migration. §3 follows enslaved labor to the rice coast and the Stono rising of 1739. §4 explains the revivals and the print culture that carried them. §5 turns to households, the law of coverture, and the Salem witch trials. §6 asks where social strain appeared in a supposedly deferential order, including the New York conspiracy of 1741. §7 weighs the debate over deference, class conflict, revivalism, and the colonial Enlightenment.

## 1. Staples, trade, and credit

### 1.1 The staple economies and their regions

The colonies grew more different from one another because they grew different crops for an Atlantic market. Tobacco shaped the Chesapeake. Rice and indigo shaped the Carolina lowcountry. Provisions, grain, and the carrying trade shaped the middle colonies and New England. No single colonial economy existed, but the staples, shipping, and credit tied the regions together through a shared imperial market. Map 7 shows the staple regions, the major ports, and the migration streams that carried people into the interior.[^ch07-check-map]

Tobacco made the Chesapeake a plantation society. Planters sold the crop through a consignment system: they shipped it to England, where a commission agent stored it, paid the duties, sold it, and used the proceeds as the planter directed. By about 1730, roughly 40 percent of Chesapeake tobacco moved through that system. The rest passed through factors who bought directly from planters and sold imported goods on credit from stores along the rivers. Either way, the planter depended on a merchant an ocean away and on a price he could not control.[^ch07-tobacco-encyclopediava]

Rice made the lowcountry something else. The crop demanded dikes, canals, and heavy seasonal labor, and it rested on the knowledge and labor of enslaved Africans. Rice work was often organized by tasks, tobacco work by gangs under close supervision. The lowcountry became a slave-majority region, distinct from the Chesapeake in its demography, its crop, and its law. Indigo added a second cash crop, encouraged by imperial subsidies, and deepened the same dependence on enslaved labor.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution][^ch07-check-sources]

The middle colonies and New England fed the plantations and the islands. They sold flour, meat, and ships’ stores, built and sailed ships, and carried goods between ports that produced no food of their own. The diversity of staples created a division of labor among colonies: the rice coast needed northern provisions, the northern ports needed southern markets, and English merchants financed both. The consequence was not a single colonial economy but an interdependent one. The colonies were growing apart in their social forms at the same moment they were growing more tightly bound in trade.[^ch07-check-credit]

### 1.2 The Navigation system and the empire of trade

The Navigation Acts did not simply impoverish the colonies, but they did channel colonial trade to serve the empire’s shipping and revenue. The first act passed during the Commonwealth in 1651, was re-enacted in the early 1660s, and was reinforced by a royal warrant of William III in 1696. The laws reserved colonial trade to British ships manned by British crews. They also enumerated the most valuable colonial products — tobacco and rice chief among them — and required that those goods be shipped first to Great Britain before re-export. Wheat and some other colonial staples were never covered. The acts created a common market for the British world and made the colonies both a market for English goods and a supply area for the imperial economy.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution]

How the system worked in practice is another matter. Enforcement was uneven, smuggling was common, and the acts were only one influence on colonial prosperity. Chesapeake planters whose sweet-scented tobacco went to England anyway could benefit from the protection and promotion the system offered. New England shippers and merchants, who had other markets in mind, often did not. Historians disagree about the balance of cost and benefit, and the chapter should present the dispute rather than settle it by assertion. What can be said is that by 1750 the colonies were integrated into an Atlantic economy that enriched some colonists, indebted others, and depended throughout on enslaved labor.[^ch07-check-credit]

### 1.3 Credit, money, and the chain of debt

Colonial growth ran on credit. Planters bought goods on credit against the next crop and often fell into debt to English merchants. Farmers and artisans borrowed from storekeepers. Colonial governments issued paper money to lubricate exchange when coins were scarce. Massachusetts issued the first colonial paper money in 1690, to pay for a military expedition, and other colonies followed with bills of credit, land banks, and legal-tender laws. Parliament later regulated colonial paper money through the Currency Acts of 1751 and 1764, restricting New England first and then the colonies to the south. Those restraints would become politically charged after the Seven Years’ War.[^ch07-clevelandfed-currency][^ch07-currency-wiki]

The credit system reached into ordinary households through debts, wage labor, and the accounts kept for servants and the enslaved. A fire, a failed crop, or a runaway could erase a family’s solvency. Franklin’s *Gazette* reported in April 1730 that a fire near Fishbourn’s Wharf had consumed the stores on the wharf and damaged several houses, with a loss supposed to be four or five thousand pounds. The report noted that the fire might have been stopped if the town had been provided with good engines, and that a subscription was already on foot to supply them. The report is a reminder that the same commercial public that organized a subscription could read a fire as a lesson in risk.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

Credit made the colonies interdependent while concentrating risk at the bottom. The merchant’s ledger became one of the era’s most important documents: it recorded who owed whom, which crop had been pledged, and which household stood behind the debt. The same ledger recorded the enslaved as property and the servant as a term of years. The chain of debt ran from the English merchant to the colonial storekeeper to the planter and the farmer, and at the end of the chain stood the people whose labor made the debt payable.

## 2. Population and migration

### 2.1 Growth by numbers and the limits of the numbers

The colonial population grew rapidly between 1700 and 1750, through both natural increase and immigration, and it grew more African. The standard estimates compiled from *Historical Statistics of the United States* put the total population of the future thirteen colonies at about 250,888 in 1700, 466,185 in 1720, 629,445 in 1730, 905,563 in 1740, and 1,170,760 in 1750. Those totals combine White and Black populations. They rest on colonial counts and estimates, not on a modern census, and they describe the thirteen colonies that would later rebel within their chartered bounds. They are estimates, not exact counts.[^ch07-population-hs]

The series is useful precisely because its limits are visible. A colony’s count might miss people who lived beyond the settled parishes, and the enslaved were often counted as property rather than as persons. The totals for 1750 are a compilation, not a single enumeration. The chapter uses them to describe a scale and a direction, not to pretend to a precision the records do not support.[^ch07-check-population]

### 2.2 Natural increase and immigration

Natural increase and immigration together drove growth, and the sources of immigration became less English and more Scots-Irish, German, and African. Scots-Irish Presbyterians from Ulster began arriving in large numbers after 1718, and German-speaking migrants from the Rhineland and neighboring lands entered chiefly through Philadelphia. About 7,500 Scots-Irish and Irish migrants reached Pennsylvania before 1740. Many moved through Philadelphia and then into the backcountry, where land was cheaper and the reach of established churches and gentry was shorter. Philadelphia grew into the largest colonial city, its population drawn from England, Ireland, Germany, Africa, and the Caribbean; by the Revolution it would hold more than 32,000 people.[^ch07-phila-immigration]

The forced migration of enslaved Africans continued and grew. The lowcountry and the Chesapeake received the largest numbers, and their presence reshaped the populations of those regions. The slave trade was not a separate stream that can be added to free migration: it was a traffic in people, organized by merchants and insurers, and it made the colonies more African as it made them more commercial. This section states the direction and scale of the traffic and leaves its law and its resistance to §3.[^ch07-check-population]

Migration made the colonies more plural and less British. By 1750 the people of any one colony were harder to describe in a single phrase: English and Welsh, Scots-Irish and German, Dutch and Swedish, free and enslaved, Native and African. The diversity was not a modern multiculturalism. It was a set of distinctions that the law used, and that the revivals sometimes crossed.[^ch07-phila-immigration][^ch07-check-names]

### 2.3 Mobility, land, and the frontier of settlement

Population growth pushed settlement westward into Indigenous homelands, and land hunger became a force that the next chapter will carry into the Ohio Country. Settlers moved from the older seaboard into the piedmont and the backcountry, where they pressed against Native nations and against the claims of colonial governments. The movement was a cause of conflict, not simply a backdrop. Colonial governments chartered land companies, issued grants, and encouraged settlement as a claim of sovereignty; Native nations treated the same land as their own and negotiated, resisted, or divided in response.[^ch07-check-ch8]

The frontier was not empty space waiting to be filled. It was a set of Indigenous homelands, treaty relations, and trade networks that the colonies had already changed and that the next war would change again. By 1750 the colonies were not only larger and more plural but pressed harder against Indigenous territory. That pressure set up the imperial war that Chapter 8 owns.

## 3. Continental slavery and Stono

### 3.1 The plantation orders of the mainland

By 1750 slavery was continental, and the enslaved population was growing. The racial order that Chapter 5 traced through statute had become the ordinary structure of labor in every colony. This book treats Native and African slavery as one developing labor regime without collapsing their distinct histories: Native people continued to be enslaved in households and on frontiers, while the African traffic grew into the main source of bound labor in the plantation colonies. The law of property in persons reached all of them.[^ch07-stono-wiki]

The regions differed. The Chesapeake and the lowcountry held the largest enslaved populations. In the Chesapeake, tobacco plantations and smaller farms held enslaved people in gangs and in households; in the lowcountry, rice plantations concentrated large numbers of enslaved Africans on task-organized estates. The middle colonies and New England held smaller numbers in households, workshops, and ports, where enslaved people worked as domestics, laborers, and skilled artisans. The institutions differed, but the principle did not: enslaved people were property, and their children inherited their condition.[^ch07-negro-act-text][^ch07-check-sources]

Enslaved people worked in tobacco and rice fields, as domestic servants, and as skilled artisans. They also resisted. Flight was the most common form of resistance, but there were work slowdowns, tool breaking, theft, and open revolt. The section shows the regime in operation, and it shows that the people held inside it made choices of their own.[^ch07-stono-wiki]

### 3.2 The Stono rising of 1739

On Sunday, September 9, 1739, a group of enslaved people rose near the Stono River in St. Paul’s Parish, South Carolina, less than twenty miles from Charlestown. It became the largest slave rebellion of the colonial South. The rising failed, but it terrified White colonists across British America.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-stono-wiki]

The course of the rising survives in a white official’s account and in a later family account of Cato, one of the leaders. The official wrote that “Some Angola Negroes assembled to the number of Twenty; and one who was called Jemmy was their Captain.” They broke into a store that sold firearms, armed themselves, and killed the two shopkeepers, Robert Bathurst and a man named Gibbs. They burned the house of a Mr. Godfrey and killed him, his daughter, and his son. They marched south along the road toward Georgia and Augustine, calling out “Liberty,” with colors displayed and two drums beating. By eleven in the morning their number had grown to about fifty. They killed between twenty and twenty-five White settlers before they stopped in a field near the Edisto River.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-stono-pbs]

A party of White pursuers caught them in the afternoon. The rebels fired two shots; the militia returned fire and brought down fourteen. By dusk about thirty of the rebels were dead and at least thirty had escaped. Most of the survivors were captured over the next month and executed, and the heads of several leaders were placed on posts as a warning. The numbers vary among the sources, and the chapter gives them as estimates, not as a count.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-check-dating]

The marchers had a destination as well as a grievance. Spain had issued a proclamation offering protection and freedom to enslaved people who reached St. Augustine, and small groups had already made the journey. Britain and Spain were moving toward open war, which was declared in October. In mid-August, a Charlestown newspaper announced a Security Act requiring White men to carry firearms to church on Sundays; the act took effect at the end of September. The rising came before the deadline. The destination was Spanish Florida, and the timing belonged to a world in which the empires were already contesting the loyalty of the enslaved.[^ch07-stono-pbs][^ch07-stono-wiki]

Stono showed that enslaved people would choose freedom and that the colony would answer with law. It is the hinge from the labor regime to the code of 1740.

### 3.3 The Negro Act of 1740 and the racial order

South Carolina’s law of May 10, 1740, usually called the Negro Act, codified the racial order that Stono had exposed. Its preamble declared that “the people commonly called negroes, Indians, mulattoes and mustizoes have been deemed absolute slaves, and the subjects of property in the hands of particular persons.” The enacting clause made all Negroes and Indians, except those already free, “absolute slaves,” decreed that they would “follow the condition of the mother,” and judged them “chattels personal” in the hands of their owners. The statute used the word “Negro” in its own title and text; the chapter reproduces the term only in the law’s name and in quotation, and it is a historical term, not a neutral description.[^ch07-negro-act-text]

The act restricted movement, assembly, writing, and manumission. Enslaved people could not leave their plantations without a ticket, could not assemble in groups, and could not use drums, horns, or other loud instruments that might call others together. They could not work for themselves for money, and no one was permitted to teach them to write. The law made it harder for enslavers to free the people they held. It answered a specific rising by generalizing suspicion, and it linked Blackness to bondage in statute across the region. It was the mainland counterpart to the codes Chapter 6 traced to 1713.[^ch07-negro-act-text][^ch07-negro-act-zinn][^ch07-stono-nhc]

Stono and the act of 1740 together show the two faces of the era’s slave society: the refusal to accept bondage and the determination to make bondage permanent in law. The rising was defeated; the order that defeated it was written into the statute book.

## 4. Revivals and print

### 4.1 The awakenings and the Atlantic revival

In the 1730s and 1740s a wave of evangelical revival moved through Britain and its colonies, cutting across denominations and dividing them. Heyrman describes it as a “revitalization of religious piety” that ran from the 1730s to the 1770s, part of a much wider Atlantic movement that also swept England, Scotland, and Germany. In those Protestant cultures, she writes, a new age of faith rose to counter the Enlightenment, trusting the heart rather than the head.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

The earliest revivals appeared among Presbyterians in the middle colonies, led by the Tennent family and their “Log College,” the seminary that became Princeton. The revival then spread to the Congregationalists and Baptists of New England. Preachers like Edwards used vivid images of hell to move hearers, and the sermons were often delivered extemporaneously, without a written text. The theology stressed the “new birth” and the conversion of the heart rather than the assent of the head.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

The awakenings created a common evangelical identity while splitting congregations and denominations into those who welcomed them and those who did not. Advocates became known as New Lights and opponents as Old Lights. In colonies where one denomination received state support, other churches lobbied legislatures for disestablishment. The revival was not a single event with a single meaning; it was a set of connected arguments about who had the authority to preach, who could testify, and what conversion looked like.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-fga-wiki]

### 4.2 Whitefield, itinerancy, and the making of a public

George Whitefield turned revival into a public event. An English Anglican priest allied with John and Charles Wesley in the evangelical movement that became Methodism, Whitefield made several trips across the Atlantic after 1739 and preached everywhere in the colonies, often outdoors when no building could hold the crowd. He was, in Heyrman’s phrase, the only public figure whose name was known to virtually all colonials.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

Whitefield landed at Lewes Town in Sussex County in the fall of 1739 and reached Philadelphia on Friday, November 2. On Thursday, November 8, he began to preach from the Court-House gallery; the *Gazette* reported it in its issue of November 15: “about six at Night, to near 6000 People before him in the Street, who stood in an awful Silence to hear him.” The report said the preaching continued every night until Sunday, and that Whitefield planned to preach in every English province in America before he returned to England. The crowd in the street was the revival’s most visible fact, and the newspaper was the means by which the fact traveled.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

Not everyone welcomed it. Moderate and conservative clergy objected to the emotionalism and especially to “itinerants” who traveled from community to community, preaching and often criticizing the local minister. Some objected still more when “some white women and African Americans shed their subordinate social status long enough to exhort religious gatherings.” The line is worth quoting because it names the social boundary that revival crossed. A movement that let women and African Americans speak in public disturbed the order of the household as well as the order of the church.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

The press and the man grew together. Franklin published Whitefield’s sermons and journals and advertised them in the *Gazette*. Whitefield also took up 5,000 acres on the forks of the Delaware for a “Negro School,” a scheme the *Gazette* carried in the same month as his farewell sermon, showing how revival, philanthropy, print, and slavery met in one paper. The awakenings created new audiences and new divisions at once, and the links they forged ran through the press as much as the pulpit.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

### 4.3 In Focus: one issue of the *Pennsylvania Gazette*

**In Focus.** The *Pennsylvania Gazette* of November 15, 1739, carried a report in its own voice: “On Thursday last, the Rev. Mr. Whitefield began to preach from the Court-House Gallery in this City, about six at Night, to near 6000 People before him in the Street, who stood in an awful Silence to hear him.” The sentence is a piece of reporting, but it is also an advertisement for a religious event and for the printer who sold the account. The same paper, in other weeks, announced the forthcoming publication of Whitefield’s sermons and journals, reported the closing of a dancing school after his preaching, and reported the farewell sermon that raised money for an orphan house in Georgia. In other issues, the *Gazette* offered an enslaved woman and her two children for sale, reported a Philadelphia man convicted of killing his enslaved boy, and printed Richard Leadame’s warning that his wife had run him into debt. Read together, these columns are not separate departments of life. The paper that carried the revival carried the slave sale and the household quarrel in the same typography. Print connected the colonies through commerce as much as through ideas. The issue number and page still need to be confirmed against the digitized run, and the dates of the surrounding items must be kept distinct: the box reads one issue, not a composite week.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc][^ch07-check-focus]

## 5. Households, coverture, and Salem

### 5.1 The household as an economic and legal unit

The household, not the individual, was the basic unit of production, labor, and authority in the colonial order. Farms, shops, and plantations ran on family labor, servants, and enslaved people under the legal headship of the householder. The household was also a governing institution: it set the terms of work, inheritance, apprenticeship, and punishment for the people inside it.[^ch07-check-sources]

Women’s labor was essential to the household economy even when it was not counted in wages. Women produced food, textiles, and dairy goods; they nursed the sick, managed servants and enslaved people, and kept the accounts that made a farm or shop viable. Midwives and healers held knowledge and authority that the law did not register. Their work appears in probate inventories and account books, but rarely in the formal categories of trade.[^ch07-check-sources]

Servants and enslaved people lived inside the household and under the same authority, which tied the family to the labor regime of §3. The household was an institution of power as well as affection. Its head could command, correct, and dispose of the labor of dependents, and the law gave him the authority to do so. The household was the place where credit, labor, and gender met.[^ch07-check-sources]

### 5.2 Coverture and the law of women

English common law made a married woman a *feme covert*, her legal existence merged into her husband’s, while an unmarried woman remained a *feme sole* who could own property and make contracts. Under coverture a married woman generally could not hold property, sign contracts, or keep wages in her own name. The doctrine was inherited by the American colonies as part of English law, and it structured what women could own, sue for, and keep.[^ch07-coverture-wiki]

The rule was unevenly enforced. Widows, women who worked in trades, and some married women acted beyond it, and the law itself created exceptions. Coverture was a legal framework and a lived negotiation, not an absolute. The gap between the doctrine and the practice is one of the places where the historian can see households making their own arrangements inside the law.[^ch07-coverture-nwhm]

The *Gazette* shows the doctrine in daily operation. In December 1742 a Philadelphia man named Richard Leadame printed a notice that his wife Mary had “misused her said Husband, and doth run him in debt unnecessarily,” and warned all persons against trusting her on his account. A week later he published a retraction: “I acknowledge I had no Reason to do what I have done: For what I did was entirely thro’ others’ Persuasions, and my own Passion.” The exchange shows how credit, reputation, and marital authority could be argued in a newspaper, and how a husband’s control over his wife’s debts was itself a public claim.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

Coverture made marriage a transfer of legal capacity, and it gave the household its legal shape. Its reform would wait for the married women’s property acts of the nineteenth century.[^ch07-coverture-wiki]

### 5.3 Salem, 1692

The Salem witch trials of 1692 grew from local conflict and religious fear and ended with the execution of accused people on the testimony of the afflicted. Salem is the chapter’s case for social strain inside a godly community.[^ch07-salem-possessed-review]

In the winter of 1692, Betty Parris, the nine-year-old daughter of the minister Samuel Parris, and her eleven-year-old cousin Abigail Williams fell into fits. A doctor diagnosed bewitchment, and accusations spread through Salem Village and its neighbors. By the end of February, the first warrants had been issued for Tituba, Sarah Good, and Sarah Osborne. Tituba was an enslaved woman in the Parris household; the court records call her an “Indian Woman, servant,” and her origins remain uncertain. She was one of the first three accused and the only one of the three to survive the year. Her confession helped legitimize the accusations, but the popular picture of her as the “Black Witch of Salem” rests on tradition and fiction rather than on the court record, and the chapter says so.[^ch07-tituba-uva][^ch07-check-salem]

Nineteen people were hanged, fourteen women and five men. Giles Corey was pressed to death with stones for refusing to enter a plea, and at least five people died in prison. Between 150 and 200 people were jailed in the course of the year.[^ch07-salem-museum]

Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum’s *Salem Possessed* (1974) reads the trials out of a factional and economic conflict between a village faction tied to agriculture and the Putnam family and a faction tied to the mercantile town and the Porter family. The book is a model of local record work, but its focus on communal conflict can lose the religious, gendered, and individual forces that also mattered. Later work on gender and religion asks who was accused and why. The social explanation is one of the strongest, not the whole.[^ch07-salem-possessed-review]

Salem shows how a community under stress can turn fear into law.

## 6. Social strain

### 6.1 Poverty and the laboring sort

Colonial growth produced inequality and poverty, especially in the port cities, and the poor were a visible part of the era’s society rather than an exception to it. Wage labor, seafaring, and the casual poor of Philadelphia, New York, and Boston appear in poor-relief records, almshouse accounts, and the criminal courts. These records name people who owned little, moved often, and depended on the wages of a day or a voyage.[^ch07-check-strain]

Gary B. Nash’s *The Urban Crucible* (1979) argues that the seaport towns became more stratified over the eighteenth century and that the laboring classes developed a political consciousness before the Revolution. The argument connects social change to the coming Revolution. It is an argument to present, not a settled fact. Nash’s evidence comes chiefly from the seaports, and the towns were not the whole of colonial society. His work asks how inequality was experienced and organized, not whether every farmer or frontier settler shared the same politics.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

Inequality was one of the forces that made the colonial order. It complicates any claim that the colonies were a broadly egalitarian society, and it gives the lie to the idea that scarcity and hierarchy were confined to the South. The ports that carried the revival and the newspaper also carried the poor.

### 6.2 The New York conspiracy of 1741

In 1741 a series of fires in lower Manhattan touched off a panic that courts turned into the largest execution of enslaved and poor people in the northern colonies. The fires included one that destroyed Fort George. Nearly 200 people were arrested, including at least twenty Whites. The accused were tried under Daniel Horsmanden, a judge who turned the proceedings into something like a witch hunt. The case rested heavily on the testimony of Mary Burton, a sixteen-year-old Irish indentured servant who named people only when pressed and who had been promised a reward for her cooperation. The plot she described linked enslaved people with poor Whites and, later, with Catholic Spain.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]

Thirty to forty enslaved people were hanged or burned at the stake, and four Whites were hanged, including John Ury, accused of being an undercover Catholic priest and an agent of Spain, and John Hughson, Mary Burton’s master. Others were deported. Historians disagree about whether any plot existed. The economic competition between enslaved people engaged in commercial activity and working-class Whites, the anti-Catholic fever of the war with Spain, and the city’s memory of the 1712 revolt all shaped the panic. So did the court’s willingness to accept testimony that it had helped to produce.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]

Horsmanden published his *Journal of the Proceedings* in 1744. It is both the principal source for the trials and a prosecution brief. At the end of it he admitted that “the Veil is in some Measure still upon it!” The record that lets us see the trials is the record of the people who conducted them.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder]

The panic of 1741 shows that the fear of insurrection, not only insurrection itself, shaped colonial law and society. It links the northern ports to the slave societies of the South, and it belongs beside Stono as a case of resistance, fear, and law.

### 6.3 The strain inside the order

The phrase “deferential society” describes a norm more than a fact. Colonial politics and religion ran on the expectation that ordinary people would follow their betters, but migration, revival, and poverty repeatedly strained that expectation. The norm was real: it shaped sermons, elections, courtroom behavior, and the language of petition. It was also contested, and the contest is part of the history.[^ch07-bushman-king-people]

Revival preaching let laypeople and even women and African Americans exhort, which Heyrman notes drew objections precisely because it disturbed “subordinate social status.” Frontier mobility weakened the hold of established churches and gentry. Poverty made dependence visible and made the promise of deference harder to sustain. Together these pressures point away from a settled hierarchy and toward an order that had to be asserted, negotiated, and sometimes enforced.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

This account does not attribute a generic “deferential society” thesis to David Hackett Fischer. His *Albion’s Seed* (1989) argues regional folkways, not a general theory of deference, and the two should not be conflated. When the chapter names a supporting work for a deference claim, it uses Bushman or Sydnor and cites it directly.[^ch07-check-strain]

The era’s order was real and contested at once. That is the ground the Debate section takes up.

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

The colonies were interdependent and unequal. Staple trade, credit, migration, print, and coerced labor bound regions that grew more different, and the benefits were not shared equally.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]

Slavery was continental. Stono was the largest colonial-era slave rebellion in the South, and the Negro Act of 1740 answered it with harsher law. The law did not invent racial slavery, but it codified the assumption that Blackness and bondage were the same condition.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-negro-act-text]

The awakenings happened and they divided people. A transatlantic evangelical revival swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s and split denominations into New Lights and Old Lights.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-fga-wiki]

Salem ended in executions on contested evidence. Nineteen people were hanged and one was pressed to death, and the accused included an enslaved woman whose confession helped drive the trials.[^ch07-tituba-uva][^ch07-salem-museum]

The 1741 New York panic produced mass executions on doubtful proof. Dozens were executed, and modern historians question whether a plot existed.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]

### 7.2 What is open

How much the colonies were deferential is open. Was colonial society a hierarchy in which ordinary people deferred to their betters, or an arena of emerging class conflict and popular politics?[^ch07-bushman-king-people][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

What the revivals did politically is open. Did the awakenings democratize communication and challenge the elite, or were they a conservative continuation of older religion that historians have over-titled a “Great Awakening”? Heyrman frames the split between Nash and Rhys Isaac on one side and Heyrman, Christopher Jedrey, and Jon Butler on the other.[^ch07-nhc-awakening]

Whether there was a colonial Enlightenment at all is open. Does “Enlightenment” name a real movement in the colonies, or does it overstate the reach of a few printers and natural philosophers such as Franklin?[^ch07-check-sources]

The scale of the population and the slave trade is open. The totals are estimates built from incomplete counts, and the enslaved traffic is reconstructed from shipping and merchant records that undercount it.[^ch07-population-hs][^ch07-check-population]

What the archives cannot show is part of the problem. The poor, the enslaved, and women appear mainly in the records of those who governed, owned, or accused them. Salem reaches us through the interrogators, Stono through a white official and a family account, and the 1741 trials through a prosecutor’s journal. This history names these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which ordinary colonists, enslaved people, and Native nations made their own decisions inside structures they did not control. Edwards, Franklin, Whitefield, Tituba, the Stono marchers, and the accused of 1741 were actors, not symbols.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-stono-nhc]

Richard Bushman’s *King and People in Provincial Massachusetts* shows that monarchy was a whole political culture of deference, not just a form of government. It explains how authority worked and why ordinary people might have experienced hierarchy as legitimate. It can understate conflict from below, and it should not be stretched into a general theory of colonial society.[^ch07-bushman-king-people]

Nash’s *Urban Crucible* and Rhys Isaac’s *The Transformation of Virginia* argue that revival and economic change let humbler people challenge their betters. Critics such as Butler, Heyrman, and Jedrey read the revivals as less transformative, and Butler went so far as to call the “Great Awakening” an interpretive fiction.[^ch07-nash-urban-crucible][^ch07-nhc-awakening]

Read together, the positions say that colonial society was ordered by hierarchy and credit, strained by migration, revival, and poverty, and contested by the people the order excluded. The best history refuses both the story of a harmonious provincial society and the story of an early class revolution, and it refuses to let either the elite or the crowd stand in for everyone.[^ch07-bushman-king-people][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]

## Legacies

The provincial order of 1750 became the raw material of the Revolution and the early republic. The credit chains, staple economies, and print networks that bound the colonies also gave them the grievances and the publics that would turn imperial reform into resistance. Franklin’s press and Whitefield’s revivals showed that a shared audience could be mobilized; within a generation such audiences would be mobilized for politics.[^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc][^ch07-nhc-awakening]

Slavery and its law outlived the empire that made them. Stono and the Negro Act of 1740 hardened the racial order of the lowcountry, and the enslaved population that grew through the eighteenth century would become the central fact of the nineteenth-century republic.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-negro-act-text]

Revival and reform left a durable inheritance. Evangelical religion became a permanent feature of American life, and the abolition and rights movements of later centuries would borrow its language and its networks. Coverture, meanwhile, survived the Revolution and was dismantled only by the married women’s property acts of the nineteenth century, a reminder that the era’s settlements changed some social terms and left others intact.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-coverture-wiki]

Salem and New York 1741 entered memory as warnings about fear and law. The trials became a byword for injustice, and the executions of 1741 remained for a long time largely outside the story the nation told about itself. The legacies are double: a society that built a public argument and a revival also built a racial order and a habit of executing the accused.[^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki][^ch07-salem-possessed-review]

## Optional threads

- **The Atlantic frame.** Whitefield’s transatlantic tours, the migration streams from Ulster and the Rhineland, the rice coast’s African knowledge, and the imperial credit system all placed the colonies inside an Atlantic world they did not control. The awakenings were British and German as much as American.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-phila-immigration][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- **Archives and silences.** The poor appear in relief rolls, the enslaved in inventories and court minutes, and women in the records that governed them; the accused at Salem and the rebels at Stono reach us through the people who tried and killed them. The chapter names these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch07-stono-nhc][^ch07-tituba-uva][^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 5 supplies the statutory making of hereditary slavery; Chapter 6 supplies the wars and the imperial order; Chapter 8 owns the Seven Years’ War and begins with Washington’s 1753 mission. Later chapters carry revival, print, and racial slavery into the Revolution and the republic.[^ch07-check-ch8]

## Summary

- The colonies grew more different through staple economies — tobacco, rice, provisions, and shipping — yet were bound together by trade and credit.[^ch07-tobacco-encyclopediava][^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution]
- The Navigation Acts channeled colonial trade to the empire, with disputed costs and benefits, and colonial paper money was later restrained by the Currency Acts of 1751 and 1764.[^ch07-navigation-ouramericanrevolution][^ch07-currency-wiki]
- The population of the future thirteen colonies rose from about 250,888 in 1700 to about 1,170,760 in 1750, an estimate that combines White and Black populations.[^ch07-population-hs]
- Scots-Irish, German, and African migration made the colonies more plural and less British, and Philadelphia grew into the largest colonial city by the Revolution.[^ch07-phila-immigration]
- On September 9, 1739, enslaved people rose near the Stono River in South Carolina, the largest colonial-era slave rebellion in the South, and were defeated near the Edisto.[^ch07-stono-wiki][^ch07-stono-nhc]
- South Carolina’s Negro Act of 1740 answered Stono with restrictions on writing, assembly, movement, and manumission.[^ch07-negro-act-text]
- A transatlantic evangelical revival swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s, and Whitefield drew thousands when he preached in Philadelphia in November 1739.[^ch07-nhc-awakening][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- The awakenings divided denominations into New Lights and Old Lights and became a public event carried by the press.[^ch07-fga-wiki][^ch07-pa-gazette-nhc]
- The household was the era’s basic unit of labor and authority, and coverture merged a married woman’s legal identity into her husband’s.[^ch07-coverture-wiki]
- The Salem witch trials of 1692 ended with nineteen hanged and Giles Corey pressed to death, on evidence that grew from local conflict and fear.[^ch07-salem-museum][^ch07-salem-possessed-review]
- The New York conspiracy of 1741 led to the execution of dozens on testimony many historians doubt.[^ch07-nyc-1741-gilder][^ch07-nyc-1741-wiki]
- Historians disagree about whether deference, class conflict, revival, or print best explains colonial society, but they agree that the order was contested and unequal.[^ch07-bushman-king-people][^ch07-nash-urban-crucible]
