## Epigraphs

> “As to the summons you send me to retire, I do not think myself obliged to obey it.”[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]

> “… it is just and reasonable, and essential to our Interest, and the Security of our Colonies, that the several Nations or Tribes of Indians with whom We are connected, and who live under our Protection, should not be molested or disturbed in the Possession of such Parts of Our Dominions and Territories as, not having been ceded to or purchased by Us, are reserved to them. or any of them, as their Hunting Grounds.”[^ch08-proc-avalon]

> “Coming to grips with the Seven Years’ War as an event that decisively shaped American history, as well as the histories of Europe and the Atlantic world in general, may … help us to begin to understand the colonial period as something more than a quaint mezzotint prelude to our national history.”[^ch08-anderson-past]

- **Why these three.** The French commandant Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre answers the Virginia governor’s demand with the flat refusal of a rival empire. The Proclamation of 1763 speaks in the paternal voice of a sovereign that claims to protect the interior nations whose lands it also claims. Anderson steps back and argues that the war was a hinge of American history, not a decorative preface to the Revolution. The three disagree about who owned the interior, whether British power protected or threatened the nations there, and whether the war’s meaning is imperial, Indigenous, or simply the prelude to independence.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Saint-Pierre’s reply is quoted from the American Battlefield Trust’s account, which summarizes Washington’s report; it must be collated against the printed journal or the French letter itself before it is set in type. The Proclamation is quoted from the Avalon Project’s transcription of the London printing and must be checked against an authoritative edition for punctuation and capitalization. Anderson’s sentence is quoted from a magazine appreciation that reproduces it from his introduction, so the book’s own text must be checked before use.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-anderson-past][^ch08-check-epigraphs]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the Ohio Country belonged to France, that the Proclamation protected Native nations in practice, or that the war caused the American Revolution by itself. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The mission.** In October 1753, George Washington, a 21-year-old major in the Virginia militia, volunteered to carry a letter from Lieutenant Governor Robert Dinwiddie of Virginia to the French commander of the forts on the upper Ohio. Dinwiddie demanded that the French withdraw from territory that Virginia claimed. Washington left Williamsburg on October 31, 1753, and completed a round trip of more than 1,000 miles by horse, foot, canoe, and raft in about ten weeks, returning on January 16, 1754.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]
- **The party.** Washington traveled with Christopher Gist, a surveyor and explorer employed by the Ohio Company; Jacob Van Braam, a French interpreter; four traders and baggage-men; and various Native delegates and guards, including Tanacharison, the Seneca leader whom the English called the “Half-King.” The README forbids inventing departure companions beyond what the record names.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-check-scene]
- **The route.** Washington went by way of Fredericksburg and Will’s Creek (modern Cumberland, Maryland), reached the Native village of Logstown in late November, and traveled on to Venango (modern Franklin, Pennsylvania), where the French had turned a British trading post into an outpost. There he met the French officer Philippe-Thomas Chabert de Joncaire on December 4, 1753, and was told to carry Dinwiddie’s letter farther, to Fort Le Boeuf.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-mountvernon-timeline]
- **The intelligence and the reply.** Washington’s report recorded the French officers’ talk. Joncaire told him that “it was their absolute Design to take Possession of the Ohio.” At Fort Le Boeuf, Washington met the regional commander, Saint-Pierre, delivered Dinwiddie’s letter, and inspected the fort’s cannon and canoes. Saint-Pierre’s answer was polite and firm: “as to the summons you send me to retire, I do not think myself obliged to obey it.” Washington left Fort Le Boeuf on December 16, 1753.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]
- **The crossing and the journal.** On December 29, Washington and Gist tried to cross the Allegheny River on a makeshift raft; Washington fell into the icy water and nearly drowned. He reached Williamsburg on January 16, 1754, and Dinwiddie had his report printed. *The Journal of Major George Washington* was reprinted in colonial newspapers and issued in London the same year, with materials Washington supplied for a map.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-wash-journal-zea]
- **The weather.** Washington’s journal records “excessive rains and vast quantity of snow” during the journey; that phrase is the documented weather, not a continuous storm invented for atmosphere.[^ch08-washingtonstrail][^ch08-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A documented mission, not a tableau.** The scene should open in the winter of 1753–54, move through the documented stops, and rest on the crossing at the Allegheny and on the printed journal that carried Washington’s account to readers. Every action and place named should be one the record supports.
- **A young man on someone else’s errand.** Washington was 21, had no diplomatic experience and spoke neither French nor any Native language, and was connected by family and ambition to the land speculators whose claims he carried. He served Dinwiddie, who was himself an investor in the Ohio Company. The scene should show the mission as the work of empire and of private investment at once.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-ohio-company-wiki]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: Why did victory destabilize Britain’s empire?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that one empire was right or that the French were simply expelled. Military success changed debts, land claims, and alliances without resolving the political terms of imperial rule; the war settled who would lose and left open who would rule.
- **The widening.** The scene sits in the Ohio Country in 1753. The framing paragraph follows and sends the reader to Quebec and Montreal, to the villages of the Shawnee and Delaware, to the colonial assemblies, and to Paris and London, all inside one contest for a continent.[^ch08-newfrance-wiki][^ch08-state-paris]

## 1. New France’s networks

### 1.1 The fur trade and the alliance system

- **The claim.** New France was not a colony of dense settlement but a network of forts, missions, and trading posts bound to Indigenous nations by trade, kinship, and diplomacy. Its power rested less on people than on relationships.
- **The mechanism.** French traders exchanged manufactured goods for furs, and French officers distributed gifts and medals to allied leaders. In the Great Lakes region and the pays d’en haut, this produced what the historian Richard White has called a “middle ground”: a space in which neither Europeans nor Native nations could fully impose their will, and in which both accommodated each other’s customs.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The alliance.** France’s ability to govern a vast territory with few settlers depended on controlling waterways — the St. Lawrence, the Great Lakes, the Ohio, and the Mississippi — and on cultivating strong political and economic relationships with powerful nations. When those relationships held, a small population could project power far beyond its numbers.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The consequence.** New France’s strength and its fragility came from the same source. It could not defend the interior alone, and its Native allies had their own interests in the balance of empires.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground]

### 1.2 Governance, the seigneurial order, and the Catholic church

- **The claim.** New France organized its settler society around seigneurial land tenure, the Catholic church, and royal administration, a different order from the charter colonies to the south. The chapter should define the seigneurial system at first use.
- **The order.** Land was held in seigneuries, large grants whose holders owed services and dues to the crown and who in turn settled and rented plots to habitants. The church ran parishes, schools, and hospitals, and Catholic clergy held public authority. Government combined a royal governor and intendant with local militia captains.[^ch08-newfrance-wiki]
- **The population.** In 1754 New France’s population consisted of about 10,000 Acadians, 55,000 Canadiens, and about 4,000 settlers in upper and lower Louisiana; the mid-eighteenth-century total is often given between 75,000 and 90,000 colonists. These are estimates, not counts, and the chapter must define which region they cover.[^ch08-newfrance-wiki][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-check-population]
- **The consequence.** Settlement concentrated along the St. Lawrence, leaving a vast interior held by alliance rather than by population, which is exactly the structure that the war would test.[^ch08-newfrance-wiki]

### 1.3 The demographic imbalance and what it meant

- **The claim.** The British colonies outnumbered New France by roughly twenty to one in the 1750s, yet France held the interior for decades. Numbers alone did not decide the contest, and the chapter must explain why.
- **The figures.** The standard compiled estimates put the total population of the future thirteen colonies at about 1,170,760 in 1750 and 1,593,625 in 1760, combining White and Black populations. By 1750 the British colonies held about 1.5 million people, against New France’s tens of thousands; one widely used account says the British outnumbered the French almost twenty to one in 1754. These totals rest on colonial counts and estimates, not a modern census.[^ch08-pop-hs][^ch08-thirteen-wiki][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-check-population]
- **Why numbers did not settle the war.** France’s interior position, Indigenous alliances, and control of rivers offset British population, and British colonists were reluctant to fund and man distant campaigns. The chapter should present the imbalance as a problem the British had to solve rather than a guaranteed victory.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- **The consequence.** The comparison sets the chapter’s central puzzle: how a small colony nearly held a continent, and how the larger one eventually broke it. Table 1 presents the comparison.[^ch08-check-table1]

## 2. The Ohio Country and Indigenous brokers

### 2.1 A contested middle ground

- **The claim.** The Ohio Country was not an empty frontier between empires but a set of Indigenous homelands whose nations played the rivals against each other. The chapter must name the nations rather than call them “the Indians” collectively.
- **The nations and the stake.** The region between Lake Erie and the Ohio River held Mingo, Shawnee, Delaware (Lenape), and Wyandot communities, among others, many of them recent migrants pushed west by earlier colonial expansion. They traded with both empires and were courted by both.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-ohio-company-wiki]
- **The Haudenosaunee claim.** The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy claimed authority over the Ohio nations and dealt with the British through the Covenant Chain, so the contest for the interior was also a contest among Native powers. The chapter should present the Haudenosaunee claim as a claim, not as a settled fact.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]
- **The consequence.** Whoever won the favor of the Ohio nations could control the river, and the nations knew it. This is the ground on which Washington’s mission and the war’s first shots were fought.

### 2.2 The Ohio Company and Virginia’s land claims

- **The claim.** The war grew partly from a private land company’s ambition. Virginia’s claim to the Ohio Country was pressed by investors who expected to profit from the lands they claimed.
- **The company.** In 1748 Thomas Lee and George Washington’s half-brothers Lawrence and Augustine Washington organized the Ohio Company of Virginia, whose investors included Robert Dinwiddie himself. In 1749 the crown granted the company 200,000 acres on the Ohio, with another 300,000 to follow if it settled a hundred families and built a fort; the grant was rent- and tax-free for ten years.[^ch08-ohio-company-wiki][^ch08-mountvernon-timeline]
- **The exploration.** The company hired Christopher Gist in 1750 to explore and survey the Ohio Valley, and Gist’s journals described the country from the Alleghenies toward the Miami villages. In 1752 the company signed a treaty of friendship at Logstown with nations of the region, though Virginia and Pennsylvania continued to dispute the same lands.[^ch08-ohio-company-wiki][^ch08-mountvernon-timeline]
- **The consequence.** Dinwiddie’s demand that the French leave the Ohio was made on behalf of a colony and an investment at once. The chapter should make that double interest explicit without reducing the war to a land deal.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-ohio-company-wiki]

### 2.3 Tanacharison and the brokers

- **The claim.** The war’s opening depended on Native diplomats and warriors whose decisions were their own. Neither empire could act in the Ohio Country without them.
- **Tanacharison.** Tanacharison, a Seneca leader the English called the “Half-King,” had been sent by the Haudenosaunee Confederacy to speak for the Ohio nations in dealings with the empires. French expansion threatened Haudenosaunee influence, and a French commander’s insult — the Canadian officer Paul Marin de la Malgue reportedly threw down Tanacharison’s wampum and told him he would “tread under my feet all that oppose me” — turned him against the French.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The choice.** Tanacharison allied with the British because the alliance served his nations’ interests, not because he accepted British sovereignty. His presence on Washington’s 1753 journey and his warriors at Jumonville Glen show how much of the war’s course ran through Indigenous decisions.[^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry][^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should credit the brokers without romanticizing them or pretending that the records preserve their own voices fully. What survives is filtered through European reports.[^ch08-check-names]

### 2.4 The Céloron expedition and the French counter-claim

- **The claim.** France answered Virginia’s claim with its own, marked on the land. The rival claims were not settled by law but by forts and war.
- **The counter-claim.** In 1749 Pierre Joseph Céloron de Blainville set out from Montreal to mark the bounds of French claims, burying lead plates at the mouths of key rivers, including at the forks of the Ohio. In 1753 the French built a series of forts — Presque Isle, Le Boeuf, and Machault — to link Canada to Louisiana and block British expansion.[^ch08-mountvernon-timeline][^ch08-washingtonstrail]
- **The forks.** When Dinwiddie sent men to build a fort at the forks of the Ohio in early 1754, a larger French and Canadian force under Claude-Pierre Pécaudy de Contrecœur arrived in April, compelled the small British party to leave, tore down its works, and began Fort Duquesne.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The consequence.** By the spring of 1754 two empires were building forts on the same ground, and the question of who would fire first belonged to the next section.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]

## 3. Washington, Albany, and the war’s beginning

### 3.1 The mission’s violent sequel

- **The claim.** The war began not on a European battlefield but in the Ohio Country, in a chain of decisions taken by a colonial governor and a young officer without London’s direction.
- **The instructions.** In March 1754 Dinwiddie sent Washington back to the frontier with authority to restrain anyone obstructing Virginia’s works and, “in Case of resistance[,] to make Prisoners of or kill & destroy them.” The historian Fred Anderson describes those instructions, issued without the knowledge or direction of the British government, as “an invitation to start a war.”[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The fort and the fort.** Washington marched west with a small Virginia force and built Fort Necessity in the Great Meadows. The French held the forks and Fort Duquesne, and both sides sent small parties into the same disputed ground.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]

### 3.2 Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity

- **The claim.** The first bloodshed of the war came on May 28, 1754, when Washington’s party and Tanacharison’s warriors ambushed a French detachment near Jumonville Glen. The episode made a diplomatic incident into a war.
- **The skirmish.** Washington attacked the French encampment with about 40 soldiers and 12 of Tanacharison’s warriors, killing or wounding most of the detachment and taking 21 prisoners. Among the dead was the French officer Joseph Coulon de Villiers de Jumonville; the manner of his death is uncertain, and one account has Tanacharison killing him while a summons was being read.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The surrender.** On July 3, 1754, a larger French and Native force under Jumonville’s brother, Louis Coulon de Villiers, attacked Fort Necessity in heavy rain. Washington surrendered the next day. The surrender document, which Washington signed without reading French, used the word “assassinated” for Jumonville’s death, a charge Washington later insisted was a translation error. He was the only military surrender of his career.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- **The consequence.** News of the two fights reached London, and the British government decided to send an army the following year. What had begun as rival claims to a river valley was becoming an imperial war.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]

### 3.3 The Albany Congress and the Plan of Union

- **The claim.** While Washington fought in the Ohio Country, seven colonies met at Albany to secure the Haudenosaunee alliance and to consider a common government. The plan failed, but it revealed both the demand for cooperation and the colonies’ refusal to surrender authority.
- **The congress.** The Albany Congress met in June and July 1754, with commissioners from seven colonies, chiefly to repair a breakdown in negotiations between New York and the Mohawk nation and to set a common policy toward the Haudenosaunee.[^ch08-state-albany][^ch08-join-or-die]
- **The plan.** Benjamin Franklin proposed a general government with a crown-appointed President-General and a Grand Council chosen by the colonial assemblies, with power to treat with the nations and to raise forces — and, most significantly, to tax. The delegates adopted a version on July 10, 1754.[^ch08-albany-avalon][^ch08-albany-wiki]
- **The rejection.** The colonial assemblies thought the plan took too much of their independence, and British officials thought it took too much for the colonies. Franklin later wrote that the assemblies “were narrowly provincial in outlook, mutually jealous, and suspicious of any central taxing authority.” His *Join, or Die* woodcut, published in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* in May 1754, pictured the colonies as segments of a severed snake.[^ch08-albany-wiki][^ch08-join-or-die]
- **The consequence.** Albany showed that a shared danger could produce a proposal for union and that the proposal could still fail. The chapter should note that the plan resurfaced in later debates without treating it as the inevitable ancestor of the Constitution.[^ch08-state-albany][^ch08-albany-wiki]

### 3.4 1755 and the widening war

- **The claim.** In 1755 Britain sent a general and a plan of four offensives, and the most important of them ended in disaster. The war widened from a frontier quarrel into a contest of regular armies.
- **Braddock’s defeat.** Major General Edward Braddock led about 2,100 regulars and provincials toward Fort Duquesne. On July 9, 1755, French and Native forces ambushed his column near the Monongahela; Braddock was mortally wounded, more than 500 of his troops were killed, and the survivors fled. Washington, serving as an aide-de-camp, helped rally the retreat.[^ch08-braddock-wiki][^ch08-monongahela-mountvernon]
- **The wider war.** Britain also struck at sea. In June 1755 Admiral Edward Boscawen fired on the French ship *Alcide* and captured troop transports bound for Canada. France and Britain declared war on each other in the spring of 1756, marking the formal start of the Seven Years’ War, of which the American fighting was one theater.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- **The consequence.** The disasters of 1754–55 gave way to years of French success. The chapter should resist reading 1755 as the story’s ending; the war’s outcome was still open.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki]

## 4. Campaigns and colonial mobilization

### 4.1 French victories, 1756–57

- **The claim.** For the first years of the war the French and their allies held the advantage, and the British suffered a string of defeats that made the war’s outcome uncertain.
- **The pattern.** French regulars, Canadian militia, and Native allies used interior lines and river routes to strike at British frontier posts. In August 1757 a French force under Louis-Joseph de Montcalm besieged Fort William Henry at the southern end of Lake George, and its commander surrendered after several days of bombardment.[^ch08-fwh-wiki]
- **The massacre.** Montcalm’s Native allies violated the terms of surrender and attacked the departing British column, which had been deprived of ammunition, killing and capturing hundreds; the episode became one of the war’s most notorious incidents and poisoned British attitudes toward Native people.[^ch08-fwh-wiki]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the massacre as a specific failure of imperial control over diverse allies, not as evidence about Native warfare in general.[^ch08-fwh-wiki][^ch08-check-names]

### 4.2 Pitt’s strategy and British mobilization

- **The claim.** The war turned when William Pitt took direction of the British war effort and committed money and troops to North America on a new scale, while subsidizing allies in Europe and attacking France’s empire worldwide.
- **The strategy.** From 1758 Pitt’s ministry financed Prussia and others to keep French armies busy in Europe, expanded the Royal Navy, and dispatched large forces to North America. Britain’s ability to borrow and tax at home let it pay for a war the colonies could not fund alone.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki][^ch08-pitt-wiki]
- **The effect in North America.** A British and provincial expedition under John Forbes took Fort Duquesne in November 1758, and other forces captured Louisbourg and struck French posts on the frontier. Colonial assemblies raised provincial regiments, but they did so unevenly and on their own terms, which the chapter should show rather than treat as unified loyalty.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- **The consequence.** British mobilization changed the war’s scale. The colonies that helped win it would, within a generation, be asked to help pay for it, which sets up Chapter 9.[^ch08-state-taxation][^ch08-check-ch9]

### 4.3 The fall of Quebec and Montreal

- **The claim.** The decisive year was 1759, when a British army took Quebec after a siege and a short battle on the Plains of Abraham. Both commanders died, and the battle lasted about an hour.
- **The battle.** On September 13, 1759, British troops under James Wolfe defeated French troops and Canadian militia under Montcalm on the plateau outside Quebec. British forces numbered about 4,400 regulars and colonial rangers against about 3,400 French and Canadian troops; both Wolfe and Montcalm were mortally wounded.[^ch08-plains-wiki]
- **The completion.** Quebec surrendered after the battle, and Montreal fell in 1760, ending French military power in Canada. The chapter should state the sequence plainly and not let the drama of the battle stand in for the sieges and campaigns around it.[^ch08-plains-wiki][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- **The consequence.** By 1760 Britain had won the main object of the war in North America, though the war continued elsewhere and the peace was still three years away.[^ch08-plains-wiki][^ch08-state-paris]

### 4.4 The war beyond North America

- **The claim.** The American fighting was one theater of a global war. Britain’s victories in the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and Europe shaped the peace as much as the campaigns in Canada.
- **The reach.** During the war British forces also fought in India and captured French and Spanish colonies in the Caribbean. In 1762 Britain took Havana, the capital of Spanish Cuba, which strengthened its hand in the negotiations.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-paris-wiki]
- **The stakes.** Because the war was global, the peace required exchanges across the world. Britain would return some captured islands and choose which conquests to keep, a decision that shaped the North American settlement.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-calloway-review]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should hold the American story inside the imperial one, so that the 1763 settlement appears as a global bargain rather than a purely American outcome.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-check-scope]

## 5. Paris, Pontiac’s War, and the Proclamation

### 5.1 The Peace of Paris and the reshuffling of empires

- **The claim.** The Treaty of Paris of February 10, 1763, ended the war and redrew the map of North America. France gave up its mainland empire east of the Mississippi, and Spain gave up Florida to Britain while acquiring Louisiana from France.
- **The settlement.** By the treaty France ceded Canada and all its claims east of the Mississippi to Britain, keeping only fishing rights and the small islands of Saint Pierre and Miquelon. Spain, an ally of France, ceded Florida to Britain and received Louisiana from France through the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau of November 3, 1762. France had first ceded Louisiana to Spain to keep it out of British hands.[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-paris-wiki]
- **The choice.** In negotiating, British leaders chose to keep conquered territory in North America rather than the more valuable captured sugar islands of Guadeloupe or Martinique. That decision, Colin Calloway writes, “set people and events in motion” across much of the continent.[^ch08-calloway-review][^ch08-state-paris]
- **The consequence.** Britain now claimed an interior it did not control and a debt it could not easily pay. Table 2 lays out the settlement.[^ch08-check-table2][^ch08-check-fontainebleau]

### 5.2 Pontiac’s War and the Indigenous answer

- **The claim.** The British victory did not end the war in the interior. In 1763 a broad Indigenous coalition attacked British forts and settlements from the Great Lakes to the Ohio Valley, and the British had to fight for the ground they had just won.
- **The cause and the leader.** British officials halted the gift-giving and trade practices that had sustained the French alliance, alarming interior nations. A Delaware (Lenape) prophet, Neolin, preached a vision in which the Master of Life told him to reject colonial ways and drive the Europeans out; his message influenced the Odawa leader Pontiac (Obwandiyag), who helped build a coalition of Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wyandot, Seneca, and others.[^ch08-pontiac-battlefields][^ch08-pressbooks-debt][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]
- **The course.** Beginning in May 1763, warriors attacked British posts; Pontiac laid siege to Fort Detroit, and other nations attacked Fort Pitt. Nine British forts were destroyed, hundreds of colonists were killed or captured, and many more fled; the British held Fort Pitt and Detroit.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]
- **The frontier reprisal.** In December 1763, Scots-Irish frontier settlers called the Paxton Boys attacked the Conestoga of the Susquehannock nation in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, killing the twenty residents of Conestoga Indian Town, six in their homes and fourteen under protective custody. No one was ever prosecuted, which showed how far frontier violence outpaced imperial control.[^ch08-zinn-conestoga][^ch08-pressbooks-debt]
- **The end and the consequence.** The war continued into 1766, when Pontiac agreed to a treaty signed at Fort Ontario on July 25, 1766, after it became clear that the French would not return. The conflict was the first broad, cross-nation Indigenous war against the British, and it forced Britain to reconsider how it would govern the interior.[^ch08-state-proclamation-line][^ch08-pontiac-wiki]

### 5.3 In Focus: the Royal Proclamation of 1763

**In Focus.** On October 7, 1763, King George III issued a proclamation that created four new governments — Quebec, East Florida, West Florida, and Grenada — and reserved the interior for the Native nations. The document declares it “just and reasonable, and essential to our Interest,” that the nations “with whom We are connected, and who live under our Protection, should not be molested or disturbed in the Possession of such Parts of Our Dominions and Territories as, not having been ceded to or purchased by Us, are reserved to them … as their Hunting Grounds.” It forbids colonial governors to grant land beyond the bounds of the new governments, forbids settlement on reserved lands, and requires that any purchase of Native land be made by the crown at a public meeting. Read closely, the Proclamation does two things at once. It recognizes Indigenous possession of the interior — a real concession that Native nations would later cite — and it asserts British sovereignty over those same nations and lands, reserving to the crown the exclusive right to buy them. It also angered colonists who had expected the war to open the West to settlement, and it proved unenforceable almost at once. The chapter should quote the document directly, name the line it drew along the spine of the Appalachian Mountains, and avoid treating it as either pure protection or pure land grab. Its object remains a research check.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line][^ch08-check-focus]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The war was decided by British money, sea power, and mobilization, not by population alone.** Britain’s ability to borrow and to subsidize allies let it sustain a global war that France could not match.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-pitt-wiki]
- **France lost its mainland empire east of the Mississippi in 1763.** The Treaty of Paris transferred Canada and the interior east of the Mississippi to Britain, and Spain acquired Louisiana from France.[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-state-paris]
- **The settlement did not end conflict in the interior.** Pontiac’s War followed the peace, and the Proclamation of 1763 tried and failed to control settlement beyond the Appalachians.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]
- **The war was global.** Fighting in North America was one theater of a conflict that reached Europe, the Caribbean, West Africa, and India.[^ch08-french-indian-wiki][^ch08-state-paris]
- **The war left a large British debt and an enlarged empire.** Britain’s debt and the cost of defending the interior became central political problems after 1763.[^ch08-pressbooks-debt][^ch08-state-taxation]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Whether the war led to the Revolution.** Did the Seven Years’ War set the American Revolution in motion by creating the debt and the imperial reforms, or is that a retrospective story that reads independence back into a war fought for empire?[^ch08-anderson-past][^ch08-state-taxation]
- **How much agency the Native nations had.** Were they junior allies of European empires, or independent powers whose decisions shaped the war and the peace? The middle-ground and Indigenous-agency interpretations answer that they were independent, and the question is how far the evidence supports the strongest form of that claim.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-white-middle-ground]
- **What the interior was like after 1763.** Did the Proclamation protect Native lands, or did it mainly transfer the right to buy them to the crown? The document supports both readings, and the chapter should not collapse them.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]
- **The scale of the fighting.** Troop strengths, casualty figures, and the number of forts destroyed vary across sources, and the numbers must be given as estimates with attributed sources.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-check-dating]
- **What the archives cannot show.** Native diplomacy and warfare reach us mainly through European reports and interpreters; Pontiac’s own words survive in translation, and the nations’ decisions are visible chiefly through their effects.[^ch08-check-names][^ch08-pontiac-battlefields]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which empires, colonists, and Native nations all made decisions inside structures none of them controlled, and in which the outcome was contingent at every stage.
- **Anderson and contingency.** Fred Anderson’s *Crucible of War* (2000) treats the war as a decisive event in its own right and insists that historians who look ahead to the Revolution lose the contingency of the moment. The war created the conditions of the later crisis without intending or foreseeing it.[^ch08-anderson-past]
- **White, Calloway, and Indigenous agency.** Richard White’s *The Middle Ground* (1991) and Colin Calloway’s *The Scratch of a Pen* (2006) read the war and the 1763 settlement from Native ground, showing that the nations were neither passive nor simply defeated, and that the peace “set people and events in motion” across the continent.[^ch08-white-middle-ground][^ch08-scratch-calloway][^ch08-calloway-review]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, the positions say that British victory was real but incomplete: it changed who claimed the interior without settling who would control it. The chapter should hold the imperial and the Indigenous accounts together rather than choosing a single cause.
- **The lesson.** The best history refuses both the story of an inevitable British triumph and the story of the war as a mere prelude to independence, and it refuses to let the empires stand in for the nations whose homelands they claimed.[^ch08-anderson-past][^ch08-white-middle-ground]

## Legacies

The war left an empire that was larger and more expensive than the one that began it. Britain’s national debt nearly doubled, from about £75 million in 1756 to about £133 million in 1763, and interest payments consumed much of the national budget; the cost of defending the new interior and the former French posts added a continuing drain.[^ch08-pressbooks-debt] When Parliament turned to the colonies for revenue, first through stricter enforcement and then through the Stamp Act, it was acting on the war’s bill. That is the ground on which Chapter 9 begins.[^ch08-state-taxation][^ch08-check-ch9]

The Proclamation of 1763 left a double inheritance. It recognized Native possession of the interior and reserved the crown’s exclusive right to buy it, a framework that Native nations and later treaty law would cite; and it failed to stop settlers from crossing the Appalachians, so that the line became a grievance rather than a boundary.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line] In the Great Lakes and Ohio Country, the middle ground that had sustained French power eroded after 1763, and the pressure that Pontiac’s War had answered only grew.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-pontiac-wiki]

The war also became a national memory. Generations of Americans learned it as the French and Indian War, a story of Braddock’s defeat, Wolfe’s victory, and a young Washington’s first fame, told as a prelude to independence. The chapter should note that memory without adopting it, since the same war was for Native nations a war for their homelands and for France the end of a mainland empire.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-calloway-review][^ch08-pontiac-wiki]

## Optional threads

- **The global frame.** Pitt’s subsidies to Prussia, the naval war, the capture of Havana and the Caribbean islands, and the fighting in India all placed the American war inside a world conflict whose peace was negotiated far from the Ohio Country. The chapter should show that the 1763 settlement was a global bargain.[^ch08-state-paris][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- **Archives and silences.** Washington’s journal, Dinwiddie’s letters, and British officers’ reports preserve the imperial side in detail; the Native nations and their diplomats reach us mainly through European interpreters and after-the-fact accounts, and Pontiac’s words survive in translation. The chapter should name those mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore Indigenous agency.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-pontiac-battlefields][^ch08-check-names]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 6 carries the imperial wars to 1713 and the Peace of Utrecht; Chapter 7 describes the provincial society that produced the war; Chapter 9 owns the fiscal crisis the war created. Later chapters will carry the Proclamation Line into the Revolution and the erosion of the middle ground into removal.[^ch08-check-ch7][^ch08-check-ch9][^ch08-check-scope]

## Summary

- New France was a small settler colony that governed a vast interior through the fur trade, alliance, and the accommodations historians call the middle ground.[^ch08-nps-middle-ground][^ch08-newfrance-wiki]
- The British colonies outnumbered New France by roughly twenty to one in the 1750s, yet France held the interior for decades, so numbers alone did not decide the contest.[^ch08-pop-hs][^ch08-thirteen-wiki]
- Virginia’s claim to the Ohio Country was pressed by the Ohio Company, whose investors included Governor Dinwiddie and George Washington’s family.[^ch08-ohio-company-wiki]
- George Washington’s 1753 mission carried Dinwiddie’s demand to Fort Le Boeuf, where Saint-Pierre refused to withdraw; the journal Washington wrote was printed and widely read.[^ch08-wash-journal-zea][^ch08-battlefields-wash-backcountry]
- The war’s first bloodshed came at Jumonville Glen on May 28, 1754, and Washington surrendered at Fort Necessity on July 4, 1754.[^ch08-fort-necessity-wiki]
- The Albany Congress of 1754 adopted Franklin’s plan for a common government, and both the colonies and the crown rejected it.[^ch08-state-albany][^ch08-albany-avalon]
- Braddock’s army was nearly destroyed at the Monongahela on July 9, 1755, and the war widened into the global Seven Years’ War.[^ch08-braddock-wiki][^ch08-french-indian-wiki]
- French forces won early victories, including the siege of Fort William Henry and the massacre that followed in August 1757.[^ch08-fwh-wiki]
- William Pitt’s strategy of subsidies abroad and large forces in North America turned the war; Quebec fell in 1759 and Montreal in 1760.[^ch08-pitt-wiki][^ch08-plains-wiki]
- The Treaty of Paris of February 10, 1763, gave Britain Canada and the interior east of the Mississippi, while Spain ceded Florida and acquired Louisiana from France.[^ch08-paris-avalon][^ch08-state-paris]
- Pontiac’s War and the Paxton Boys’ massacre of the Conestoga in 1763 showed that the peace did not end conflict in the interior.[^ch08-pontiac-wiki][^ch08-zinn-conestoga]
- The Royal Proclamation of 1763 reserved the interior for Native nations under British sovereignty and forbade settlement beyond the Appalachians, satisfying almost no one.[^ch08-proc-avalon][^ch08-state-proclamation-line]
