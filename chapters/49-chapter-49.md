## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date and the occasion.** On June 24, 2022, the Supreme Court decided *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization*, No. 19-1392, by a vote of 6–3, in an opinion by Justice Alito, holding that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and overruling *Roe v. Wade* (1973) and *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey* (1992).[^ch49-dobbs-2022]
- **The holding.** The slip opinion states that “The Constitution does not confer a right to abortion; *Roe* and *Casey* are overruled; and the authority to regulate abortion is returned to the people and their elected representatives.” The opinion is dated June 24, 2022.[^ch49-dobbs-2022]
- **The prior disclosure.** On May 2, 2022, Politico published a draft of what it described as the majority opinion, an extraordinary breach of the Court’s confidentiality; the leak is documented and its source remains officially unidentified.[^ch49-dobbs-leak-2022]
- **The released text.** The Court posted the opinion on its website; the majority closes on its conclusion, and the joint dissent of Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan begins on the first page of the dissent and ends with the sentence “With sorrow — for this Court, but more, for the many millions of American women who have today lost a fundamental constitutional protection — we dissent.”[^ch49-dobbs-2022]
- **The staging.** The date of release, the slip opinion’s text and structure, and the fact of the May 2022 leak are on the record. The exact hour the slip opinion became public, the composition of the crowd outside the building, and the immediate reactions of those present are not established by the slip opinion and must not be invented.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-dobbs-leak-2022]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the holding, the authors, the vote, the leaked draft, and the published text. ## 1. Pandemic inheritance and the legislative program (2021–2022)

### 1.1 The claim: an uneven recovery and a burst of industrial policy

- **The claim.** The Biden administration inherited a pandemic economy, passed a large recovery law and then a series of industrial, infrastructure, and climate laws, and presided over a recovery that lowered unemployment while inflation reached a four-decade high; the chapter should hold the recovery, the price surge, and the distribution of both together rather than choosing one register.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-iija-2021][^ch49-ira-2022][^ch49-chips-2022][^ch49-bls-cpi-2022]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the recovery law through the infrastructure, climate, and semiconductor statutes, the labor actions of 2023, and the geography of industrial investment shown in Map 39.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-ira-2022][^ch49-uaw-2023][^ch49-check-map39]

### 1.2 The American Rescue Plan and the recovery

- **The statute.** The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 became Public Law 117-2 on March 11, 2021; it extended unemployment benefits, funded direct payments, vaccine distribution, school reopening, and state and local aid, and expanded the child tax credit.[^ch49-arp-2021]
- **The stakes.** Supporters argued the law prevented a deeper slump; critics argued it contributed to inflation. The chapter should give the enacted provisions, the estimated cost, and the later macro-economic assessments with their models, and distinguish a projection from an outcome.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-check-numbers]
- **The recovery and the price surge.** The unemployment rate fell and the Consumer Price Index rose 9.1 percent over the twelve months ending June 2022, the largest such increase since 1981, before inflation eased in 2023–2024; the chapter should give the series, their base periods, and the Federal Reserve’s rate response.[^ch49-bls-cpi-2022][^ch49-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** “The economy” is not one measure; the chapter should distinguish employment, real wages, prices, and distribution, and not credit or blame a single law for a global price movement without evidence.[^ch49-check-numbers][^ch49-check-debate-works]

### 1.3 Infrastructure, climate, and semiconductors

- **The infrastructure law.** The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act became Public Law 117-58 on November 15, 2021, funding roads, bridges, rail, broadband, water systems, and an electric-vehicle charging network, much of it through multiyear formula and grant programs.[^ch49-iija-2021]
- **The climate and tax law.** The Inflation Reduction Act became Public Law 117-169 on August 16, 2022, providing tax credits and grants for clean energy, allowing Medicare to negotiate some drug prices, and funding Internal Revenue Service enforcement; it was enacted by budget reconciliation with no Republican votes.[^ch49-ira-2022]
- **The semiconductor law.** The CHIPS and Science Act became Public Law 117-167 on August 9, 2022, subsidizing domestic semiconductor manufacturing and authorizing science and technology programs.[^ch49-chips-2022]
- **The measure.** Map 39 should show where the announced manufacturing investment landed, using county economic output and documented sector measures rather than county GDP alone, and must not infer a “tech corridor” from output concentration.[^ch49-check-map39][^ch49-check-numbers]

### 1.4 Labor

- **The actions.** In 2023 the United Auto Workers conducted a “stand-up” strike against the three large automakers, and Hollywood writers and actors struck separately, with the writers’ contract addressing generative artificial intelligence; the chapter should give the dates, the employers, and the settlements from the parties’ records.[^ch49-uaw-2023]
- **The stakes.** The strikes tested whether a tight labor market and public support could raise wages in sectors facing technological change; the chapter should present the settlements and their later effects rather than a verdict at the time.[^ch49-uaw-2023][^ch49-eo-14179-2025]
- **The silences.** The chapter should name the workers whose voices are least represented in the record — the unorganized, the undocumented, and the care workers — rather than speaking for them.[^ch49-check-silences]

## 2. Rights: *Dobbs*, firearms, affirmative action, and immunity (2022–2024)

### 2.1 The claim: a Court remaking rights and the scope of the presidency

- **The claim.** In three terms the Supreme Court ended the constitutional right to abortion, expanded the right to carry firearms, struck down race-conscious college admissions, and granted presidents broad immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts; the chapter should treat these as related but distinct decisions with distinct legal and political consequences.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

### 2.2 *Dobbs* and the In Focus

- **The decision.** *Dobbs* held on June 24, 2022, that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and overruled *Roe* and *Casey*, 6–3; the chapter should give the holding, the vote, and the dissent’s structure.[^ch49-dobbs-2022]
- **The In Focus.** In Focus reads the joint dissent of Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan: its opening sentence, that “For half a century, *Roe* … and *Casey* … have protected the liberty and equality of women,” and its closing sentence, “With sorrow — for this Court, but more, for the many millions of American women who have today lost a fundamental constitutional protection — we dissent.” The box should locate both sentences by slip-opinion page and collate them character by character.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-check-focus]
- **What the close reading does.** The box reads the dissent as a document that states a jurisprudence of liberty and equality and predicts consequences, not as proof that those consequences followed; it should quote the two sentences exactly and identify the pages.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-check-focus]

### 2.3 The post-*Dobbs* landscape

- **The state actions.** After *Dobbs*, many states restricted or banned abortion and others protected it; abortion-related ballot measures drew voters, beginning with Kansas on August 2, 2022, where 59 percent rejected a constitutional amendment that would have removed state protections.[^ch49-ks-referendum-2022][^ch49-kff-ballot-2026]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give the number of states with bans or restrictions and the number with protections as of a stated date, citing a maintained tracker, and not present a changing count as fixed.[^ch49-kff-ballot-2026][^ch49-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** The consequences for maternal morbidity, travel, and clinical capacity are studied but contested and depend on data that lag; the chapter should present estimates with their methods rather than asserting a single effect.[^ch49-check-debate-works][^ch49-check-silences]

### 2.4 Firearms, affirmative action, and presidential immunity

- **The firearms decision.** In *New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen*, decided June 23, 2022, the Court held 6–3 that New York’s requirement of a special need for a concealed-carry license violated the Second Amendment and announced a historical-tradition test; the chapter should give the holding and the vote and note the lower-court litigation it produced.[^ch49-bruen-2022]
- **The gun statute.** Congress responded in part with the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, Public Law 117-159, signed June 25, 2022, the first major federal gun-safety law in nearly three decades, which expanded background checks for buyers under 21 and addressed trafficking and domestic-violence provisions.[^ch49-bsca-2022]
- **The affirmative-action decision.** In *Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College* and its companion, decided June 29, 2023, the Court held that Harvard’s and the University of North Carolina’s admissions programs violated the Equal Protection Clause; the chapter should give the holding, the vote, and the separate opinions.[^ch49-sffa-2023]
- **The immunity decision.** In *Trump v. United States*, decided July 1, 2024, the Court held that a president has absolute immunity from prosecution for acts within core constitutional authority and at least presumptive immunity for other official acts, 6–3; the chapter should give the holding and its effect on the pending prosecutions.[^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]
- **The caution.** The chapter should distinguish a decision’s legal holding from its political interpretation and present the dissents at their strongest.[^ch49-check-debate-works]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The legislation.** The American Rescue Plan Act (Public Law 117-2, March 11, 2021), the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (Public Law 117-58, November 15, 2021), the CHIPS and Science Act (Public Law 117-167, August 9, 2022), and the Inflation Reduction Act (Public Law 117-169, August 16, 2022) were enacted.[^ch49-arp-2021][^ch49-iija-2021][^ch49-chips-2022][^ch49-ira-2022]
- **The decisions.** *Dobbs* was decided June 24, 2022, 6–3; *Bruen* was decided June 23, 2022, 6–3; *Students for Fair Admissions* was decided June 29, 2023; *Trump v. United States* was decided July 1, 2024, 6–3.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]
- **The elections.** Republicans won the House in 2022 while Democrats retained 51 Senate seats; Trump won the 2024 election, 312–226, with about 49.8 percent of the popular vote to Harris’s about 48.3 percent.[^ch49-fec-2022][^ch49-fec-2024]
- **The 2025 program and its limits.** The second administration imposed broad tariffs under IEEPA, signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act on July 4, 2025, expanded removals, and deployed the National Guard domestically; the Supreme Court held on February 20, 2026, that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, and a federal appeals court held the Los Angeles deployment unlawful.[^ch49-tariffs-crs-2026][^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-learning-resources-2026][^ch49-la-guard-ruling-2026]
- **The abroad record.** The United States withdrew from Afghanistan in August 2021; Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022; a Gaza ceasefire took effect in October 2025; and a broader conflict with Iran was under way in 2026.[^ch49-afghanistan-aar-2023][^ch49-ukraine-aid-2026][^ch49-gaza-ceasefire-2026][^ch49-iran-war-2026]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the period a democratic backslide or a resilient contest?** Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argue that democracies erode slowly through the breaking of norms; others stress the courts, elections, and the bureaucracy that checked the period’s actions. The chapter should present both and use the institutions’ actual record.[^ch49-levitsky-ziblatt-2018][^ch49-levitsky-ziblatt-2023]
- **What drove the 2024 result?** One account emphasizes trade exposure and economic dislocation; another emphasizes status threat and cultural backlash; a third emphasizes place-based resentment and party change. The chapter should present each at its strongest and test it against the returns and the surveys.[^ch49-autor-2020][^ch49-mutz-2018][^ch49-cramer-2016]
- **Did the executive gain or lose power?** The tariff ruling and the deployment ruling constrained the executive, while the immunity decision shielded it; whether the period strengthened or weakened the presidency is open and depends on which instruments are counted.[^ch49-trump-v-us-2024][^ch49-learning-resources-2026]
- **How much can be known about the present?** The events of 2025–2026 are documented in official records that are incomplete and contested, and some institutions’ records are not yet available; the chapter should state the evidentiary limits rather than declare a verdict.[^ch49-check-cutoff][^ch49-check-debate-works]
- **Is the American case comparable?** Populist and nationalist movements arose across many democracies in the period; the chapter should compare the American case with others rather than treating it as unique or as a mere copy.[^ch49-muller-2016][^ch49-levitsky-ziblatt-2023]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which a Court ended a half-century settlement, a second administration tested the executive’s reach, and elections and courts repeatedly checked and contested that reach, while a pandemic’s aftermath, a price surge, and long-run polarization shaped the terrain.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-learning-resources-2026][^ch49-autor-2020]
- **Contingency.** The 2022 midterm results, the 2024 ticket change, the 2024 result, the tariff ruling, and the deployment ruling were all contingent; the chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch49-biden-withdraw-2024][^ch49-check-debate-works]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter should hold the structural conditions — polarization, deindustrialization, the media environment, and global conflict — together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation.[^ch49-autor-2020][^ch49-check-scope]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Between 2021 and October 2026 the United States ended a half-century abortion settlement, remade its immigration and tariff policy, learned that core parts of that policy exceeded statutory authority, elected a former president to a second term, continued two wars and began a third, advanced artificial intelligence, and marked its 250th anniversary; the chapter’s question — what a history of an unsettled present can responsibly establish — is answered by holding those developments together under a strict evidentiary rule.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-iran-war-2026][^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026]

## Legacies

The period’s legal decisions outlasted the terms that produced them. The ending of the federal abortion right, the expansion of gun rights, the invalidation of race-conscious admissions, and the broad immunity for official presidential acts reshaped the ground on which later political and legal conflicts were fought; the chapter should trace each to its source rather than present a settled verdict.[^ch49-dobbs-2022][^ch49-bruen-2022][^ch49-sffa-2023][^ch49-trump-v-us-2024]

The period’s economic and administrative choices left institutions and precedents that later administrations would defend, narrow, or unwind. The industrial-policy statutes, the 2025 tariff and fiscal laws, the government-efficiency program, and the expanded immigration enforcement remained live questions at the cutoff, and the Supreme Court’s tariff ruling left the tariff regime’s legal basis unsettled.[^ch49-ira-2022][^ch49-obby-2025][^ch49-doge-2025][^ch49-learning-resources-2026]

And the contest over how to read the recent past became part of the period itself. The disputes over what the 2024 election meant, whether the executive had exceeded its authority, and how the 250th anniversary should be remembered set terms that Chapter 50 takes up as it weighs what the preceding chapters established.[^ch49-fec-2024][^ch49-check-cutoff][^ch49-archives-freedom250-2026][^ch49-check-ch50]
