Four voices, four verdicts. A governor presided over a wedding of the waters and asked heaven to bless it. The women who tended the machinery it fed called themselves the daughters of freemen. One historian made the market the force that created the modern world; another made it a choice most farm families welcomed. They disagree about whether the transformation was a triumph, a theft, or something ordinary that historians later named a revolution. Each names what the others leave out: public purpose, exploited labor, structural coercion, and the agency of ordinary people. The question is not which voice was right. It is how exchange changed independence and labor, and who paid.[^ch15-check-epigraphs]

Buffalo, October 26, 1825. The gun stood at the point where the new canal drew its first water from Lake Erie. When it fired, the artilleryman at the next station, 10 or 15 miles down the line, heard it and fired his own. The next heard that one and fired, and the signal ran east along the canal and down the Hudson toward New York City, where it arrived in about 90 minutes. No message had ever crossed the state so fast.[^ch15-cere-source-ny][^ch15-erie-canal-guide]

The boat it announced moved more slowly. The packet *Seneca Chief* left Buffalo at the pace of mule teams, about four miles an hour, with Governor DeWitt Clinton aboard and two barrels of Lake Erie water stowed for the journey. The canal ran 363 miles from the Hudson to Lake Erie, 40 feet wide, and it lifted boats 675 feet through 83 locks. The legislature had authorized about $7 million for it on April 15, 1817. Eight years later the work was finished, and the skeptics who had called it Clinton’s Ditch and Clinton’s Folly had been answered.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-erie-canal-guide]

The celebration was a civic festival as much as an engineering milestone. Two city aldermen had procured the barrels of lake water. At Albany, 600 diners toasted the canal. On November 4 the flotilla reached New York City and was met by a harbor full of decorated vessels. Off Sandy Hook, Clinton poured Lake Erie water into the Atlantic in the ceremony remembered as the Wedding of the Waters, and asked God to make the work “subservient to the best interests of the human race.”[^ch15-cere-source-ny][^ch15-erie-canal-guide][^ch15-clinton-proclamation]

The water in the barrels was lake water, not currency, and the boat carried a governor, not an army. But the ceremony announced a claim that the next quarter century would test: that a state could build a market, and that the market would make the state. Whether it made its people more independent was another question. How did exchange change independence and labor?[^ch15-check-scope][^ch15-check-scene]

Chapter 14 ended with the peace of 1815 and the expansion that followed. This chapter takes up the economic order that expansion made possible. It follows the question from the 1790s, when the first successful American cotton mill and the cotton gin appeared, to the 1840s, and it uses later figures where they measure the system those decades built. By then the railroad and the telegraph were beginning to displace the canal, and the Panic of 1837 had wrecked the credit system that financed the boom. It works from the printed records of internal improvements, banking, and the cotton trade; census returns; the accounts of cotton factors; institutional histories of the mills and the domestic slave trade; household advice literature; and the narratives of enslaved and formerly enslaved people. It treats “the market,” “improvement,” “wage labor,” “the household,” and “credit” as contested terms, not neutral descriptions. Enslaved people appear in the census and the trade’s ledgers as property measured in persons and dollars, and the chapter says so rather than letting the accounting stand as neutral.[^ch15-check-scope][^ch15-check-names][^ch15-check-dates][^ch15-check-ch14]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the transportation revolution of canals, roads, steamboats, and the first railroads, and the state finance that built it. §2 turns to the mills of Pawtucket and Lowell and the birth of American wage labor. §3 follows cotton, the credit that financed it, and the domestic slave trade that fed it. §4 sets the reorganization of household work and the ideology of separate spheres inside the same transformation. §5 examines the banks and panics that made the new market unstable. §6 asks how historians explain the Market Revolution and who paid for it. The chapter begins on an October morning in 1825, when a packet boat left Buffalo carrying a governor and two barrels of lake water.[^ch15-check-scope]

## 1. Canals, roads, steamboats, and railroads

### 1.1 The transportation revolution and the Erie Canal

Before the 1820s, distance was the republic’s most expensive commodity. It cost as much to ship a ton of freight 30 miles overland as to send the same ton across the Atlantic. Settlement clung to the coast and the rivers. In 1790 the center of the American population lay 23 miles east of Baltimore; 40 years later it had moved only into what is now West Virginia. Turnpikes, canals, steamboats, and then railroads changed the arithmetic, and with it the geography of who could sell what to whom.[^ch15-bri-erie]

New York’s canal was the decisive public investment. The legislature approved it on April 15, 1817, authorizing about $7 million for a 363-mile waterway 40 feet wide. It opened in 1825 after eight years of work, with 83 locks and a total lift of 675 feet. Within a few years it carried goods worth double the value of all freight shipped down the Mississippi to New Orleans, and tolls repaid the construction cost in the first nine years of operation. Within 15 years New York City was the busiest port in America, handling more tonnage than Boston, Baltimore, and New Orleans combined.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]

The canal was a state project because Congress would not fund a national system of roads and canals. In 1817 Representative John C. Calhoun proposed a Bonus Bill that would have applied surplus federal money to a national system of roads and canals. Calhoun argued that the Constitution’s power to establish post offices and post roads authorized the work. He urged Congress to “bind the republic together with a perfect system of roads and canals.” The bill met sectional rivalry and constitutional doubt, and President James Madison vetoed it. The veto left the states to borrow and build, a pattern that pushed public purpose onto state credit and its bondholders.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-cere-source-ny]

The consequence was a market that reached farther than the farmers who supplied it. The canal pulled the Great Lakes into the Atlantic economy. It also taught farmers along the canal to grow for a distant market whose prices they did not set. Connection was real, and so was dependence.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]

### 1.2 Steamboats, roads, and the first railroads; Map 12

Canals were only one thread in the network. Steamboats mastered the western rivers. The number of steamboats on those rivers grew from 17 in 1817 to more than 700 by 1837, and by 1860 some 3,500 vessels a year were working in and out of New Orleans. The Mississippi became the cotton kingdom’s central artery, carrying bales south to the port and manufactured goods and supplies north.[^ch15-yawp-cotton]

Railroads began as short experiments and ended the canal’s monopoly on bulk freight. The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, completed in early 1830 as the country’s first intercity line, was 13 miles long. By 1850 the network exceeded 9,000 miles. Railroads were financed mainly by private banks rather than state governments, which set them apart from the canals and shaped the next generation of American finance.[^ch15-aar-railroads][^ch15-bri-erie]

Map 12 plots the canals, roads, and railroads of 1825–1840. It distinguishes completed from projected lines, dates each element, and shows the Erie crossing the Appalachian divide through the Mohawk corridor. It also shows what the shorthand “the canal era” obscures: Pennsylvania, Ohio, and other states built canals that crossed the mountains too, and the Old Northwest was being tied to eastern markets. The claim that canals as a technology stopped at the Appalachians is false, and the map is drawn to refute it. Its subject is not the triumph of a technology but the uneven geography of connection.[^ch15-check-map][^ch15-check-canal-network][^ch15-tanner-map]

The network was uneven. Port cities, canal towns, and the newer cotton country boomed. Upland and remote districts waited decades for a railroad and paid for the wait in isolation and outmigration. Geography sorted who got connected, and money decided who got geography.[^ch15-check-map]

### 1.3 Who paid: state debt, migrant labor, and the uneven geography

The revolution was built by people who rarely appear in its celebrations. More than 9,000 laborers dug the Erie Canal. More than 75 percent came from upstate New York; most of the rest were Irish immigrants. The builders were amateurs, in the estimate of one account, without formal training in engineering, and the canal’s grade and locks were cut by hand and animal power. The “wedding of the waters” rested on their work.[^ch15-bri-erie]

States and private investors financed canal after canal. When the projects failed, the debts remained. A wave of state defaults in the 1840s pushed state governments out of direct construction and left railroads to private capital. The Panic of 1837 deepened the overhang, because the same credit that had built the canals had also fed the land speculation that collapsed.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-dpla-panic1837]

The canals also opened the interior to settlement, and settlement pressed on Native nations. Land speculation ran onto land from which Native nations had recently been removed, and the same decades that built the Erie pushed the cotton frontier across the Southeast and the Old Southwest. Internal improvement and Indigenous dispossession were connected stories, not separate ones. The chapter names that connection without carrying the removal policy itself, which Chapter 17 owns.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-check-slavery][^ch15-check-ch16]

## 2. Mills and wage labor

### 2.1 Slater and the factory system

The American factory began in a Rhode Island mill where a migrant English mechanic rebuilt British cotton-spinning machinery and put children to work on it. Moses Brown, a Providence merchant, hired Samuel Slater in December 1789 to build water-powered cotton-spinning machines. Slater had the machines running in December 1790. In 1793 the firm of Almy, Brown and Slater built the mill at Pawtucket Falls that became the first successful water-powered cotton-spinning mill in the United States. It was six windows wide and two and a half stories tall.[^ch15-nps-slater]

Slater hired children, generally between eight and 14 years old, and paid them for the hours they spent under his supervision rather than for what they produced. Families followed the work into mill villages, where the employer controlled the house, the clock, and much of the conduct of daily life. The factory system did not begin with independent wage earners. It began with child and family labor.[^ch15-nps-slater]

The mill established the template that the larger Waltham–Lowell mills would elaborate: mechanized cotton spinning, waterpower, and a labor force drawn from people with few alternatives.[^ch15-nps-slater]

### 2.2 Lowell and the Waltham–Lowell system

At Lowell, corporations integrated spinning and weaving under one roof and recruited young Yankee women to tend the machines. Beginning with the town’s first factory in 1823, the textile companies drew women from New England farms and small villages with the promise of monthly cash wages and room and board. Most were between 15 and 30 years old. Family farms offered young women few ways to earn money of their own, and many came because they had no better choice.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

The corporation housed them in its own boardinghouses and governed the life inside. A typical house had eight units, with 20 to 40 women in each. The women usually shared a room with three others, two to a bed. A female keeper or a married couple ran the house, enforced a curfew, and upheld a code of conduct. The factory bell summoned the operatives to work and marked the hours of the day. The corporation controlled the mill and much of life outside it.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

The “mill girls” gained wages, savings, and a measure of independence. They gained it on terms set by the corporation, which owned the mill, the boardinghouse, and the rules that joined them. Lowell was neither a female Eden nor a prison. It was the first large-scale American experiment in assembling, housing, and disciplining a wage labor force.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

### 2.3 The costs: hours, turnover, and the turn-outs

Mill work was long, unhealthy, and insecure. Most textile workers toiled 12 to 14 hours a day plus a half day on Saturday, and typically worked only nine or 10 months a year. Twelve hours a day for five and a half days is 66 hours a week; 14 hours is 77. The figure of about 73 hours often quoted for the 1830s and 1840s falls inside that range, though it still needs to be fixed against the mill records.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-check-hours]

When wages were cut, the operatives organized. Lowell’s female workers struck twice in the 1830s over wage cuts and conditions, and in the 1840s they campaigned for a ten-hour day. Few strikes succeeded, and the workforce remained largely unorganized. During the early protests the women asserted that they were “the daughters of freemen,” whose rights could not be “trampled upon with impunity.” The phrase survives in a National Park Service summary; its printed broadside or petition still needs to be located.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-check-epigraphs]

Some operatives wrote their way into the record. They published letters and essays in the *Lowell Offering*, and later in labor papers such as the *Voice of Industry*. Sarah Bagley became a leading labor reformer and edited the *Voice of Industry*. Their own publications are the best evidence of how they understood the work.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

By the 1840s the workforce was changing. Irish immigrants fleeing famine entered the mills in large numbers, and the operatives divided along ethnic lines just as the ten-hour movement needed unity. The turn-outs had failed in their immediate aims, but they established that the new wage labor was contested from the start. The same market that offered women wages set the terms on which those wages could be reduced.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

## 3. Cotton, credit, and the domestic slave trade

### 3.1 The cotton gin and the cotton boom

Cotton was the crop that made the market continental. Long-staple cotton, easy to separate from its seeds, grew only along the coast. The inland variety had sticky green seeds that took hours to pick out by hand. A hand worker separated about a pound a day. Eli Whitney’s gin, patented in 1794, did the work far faster. A small gin could be hand-cranked; larger versions ran on horse or water power. “One man and a horse will do more than fifty men with the old machines,” Whitney wrote to his father.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin]

The effect was a boom, not a liberation from labor. Raw cotton output roughly doubled each decade after 1800. By 1850 the United States grew three-quarters of the world’s cotton, and the South supplied three-fifths of American exports, most of it cotton. The number of slave states rose from six in 1790 to 15 in 1860. The gin “reduced the labor of removing seeds,” the National Archives observes, but “it did not reduce the need for enslaved labor to grow and pick the cotton.”[^ch15-na-cotton-gin]

The scale of the crop was enormous by 1860. Enslaved labor produced more than two billion pounds of cotton a year. About 75 percent of the southern crop was sold internationally, most of it to Britain, and American cotton accounted for about 60 percent of United States exports in 1860. In 1850, 1.8 million of the nation’s 3.2 million enslaved people were growing and picking cotton.[^ch15-nps-cotton-economy][^ch15-yawp-cotton][^ch15-dpla-cotton-gin]

Cotton did not merely enrich the South. It financed the Northern textile mills, the Atlantic shipping trade, and the credit system of the whole country. “King Cotton” was not a southern curiosity. It was the engine of the American market, and its fuel was enslaved labor.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-yawp-cotton]

### 3.2 The domestic slave trade

The transatlantic slave trade ended in 1808. The cotton frontier’s demand for labor did not. Between 1820 and 1860, roughly one million enslaved people were forced west and to the Deep South, placed on plantations along the Mississippi River Valley. The forced migration increased the slave states’ representation in Congress, because the three-fifths clause counted enslaved people for seats. The National Museum of African American History and Culture estimates that the resulting population growth gave the slave states about a third more seats. Enslavers’ interests thus gained weight in national politics from the movement of people who had no voice in it.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-gilderlehrman-berlin]

The trade was a business with firms, offices, and ships. Isaac Franklin and John Armfield, based in Alexandria, Virginia, were among the most successful traders; they sold thousands of people to the Deep South and secured millions in profit. Their ship manifest certified that the people named had “not been imported to the U.S. since January [1808]” and were “held to service in labor as slaves and are not entitled to freedom.” The language showed the firm’s compliance with the law that banned the Atlantic trade. It also showed how carefully the domestic trade distinguished itself from the traffic it had replaced.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]

The trade moved people in coffles, chained and handcuffed. Charles Ball’s 1837 account described the fittings: “A strong iron collar was closely fitted by means of a padlock round each of our necks. A chain of iron about a hundred feet long was passed through the hasp of each padlock … we were handcuffed in pairs.” At Natchez, Mississippi, the Forks of the Road held the second-busiest slave market in the Deep South from 1833 until Federal troops arrived on July 13, 1863. Traders had been pushed outside the city limits by a cholera scare in 1832. Tens of thousands of people were brought there from Virginia, Maryland, Kentucky, and the Carolinas.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-nps-forks]

The internal trade made the cotton boom possible and made the enslaved family its casualty. It was the connective tissue between the Upper South’s “surplus” and the Deep South’s demand. The ledgers counted people as property; the chapter names what the ledgers counted.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]

### 3.3 Factors, credit, and the cotton economy

Cotton moved to market through factors, the commission merchants who sold the crop and financed the year. A factor found the buyer and arranged transportation, insurance, and storage for a commission. Because banks and ready cash were scarce, planters far from towns relied on factors for money and supplies against the coming crop. The factor became the planter’s banker as well as his selling agent.[^ch15-ncpedia-factors]

The charges bound the planter tighter than a single sale. One survey of the factorage system reports a commission of about 2.5 percent to sell the crop, 2.5 percent to buy supplies, and 2.5 percent to advance cash or credit. Long-term credit sometimes ran at 8 percent, plus storage and wharfage. If the crop did not cover the year’s debts, the balance rolled into the next crop. The planter’s independence was a fiction of the account book.[^ch15-woodman-review]

The capital inside the southern credit system was northern and British as often as it was southern. Cotton alone accounted for about 50 percent of United States exports in the period. Brown Brothers and similar firms appointed agents in Charleston, Mobile, New Orleans, and Savannah who advanced money on consigned cotton. In the depression after 1837, the firm foreclosed on plantations and on enslaved people pledged as collateral. The crop lien ran through New York and Liverpool before it reached the cotton field.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade]

### 3.4 In Focus: a cotton factor’s account page

**In Focus.** The page is headed “Account Sales for 31 Bales Cotton Rec’d, per Bark Culloma from St. Marks and sold per Acc’t. and risk of the Estate of J. W. Cotton.” Smallwood Anderson and Company rendered it in New York on December 26, 1856. The cotton weighed 16,449 pounds and sold at 12⅛ cents a pound, for $1,994.44. Against that gross the factor set the charges: freight on 16,610 pounds at five-eighths of a cent, marine insurance, weighing, cartage, labor, storage, barkage, fire insurance, mending, and a commission of 2½ percent. The charges came to $220.70. The net due the estate was $1,773.74.[^ch15-smith-account]

The page is an instrument of power. It made the crop legible: marks, weights, grades, charges, and a balance. It did not name a single person who grew or picked the cotton. The estate is the seller; the enslaved people who made the crop are the estate’s capacity to produce, counted nowhere in the columns. The sale entry names Utica Mills. Reading the account against the records of the domestic slave trade restores the people the page omits.[^ch15-smith-account][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]

The document is dated a decade after this chapter’s narrative ends, and it is read here as a late complete example of the form, not as evidence for the 1830s. Its logic is the chapter’s: a crop grown by enslaved people, sold through a factor, charged with freight and commission, and converted into a balance due an estate.[^ch15-smith-account][^ch15-check-focus]

## 4. Households and work

### 4.1 The household economy and outwork

Before and beside the factory, much production remained in the household. Farm families made cotton and woolen goods and shoes for merchants who paid them by the piece. The work brought cash into households that had little of it.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

Outwork did not disappear when the mills appeared. It persisted alongside them, and many of the women who made goods at home or in small shops later supplied the early mills. The two systems overlapped, and the same families moved between them.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

The market reached the household before it built the factory, converting ordinary domestic labor into wage labor at piece rates. This is where the chapter’s question about independence and labor becomes most intimate. A woman who earned cash by the piece might gain a measure of independence from her father or husband, and she might also find her labor priced by a merchant she never met.[^ch15-check-household]

### 4.2 Separate spheres and the ideology of domesticity

As production moved out of the home, a new ideology assigned middle-class women to a separate sphere of home and family. What historians call the “cult of domesticity” was what Barbara Welter named the “cult of true womanhood” in a 1966 article. It held that women were designed for the roles of wife and mother and should cultivate piety, purity, submissiveness, and domesticity. Catharine Beecher’s *A Treatise on Domestic Economy* (1842) taught that American women held a “lofty and fortunate position” in return for their domestic role. The home was presented as a refuge from the market.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]

The ideal was designed for the wives and daughters of the White middle and upper classes. Free women forced into poorly paid work, and enslaved women throughout the South, were excluded from its protection. Harriet Jacobs’s narrative, published in 1860, measures the ideal against a household where the master’s power reached every room. The home could be a refuge for the woman who owned it and a workplace and a prison for the woman who cleaned it.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]

Separate spheres was less a description of how most people lived than a prescription that served the new market order. It gave the middle-class household a moral purpose at the very moment its economic purpose was being reorganized. It also helped mark the boundary between the women who could afford to stay home and the women who could not.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity][^ch15-check-household]

### 4.3 Enslaved households, women’s labor, and the limits of the “sphere”

Enslaved households knew nothing of separate spheres. On cotton plantations, enslaved women, men, and children worked the crop together. Planters measured each person’s daily yield of picked cotton and punished those who fell short. The household labor that sustained the workforce — cooking, cleaning, child care, gardening — was itself unpaid labor performed after the day’s field work.[^ch15-yawp-cotton][^ch15-check-slavery]

The domestic slave trade broke enslaved households by design, separating husbands from wives and parents from children. The same decades that exalted the middle-class home as a haven from the market made the plantation household a site of commodity production worked by people who could be sold at any moment. The contradiction is not a footnote to the history of the American home. It is the other half of it.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]

By 1850, 1.8 million enslaved people were growing and picking cotton, and the crop they made was the country’s leading export. The household that produced it was not a refuge from the market. It was the market’s foundation.[^ch15-nps-cotton-economy]

## 5. Banks and panics

### 5.1 The Second Bank and the Panic of 1819

The market ran on credit, and credit was unstable. The number of banks grew from three at the founding to about 200 by the end of the War of 1812. Federal land sales allowed farmers to buy on credit, and in the cotton country land, cotton, and enslaved people were financed together in a loop that held only so long as the price of cotton stayed high in Liverpool.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819]

The Second Bank of the United States, chartered in 1816, fed the boom through loans from its southern and western branches, then contracted sharply to restore its specie reserves. The currency in circulation fell to about half. Perhaps a third of the country’s banks failed. Cotton prices fell more than 50 percent in 1819. Land sales collapsed, merchant houses failed, and perhaps a fifth of wage earners nationally lost work, an estimate the sources give tentatively. In Boston alone, some 3,500 people were imprisoned for debt between 1820 and 1822.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819]

The panic discredited the bank, embittered the relief politics of the western states, and intensified southern fears about national power. Those fears became the political conflicts of the 1820s and 1830s, which Chapter 16 owns. The economic lesson was plainer: a market built on credit could fail everywhere at once.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-check-ch16]

### 5.2 The Bank War

Andrew Jackson’s war on the Second Bank destroyed the country’s central bank and left a fragmented, lightly regulated state banking system. Congress passed a bill to recharter the bank in 1832. Jackson vetoed it in July, calling the bank unconstitutional and a source of corruption and privilege, and won reelection that fall over Henry Clay by a wide margin.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]

Late in 1833 Treasury Secretary Roger B. Taney ordered the federal deposits moved out of the bank. Nicholas Biddle, the bank’s president, responded by contracting its lending. Loans fell from a high of 53 percent of assets in 1832 to about 40 percent in 1835, causing a minor panic that backfired and strengthened Jackson’s case.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]

The deposits went to state banks, many of them politically friendly “pet banks.” With no central bank to discipline them and no deposit insurance to make depositors monitor them, the state banks had little incentive to be careful. They invested heavily in land and public works. By paying off the national debt in January 1835 and distributing the surplus, the government poured money into those banks just as land speculation peaked. The stage was set for the panic that followed.[^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]

### 5.3 The Panic of 1837 and its aftermath

The Panic of 1837 began in the spring of that year and left the economy depressed into the mid-1840s. After Jackson’s veto, hundreds of new state banks issued paper currency far beyond their specie reserves. British banks began pulling back on lending to the United States in 1836, and Jackson’s Specie Circular required that federal land be purchased with gold and silver rather than paper, draining the banks of coin.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837]

In the spring of 1837 overextended banks suspended specie payments, and hundreds collapsed. Depositors were left with worthless notes, and prices plunged. The absence of a central bank made the contraction worse, because there was no institution to supply liquidity or coordinate a response. The costs fell on farmers, workers, and merchants who had borrowed against expectations that did not hold.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]

Whigs and Democrats each blamed the other, and the crisis reshaped the party conflict that Chapter 16 owns. The panic was the market’s reckoning with the speculation the previous decade had encouraged. It was also a verdict on a financial system that had been built to connect the country and had instead transmitted the shock from Liverpool and London to every county in the Union.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-check-ch16]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The evidence supports four conclusions. The first is that the economy was transformed between the 1790s and the 1840s: the United States built canals, roads, steamboats, and railroads; moved production into factories; and connected a continental interior to Atlantic markets.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-ny-canal-history]

The second is that cotton and slavery were central to the transformation. Cotton became the country’s leading export, and the labor that produced it was enslaved. The domestic slave trade moved roughly one million people to the cotton frontier, and the credit system was built on the value of their labor.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]

The third is that wage labor was contested from the start. The mills offered wages and independence on terms set by corporations, and the operatives struck against those terms almost as soon as the system matured.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]

The fourth is that the market was unstable and that it reached the household. The Panics of 1819 and 1837 showed that the new credit system could produce nationwide collapse, not just growth. Outwork and the ideology of separate spheres reorganized domestic labor around the market at the same time.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-americainclass-domesticity]

### 6.2 What is open

Five questions remain open. The first is timing. Sellers locates the revolution after 1815; Howe and others argue that market relations were already widespread in the eighteenth century. The answer determines whether the transformation was a rupture or an acceleration.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]

The second is whether it was coerced or chosen. Sellers describes a majority of farm families driven into market dependence. Howe answers that most farm families welcomed larger markets and were better off for them. The chapter has not declared a single verdict on this question, because the evidence differs by region, crop, and household.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]

The third is whether the Market Revolution is a single process or a bundle of regional and local changes that only looks unified in retrospect. New England factories, southern slavery, and western land were connected, but they were not the same experience.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-sources]

The fourth is where slavery belongs in the story. For some historians the market revolution is a northern story into which slavery intrudes. For others slavery is the engine of American capitalism. The cotton, credit, and internal trade examined here support the second view more than the first, but the debate is not closed.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-check-slavery]

The fifth is how the sources show inequality. The factor’s account, the mill’s payroll, and the census’s population columns count people in ways that reveal who held power. The chapter has read those sources critically rather than as neutral data. That reading is itself an argument, and it remains open to challenge.[^ch15-smith-account][^ch15-check-numbers]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the Market Revolution was real but not singular. It connected regions and created new freedoms while shifting risk onto workers, farm families, and enslaved people. It cannot be told apart from slavery or from the state’s role in building infrastructure.[^ch15-check-sources]

Charles Sellers’s *The Market Revolution* (1991) argued that establishing capitalist hegemony over the economy, politics, and culture between 1815 and 1846 was the defining event of the era. He described a transformation imposed on a largely subsistence farm population, and he took the coercion in the transition seriously. The book’s explanatory power made it enormously influential and also made it a target.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]

Daniel Walker Howe’s *What Hath God Wrought* (2007) rejects the term. Howe argues that market relations were earlier, that most farm families welcomed larger markets, and that a communications revolution of telegraph, postal service, and print defined the age. He reads the transformation as progress rather than tragedy.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe]

The skeptics and regionalists sharpen the argument. Daniel Feller’s warning that “a monograph that presupposes a market revolution will certainly discover one” captures a broader critique. John Lauritz Larson, Winifred Barr Rothenberg, and Christopher Clark emphasize gradualism, regional difference, and the persistence of older practices. Their work cautions against reading a single national revolution into local evidence.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-sources]

Historians of slavery and capitalism move the internal trade and the factorage system from the margins to the middle. Walter Johnson, Edward E. Baptist, Seth Rockman, and Sven Beckert argue that the transformation cannot be understood apart from enslaved labor and the credit built on it. Their work is debated in its strongest claims, and it sometimes reads later outcomes back into the 1820s and 1830s, but it has changed the questions the field asks.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-check-sources]

Read together, these positions say that the Market Revolution was a genuinely new scale of connection whose benefits and costs were distributed by power: by who owned land and enslaved people, who commanded credit, and who could sell their own labor. The debate is not a contest between teams. It is a set of overlapping arguments about which part of a connected story deserves the most weight.[^ch15-check-sources][^ch15-check-table]

## Legacies

The market order built between the 1790s and the 1840s outlasted the panic that closed the period. The canals and railroads tied the continent together and made the United States a single national economy. The cotton kingdom became the young republic’s most valuable export sector and supplied most of the world’s cotton, and with it the enslaved population grew from 697,624 in 1790 to 3,953,760 in 1860.[^ch15-pmc-slave-growth][^ch15-nps-cotton-economy]

The transformation left a divided inheritance. It created wage work, urban growth, and a national market. It did so through the dispossession of Native nations, the enslavement of millions of people, and a credit system that periodically ruined the very farmers and workers it had drawn in. The Panic of 1837 and the depression that followed showed that the market’s risks, like its rewards, were distributed by power.[^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-check-slavery]

The connections did not stop with the canal. The railroad and the telegraph, which the chapter’s last years only begin to show, would make the network faster and the shocks more immediate. The market that joined the continent would also make its sections more dangerous to one another.[^ch15-check-scope]

And the chapter’s central question endured. The same connections that made Americans more interdependent made them more exposed — to distant prices, distant banks, and distant decisions. Chapter 16 takes up the political crisis that the market’s demand for land and slavery reopened.[^ch15-check-ch16][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** British capital and British mills financed and consumed the American cotton crop. The Liverpool price set the terms of the southern planter’s year, and the Panic of 1837 followed a British contraction of credit. The American market was one part of an Atlantic economy.[^ch15-bbh-cotton-trade][^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- **Archives and silences.** The factor’s account, the census column, and the ship manifest count enslaved people as property and as commodities. The chapter names that accounting as evidence of who held power, not as neutral data, and it seeks the voices of the enslaved in narratives, testimony, and the records of the trade itself.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-smithsonian-trail-of-tears]
- **Distant connections.** The cotton frontier set up the Missouri crisis (Ch. 16) and the removal of Native nations (Ch. 17). The state-financed canals and the credit panics fed later debates over banks and the money supply (Chs. 16 and 27).[^ch15-check-ch16][^ch15-check-slavery]

## Summary

- The transportation revolution — turnpikes, canals, steamboats, and railroads — collapsed the cost of moving goods and connected the interior to Atlantic markets.[^ch15-bri-erie][^ch15-yawp-cotton]
- The Erie Canal, authorized in 1817 and opened in 1825, ran 363 miles with 83 locks and repaid its roughly $7 million cost in tolls within about nine years.[^ch15-ny-canal-history][^ch15-bri-erie]
- The factory began at Slater’s Pawtucket mill with water-powered machinery and child and family labor, and matured at Lowell, where corporations recruited young women into company boardinghouses.[^ch15-nps-slater][^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- Lowell operatives worked 12 to 14 hours a day, struck twice in the 1830s over wage cuts, and organized for a ten-hour day in the 1840s with little immediate success.[^ch15-nps-mill-girls]
- The cotton gin made inland cotton profitable; output doubled each decade after 1800, and by 1860 enslaved labor produced over two billion pounds a year, about three-quarters of the world supply by mid-century.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-nps-cotton-economy]
- The domestic slave trade forced roughly one million people from the Upper South to the Deep South between 1820 and 1860, breaking families and enriching traders like Franklin and Armfield.[^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]
- Cotton moved through factors who advanced credit and took a lien on the next crop, keeping planters in chronic debt to northern and British capital.[^ch15-ncpedia-factors][^ch15-woodman-review]
- Outwork persisted alongside the factory, while the ideology of separate spheres assigned middle-class women to the home — an ideal that excluded enslaved and poor women.[^ch15-americainclass-domesticity][^ch15-check-household]
- The Panics of 1819 and 1837 showed the new credit system’s instability; the Bank War destroyed the Second Bank and left a fragmented, lightly regulated state banking system.[^ch15-ec-hist-panic1819][^ch15-dpla-panic1837][^ch15-richmondfed-bankwar]
- The Market Revolution was real and uneven: it created new freedoms and concentrated new risks, and it cannot be told apart from the slavery and dispossession that financed it.[^ch15-hnn-sellers-howe][^ch15-check-slavery]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The market that Clinton celebrated on November 4, 1825, was real, and so was its price. The canal, the mill, and the cotton field were joined in a single system that moved goods, people, and money across a continent. It gave farm daughters wages, made New York a world port, and built the most productive export economy of its age. It also moved a million enslaved people from their homes, called mill girls out on strike at 20, and periodically wiped out the savings of farmers and workers through panics that began in distant banks. The chapter’s question is not whether the market liberated or enslaved Americans. It is how a single system could do both, and who decided which would happen to whom.[^ch15-na-cotton-gin][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade][^ch15-nps-mill-girls][^ch15-dpla-panic1837]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with Thomas Jefferson, retired at Monticello in 1820, following the Missouri controversy through his correspondence as the question of slavery’s extension returned to national politics. This chapter has followed the cotton boom, the credit that financed it, and the internal slave trade that fed it. Chapter 16 takes up the political crisis that the market’s demand for land and labor reopened.[^ch15-check-ch16][^ch15-nmaahc-domestic-trade]
