## Epigraphs

> “From the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes—tramps and millionaires.”[^ch27-omaha-platform]

— The People’s Party, Omaha Platform, adopted at Omaha, Nebraska, July 4, 1892.[^ch27-omaha-platform]

> “We cannot fly from our world duties; it is ours to execute the purpose of a fate that has driven us to be greater than our small intentions.”[^ch27-beveridge]

— Albert J. Beveridge, “The March of the Flag,” campaign speech, Indianapolis, September 16, 1898.[^ch27-beveridge]

> “It was in their more general picture of the world that they were most credulous and vulnerable.”[^ch27-hofstadter]

— Richard Hofstadter, *The Age of Reform: From Bryan to F.D.R.*, 1955.[^ch27-hofstadter]

- **Why these three.** An agrarian party declared that government injustice manufactured two new classes at the extremes. An imperial orator declared that the nation could not refuse its world duties. A historian argued that the same agrarians were at their most credulous in their general picture of the world. The three disagree about whether the decade’s movements were democratic promise, providential duty, or dangerous credulity, which is the chapter’s central disagreement.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-beveridge][^ch27-hofstadter]
- **The attribution rule.** The Omaha Platform is a party document without a single author; the preamble is sometimes attributed to Ignatius Donnelly, but that attribution rests on a study site and must be verified against the convention proceedings before it is stated. Beveridge’s sentence is transcribed from the Voices of Democracy scholarly text of the speech, whose copy-text is the newspaper printing of September 17, 1898. Hofstadter’s sentence is quoted from a secondary essay that reproduces it; it must be collated against the 1955 first edition with an exact page.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-beveridge][^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-check-epigraphs]
- **The historian’s voice.** Hofstadter supplies the required historian’s voice and frames the chapter’s first debate — whether the Populists were a democratic movement, a backward-looking protest, or a modernizing coalition. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The convention and the speaker.** The Democratic National Convention met in Chicago in July 1896 and turned to the money question. William Jennings Bryan, a former congressman from Nebraska, spoke in the debate over the platform’s silver plank and won the presidential nomination on the fifth ballot.[^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]
- **The speech.** Bryan argued that the gold standard fastened hardship on the producing classes and that bimetallism would restore them. He told the convention that “the great cities rest upon our broad and fertile prairies,” warned that if the farms were destroyed “the grass will grow in the streets of every city in the country,” and closed with the sentence that gave the speech its name: “You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.”[^ch27-cross-of-gold]
- **The setting.** The convention met in the Chicago Coliseum, and Bryan spoke on July 9, 1896. The Democratic platform adopted at the convention demanded “the free and unlimited coinage of both silver and gold at the present legal ratio of 16 to 1,” condemned the act of 1873 that had demonetized silver, and called gold monometallism “a British policy.”[^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-dem-platform-1896]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the place, the text of the speech, the platform, and the nomination ballot. It cannot show the weather in Chicago, the exact composition of the galleries, or Bryan’s private thoughts.[^ch27-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** The “cross of gold” line was famous immediately, but in July 1896 no one knew that Bryan would lose in November, that the Populist party would be destroyed by fusion, or that the nation would be at war with Spain within two years. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch27-check-scene][^ch27-vassar-populists]

### The scene’s argument

- **A revolt that had reached the convention floor.** The scene’s claim is that the agrarian revolt of the 1880s and 1890s had, by 1896, captured one of the two major parties’ presidential nomination. The distance between the cooperative store and the nominating ballot is the scene’s point, and the speech shows a movement’s demands translated into a national campaign.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-cross-of-gold]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question: how were economic democracy and overseas expansion connected? The answer begins with the currency, but it runs through the election, the war, and the colonial state as well.[^ch27-check-scope][^ch27-check-scene]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how were economic democracy and overseas expansion connected, and where did they diverge?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the convention hall to the depression’s breadlines, the Pullman yards, the Cuban harbor, and the Philippine archipelago.

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The date, the speech text, the platform, and the fifth-ballot nomination are documented. The mood of the hall, the noise of the galleries, and the private reasoning of Bryan or his opponents must not be staged beyond what the record states.[^ch27-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** In July 1896 no one knew the November result, the fusion’s outcome, or the coming war. The scene must preserve the uncertainty of the moment.[^ch27-check-scene][^ch27-check-dates]
- **No claim that the speech decided the nomination alone.** Bryan won on the fifth ballot after a contested convention; the speech is the documented high point of a campaign for the silver plank, not the whole cause of his nomination.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-check-scene]

## 1. Farmers’ movements and the Omaha Platform

### 1.1 The alliances and the cooperative movement

- **The claim.** The farmers’ movements began as an attempt to solve the problems of price and credit through cooperative buying, selling, and lending, and only later turned to independent political action. The economic program came first, and the party came out of the program’s failure.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-subtreasury]
- **The Southern Alliance.** Southern farmers organized after the Civil War as the price of cotton fell. A gathering in Lampasas County, Texas, in September 1877 began what became the National Farmers’ Alliance and Industrial Union, commonly called the Southern Farmers’ Alliance, which eventually claimed a membership of almost three million — one of the largest protest organizations in American history.[^ch27-farmers-alliance]
- **The cooperative strategy.** The Alliance tried trade agreements with merchants, then alliance-owned cooperative stores, mills, gins, and cotton exchanges, and finally a statewide exchange that bulked and sold cotton directly to manufacturers. The exchange made progress for a time, but the wholesalers’ hostility, the cost of dividends, and the foreclosure of the Texas exchange in 1889 weakened it.[^ch27-farmers-alliance]
- **The subtreasury plan.** In 1889 the Alliance adopted a plan, associated with Charles W. Macune, for federal warehouses, or subtreasuries, in counties that marketed at least $500,000 of crops a year. Farmers would deposit crops and receive federal notes for up to 80 percent of their value, with a year to sell and a small interest charge. The plan sought to move rural credit from merchants and landlords to the national government, and it was radical for exactly that reason. Sources differ on the interest rate (1 percent a year in one account, 2 percent in another) and on the authorship of the plan, so the chapter should name the version it uses.[^ch27-subtreasury][^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The Colored Alliance.** Because the Southern Alliance barred Black members, Black farmers organized the Colored Farmers’ National Alliance and Cooperative Union in Houston County, Texas, on December 11, 1886, with a white general superintendent, Richard Manning Humphrey, and a Black president and secretary. After a rival group merged into it in 1890, the Colored Alliance claimed 1,200,000 members in 1891 — a claim that should be given as a claim, since it was probably inflated.[^ch27-colored-farmers][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The strike and its cost.** The Colored Alliance was made up largely of landless cotton pickers, and in September 1891 it called for a cotton-pickers’ strike for a dollar per hundred pounds. The organization lacked the local leaders and the means of communication to unite its members, and the strike mostly failed; in one Arkansas community where pickers did strike, Whites crushed the action and killed 15 strikers. The episode contributed to the alliance’s rapid decline.[^ch27-colored-farmers]

### 1.2 The People’s Party and the Omaha Platform

- **The claim.** When cooperative self-help and the major parties failed them, the alliances moved into independent politics, and the People’s Party adopted a platform that proposed a national currency, public ownership of the railroads, and a federal warehouse and loan system. The platform is the chapter’s best single statement of what the movement wanted.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-farmers-alliance]
- **The party.** A third party became a reality in May 1891 in Texas, and the national People’s Party was founded at Omaha in 1892; its platform was closely tied to the national Alliance platform. In 1892 its presidential candidate, James B. Weaver, won 22 electoral votes and 1,029,357 popular votes, 8.5 percent of the total, carrying several western states.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-election-1892]
- **The Omaha Platform.** Adopted on July 4, 1892, the platform’s preamble charged that “corruption dominates the ballot-box, the Legislatures, the Congress, and touches even the ermine of the bench,” that “the newspapers are largely subsidized or muzzled,” and that from governmental injustice “we breed the two great classes—tramps and millionaires.” The platform proper demanded a national currency issued by the government through the subtreasury plan; the free and unlimited coinage of silver and gold at 16 to 1; circulating medium of not less than $50 per capita; a graduated income tax; postal savings banks; government ownership of the railroads, telegraph, and telephone; and the reclamation of land held by railroads and aliens.[^ch27-omaha-platform]
- **The wider program.** The platform’s resolutions, offered as expressions of sentiment rather than as planks, demanded the secret ballot, the initiative and referendum, the direct election of senators, a one-term presidency, the eight-hour day on government work, the abolition of the Pinkerton system, and further restriction of immigration. The chapter should present the reform program in full, because the retrospective image of Populism as merely a currency crusade distorts it.[^ch27-omaha-platform]
- **The limit.** The Omaha Platform said nothing that would dismantle the racial order; the Populists’ southern wing depended on Black votes while the party as a whole would not defend them, and the Southern Alliance condemned the federal elections bill that the Colored Alliance supported. The chapter should state this contradiction as the movement’s central weakness, and leave the making of the southern racial order to Chapter 26.[^ch27-colored-farmers][^ch27-check-ch26]

### 1.3 Orators, factions, and the road to fusion

- **The claim.** The movement produced a culture of mass meetings, newspapers, and orators, and by 1896 it had split between those who wanted to build an independent party and those who wanted to fuse with the Democrats.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-lease]
- **The orators.** Mary Elizabeth Lease, a Kansas Populist lecturer, became the movement’s most famous woman orator. The saying “raise less corn and more hell,” widely attributed to her, probably was not hers; the Kansas Historical Society records that she admitted it characterized her fight but did not originate it. The chapter should use the quotation, if at all, as an attribution problem rather than as an undisputed line.[^ch27-lease][^ch27-check-lease]
- **Thomas E. Watson.** Watson, a Georgia Populist, was among the movement’s most committed southern voices and, in 1896, warned that “fusion means the Populist party will play Jonah, and they will play the whale.” His later turn to White supremacy belongs to Chapter 26’s account of the new order; here he stands for the southern wing’s dilemma.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-check-ch26]
- **The two factions.** By 1896 the “fusion” Populists wanted to join the Democrats, while the “mid-roaders” wanted to keep an independent party. The chapter should present the split as a strategic argument within a movement under pressure, not as a contest of personalities.[^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The connection.** The movement’s decision to stake everything on free silver, and the fusion that followed, set up §3’s election. The chapter should close §1 with the movement at its height and hand the resolution to the next section.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-check-scope]

## 2. Depression, Coxey, and Pullman

### 2.1 The Panic of 1893

- **The claim.** The Panic of 1893 was one of the most severe financial crises in American history, and it turned the farmers’ grievance into a general social emergency. The depression’s scale — and its human cost — set the conditions for everything that followed.[^ch27-panic-1893]
- **The cause.** Two instabilities drove the panic. First, the Treasury’s gold reserves fell from about $190 million in 1890 to about $100 million, raising fears that the United States might suspend the convertibility of its notes. Second, economic activity had slowed and loan defaults had weakened bank balance sheets, so depositors began to withdraw funds, and withdrawals accelerated into runs.[^ch27-panic-1893]
- **The scale.** In June 1893 bank runs swept through midwestern and western cities such as Chicago and Los Angeles, and more than 100 banks suspended operations; from mid-July to mid-August another 340 banks suspended. As country banks drew down their deposits in New York, the money-center banks sold assets at falling prices, and the interior was left without currency.[^ch27-panic-1893]
- **The human cost.** Industrial production fell by 15.3 percent between 1892 and 1894, and unemployment rose to between 17 and 19 percent. The economy slumped again in late 1895 and did not fully recover until mid-1897.[^ch27-panic-1893][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The political consequence.** The panic discredited the governing party and made the currency the central issue of the decade. Its scale is the measure against which the chapter’s political responses — Coxey’s march, the Pullman injunction, and the silver campaign — should be read.[^ch27-panic-1893][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]

### 2.2 Coxey’s Army and the politics of unemployment

- **The claim.** The depression produced the first mass protest march on Washington, and the federal government’s response to unemployment was to treat the marchers as a public nuisance rather than as a claim on the state.[^ch27-coxey]
- **The march.** Jacob Coxey, a wealthy Ohio sand quarry operator whose own fortunes had suffered in the panic, believed the federal government should finance public works to create jobs. In the spring of 1894 he led several hundred unemployed followers from Massillon, Ohio, to Washington — one of the first mass protest marches on the capital — and drew extensive press coverage.[^ch27-coxey]
- **The reception.** The march was widely mocked; *Harper’s Weekly* called the mission a quixotic “farce comedy,” and the marchers’ ragged appearance was derided. Coxey’s journey ended when he was arrested for trespassing on the Capitol lawn on May 1, 1894, and the press declared that Washington “looked upon the movement as a joke.” Congress eventually enacted legislation in the spirit of Coxey’s proposals, but only in later depressions.[^ch27-coxey]
- **The argument.** The episode shows that the era’s political economy offered no recognized place for the unemployed as a class with claims on the federal government, and the chapter should state that absence rather than treating the march as an eccentric sideshow.[^ch27-coxey][^ch27-panic-1893]

### 2.3 Pullman and the injunction

- **The claim.** The Pullman strike of 1894 turned a wage dispute into a national test of whether the federal courts could break a strike by injunction, and the courts answered that they could.[^ch27-pullman][^ch27-in-re-debs]
- **The strike.** On May 11, 1894, thousands of workers at the Pullman Palace Car Company struck for wage increases and rent reductions in the company town. The American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs, launched a boycott in support, with members refusing to run trains carrying Pullman cars. The action began peacefully, then became violent, and it disrupted mail, food delivery, and passenger transportation.[^ch27-pullman]
- **The state’s response.** The federal government sought and obtained an injunction against the union, and President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago on July 3, 1894, over the protest of Governor John Peter Altgeld, who had not requested them and had not been informed. Debs and other leaders were convicted of contempt and jailed.[^ch27-pullman]
- **In re Debs.** In *In re Debs* (1895) the Supreme Court denied Debs’s petition for a writ of habeas corpus and upheld the government’s power to act against obstructions of interstate commerce and the mails. The Court wrote that “the strong arm of the national government may be put forth to brush away all obstructions to the freedom of interstate commerce or the transportation of the mails,” and it held that a defendant in a contempt proceeding is not entitled to a jury trial. The decision gave the labor injunction its constitutional warrant.[^ch27-in-re-debs]
- **The limit.** The strike’s wage figures — for example, an average cut of about 25 percent while rents held — appear in survey sources and need collation against the strike commission’s report before they are used. The chapter should state the mechanism (wages cut, rents unchanged, workers organized, courts intervened) and let the figures follow their sources.[^ch27-pullman][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The connection.** Pullman shows the state acting on behalf of the railroads. Six years later, the same federal power would be turned outward, in the Caribbean and the Pacific, and §4 takes up that turn.[^ch27-in-re-debs][^ch27-check-scope]

## 3. Silver and the 1896 election

### 3.1 The silver question

- **The claim.** The money question was a fight over the definition of money itself, and the sequence of silver legislation — the act of 1873, the Bland–Allison Act, the Sherman Silver Purchase Act, and its 1893 repeal — is the record of that fight.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896][^ch27-sherman-repeal]
- **The “crime of ’73.”** The Democratic platform of 1896 declared that “the act of 1873 demonetizing silver without the knowledge or approval of the American people” had appreciated gold, depressed prices, and enriched “the money-lending class at home and abroad.” Advocates called it a crime; the act was a technical revision of the coinage laws, and the chapter should distinguish the label from the statute.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896]
- **The silver acts.** Congress required limited silver purchases under the Bland–Allison Act of 1878 and enlarged them under the Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890, but neither restored free coinage and neither stopped deflation. When the crisis of 1893 struck, President Cleveland called a special session and won repeal of the Sherman Act’s silver-purchase provisions, overcoming an 88-day Senate filibuster.[^ch27-sherman-repeal][^ch27-panic-1893]
- **The stakes.** The platform’s money plank demanded “the free and unlimited coinage of both silver and gold at the present legal ratio of 16 to 1 without waiting for the aid or consent of any other nation,” declared gold monometallism “a British policy,” and called for an income tax and for curbs on the labor injunction. The chapter should read the platform as a program, not as a single-issue document.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896]

### 3.2 Bryan and the cross of gold

- **The claim.** Bryan’s speech and nomination converted the silver Democrats’ cause into a national campaign and forced the Populists to choose between independence and fusion.[^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The speech.** Speaking on July 9, 1896, Bryan defended the producing classes against the holders of capital. He said that “the man who is employed for wages is as much a business man as his employer,” that “the great cities rest upon our broad and fertile prairies,” and that if the farms were destroyed “the grass will grow in the streets of every city in the country.” He closed: “You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.”[^ch27-cross-of-gold]
- **The nomination.** Bryan won the nomination on the fifth ballot. His campaign staked the party on free silver and, in doing so, took the Populists’ central issue and left the third party with little distinctive ground.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The limit.** The speech text used here is a transcription; it must be collated against the official proceedings of the convention and the 1896 campaign volume before quotation. The chapter should say which text it follows.[^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-check-sources]

### 3.3 Fusion, the campaign, and the result

- **The claim.** The election of 1896 was a contest between two conceptions of the economy and two campaign styles, and its result realigned national politics for a generation while destroying the Populist party.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The fusion.** The Populists met at St. Louis on July 24–26, 1896, and endorsed Bryan for president, despite the warnings of the mid-roaders. The convention refused to accept Arthur Sewall, the Democrats’ vice-presidential choice, and nominated Tom Watson instead; when Bryan did not renounce Sewall, Watson refused to campaign. Bryan “virtually ignored the People’s Party, even though he was its nominee,” and the fusion “killed the party’s hopes for national power.”[^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The McKinley campaign.** The Republicans nominated William McKinley at St. Louis on a platform endorsing protective tariffs and the gold standard, with a plank leaving the door open to international bimetallism. McKinley’s manager, Marcus Hanna, raised a reported $4 million for a campaign that printed about 200 million pamphlets and employed about 1,400 speakers, while McKinley delivered some 350 speeches from his front porch in Canton, Ohio, to about 750,000 visiting delegates. The chapter should present the campaign’s organization as part of the era’s political economy, not as mere spectacle.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign]
- **The result.** McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan’s 176, and he took 7,105,076 popular votes (51.1 percent) to Bryan’s 6,370,897 (45.8 percent), out of a total of 13,905,691. Other compilations give slightly different popular totals because they count fusion votes differently, and the chapter should give the source of its series. McKinley’s victory launched a period of Republican national power that lasted until 1932, broken only by Woodrow Wilson’s victory in 1912.[^ch27-election-1896][^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **What the election settled.** The result ended the Populists’ national hopes and confirmed the Republicans as the majority party. It also confirmed the “Solid South”: because disfranchisement had already removed most Black voters, the southern electoral college was safe for the Democrats, and the fusion politics that had threatened the new order was defeated in the South along with Bryan nationally.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-check-ch26]

### 3.4 The end of the third party and the survival of its ideas

- **The claim.** The Populist party vanished from the national map after 1896, but its program survived in the Progressive and New Deal eras.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]
- **The end.** Watson’s prediction was borne out: fusion on free silver “de-railed the rest of Populists’ agenda and killed the party’s hopes for national power,” and while Populists continued to hold power in a few western states, the party left the national stage.[^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The survival.** Direct election of senators became law by constitutional amendment in 1913; the graduated income tax came with the Sixteenth Amendment the same year; and aid to farmers and public works in depression became reality in the New Deal. The chapter should present this as a legacy with dates, not as vindication.[^ch27-vassar-populists]
- **The connection.** The farmers’ revolt had been answered with a fusion that absorbed and then dissolved it, and the currency issue had been settled not by free silver but by the discovery of new gold. The chapter should close §3 by turning from the currency to the war, and note that the same election year that destroyed the Populists also brought the coming of empire closer.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-check-scope]

## 4. The Spanish-American War and Hawaii

### 4.1 Cuba, the Maine, and the decision for war

- **The claim.** The war with Spain grew out of Cuba’s rebellion, Spain’s reconcentration policy, the sinking of the USS *Maine*, and a press war at home; the decision for war was McKinley’s, and it came after he had resisted it.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-de-lome][^ch27-check-decision]
- **Cuba’s rebellion.** Cuban revolutionaries had been fighting Spain since 1895, and the conflict produced economic and political instability close to the United States. American sympathy for the rebels grew from outrage at Spanish tactics and from long-standing interest in removing European colonial power from the hemisphere.[^ch27-spanish-war-state]
- **The de Lôme letter.** Cuban revolutionaries intercepted a private letter from the Spanish ambassador, Enrique Dupuy de Lôme, calling McKinley “weak and a bidder for the admiration of the crowd,” and released it to the Hearst press, which published it on February 9, 1898, in the New York *Journal*. The letter helped fuel readiness for war.[^ch27-de-lome]
- **The Maine.** On February 15, 1898, the battleship USS *Maine* exploded and sank in Havana harbor, killing 266 of her 354 crew members. The cause was never settled; a court of inquiry blamed a submarine mine, later investigations suggested an internal explosion, and the chapter should give the competing findings along with the figure and its variation across sources.[^ch27-maine][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The decision.** McKinley asked Congress on April 11, 1898, for authority to end the fighting and establish “a stable government”; Congress passed a joint resolution on April 20 that acknowledged Cuban independence and disclaimed any intention to annex Cuba, and Congress declared war on April 25. The Teller Amendment, proposed by Senator Henry M. Teller of Colorado, stated that the United States “hereby disclaims any disposition or intention to exercise sovereignty, jurisdiction, or control over said Island except for pacification thereof.”[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-teller]

### 4.2 The “splendid little war”

- **The claim.** The war’s Caribbean and Pacific campaigns lasted only months, and the ease of the victories concealed how much the settlement would change.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-loc-1898]
- **Manila Bay.** On May 1, 1898, Commodore George Dewey’s Asiatic Squadron destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay. Dewey brought the exiled Filipino leader Emilio Aguinaldo back to the islands aboard an American warship, and Aguinaldo’s forces kept up operations until American troops arrived at the end of July.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]
- **Cuba.** U.S. troops landed at Guantánamo Bay in June and near Santiago later that month. On July 1, 1898, dismounted cavalry including the African American Ninth and Tenth cavalries and the Rough Riders under Lieutenant Colonel Theodore Roosevelt attacked the San Juan heights. The Spanish Caribbean squadron was destroyed on July 3, and Santiago surrendered on July 16. The chapter should name the Black regiments that fought there, because the war’s racial order and the army’s segregation are part of its history.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-check-race]
- **The armistice.** A cease-fire was signed on August 12, 1898. The United States also invaded Puerto Rico in late July with little resistance and installed a military government in October.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico]
- **The cost.** The war cost the United States about $250 million and about 3,000 lives, of whom roughly 90 percent died of infectious disease rather than combat. The chapter should give the disease share, because it explains the war’s human cost better than the battlefield accounts alone.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-check-numbers]

### 4.3 The Treaty of Paris and the annexations

- **The claim.** The Treaty of Paris gave the United States an overseas empire, and the Senate ratified it by the narrowest possible margin.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-treaty-ratification]
- **The treaty.** Signed on December 10, 1898, the treaty required Spain to relinquish claims on Cuba and to cede Guam and Puerto Rico to the United States, and it provided that Spain would sell the Philippines to the United States for $20 million. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, by a vote of 57 to 27 — just over the two-thirds required.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-treaty-ratification]
- **The Senate debate.** The vote turned on empire. Senator George Frisbie Hoar warned that the treaty would “make us a vulgar, commonplace empire, controlling subject races and vassal states,” while Senator Knute Nelson held that “Providence has given the United States the duty of extending Christian civilization” and Henry Cabot Lodge argued that rejection would brand the nation as incapable of taking “rank as one of the greatest world powers.” The chapter should stage these as the era’s central argument over what the republic was for.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-check-race]
- **Map 21.** The registered visual `ch27-map-01` (Map 21, “American empire, 1898–1902”) belongs in §§4.3–5.1. It should distinguish three things the register requires: annexation (Hawaii by joint resolution, and the cessions and purchase under the Treaty of Paris), occupation (Cuba, under the Teller Amendment and later the Platt Amendment), and control (the bases and the later instruments that belong to Chapter 30). It should state the observation years, mark boundaries and claims as uncertain where they were contested, and carry a caption, source line, credit, and accessible description. The underlying evidence is the Treaty of Paris, the Newlands Resolution, the Platt Amendment, and the war records named in the register.[^ch27-check-figure][^ch27-check-ch30][^ch27-platt][^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-spanish-war-state]
- **The connection.** With the treaty ratified, the republic held the Philippines, and the war that had been fought to free Cuba had produced a colonial state in Asia. §5 takes up that state and the war it required.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-philippine-war-state]

### 4.4 Hawaii and the annexation by joint resolution

- **The claim.** Hawaii was annexed during the war by a joint resolution of Congress rather than by treaty, after a mass petition by Native Hawaiians failed to stop a government established by a coup.[^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-hawaii-petition]
- **The coup.** In 1893, a group of non-native businessmen and politicians — the “Committee of Safety,” led by Sanford Dole, and supported by the U.S. minister John Stevens and Marines from the USS *Boston* — overthrew Queen Lili‘uokalani in a bloodless coup and proclaimed a provisional government. A special investigator found that the queen had been overthrown illegally and ordered the American flag lowered, but she never regained power; the provisional government proclaimed the Republic of Hawaii in 1894 with Dole as president.[^ch27-hawaii-archives]
- **The treaty and the petition.** In June 1897 McKinley signed a treaty of annexation with representatives of the Republic of Hawaii and submitted it to the Senate. Native Hawaiian organizations mounted a mass petition drive that gathered 21,269 signatures against annexation — more than half of the roughly 39,000 Native Hawaiians and part-Hawaiians counted that year — and the treaty was defeated. The chapter should use the petition number and the census denominator from the same source.[^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-hawaii-petition][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The Newlands Resolution.** After the *Maine*’s destruction made Hawaii look strategically valuable as a mid-Pacific fueling station and naval base, pro-annexation forces used a joint resolution, which required only a simple majority in both houses rather than a two-thirds treaty vote. House Joint Resolution 259, the Newlands Resolution, passed Congress and was signed by McKinley on July 7, 1898, formally annexing the islands; the transfer ceremony followed on August 12, and Dole became the first governor of the Territory of Hawaii.[^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-spanish-war-state]
- **The apology.** In 1993, the hundredth anniversary of the overthrow, Congress acknowledged that the Kingdom of Hawaii had been overthrown illegally, apologized for the overthrow and the deprivation of Native Hawaiians’ right to self-determination, and committed itself to reconciliation. The chapter should carry this as the later acknowledgment of the injustice, clearly labeled as a consequence and not as the view of 1898.[^ch27-hawaii-apology][^ch27-check-ch30]

## 5. The Philippine-American War, anti-imperialism, and the Open Door

### 5.1 The war in the Philippines

- **The claim.** Filipinos who had fought for independence from Spain fought the United States for independence in turn, and the war that followed was longer, deadlier, and more divisive than the war with Spain.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]
- **The comity that broke.** Aguinaldo had returned to the islands with American forces and believed the United States would support independence; when the United States made clear that it intended to impose colonial control, the early clashes of 1899 became an all-out war. Filipinos proclaimed an independent republic, and Aguinaldo was its president.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-philippine-war-state]
- **Aguinaldo’s protest.** Aguinaldo issued a manifesto protesting the American military government: “General Otis styles himself Military Governor of these Islands, and I protest one and a thousand times … against such authority. … I hereby protest against this unexpected act of the United States claiming sovereignty over these Islands.” The chapter should let the Filipino side speak in its own words, and should explain that “insurrection” was the American term for a war the Filipinos saw as a defense against invasion.[^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-check-names][^ch27-check-race]
- **Benevolent assimilation.** McKinley’s executive order of December 21, 1898, instructed the military commander to tell the islanders that the United States came “not as invaders or conquerors, but as friends,” and that “the mission of the United States is one of benevolent assimilation, substituting the mild sway of justice and right for arbitrary rule.” The war began on February 4, 1899, two days before the Senate ratified the treaty.[^ch27-benevolent][^ch27-philippine-war-state]
- **The course of the war.** The first phase, from February to November 1899, was dominated by Aguinaldo’s conventional attempts against better-equipped American troops; the second was guerrilla war, beginning in November 1899 and lasting through Aguinaldo’s capture in 1901 and into 1902. President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed a general amnesty and declared the conflict over on July 4, 1902, although resistance continued in some areas for years, and Muslim Moro communities in the south resisted until well after 1902.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga]
- **The methods.** The war was brutal on both sides. U.S. forces burned villages, implemented reconcentration, and tortured suspected guerrillas; Filipino fighters also tortured captured soldiers and terrorized civilians who cooperated with the Americans. Many civilians died from fighting, cholera and malaria, and famine. On September 28, 1901, Filipino fighters armed largely with bolos attacked an American company at Balangiga on Samar, killing 48 of the 74 men; the American response was a devastating campaign that crushed resistance on the island by mid-1902. The chapter should name the brutality on both sides and the war’s cost to civilians, and should not let “pacification” stand as a description of the campaign.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga][^ch27-samar-michigan][^ch27-check-race]
- **The cost.** The war killed over 4,200 American service members and over 20,000 Filipino combatants, and as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died from violence, famine, and disease. The chapter should give the figures as estimates with their source and should distinguish combatant from civilian deaths.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-check-numbers]
- **The colonial state.** Even as the fighting continued, the United States established a colonial government under William Howard Taft in 1900 and pursued a “policy of attraction” that offered a degree of self-government and social reform while undermining the revolutionaries. Congress passed the Philippine Organic Act in 1902, providing for a civil government with an elected lower house; the first elected assembly convened in 1907.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-philippine-organic-act]

### 5.2 In Focus: “The White Man’s Burden”

**In Focus.** Rudyard Kipling’s “The White Man’s Burden” is the chapter’s close reading. Published in February 1899, as the Senate debated the treaty and the Philippine war began, the poem urges the United States to assume colonial rule: “Take up the White Man’s burden— / Send forth the best ye breed— / Go, bind your sons to exile / To serve your captives’ need.” Its first stanza calls the Filipinos “new-caught, sullen peoples, / Half devil and half child,” and its later stanzas warn that empire brings “the blame of those ye better” and “the hate of those ye guard.” The box should read the poem as an argument for empire that is also a warning about its costs, and should note that American imperialists took from it a justifying phrase while anti-imperialists — and Filipinos — answered it. The poem was read aloud in the Senate by Benjamin Tillman as an argument against the treaty, which shows that the same text served both sides. The box must not treat the poem as evidence about Filipinos; it is evidence about how empire was justified, and the poem’s title and wording should be collated against the February 1899 *McClure’s Magazine* printing.[^ch27-kipling][^ch27-kipling-wiki][^ch27-check-focus][^ch27-check-race]

- **The answers.** The surrounding section carries the answers to Kipling. Henry Labouchère’s “The Brown Man’s Burden,” published in February 1899, parodied Kipling’s form and diction to indict conquest: “Pile on the brown man’s burden / To gratify your greed,” and “in the name of freedom, / Don’t hesitate to shoot.” The poem’s reader’s note warns that Labouchère reproduces racist language deliberately and that the words are degrading even in satire; the chapter should name that tension rather than quote the degrading terms without comment. Aguinaldo’s protest and the Anti-Imperialist League’s platform supply the Filipino and American answers in the same section.[^ch27-brown-burden][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-check-race]

### 5.3 Anti-imperialism at home

- **The claim.** The annexation of the Philippines produced an organized anti-imperialist movement that joined old free-trade Democrats, reformers, and public figures, and it argued in the language of the Declaration of Independence.[^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-twain-loc]
- **The League.** The American Anti-Imperialist League was founded in 1899, after the United States occupied Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, by citizens including Andrew Carnegie and William James. Its platform held that “the policy known as imperialism is hostile to liberty and tends toward militarism,” that “the subjugation of any people is ‘criminal aggression,’” and that governments “derive their just powers from the consent of the governed.” It demanded the immediate cessation of the war and independence for the Filipinos.[^ch27-anti-imperialist]
- **Mark Twain.** Twain joined the League and became one of its most quoted voices. He recalled that he had set out “a red-hot imperialist,” but after reading the Treaty of Paris had concluded that “we do not intend to free, but to subjugate the people of the Philippines,” and declared, “I am an anti-imperialist.” His essay “To the Person Sitting in Darkness” (1901) treated the mission of civilization as a fraud practiced on the world. The chapter should use Twain as a public voice, not as a neutral witness.[^ch27-twain-loc][^ch27-anti-imperialist]
- **The argument’s limits.** Anti-imperialists did not speak with one mind. Some opposed empire because they thought it wrong to rule other peoples without consent; others feared that annexation would bring non-White subjects into the American political nation. The chapter should give both motives and should not let the League’s noble language hide the racism of part of its coalition.[^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-kipling-wiki][^ch27-check-race]
- **Bryan and the treaty.** William Jennings Bryan opposed empire but urged ratification of the treaty so that the United States could then grant the Philippines independence; the chapter should record this position and the 1900 rematch, in which Bryan made anti-imperialism a central issue and lost again, this time with Roosevelt on the Republican ticket.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]

### 5.4 The Open Door and the construction of colonial rule

- **The claim.** While the Philippines were being conquered, the United States announced a China policy of equal commercial access, and it built a system of colonial government in the Caribbean and the Pacific whose legal categories were invented as it went.[^ch27-open-door][^ch27-platt][^ch27-downes]
- **The Open Door.** Secretary of State John Hay sent the first Open Door Note on September 6, 1899, asking the powers active in China to keep the market open to all nations and to respect China’s territorial integrity; a second note on July 3, 1900, during the Boxer Rebellion, repeated the request. The notes were not binding and committed no one, but they became the official statement of American policy in East Asia. The chapter should note the irony that Hay announced an open door for trade in China while the United States was closing the door on Chinese immigration.[^ch27-open-door][^ch27-check-race]
- **Puerto Rico and the Insular Cases.** The Foraker Act of 1900 ended military rule in Puerto Rico and established a civil government with an appointed governor and a limited elected house, designating the island an unorganized territory whose residents were not U.S. citizens. When the Supreme Court upheld the tariff the act imposed, Justice Henry Brown wrote in *Downes v. Bidwell* (1901) that Puerto Rico was “a territory appurtenant and belonging to the United States, but not a part of the United States within the revenue clauses of the Constitution.” The Insular Cases thus created the category of the unincorporated territory, in which the Constitution applied only in part.[^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-downes]
- **The Platt Amendment.** In 1901 Congress attached to the Army Appropriations Bill the Platt Amendment, which required Cuba to accept limitations on its sovereignty as a condition of the withdrawal of American troops. Article III gave the United States “the right to intervene for the preservation of Cuban independence,” and Article VII required Cuba to sell or lease lands for coaling or naval stations, the principal one at Guantánamo Bay. Cuba reluctantly wrote the amendment into its constitution, and it governed United States–Cuba relations until its repeal in 1934 during the Good Neighbor policy. The chapter should present the amendment as the document that qualified the Teller Amendment, and should leave the later instruments of Caribbean control to Chapter 30.[^ch27-platt][^ch27-teller][^ch27-check-ch30]
- **The connection.** By 1902 the republic held an empire, governed it through invented legal categories, and had declared its war in the Philippines over. The chapter should close §5 by handing the later instruments of control to Chapter 30 and the culture of the new century to Chapter 28.[^ch27-check-ch30][^ch27-check-ch28]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The decade’s movements were connected.** The farmers’ revolt, the depression, the silver campaign, the election of 1896, and the turn to empire followed one another, and the political economy tied them together: falling prices, credit scarcity, and the search for markets and outlets ran through the Populist program and the argument for expansion.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-panic-1893][^ch27-empire-debate]
- **The Populists were not merely a currency crusade.** The Omaha Platform proposed a national currency, government railroads, a graduated income tax, the secret ballot, and a broad reform program, and the subtreasury plan sought to restructure rural credit. Any account of the movement must engage the program, not only the silver plank.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-subtreasury]
- **The war and the empire were contested as they were made.** The Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris by a single vote over two-thirds, and the Anti-Imperialist League, Mark Twain, and Filipino leaders argued against annexation in the language of the Declaration. The empire was not a consensus.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]
- **The cost fell on the colonized.** The Philippine-American War, however named, killed thousands of American and Filipino combatants and as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians, and U.S. forces used torture, reconcentration, and village burning. The chapter’s account must carry that cost.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga][^ch27-samar-michigan]
- **The decade realigned politics.** McKinley’s victory began a period of Republican ascendancy, the Populist party dissolved, and the territorial acquisitions set the terms of American foreign policy for a generation.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-open-door]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Were the Populists democratic radicals or backward-looking losers?** Hofstadter made them “atavistic losers in the process of modernization,” driven by an “agrarian myth” and prone to conspiracy thinking; Goodwyn, Kazin, and Postel recast them as forward-looking radicals whose cooperative movement created a culture of democratic promise, and Postel argued they were commercially and intellectually engaged with the wider world. The question is interpretive and shapes how the whole decade is read.[^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-hofstadter-wiki]
- **Why did the United States take the Philippines?** Economic accounts hold that the search for markets and outlets drove expansion; strategic accounts stress naval power, coaling stations, and the “large policy” of Lodge, Roosevelt, and Mahan; ideological accounts stress race, civilization, and manhood; and contingency accounts stress McKinley’s incremental decision and the absence of a plan. The chapter should state the strongest version of each and test it against the timetable of 1898.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-tnsr][^ch27-bederman]
- **Was empire a fulfillment or a departure?** Samuel Flagg Bemis treated 1898 as an “aberration” in an otherwise anti-imperial history; William Appleman Williams and Walter LaFeber argued that the United States had been expanding economically long before 1898 and that empire was continuous with its history. The chapter should show that the choice is partly a definitional one about what counts as empire.[^ch27-empire-debate]
- **What connected Populism to empire?** Some accounts link the agrarian crisis to the search for foreign markets, and Hofstadter linked the 1890s’ domestic tensions to a “psychic crisis” that made war attractive. The evidence for each mechanism is partial, and the chapter should state the connection as contested rather than mechanical.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-tnsr][^ch27-hofstadter]
- **When did the Philippine-American War end?** The conventional date is July 4, 1902, when Roosevelt declared the conflict over, but organized resistance continued, and Moro communities in the south resisted into the 1910s. The chapter’s 1902 endpoint is a planning choice, and anything after it must be labeled as consequence.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-check-dates]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the 1890s’ movements were at once democratic and exclusionary, modernizing and backward-looking, and in which the turn to empire was driven by economics, strategy, ideology, and contingency together. No single explanation accounts for it, and the chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-check-sources]
- **The Populist positions.** Hofstadter made Populism a problem of political style and status; Goodwyn made it a problem of democratic movement culture; Postel and Kazin made it a modernizing, commercially engaged politics. Each captures part of the record and each reflects its author’s own political moment.[^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-hofstadter-wiki]
- **The empire positions.** Economic, strategic, ideological, and contingent accounts each explain a different part of the 1898 decision, and the strongest accounts combine them rather than choosing one.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-tnsr][^ch27-bederman]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The republic took up both projects — a more democratic political economy at home and colonial rule abroad — in the same decade, and it pursued the second while failing at the first. The chapter’s synthesis should hold the two together without claiming that one was the other’s hidden purpose.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-check-table]

## Legacies

The decade’s settlements outlasted the people who made them. The election of 1896 confirmed Republican national power until 1932, and it buried the Populist party, though direct election of senators, the income tax, and depression-era farm and public-works programs realized parts of the Omaha Platform. The currency question that had convulsed the decade was settled not by free silver but by new gold discoveries, and the issue faded within a decade.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-omaha-platform]

The territorial settlements proved more durable. The Philippines remained an American possession until independence in 1946; Puerto Rico became an unincorporated territory whose residents would receive citizenship only in 1917 and whose status remains unresolved; and the United States kept its naval base at Guantánamo Bay after the Platt Amendment was repealed in 1934. Hawaii became a state in 1959, and Congress apologized for the 1893 overthrow only in 1993.[^ch27-philippine-organic-act][^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-platt][^ch27-hawaii-apology]

The chapter’s central problem — how a republic that declared that governments derive their powers from the consent of the governed came to rule other peoples without their consent — did not end in 1902. The debate over the Philippines was the nation’s first sustained argument about the meaning of its own founding principles abroad, and the arguments then made for and against empire, in the language of liberty and of civilization, would recur. The chapter should present that contested memory as part of the history, with the evidence on each side.[^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-check-race]

## Summary

- The farmers’ alliances grew from cooperative buying and selling into a political movement; the People’s Party was founded at Omaha in 1892 and its platform demanded a national currency through the subtreasury plan, free silver at 16 to 1, a graduated income tax, and government ownership of railroads.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-subtreasury][^ch27-omaha-platform]
- The Colored Farmers’ National Alliance organized Black farmers separately, claimed 1,200,000 members in 1891, and saw its 1891 cotton-pickers’ strike crushed; the Southern Alliance would not defend Black voting rights.[^ch27-colored-farmers]
- The Panic of 1893 brought the failure of more than 100 banks in June and 340 more by mid-August, a 15.3 percent fall in industrial production, and unemployment of 17 to 19 percent, setting off years of protest and political realignment.[^ch27-panic-1893]
- Jacob Coxey’s 1894 march was the first mass protest march on Washington and ended with his arrest for trespassing on the Capitol lawn; the Pullman strike of 1894 ended with a federal injunction, federal troops in Chicago, and the Supreme Court’s decision in *In re Debs* upholding the labor injunction.[^ch27-coxey][^ch27-pullman][^ch27-in-re-debs]
- The 1896 Democratic platform demanded free silver and called gold monometallism a British policy; Bryan won the nomination on the fifth ballot with the “cross of gold” speech, and the Populists fused with the Democrats and nominated Watson for vice president.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896][^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-vassar-populists]
- McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan’s 176 and 51.1 percent of the popular vote to Bryan’s 45.8 percent; the result began a period of Republican power and destroyed the Populist party.[^ch27-election-1896][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]
- The sinking of the USS *Maine* on February 15, 1898, killing 266 of 354 crew members, helped produce the war with Spain; the United States won the war in months and the Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris on February 6, 1899, by 57 to 27.[^ch27-maine][^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-treaty-ratification]
- Under the Treaty of Paris, Spain gave up Cuba, ceded Guam and Puerto Rico, and sold the Philippines to the United States for $20 million; Hawaii was annexed by the Newlands Resolution on July 7, 1898, after a petition of 21,269 Native Hawaiians failed to stop the treaty.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-hawaii-archives]
- The Philippine-American War began on February 4, 1899, and killed over 4,200 American service members and over 20,000 Filipino combatants, while as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died; Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902, though resistance continued.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga]
- Kipling’s “The White Man’s Burden” (1899) justified empire as a civilizing duty, and the Anti-Imperialist League, Mark Twain, and Filipino leaders answered it in the language of the Declaration of Independence.[^ch27-kipling][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-twain-loc][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]
- The Platt Amendment gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba and a naval base at Guantánamo Bay; the Foraker Act and the Insular Cases made Puerto Rico an unincorporated territory; and the Open Door Notes made equal commercial access to China an official policy.[^ch27-platt][^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-downes][^ch27-open-door]

> *A closing note.* In the dozen years between the Omaha Platform and the declared end of the Philippine-American War, the republic answered its farmers with a fusion that dissolved their party and its declared principles with an empire that its own citizens never fully endorsed. It promised economic democracy at home and preached civilization abroad, and it did the second while leaving the first unfinished. The chapter’s question — how economic democracy and overseas expansion were connected, and where they diverged — is answered by the record of a decade in which the producing classes were heard and then absorbed, and in which the republic that had declared that governments rest on the consent of the governed claimed rule over peoples who had not consented.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with a small box camera sold to the public in 1900, an object almost anyone could afford and almost anyone could point at the world. The empire and the mass publics of the 1890s had made an audience that the new century would organize in ways the Populists and the anti-imperialists could not have imagined. Chapter 28 takes up that audience — its press, its goods, its entertainments, and its universities — and asks whether a shared culture dissolved the boundaries of class, race, and region, or only reorganized them.[^ch27-check-ch28][^ch27-check-bridge]
