# Chapter 34. The New Deal (1933–1939)

> “So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself—nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance.”[^ch34-first-inaugural]
>
> — Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933.[^ch34-first-inaugural]

> “We have to limit fortunes. Our present plan is that we will allow no one man to own more than $50 million.”[^ch34-huey-long-share]
>
> — Huey P. Long, “Every Man a King” radio address, February 23, 1934.[^ch34-huey-long-share]

> “[The] major policies of the New Deal and Fair Deal of the 1930s and 1940s, inflected by the preferences of the southern wing of the Democratic Party, had massively advantaged American whites while often excluding African-Americans, especially the majority who still lived in the seventeen ‘Jim Crow’ southern states that mandated racial segregation.”[^ch34-katznelson]
>
> — Ira Katznelson, “When Affirmative Action Was White,” History & Policy, 2005.[^ch34-katznelson]

A president told a frightened country that its worst enemy was fear. A senator from Louisiana told radio audiences that the real enemy was concentrated wealth, and that the New Deal had not gone far enough. A political scientist argued that the New Deal had built security for some Americans by excluding others. The three disagree about whether the problem was confidence, distribution, or exclusion. That disagreement is the chapter’s subject: what kind of security did the New Deal create, and for whom? The three quotations come from different moments and genres, and the disagreements among them are part of the evidence.[^ch34-first-inaugural][^ch34-huey-long-share][^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-check-epigraphs]

On the evening of Sunday, March 12, 1933, Roosevelt spoke to the nation by radio about the banks. The banking system had been closing for weeks, and he had proclaimed a national holiday on March 6 that shut the remaining banks, including the Federal Reserve. On March 9, Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]

He began by addressing “the overwhelming majority who use banks for the making of deposits and the drawing of checks.” He explained that a bank invests deposits in loans and securities rather than storing them in a vault, keeping only enough currency for ordinary withdrawals. When depositors rushed to convert their deposits into cash, even sound banks could not sell assets fast enough except at panic prices. The holiday had given the government time to examine the banks and to print currency against their good assets. Reopening would begin the next morning in the twelve Federal Reserve Bank cities, continue in cities with clearinghouses, and then extend to smaller places. A bank that opened later, he said, was “in exactly the same status as the bank that opens tomorrow.”[^ch34-fireside-chat]

“I can assure you that it is safer to keep your money in a reopened bank than under the mattress,” Roosevelt said. Near the end he returned to the theme of his inaugural address: “there is an element in the readjustment of our financial system more important than currency, more important than gold, and that is the confidence of the people.” He closed: “It is your problem no less than it is mine. Together we cannot fail.”[^ch34-fireside-chat]

The record establishes the date, the medium, the text, and the sequence: banks reopened on March 13, and depositors lined up to return money. Currency held by the public had risen by $1.78 billion in the four weeks ending March 8, and by the end of March about two-thirds of that cash had been redeposited. The record does not establish how many people listened, what they thought, or that the speech by itself ended the panic. Roosevelt’s adviser Raymond Moley later wrote that “Capitalism was saved in eight days,” but that was a participant’s retrospective judgment, not a finding.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-check-scene]

In March 1933 no one knew that the phrase “New Deal” would name an era, that deposit insurance would become permanent, or that the recovery would be interrupted by a second collapse in 1937. What kind of security did the public’s trust purchase, and for whom?

The chapter covers the years from 1933 to 1939. It asks how a banking rescue became a set of institutions, how those institutions distributed protection, and why the New Deal’s political momentum stalled so quickly after its greatest victory. It draws on federal statutes and agency records, presidential addresses, the Social Security Board’s and other agencies’ reports, congressional and Supreme Court records, labor and census statistics, and the secondary literature. It treats “recovery,” “relief,” “security,” “reform,” “parity,” “the forgotten man,” and “rugged individualism” as contested period terms that require definition rather than neutral descriptions. By “security” the chapter means a claim on income or protection that a person could expect to hold: a bank deposit, a pension, an unemployment check, a union contract. By “recovery” it means the return of output and employment toward their 1929 levels; by “relief,” aid to people without income, whether in cash or in work; by “reform,” changes in the rules of finance and labor. “Parity” was the farm program’s standard for restoring agricultural purchasing power, and “the forgotten man” and “rugged individualism” were rival claims about who deserved help and who was expected to help himself. The New Deal made that kind of security a federal responsibility. It did not make it universal.[^ch34-check-scope][^ch34-check-names][^ch34-check-silences]

The chapter also states a limit at the start. The New Deal’s records were built by agencies that counted some people and not others. Social insurance covered about half the jobs in the economy. Relief programs served some groups more readily than others. Tenant farmers, domestic workers, migrant laborers, and women without independent claims to work left fewer records than the insured, the enrolled, and the organized. The asymmetry is part of the evidence, not a gap to be filled by invention.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-check-silences]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the banking rescue and the Hundred Days. §2 follows agricultural and industrial recovery and the courts that limited it. §3 turns to the Social Security Act and the two-track welfare state, and reads the act’s employment exclusions in the In Focus. §4 examines the Wagner Act, the CIO, and the workplace conflicts of 1936–37. §5 traces the 1936 landslide, the court-packing plan, the recession of 1937–38, and the conservative coalition. §6 asks how historians have explained the New Deal’s reach and its limits. The chapter begins on March 12, 1933, when Roosevelt told the country by radio why the banks had been closed and when they would reopen.

## 1. Banking and the Hundred Days

### 1.1 A rescue that became a template

The New Deal began as a banking rescue. Its success in reopening the banks under federal supervision established the pattern that later programs followed: federal action, explained to the public, to make an essential system work again. The pattern was not a theory. It was improvised under pressure, and its authors disagreed about how far it should go.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-frh-eba]

Three connected stories belong to this section: the emergency of March 1933, the institutional rebuilding through deposit insurance and securities regulation, and the first relief and public-works programs. They are not three separate stories. The emergency created the political authority; the institutions made the authority durable; the relief programs showed what federal power could do beyond the banks.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-hopkins-relief]

Statutes, dates, bank counts, and deposit figures are documented. They cannot show that the rescue alone ended the banking crisis, or that confidence returned evenly across regions and classes. Causation is a question for §6, not a premise.[^ch34-check-numbers][^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 The holiday and the Emergency Banking Act

Roosevelt used the emergency to reopen the banks under federal examination. He called Congress into special session the day after his inauguration and declared a banking holiday that closed the system. The Emergency Banking Act, signed on March 9, 1933, provided for reopening banks as examiners found them sound, broadened presidential authority during a banking crisis, and was divided into five titles. Title I expanded presidential power over banking transactions and gold. Title II gave the comptroller of the currency power to appoint conservators for impaired banks. Title III allowed the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to buy preferred stock in banks that needed capital. Title IV let the Federal Reserve issue emergency currency against banks’ assets. Title V made the act effective.[^ch34-frh-eba]

The speed was extraordinary. Treasury staff had prepared much of the bill during Herbert Hoover’s administration. It was introduced on March 9 and passed that evening amid a chaotic scene; many members of Congress did not have an opportunity to read it before the vote. The haste was a decision with consequences. It reassured the country and preserved the banking system, and it concentrated discretion in the executive at the moment when Congress was least able to deliberate.[^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-check-dates]

The record can show the proclamation, the statute, the titles, and the reopening schedule. It cannot show what individual members understood they were voting for. That gap matters because the act did more than reopen banks. It made the federal government the arbiter of which banks would live, which would be reorganized, and which would be liquidated.

### 1.3 The fireside chat and the return of deposits

The March 12 broadcast converted a legal reopening into a public act of confidence. Roosevelt explained banking in plain terms, insisted that the new currency was “backed by actual, good assets,” promised that reopening would be phased so that sound banks could be identified, and warned that a bank opening later was no less sound than one opening first. The following morning, depositors began returning money to the banks.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]

The address worked as political education. It told listeners what a bank does with their money, why a run could destroy a sound institution, and what the government had done to stop the spiral. It asked them to act on the explanation. The request was not neutral: it made the public’s confidence an instrument of policy, and it made the president the country’s banker-in-chief.[^ch34-fireside-chat]

The result and its limits belong together. The FDR Library reports that the crisis eased and that Moley later said “Capitalism was saved in eight days.” The library’s own chronology also shows that the mechanical work was done by the holiday, the examinations, the emergency currency, and the promise of federal support. The speech did not substitute for those measures. It made them legible and gave them public consent. The chapter should not attribute a measurable effect to a single broadcast, and it should not dismiss the broadcast as decoration. Rhetoric and administration were partners.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-check-scene]

### 1.4 Glass-Steagall, the FDIC, and securities regulation

The emergency rescue was followed by permanent institutions. The Banking Act of 1933, commonly called Glass-Steagall, created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, separated commercial from investment banking, and gave the Federal Reserve greater control over bank credit. The FDR Library reports that bank failures fell from more than 4,000 in 1933 to 61 in 1934. Congress dismantled the separation of commercial and investment banking in 1999, a later change that should not be read back into the 1933 debate.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-check-numbers]

The failure counts are striking, but they do not prove that deposit insurance alone caused the decline. The banking system had already begun to stabilize after the March reopening, before federal insurance began, and the 1933 count includes the wave of failures that preceded Roosevelt’s inauguration. The honest claim is that Glass-Steagall made the stabilization durable by giving depositors a reason to stop running. It converted a temporary guarantee into a standing one.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-check-debate-works]

Securities regulation supplied the financial counterpart. The Securities Act of 1933 required issuers to disclose material information to investors. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 regulated exchanges and created the Securities and Exchange Commission. Together they made the federal government a permanent supervisor of the capital markets, not merely a rescuer of banks. The New Deal’s financial settlement was thus both a rescue and a reconstruction: it saved the system and changed the rules under which it operated.[^ch34-check-hundred-days][^ch34-check-numbers]

### 1.5 Relief, public works, and Map 26

The Hundred Days also created the first large federal experiments in work relief and regional development. The Civilian Conservation Corps was signed into law on March 31, 1933. The Federal Emergency Relief Administration was created in May 1933 under Harry Hopkins. The Civil Works Administration followed in the winter of 1933–34. The Tennessee Valley Authority Act was signed on May 18, 1933. The Works Progress Administration came in 1935. Each program had its own design, but they shared a premise: the federal government could hire people directly, not merely send money to states and localities.[^ch34-ccc][^ch34-hopkins-relief][^ch34-tva-act]

The scale was enormous. The CCC eventually employed more than 2.5 million men in more than 4,500 camps and planted over 3 billion trees. It also segregated its camps by race and excluded women entirely. The TVA built dams for flood control, navigation, and electric power, and its construction displaced families and sometimes entire towns. The benefits and the costs belong in the same sentence. The CCC gave young men wages, food, and work; it did not give them all the same work or the same camp. The TVA brought electricity and industry to a region; it also chose which valleys would be flooded.[^ch34-ccc][^ch34-tva-act]

Map 26, “New Deal landscape, 1933–1939,” gathers the geography of that activity. It plots public-works and conservation locations, TVA and CCC sites, and Dust Bowl migration routes and destinations, with counts and dates. Its state-level counts belong in the map’s production file; Appendix B, Table 8 is specified to supply the selected TVA dams, national CCC enrolled-strength benchmarks, land use, and migration context. The caption must state the definitions, years, and units, and the map must distinguish locations of program administration from locations of program spending. The map is a registered visual, not an illustration of a general impression.[^ch34-check-map26][^ch34-dustbowl][^ch34-ccc]

The record can show program dates, counts, and locations. It cannot show how every participant experienced relief. It over-represents the enrolled and under-represents those the programs did not reach. The CCC’s own records, for example, document the men in the camps far better than they document the young women who were turned away.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-hopkins-relief]

## 2. Agricultural and industrial recovery and the courts

### 2.1 Recovery by organized scarcity

The first New Deal tried to raise prices by organizing production. It paid farmers to cut acreage and let industries write codes that set prices, wages, and hours. The Supreme Court struck down the core of both efforts, forcing the administration toward regulation that could survive judicial review. The two programs and the two decisions belong together, because the judicial defeats set the terms for the “Second New Deal” of 1935.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler]

The strategy was a response to deflation. When prices fall, farmers and firms face debts fixed in dollars that are worth more each month. If production can be reduced, prices may rise, and the burden eases. The logic was coherent. Its distributional consequences were not. The people who owned the land and the codes tended to capture the benefits; the people who worked on the land and in the mills tended to bear the costs.[^ch34-usda-aaa][^ch34-nira]

The record can show the statutes, the codes, the taxes, and the opinions. It cannot show that the programs would have ended the Depression if the Court had upheld them. That counterfactual is open, and the chapter should mark it as open.[^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 2.2 The AAA, the farm program, and the Dust Bowl

The Agricultural Adjustment Act, approved on May 12, 1933, sought to restore farm purchasing power to the level of the prosperous years 1909–14. That goal became known as parity. The act authorized the secretary of agriculture to secure voluntary reductions in acreage through agreements with producers, to regulate marketing through agreements with processors, and to levy a processing tax on commodities. The tax financed the payments. The theory was that the government could raise farm prices by reducing surpluses without simply buying them up.[^ch34-usda-aaa]

The cotton program was the most dramatic. The price of cotton had fallen from 29 cents a pound in 1923 to 6.5 cents in 1932. In June 1933 the Agricultural Adjustment Administration announced a plow-up campaign with the objective of eliminating 10 million acres, or 25 percent of the growing crop. The objective was reached. Farmers plowed under cotton while millions of people were out of work and hungry. Secretary of Agriculture Henry A. Wallace called it “a shocking commentary on civilization” and said he could tolerate it “only as a cleaning up of the wreckage of the old days of unbalanced production.”[^ch34-usda-aaa]

The program’s benefits were uneven. Landowners signed the contracts and received the payments. Tenant farmers and sharecroppers often received little, and landlords sometimes used the contracts to displace them. The 1938 Agricultural Adjustment Act added provisions to safeguard tenants’ shares, which was an admission that the earlier contracts had left them exposed. The distributional effect was not an afterthought to the program. It was part of what the program did.[^ch34-usda-aaa][^ch34-check-silences]

The Dust Bowl made the farm crisis visible. Drought and erosion drove an estimated 300,000 to 400,000 Oklahomans, Texans, Arkansans, and Missourians toward California in the 1930s, most visibly after 1935. Most came from eastern Oklahoma, Texas, Arkansas, and Missouri, not from the sparsely populated counties at the center of the dust storms. About 95 percent were White. The “Okie” story became a national parable about White poverty, and its prominence obscured the Mexican, Filipino, and Black workers who had been in the fields before the migrants arrived and who remained after many of the migrants left.[^ch34-dustbowl][^ch34-check-map26]

The migration was one episode of displacement among many, not the emblem of the decade. The chapter uses it to show how environmental disaster, agricultural policy, and labor markets converged on the same families. It does not use it to stand for the whole Depression.[^ch34-dustbowl][^ch34-check-silences]

### 2.3 The NIRA, the NRA, and Section 7(a)

The National Industrial Recovery Act, signed on June 16, 1933, suspended antitrust law to let industries write codes of fair competition. It was to remain in effect for two years. Title I created the National Recovery Administration and authorized codes covering prices, wages, hours, and conditions. Title II provided $3.3 billion for public works. Section 7(a) guaranteed workers the right to organize and bargain collectively. The codes proved hard to administer; the organizing right proved the more durable legacy.[^ch34-nira]

The NRA’s “Blue Eagle” campaign enrolled employers who agreed to the codes. More than 540 codes were promulgated, and a single business might be governed by several of them. The codes could raise prices, restrain competition, and burden small firms. Compliance was uneven, and the agency had too few staff to enforce the rules. The NRA was a genuine attempt at planning, and its administration exposed the contradictions of planning through industry committees: the same firms that were supposed to be regulated helped write the regulations.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-schechter]

Section 7(a) was different. It gave workers a federal language of rights. Union membership grew rapidly after 1933, and the Amalgamated Clothing Workers doubled its membership between early 1933 and mid-1934 while the United Mine Workers quadrupled it. The NRA interpreted the section cautiously and did not aggressively prohibit company unions. The right was real but weakly enforced. Its importance lay in what came next: the Wagner Act of 1935 turned the promise into an enforceable legal regime.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-nlra]

The record can show the statute, the codes, and the administrative record. It cannot show that the codes raised employment. The recovery claim is contested, and the chapter treats it as contested.[^ch34-check-debate-works][^ch34-nira]

### 2.4 Butler and Schechter: the courts strike down the recovery state

In 1935 and 1936 the Supreme Court struck down the core of the first New Deal. On May 27, 1935, in *A. L. A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, the Court unanimously held that §3 of the NIRA was an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power to the president and that the act, as applied to a poultry business, exceeded the commerce power. The decision came less than three weeks before the act would have expired. In *United States v. Butler* (1936), the Court held by a 6–3 vote that the AAA’s processing tax was “but means to an unconstitutional end,” because it used the taxing power to control agricultural production reserved to the states. Justices Stone, Brandeis, and Cardozo dissented.[^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler]

The two decisions rested on different grounds. *Schechter* was about delegation and the reach of the commerce power. *Butler* was about the boundary between taxing and spending for the general welfare and regulating production. Together they told the administration that the first New Deal’s instruments could not survive. They did not tell the administration that federal action itself was illegitimate. That distinction mattered. The Court was striking down particular means, not the end of federal responsibility.[^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler]

The turn came in 1937. In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, the Court upheld the National Labor Relations Act as a valid exercise of the commerce power. Observers later debated whether Justice Owen Roberts’s shift was a response to Roosevelt’s court-packing plan or a reconciliation with his earlier jurisprudence. The “switch in time that saved nine” is a memorable phrase and a contested explanation. The chapter presents the dispute rather than choosing a side.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]

The record can show the holdings, the votes, and the dates. It cannot settle the judges’ motives. A decision can be explained by doctrine, by politics, or by both, and the evidence may not distinguish among them.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]

### 2.5 What survived

The judicial defeats ended the first New Deal’s planning experiments without ending federal recovery efforts. Public works, the TVA, and the regulatory approach of the Second New Deal survived. The shift from production controls to spending, public works, and legal protection of organizing was a response to judicial constraint as well as to political calculation. The Court closed some doors; the administration found others.[^ch34-tva-act][^ch34-nlra]

The link to the next section is direct. The administrative defeats left the Social Security Act as the New Deal’s most durable domestic achievement. That makes the question of its coverage the more pressing. A program that survived where the AAA and the NIRA did not would define the shape of American social provision for generations. Who was inside it, and who was left outside?[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

## 3. Social Security and the two-track welfare state

### 3.1 Security for some, assistance for others

The Social Security Act of 1935 created a national framework for old-age and unemployment insurance for workers in commerce and industry, a separate, means-tested system of assistance for others, and thereby fixed a two-track welfare state that distributed protection unequally. The insurance titles and the assistance titles were one design, not a set of unrelated programs. The design chose which risks would be pooled nationally and which would be left to states, charities, and families.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-gordon-welfare]

The choice was not inevitable. Congress could have built a universal, noncontributory pension. It could have set national standards for assistance and required states to meet them. It did neither. It built contributory insurance for the steadily employed and matching grants for the poor, and it left the second track under state administration. The result was a system that rewarded work histories and punished irregular ones.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-gordon-welfare]

The record can show the statute, the definitions, and the coverage figures. It cannot show the drafters’ motives from the text alone. The competing explanations belong in §6.[^ch34-check-debate-works][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

### 3.2 The Committee on Economic Security and the 1935 Act

The act was the product of a deliberate planning process. Roosevelt created the Committee on Economic Security in 1934. Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins, the first woman to serve in a presidential cabinet and the secretary of labor from March 1933 to July 1945, chaired the committee. Edwin Witte served as its executive director. The committee’s staff and advisory council produced the proposals that became the administration’s bill. Roosevelt signed the act on August 14, 1935.[^ch34-perkins][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]

The act separated social insurance from assistance. Insurance would be financed by payroll taxes, earned through contributions, and paid as a right. Assistance would be financed from general revenues, administered by the states, and conditioned on a means test. The separation was a political choice as well as an administrative one. A contributory program could claim to be insurance rather than charity, and that claim made it more durable in American politics. It also made it less redistributive than a universal pension would have been: the people who paid in were, by and large, the people who collected.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-gordon-welfare]

The titles carried the design. Title I provided grants to states for old-age assistance. Title II provided federal old-age benefits. Title III provided grants for unemployment compensation administration. Title IV provided aid to dependent children. Title V provided maternal and child welfare. Title VI provided public health work. Title VII created the Social Security Board. Title VIII imposed payroll taxes. Title IX taxed employers of eight or more. Title X provided aid to the blind. Title XI contained general provisions. The act was an omnibus, not a single program, and its parts had different politics and different beneficiaries.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act]

The record can show the process, the officials, and the titles. It cannot show that the design was inevitable. The committee’s final report recommended including farm and domestic workers; the Treasury objected that collecting payroll taxes from them would be administratively impossible; the exclusion was then adopted in the House Ways and Means Committee “practically without dissent.” That sequence is the foundation of the next section’s dispute.[^ch34-perkins][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

### 3.3 In Focus: Section 811’s employment exclusions

**In Focus.** Section 811(b) of the Social Security Act defines “employment” for the payroll tax. It begins with a universal phrase: “any service, of whatever nature, performed within the United States by an employee for his employer.” Then it lists what the phrase does not mean: “(1) Agricultural labor; (2) Domestic service in a private home; (3) Casual labor not in the course of the employer’s trade or business; (4) Service performed by an individual who has attained the age of sixty-five.” Four more exceptions follow: officers and crew of vessels, federal employees, state and local employees, and employees of religious, charitable, scientific, literary, or educational nonprofit organizations, or of organizations for the prevention of cruelty to children or animals. Section 811(a) caps taxable wages at $3,000 per employer per year. The parallel definition in Title II, §210(b), repeats most of the list but omits the age-65 exception. The chapter uses the original 1935 text rather than a later amended compilation.[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-check-ssa-exclusions]

The grammar is the argument. A universal definition is narrowed by a list, and the list describes the workers the Treasury said it could not easily tax: people who worked in scattered households and fields, without payroll records or a single employer. The effect was larger than the administrative rationale. In 1935, about 65 percent of gainfully employed African Americans worked in agriculture or domestic service. The section did not mention race. It did not have to. A race-neutral rule applied to a racially segmented labor market produced a racial result. The box reads the section as a legal and social document, not as proof of a single motive. What kind of security did the act create, and for whom?[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-check-focus][^ch34-check-image]

### 3.4 Insurance and assistance: the two tracks

The act’s two tracks gave workers in covered employment an earned, contributory benefit and gave others a stigmatized, means-tested payment. Title II provided federal old-age benefits financed by payroll taxes on covered workers and employers, with taxes scheduled to begin in 1937. The contributory principle was the source of the program’s political durability: a worker who had paid in could claim a benefit without proving poverty. The first monthly benefits were scheduled for 1942, which meant that the program asked workers to pay for years before they could see what they would receive.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-ssa-section-811]

Titles I, IV, and X provided federal matching grants for old-age assistance, aid to dependent children, and aid to the blind. The states administered the programs and set the benefit levels within federal limits. The assistance track was means-tested, state-administered, and more discretionary. It reached people the insurance track excluded, but it reached them as supplicants. The difference between “social insurance” and “welfare” was built into the statute.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-gordon-welfare]

Linda Gordon and Theda Skocpol trace the stratified structure to the maternalist and bureaucratic compromises of the Progressive Era and the 1930s. Single mothers received “welfare” while mostly male workers received “social insurance.” The maternalist account explains why the assistance track was organized around mothers and children, and why it carried a moral test that insurance did not. It explains less well why the insurance track excluded so many workers in the first place. The two explanations are complements, not rivals.[^ch34-gordon-welfare][^ch34-skocpol]

### 3.5 Who was covered, who was left out

The act covered about half the jobs in the economy. The excluded occupations — farm labor, domestic service, and much government and nonprofit work — were disproportionately held by African Americans and women, especially in the South. The Committee on Economic Security estimated that 20.1 million gainfully employed workers were excluded; at least 15 million of them were White. About 65 percent of gainfully employed Black workers were in agriculture or domestic service. The exclusion was not a rule aimed at Black workers. It was a rule aimed at occupations, and the occupations were racially arranged.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

Ira Katznelson argues that southern Democrats shaped the New Deal’s occupational exclusions to protect the region’s racial order, so that federal social provision entrenched a racial gap. His account spans Social Security, protective labor laws, and the GI Bill, and it treats the New Deal and Fair Deal as a single regime of “affirmative action for whites.” The strongest part of the argument is structural: southern members held key committee chairs, the New Deal needed their votes, and the resulting programs decentralized administration and excluded the occupations in which most Black southerners worked. The effect was to build a welfare state that was universal in rhetoric and particular in reach.[^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

Larry DeWitt, writing for the Social Security Administration, argues that the racial-bias thesis is flawed and unsupported. He shows that the Committee on Economic Security’s final report recommended including farm and domestic workers, that Secretary of the Treasury Henry Morgenthau Jr. then intervened to exclude them on administrative grounds, and that the exclusion was adopted in the House Ways and Means Committee “practically without dissent.” He notes that 74 percent of the excluded workers were White and that the administration, not southern Democrats, was the source of the proposal. On his account, the exclusions reflected the difficulty of collecting payroll taxes from scattered workers, not a plan to exclude Black Americans.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

Both accounts contain evidence the other must explain. DeWitt is persuasive that the Title II exclusion was not drafted by southern Democrats as a racial device. Katznelson is persuasive that the New Deal’s overall architecture was shaped by southern power and that its effects were racial. The two claims operate at different levels: one concerns the motive for a specific statutory definition, the other the design of a welfare state. A race-neutral administrative rule can produce a racial result when the labor market is segregated. The chapter therefore treats the exclusions as a choice with mixed motives and racial effects, not as a single conspiracy or a purely technical necessity.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

Map 26’s second placement belongs here. It shows the geography of program reach and migration together: the counties where the CCC and TVA operated, the routes that carried migrants to California, and the regions where the excluded occupations were concentrated. The map cannot prove causation. It can show that the geography of protection and the geography of exclusion were not the same.[^ch34-check-map26][^ch34-dustbowl]

The record can show coverage shares and occupational distributions. It cannot fully separate intent from effect. That limit is not a reason to avoid judgment. It is a reason to make the judgment precise.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

## 4. The CIO and workplace conflict

### 4.1 A labor regime remade from below and by law

Between 1935 and 1937 the federal government protected the right to organize, industrial unions organized the mass-production industries, and workers used the sit-down strike to win recognition. The transformation depended on law and mobilization together. The Wagner Act gave workers a legal claim; the CIO gave them an organization; the sit-down gave them a tactic that employers could not easily break.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-cohen][^ch34-lichtenstein]

The chapter connects the statute, the founding of the CIO, the Flint sit-down, and the violence at Little Steel as one story about who would control the workplace. The story is not a simple triumph. It is a story of gains that were real, bounded, and contested.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-memorial-day]

The record can show the statute, the elections, the strike dates, and the casualty figures. It cannot show what every worker intended. The movement was diverse, and the chapter should not reduce it to a single motive.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-cohen]

### 4.2 The Wagner Act and the National Labor Relations Board

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 made the federal government an enforcer of collective bargaining, not merely a mediator. Senator Robert F. Wagner introduced the bill in February 1935. It passed the Senate in May and the House in June, and Roosevelt signed it on July 5, 1935. Section 7 guaranteed employees the right to organize and bargain collectively. The act required employers to bargain with unions chosen by a majority of employees in an appropriate unit, created the National Labor Relations Board to run representation elections, and defined unfair labor practices. It covered most workers in industries whose operations affected interstate commerce.[^ch34-nlra]

The coverage followed the commerce power, and that limit mattered. Farmworkers, domestic workers, and many public employees remained outside the act’s protection. The same boundary that ran through the Social Security Act ran through labor law. The Wagner Act protected the workers who were already most likely to be organized, and it left the most vulnerable workers to state law, local politics, and the market.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

The constitutional test came in 1937. In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, the Supreme Court upheld the act as a valid exercise of the commerce power. The decision anchored the “switch in time” debate and gave the board the authority it needed. It did not create the organizing wave by itself. Workers had been organizing before the decision, and they continued after it. The law gave their action legal protection; their action gave the law force.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-cohen]

### 4.3 The CIO and the sit-down strikes

The Congress of Industrial Organizations began in November 1935 as the Committee for Industrial Organization, a group within the American Federation of Labor led by John L. Lewis. It organized entire industries rather than single crafts. The AFL expelled the member unions in 1938, and the committee became the Congress of Industrial Organizations. The CIO’s strategy was to organize all the workers in a plant or an industry, regardless of craft, and to use the Wagner Act’s election machinery to win recognition.[^ch34-lichtenstein][^ch34-cohen]

The sit-down strike at General Motors in Flint, Michigan, was the CIO’s breakthrough. It began on December 30, 1936, and lasted forty-four days. Workers occupied the Fisher Body plants and refused to leave. The tactic prevented the company from bringing in strikebreakers and from moving production to other plants. It also raised a question the law had not settled: who controlled company property when workers claimed the right to bargain? On February 11, 1937, General Motors recognized the United Auto Workers. Within two weeks, eighty-seven sit-down strikes began in Detroit alone.[^ch34-loc-flint][^ch34-cohen]

The victory spread. On March 2, 1937, the United States Steel Corporation recognized the Steel Workers Organizing Committee. The agreement included a wage increase, an eight-hour day and forty-hour week with overtime, seniority protection, and a grievance procedure. U.S. Steel’s recognition was a shock to anyone who remembered the company’s anti-union history. It was also a calculation: the company faced growing union strength and an approaching war market for steel. Recognition was cheaper than a strike.[^ch34-pa-steel][^ch34-lichtenstein]

The “Little Steel” companies refused to follow. Their resistance set up the confrontation of Memorial Day 1937.

### 4.4 Little Steel and the limits of the settlement

On May 30, 1937, several hundred White, Black, and Latino men, women, and children demonstrated at the Republic Steel plant on Chicago’s far Southeast Side. They demanded union recognition, a contract, higher wages, safer work, and an end to retaliation for union activity. Republic Steel had anticipated the demonstration. The company bought more than $50,000 worth of tear gas for the Chicago police and procured white clubs made from hatchet handles. The police increased their force to 264 officers.[^ch34-memorial-day]

The police attacked. They threw tear gas, fired bullets, and beat demonstrators with clubs. Ten people were killed, forty were shot, and more than ninety were wounded. Among the injured was Guadalupe “Lupe” Gallardo Marshall, a Mexican immigrant and volunteer social worker at Hull-House, who was beaten and then, in a packed wagon, attended to other injured demonstrators. Max Guzmán, a Mexican steelworker, was struck in the head, interrogated about alleged communist ties, and threatened with deportation. The dead and wounded were not abstract casualties. They were the people the Wagner Act was supposed to protect.[^ch34-memorial-day]

A Senate investigating committee, chaired by Robert M. La Follette Jr., took testimony on the massacre and on the labor spy industry. Newsreel footage, withheld at first and then released during the hearings, contradicted the police account. The committee found that the police had used excessive force and had not conducted an honest investigation. The massacre showed that the state’s force could fall on the side of employers even after the federal government had guaranteed the right to organize. A law on the books was not the same as a right in the streets.[^ch34-memorial-day][^ch34-check-labor]

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 added the wage-and-hour floor to the labor settlement. It set a minimum wage of 25 cents an hour, a maximum workweek of forty hours, and a ban on child labor in interstate commerce. It also excluded agricultural and domestic workers, the same categories the Social Security Act had excluded. The boundary was not an accident of one statute. It was a pattern.[^ch34-check-labor][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

### 4.5 What the labor settlement did not include

The new labor regime reached the organized core of mass-production industry and left much of the workforce outside its protection. Farmworkers, domestic workers, and many service and public employees remained outside the Wagner Act and the Fair Labor Standards Act. The CIO organized the steel mills and the auto plants; it did not organize the fields and the kitchens. The boundary ran along the same lines as the Social Security exclusions: occupation, race, gender, and region.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-lichtenstein]

The pattern is the point. A worker in a covered industry could win a contract, a pension, and a grievance procedure. A worker in an excluded occupation could not. The difference was not a measure of the workers’ contribution. It was a measure of their political and administrative visibility. The New Deal’s labor settlement was real, and it was partial.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

The record can show union membership, contract terms, and coverage exclusions. It cannot show what non-union workers thought about a movement that did not include them. That silence is part of the history.[^ch34-check-silences]

## 5. The 1937–38 recession and the conservative coalition

### 5.1 High tide and ebb

The New Deal reached its political peak with the 1936 landslide. It then lost momentum through the court-packing fight, the recession of 1937–38, and the elections of 1938. The triumph and the reversal belong to one story about the limits of the New Deal coalition. The coalition was broad enough to reelect Roosevelt overwhelmingly and too fragile to discipline its own conservative wing.[^ch34-app-1936][^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-e1938]

The record can show the returns, the votes, and the economic series. It cannot show that a different strategy would have preserved momentum. The counterfactual is open, and the chapter should mark it as open.[^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The 1936 landslide and the court-packing plan

Roosevelt won a second term in one of the most lopsided elections in American history. He carried 523 electoral votes and about 60.8 percent of the popular vote, with 27,750,866 votes. Alfred M. Landon carried 8 electoral votes and about 36.5 percent, with 16,679,683 votes. The totals come from compiled returns and differ slightly among compilations. The victory gave Roosevelt a mandate and a larger Democratic majority, but it did not remove the Supreme Court.[^ch34-app-1936]

On February 5, 1937, Roosevelt proposed adding one justice for each sitting justice over the age of 70, up to six additional justices. His motive was to change the Court’s ideological balance so that it would stop striking down New Deal legislation. The plan was widely and vehemently criticized. Congress never enacted it, and the fight cost Roosevelt political support. The proposal was a miscalculation about how far his mandate reached.[^ch34-fjc-courtp]

The Court upheld the National Labor Relations Act in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* in April 1937, shortly after the plan became public. Some called the decision “the switch in time that saved nine.” Legal scholars have disputed whether Justice Roberts responded to the plan or reconciled his jurisprudence with his earlier views. The chapter presents the dispute rather than choosing a side.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]

The episode is a study in the limits of a landslide. Roosevelt had won the votes; he had not won the Court, the Senate’s procedural loyalties, or the country’s attachment to the institution. The plan turned a judicial problem into a constitutional controversy, and it gave the president’s opponents a language for describing his ambition.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-e1938]

### 5.3 The 1937–38 recession

In the middle of recovery the economy contracted sharply again. The contraction ran from May 1937 to June 1938. Real gross domestic product fell 10 percent, unemployment reached 20 percent, and industrial production fell 32 percent. The downturn was the third-worst recession of the twentieth century, and it occurred before the economy had recovered from the first one.[^ch34-frh-1937]

The causes interacted. Federal Reserve policymakers doubled reserve requirements in 1936 to absorb excess reserves. The Treasury began sterilizing gold inflows in late June 1936, which severed the link between gold imports and monetary expansion. The combined effect reduced the growth of the money stock and then turned it negative. Fiscal policy also tightened: the Social Security payroll tax began in 1937, the Revenue Act of 1935 raised taxes, and federal fiscal policy turned contractionary. The recession was not the result of one mistake. It was the result of several policies that made sense separately and contracted the economy together.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-check-recession]

The political consequences were severe. The recession discredited the claim that recovery was self-sustaining. It strengthened the argument for deficit spending and contributed to the administration’s later embrace of compensatory fiscal policy. Alan Brinkley argues that the shift marked the end of structural reform and the beginning of a compensatory state that managed demand rather than redistributed power. The argument is an interpretation, not a consensus, but it captures the change in the New Deal’s ambitions.[^ch34-brinkley][^ch34-frh-1937]

The recession also gave the New Deal’s opponents a case. If the New Deal had not ended the Depression after four years, they asked, why should the country trust it with more power? The question misdescribed the record and was politically effective. It helped set the terms of the 1938 elections.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-e1938]

### 5.4 The 1938 purge and the conservative coalition

Roosevelt tried and failed to defeat conservative Democrats in the 1938 primaries. He campaigned against senators and representatives who had opposed his programs, beginning in the summer of 1938. The effort largely failed. Most of the targeted incumbents survived, and the campaign made the president’s intervention in local primaries an issue in its own right.[^ch34-e1938]

The midterm elections then produced Republican gains in both chambers. The precise seat changes are reported differently in different compilations, but the direction is not in doubt. The elections left Republicans and southern Democrats able to block the New Deal’s remaining agenda. The House’s own history describes the period as the forging of a durable “conservative coalition” that shaped Congress for decades. The coalition was an institutional constraint on the New Deal’s later reach.[^ch34-e1938][^ch34-check-recession]

The coalition was not the only reason reform ended. The recession had weakened the case for further experimentation, the court fight had exhausted political capital, and the approaching war in Europe was beginning to rearrange the agenda. The conservative coalition is best understood as one condition among several, not as a single cause. It gave the opposition a structure, and the structure outlasted the immediate issues.[^ch34-e1938][^ch34-check-dates-appD]

### 5.5 The shape of the New Deal state by 1939

By 1939 the New Deal had built durable federal institutions: deposit insurance, securities regulation, social insurance, and legal protection for unions. It had also left large parts of the workforce outside them. The balance sheet is the chapter’s argument in miniature. The New Deal created a new kind of security, real and durable, and it defined that security by who was inside the program and who was left outside.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

The institutions outlasted the coalition that built them. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Social Security Board, and the National Labor Relations Board survived the political reversals of 1937–38. The programs changed over time, but the federal responsibility they embodied did not disappear. That is the New Deal’s most important legacy.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-nlra]

The boundaries also outlasted the coalition. The occupational exclusions, the state administration of assistance, and the labor law’s coverage limits shaped American social provision for generations. The New Deal did not create a universal welfare state. It created a segmented one, and the segments were not equal.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The New Deal expanded federal responsibility. Banking supervision, deposit insurance, securities regulation, social insurance, and labor law all made the federal government a permanent participant in the economy.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-nlra]

It created lasting institutions. The FDIC, the SEC, the Social Security Board, and the National Labor Relations Board survived the New Deal’s political reversals.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]

It distributed protection unequally. The Social Security Act covered about half the jobs in the economy, and the labor and wage laws left farm and domestic workers outside their protection.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-nlra]

It faced real judicial and political limits. The Court struck down the NIRA’s code authority and the AAA’s processing tax, and the conservative coalition checked further reform after 1938.[^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler][^ch34-e1938]

The recession of 1937–38 was severe. Real output, employment, and industrial production all fell sharply in the middle of recovery.[^ch34-frh-1937]

### 6.2 What is open

How much did the New Deal end the Depression? One position credits federal spending and relief with pulling the economy forward. Another argues that recovery was incomplete until the war and that the New Deal’s own policies, including higher labor costs and taxes, slowed it. Each position has a strong version. The spending account explains the recovery from 1933 to 1937; the wartime account explains why full employment arrived only with the war. The evidence supports a combined account in which the New Deal helped and the war finished the job, but the weights remain disputed.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-check-debate-works]

Did the exclusions reflect racial design or administrative necessity? Katznelson and others argue for racial design through southern power. DeWitt argues for administrative feasibility and finds no racial motive. The evidence supports a mixed judgment: the specific Title II exclusion was not drafted as a racial device, but the broader architecture was shaped by southern power and produced racial effects. The question is which level of analysis should carry the most weight.[^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

Was the New Deal a revolution or a rescue? Leuchtenburg sees an incomplete revolution in federal responsibility. Bernstein sees conservative achievements that preserved capitalism. The two are not as far apart as they sound. A revolution in federal responsibility can also be a stabilization of the existing order. The disagreement is about which change matters more.[^ch34-leuchtenburg][^ch34-bernstein]

Who drove the labor settlement? One account credits federal law and the Wagner Act. Another credits workers’ own mobilization and institutions. The evidence supports both mechanisms. The law created a framework; the workers supplied the power; the state then helped contain the power it had helped unleash.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-cohen]

Did the two-track welfare state reflect maternalist choices or southern racial politics? Gordon and Skocpol locate the split in earlier maternalist policy. Others emphasize southern power in 1935. The question remains open. The maternalist account explains the assistance track’s structure; the southern-power account explains the insurance track’s exclusions. A full explanation needs both.[^ch34-gordon-welfare][^ch34-skocpol][^ch34-katznelson]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the New Deal genuinely transformed the federal government’s role while building its protections around boundaries of occupation, race, gender, and region that the same legislation helped to fix. The transformation and the boundaries were not opposites. They were produced together.[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-katznelson]

The outcome was contingent. The New Deal’s shape was the result of decisions taken by people who could have chosen differently: the exclusions in the 1935 act, the court-packing gamble, the fiscal contraction of 1937. None of these was inevitable. Each was a choice made within constraints.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-frh-1937]

Structure and choice belong together. The structural constraints — southern power in Congress, the administrative reach of the tax system, the Court, a weak recovery — set the stage. The contingent choices determined how the New Deal navigated them. A history that emphasizes only structure cannot explain why the exclusions took the form they did. A history that emphasizes only choice cannot explain why the same boundaries reappeared in program after program.[^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-e1938]

The synthesis for this chapter is that the New Deal created a new kind of security, real and durable, and that it defined that security by who was inside the program and who was left outside. The question is not whether the glass was half full or half empty. It is who was covered by the program and who was not.[^ch34-check-table1][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

## Legacies

The New Deal left a durable legacy of federal responsibility. It made the federal government the guarantor of bank deposits, the regulator of securities markets, the backstop for old-age and unemployment insurance, and the protector of the right to organize. Americans would argue for generations about the size and shape of that responsibility, but the argument itself assumed the federal role the New Deal created.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-nlra]

The legacy was also exclusionary. Because social insurance and labor law reached workers in commerce and industry, the workers left outside — farmworkers, domestic workers, and many service and public employees, disproportionately Black and female — built their later claims against a program that had not originally included them. That story runs into the civil rights era and Chapter 39.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

The institutional legacy outlasted the coalition. The Social Security Board became the Social Security Administration, the National Labor Relations Board endured, and deposit insurance survived the later separation of commercial and investment banking in 1999. The New Deal’s agencies carried the settlement into decades when its politics had changed.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]

The political legacy was contested from the start. The conservative coalition that formed after 1938 set the terms of a long argument over the New Deal’s reach, an argument that continues in later chapters and in the present. The New Deal did not settle the country’s argument about security. It gave the argument a vocabulary and a set of institutions.[^ch34-e1938][^ch34-check-dates-appD]

## Summary

- The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 paid farmers to cut acreage through a processing tax, and the Supreme Court struck the tax down in *United States v. Butler* (1936).[^ch34-butler][^ch34-usda-aaa]
- The National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 created the National Recovery Administration and protected collective bargaining in Section 7(a); the Supreme Court struck down its code authority in *Schechter* (1935).[^ch34-nira][^ch34-schechter]
- The Social Security Act of 1935 created old-age and unemployment insurance for covered workers and means-tested assistance for others; its §811(b) excluded agricultural labor, domestic service, and other categories, and its coverage reached about half the jobs in the economy.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]
- The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 protected the right to organize and created the National Labor Relations Board, which the Supreme Court upheld in 1937; the CIO organized mass-production industries, and the Flint sit-down forced General Motors to recognize the United Auto Workers.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-loc-flint]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1933 and 1939 the United States built a new relationship between the federal government and the economy. A banking rescue became deposit insurance and securities regulation; a farm program and an industrial code became, after the Court struck them down, a turn to social insurance and public works; a guarantee of the right to organize became the CIO and the sit-down strikes; and a recession and a failed purge left a conservative coalition. The New Deal created real security for millions, and it drew the boundaries of that security around occupation, race, gender, and region. The chapter’s question — what kind of security did the New Deal create, and for whom? — is answered by that double record.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-e1938]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on October 5, 1937, when President Roosevelt spoke in Chicago and called for a “quarantine” of aggressor nations. Chapter 35 takes up the coming of the Second World War and asks how neutrality gave way to intervention. The speech falls inside the years this chapter has covered, but its subject is foreign policy, and the two chapters are meant to be read together. The domestic New Deal and the approach of war ran on separate clocks, and the country would soon be living on both.[^ch34-check-ch35]
