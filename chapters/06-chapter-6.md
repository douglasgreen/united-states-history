## Epigraphs

> “They said thay had bine the first in doing Good to the English, and the English in the first in doing Rong; said when they English first came, their King’s Father was as a great Man, and the English as a littell Child; he constrained other Indians from ronging the English, and gave them Corn and shewed them how to plant, and was free to do them ani Good.”[^ch06-easton-relation]

> “You say that you are our father and I am your son … We will not be like Father and Son, but like Brothers.”[^ch06-richter-longhouse]

> “New England colonists waged war to gain Indian lands, to erase Indians from the landscape, and to free themselves of doubts about their own Englishness.”[^ch06-lepore-name]

- **Why these three.** The Wampanoag statement, recorded by a Rhode Island official trying to prevent war, presents the English as the ones who broke faith and as junior partners who had forgotten their debts. The Haudenosaunee speaker at the 1692 Covenant Chain council rejects the language of father and son and claims the standing of brothers — a claim about sovereignty inside an alliance. Lepore names what the English fought for. The three disagree about who was the wronged party and whether the relationship between peoples was patronage, kinship, or conquest.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Easton’s 1675 relation survives in a nineteenth-century reprint and in modern documentary excerpts; the quotation uses its original spelling and must be collated against the consulted edition before it is set in type. The 1692 line is quoted from a scholarly monograph as reproduced in a reference summary; it must be verified in the treaty record or in Richter’s book.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-richter-longhouse]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that all Wampanoags shared one grievance, that the Covenant Chain made the Haudenosaunee and the English equals in practice, or that the English fought only for land and identity. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and the boundary.** Swansea sat in Plymouth Colony on the border between English settlement and Wampanoag country, on land the English had bought from the sachem Ousamequin’s people. Its proximity to Mount Hope, Metacom’s seat, made it the fault line of a crisis that had been building for years.[^ch06-swansea-ahc]
- **The grievance and the trial.** In January 1675 a Christian Indian named John Sassamon was found dead under the ice of Assowampsett Pond, in present-day Lakeville. Sassamon had reportedly warned Governor Josiah Winslow of a Wampanoag plot. A Native witness named three men as the killers, and a Plymouth court convicted them in June; two were hanged on June 8, 1675, and the third was shot a month later. Metacom read the trial as an assault on his authority as sachem — a claim on jurisdiction over his own people.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-congregational]
- **The first blood at Swansea.** According to John Easton’s relation, an Indian was shot and wounded at Swansea on June 18, 1675. Some accounts say a settler fired on Indians pilfering from a house while its owners were at church; when neighbors asked why the man had been shot, one Englishman reportedly answered that “it was no matter.” Easton’s account is a Quaker official’s attempt to record the Wampanoag side as well as the English, and it does not fix every detail.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-swansea-ahc]
- **The escalation.** On June 20, 1675, Wampanoag warriors raided the outlying farms, looting and burning them; residents fled to garrison houses and called for militia help. Reinforcements from Bridgewater and Taunton arrived on June 21, and further raids and ambushes followed. The dates and the number of the first attackers are reported differently across accounts.[^ch06-swansea-ahc]
- **The June 24 attacks.** The heaviest attacks came as Swansea’s people were returning from a day of humiliation and prayer. Wampanoag parties ambushed colonists who had left the garrison houses; several were killed and others mortally wounded. Two men sent to Plymouth for help were killed on the road, and the commissioners traveling to treat with Metacom found their bodies and turned back to Boston.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-swansea-ahc]
- **Benjamin Church’s arrival.** A force under Captain Benjamin Church reached Swansea, and Plymouth and Massachusetts troops gathered there over the following days. The accounts agree that Church learned of the Wampanoag preparations in advance and that several Wampanoag leaders, including Awashonks of the Sakonnet, weighed whether to join Metacom.[^ch06-swansea-ahc][^ch06-great-swamp]
- **The silence and the mediation.** No Wampanoag account of the first days survives in the participants’ own words; the event reaches us through Easton’s sympathetic but partial relation, through colonists writing later for English readers, and through the records of the war the English won. The scene may read what those records say and what they cannot confirm.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-lepore-name]

### The scene’s argument

- **A documented day, not a tableau.** The scene should open on Swansea in the second half of June 1675, moving from the shooting of an Indian to the burning of the farms to the June 24 ambushes. Every action named should be one the records describe.
- **A war that may not have been planned to begin there.** Historians doubt that Metacom chose Swansea as the place to start the war; the early violence may have been set off by young warriors acting without his leave. The scene should present the outbreak as contingent, not as the first move of a prearranged plan.[^ch06-swansea-ahc][^ch06-lepore-name]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: How did colonial wars alter sovereignty and alliance?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that one side was simply aggressor and the other victim. Indigenous decisions, settler expansion, imperial competition, and enslavement changed one another’s possibilities.
- **The widening.** The scene sits at Swansea in 1675. The framing paragraph follows immediately and sends the reader across the continent to the Rio Grande, where the Pueblos rose five years later, and to the broader imperial order that the wars would recompose.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-covenant-wiki]

### What must not be invented

- No invented weather, cabin dialogue, thoughts, or a reenacted council. The records are the evidence and the story.[^ch06-check-scene]
- No single fixed date or casualty total for the first attacks; the accounts disagree, and the chapter should give a range or attribute the figure.[^ch06-check-scene][^ch06-check-dating]
- No claim that Metacom personally ordered the first strike at Swansea; the evidence suggests the outbreak surprised both sides.[^ch06-swansea-ahc]
- No claim that the English victims were innocent of the grievances Easton recorded, or that the Wampanoag actions were simple “massacre” without cause. Both framings appear in the sources and in the memory of the war.[^ch06-lepore-name]
- No claim that the war was one war. It spread from Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay to Rhode Island, Connecticut, and the northern frontier, and its northern phase lasted to 1678.[^ch06-congregational]

## 1. Metacom’s War and captives

### 1.1 The war in New England

- **The claim.** What the English called King Philip’s War and the Wampanoag and their allies fought as a war for sovereignty and survival was the most destructive conflict in seventeenth-century New England. It began at Swansea and spread across the region, ending with Metacom’s death in August 1676 and a colonial victory that did not restore the old alliance.
- **The participants.** Metacom (also called Metacomet or King Philip), sachem of the Pokanoket Wampanoag, led a coalition that came to include Nipmuc, Narragansett, and Pocumtuc peoples and, later, Abenaki communities to the north. The English drew on the New England Confederation’s militias and on Indigenous allies, including the Mohegan under Uncas and some Christian “praying Indians.”[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-congregational]
- **The Great Swamp Fight.** On December 19, 1675, a combined colonial force attacked a Narragansett fort in the Great Swamp of present-day Rhode Island. The Narragansetts had declared neutrality, but the English distrusted them and feared they would join Metacom in the spring. The assault killed hundreds of Narragansett warriors and non-combatants; historians describe it as one of the most brutal and lopsided engagements in New England’s history. Many of the survivors were later sold into slavery, sentenced to death, or forced to flee.[^ch06-great-swamp]
- **The end and the aftermath.** Metacom was killed on August 12, 1676, by John Alderman, an Indigenous soldier serving in Benjamin Church’s company. The English drew and quartered his body and displayed his head at Plymouth, a punishment reserved for treason. Fighting continued in northern New England into 1678.[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-congregational]
- **The cost.** By the war’s end more than 600 colonists had been killed and about a dozen towns destroyed; an estimated 3,000 Native people died at the English colonists’ hands. These figures are widely repeated estimates rather than precise counts, and the chapter should present them as such.[^ch06-umassd][^ch06-check-dating]
- **The consequence.** The war broke the Wampanoag and Narragansett as independent military powers in southern New England and opened their lands to English settlement. It also produced the era’s largest single flow of Native captives into slavery.

### 1.2 The treatment of allied and neutral Indians

- **The claim.** The war did not divide cleanly into English and enemy. Christian and neutral Indians suffered internment, dispossession, and sale, and their wartime treatment shows how quickly an alliance could be converted into a claim of conquest.
- **The praying towns and Deer Island.** The “praying Indians” were Algonquian converts, mostly Nipmuc and Massachusett, who lived in towns associated with John Eliot’s mission. During the war many praying towns were depopulated, and hundreds of Christian Indians were removed to Deer Island in Boston Harbor, where they endured exposure and hunger and many died.[^ch06-congregational][^ch06-praying-town]
- **The surrenderers.** Linford D. Fisher’s study shows that Native people who surrendered to avoid enslavement — individuals, families, and whole communities — were enslaved at nearly the same rate as captured combatants. English authorities disarmed surrenderers, sold their guns, and then often sold the people themselves, overseas or into local servitude, because the colonies objected to freeing them and found housing and feeding them inconvenient.[^ch06-fisher][^ch06-brown-fisher]
- **The fear of export.** Because New Englanders routinely shipped Native captives to Barbados, Bermuda, Jamaica, the Azores, Spain, and Tangier, surrenderers understood that captivity could mean removal from their homelands forever. That fear drove some to fight on and others to surrender, making enslavement a strategic weapon in the war itself.[^ch06-fisher][^ch06-brown-fisher]
- **The Indigenous counter-efforts.** English-allied Native leaders worked to keep surrenderers out of English households and off English ships. Uncas of the Mohegans tried to prevent captives from being carried to the Caribbean, sheltered runaways, and helped them resettle. Native people also bought and held captives as servants, sometimes to keep them from English owners.[^ch06-brown-fisher]
- **The consequence.** The war’s human cost fell hardest on people the English had promised to protect. The alliance that had sustained Plymouth a half-century earlier ended as a license to enslave.

### 1.3 In Focus: the Seaflower and one shipping record

**In Focus.** In the fall of 1676, as the war in southern New England ended, a ship called the *Seaflower* sailed from Boston Harbor with a cargo of Native captives. The number borne on the record is usually given as about 180, described in the language of the time as “Heathen Malefactors” being exported as “the reward of war” — a phrase that turned prisoners into property and their sale into the soldiers’ wages. Governor John Leverett defended the shipment in a letter by arguing that the captives had rebelled against a king whose authority Metacom had acknowledged. The captives were men, women, and children; some were the families of the defeated sachems, and the *Seaflower* carried them toward markets in Barbados and Jamaica that proved hard to sell in. Historians including Jill Lepore have traced the ship in the records, and Margaret Ellen Newell places it within a wider traffic in Native slaves from New England. Read closely, the record is not a complete manifest and it is not unique: it is one documentable instance of a pattern — the conversion of a defeated people into an export commodity. The chapter should cite the specific record and the *Seaflower* by name, note that the exact count varies among sources, and avoid composing a “typical” ship from several accounts. The larger scale is reported, not precisely counted: New England armies, courts, and magistrates enslaved more than 1,200 Native people in the seventeenth century.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-seaflower][^ch06-check-focus]

### 1.4 The scale and meaning of Native enslavement

- **The claim.** The enslavement of Native people was not incidental to the war; it was one of its central outcomes and one of the ways the English paid for it. Native slavery and African slavery grew together in the same decades.
- **The numbers.** Recent scholarship argues that Native Americans made up the vast majority of people enslaved by European regimes in the Americas before 1700. The *Seaflower* was one vessel among many; hundreds of New England captives were sold locally or overseas.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-fisher]
- **The destinations.** New England colonies shipped Native captives to Barbados, Bermuda, Jamaica, the Azores, Spain, and Tangier, and also bound them in English households for terms that often lengthened into lifetime and heritable servitude. A Rhode Island law of 1676 that seemed to forbid Indian slavery in practice “disposed of” surrenderers for the colony’s benefit.[^ch06-brown-fisher][^ch06-commonplace-shoemaker]
- **The legal blur.** Newell argues that ambiguity was essential: colonists held Native people as slaves or under designations that amounted to perpetual, heritable bondage, while the law governing Native slavery remained sparse and contradictory. Small loopholes and dishonest practice turned limited-term service into slavery.[^ch06-commonplace-shoemaker][^ch06-brown-fisher]
- **The aftermath.** The shadow of wartime enslavement reached into the eighteenth century. In 1721 Connecticut debated the status of second-generation Native child servants, the descendants of people first enslaved during the war. Free-descendant petitions continued into the 1740s.[^ch06-brown-fisher]
- **The consequence.** Metacom’s War both shattered Indigenous power in southern New England and deepened the region’s dependence on unfree labor, Native and African alike. That is the bridge from the war to the imperial slave system of §5.

## 2. The Pueblo Revolt and the reconquest

### 2.1 Eight decades of Spanish rule

- **The claim.** The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 grew from nearly a century of Spanish demands on Pueblo land, labor, and religion. It was the most successful Indigenous uprising against colonial rule in what became the United States.
- **The conquest and its pattern.** Juan de Oñate led Spanish soldiers and Franciscans into the Rio Grande valley in 1598. When Acoma Pueblo resisted, Oñate’s forces killed and enslaved hundreds and punished survivors with mutilation, setting a template of terror that endured for decades. By 1680 some 40,000 Pueblo people lived in the region.[^ch06-pueblo-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]
- **Theocracy and labor.** Franciscan missionaries, backed by armed force, worked to destroy the ancestral Pueblo world: they suppressed kachina worship, banned traditional marriage and religious practice, and imposed Christian observance and forced labor through the encomienda and repartimiento systems. The Pueblo religious leader Po’pay had himself been arrested for practicing Pueblo rites before he organized the revolt.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-popay-smithsonian]
- **Drought, famine, and Apache raids.** A severe drought in the 1660s and 1670s worsened hunger and strained the Pueblo–Spanish relationship, while Apache raiding pressured Pueblo communities. The crisis made revolt both more necessary and more difficult to coordinate.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- **The consequence.** By 1680 the Pueblo nations shared a grievance against Spanish rule strong enough to overcome, for a time, their own long-standing divisions.

### 2.2 The revolt of 1680

- **The claim.** On August 10, 1680, most of the Pueblo nations rose together, killed some 400 Spaniards, drove about 2,000 survivors out of the province, and ended Spanish rule in New Mexico for twelve years.
- **The coordination.** Po’pay, a Tewa religious leader from San Juan Pueblo (present-day Ohkay Owingeh), organized the revolt across the pueblos. Because the communities spoke different languages — Keres, Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Piro, and Zuni — he used runners carrying knotted cords, each knot to be untied one day at a time, to set a common date. When the plan was discovered early, the revolt began a day sooner than planned.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]
- **The attack.** The rebels attacked Spanish settlements, killed twenty-one Franciscan priests, sacked mission churches, desecrated Catholic objects, and besieged the governor, Antonio de Otermín, in Santa Fe. Otermín broke out and led a retreat south to El Paso.[^ch06-pueblo-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]
- **The twelve years.** For twelve years the Pueblos lived free of Spanish rule. They rebuilt the kivas and restored the practices the missions had suppressed. The Hopi, the westernmost participants, were never reconquered.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]
- **The strains.** Unity did not last. Drought and Apache raids continued, villages that had moved for defense did not return, and Po’pay’s insistence on abandoning all Spanish material culture and his heavy-handed leadership cost him support. By the time the Spanish returned, Pueblo unity had eroded.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]
- **The consequence.** The revolt preserved Pueblo lands, languages, and religion in ways the missions had threatened to erase, and it showed that the Spanish empire could be driven out by the people it had ruled.

### 2.3 The reconquest, 1692–1696

- **The claim.** The Spanish reconquest was achieved less by conquest than by negotiation and the exploitation of Pueblo divisions, though force remained in reserve.
- **Vargas’s commission.** In 1688 the Spanish crown commissioned Diego de Vargas to reconquer New Mexico. Vargas, a veteran colonial administrator, arrived at El Paso in 1691 and organized a return. He intended to retake the province without firing a shot, keeping his soldiers’ weapons holstered and inviting each pueblo to return to Spanish rule and the Catholic faith — while keeping two cannons in the rear guard.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- **The return and the resistance.** Vargas’s first foray in the fall of 1692 met little resistance and secured a formal return in Santa Fe, but the peace was fragile. Resistance continued, and by 1696 Spanish and Pueblo forces were again at war. The full consolidation of Spanish rule took years.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- **The terms.** The reconquest did not restore the old theocracy unchanged. Spanish officials moderated some demands, and Pueblo ceremonies survived in altered form. Recent scholarship stresses that what was lost and what was preserved came from Pueblo choices as much as Spanish policy.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]
- **The consequence.** New Mexico remained a Spanish, then Mexican, then American borderland, but its Pueblo nations survived as distinct political communities. The revolt and the reconquest are both founding events of the Southwest’s later history.

## 3. The Glorious Revolution in America

### 3.1 The Dominion of New England

- **The claim.** Before 1689 the English crown had tried to centralize its American colonies under a single royal government. The Dominion of New England was that experiment, and it failed.
- **The consolidation.** After revoking the Massachusetts Bay charter in 1684, James II created the Dominion of New England in 1686 under Governor Sir Edmund Andros. It eventually joined Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, New York, East Jersey, and West Jersey under one administration without a local assembly.[^ch06-boston-revolt][^ch06-mass-glorious]
- **Andros’s rule.** Andros enforced the Navigation Acts, denied the validity of Massachusetts land titles that rested on the old charter, restricted town meetings, and promoted the Church of England in largely Puritan regions. He appointed regular officers to lead colonial militia. His rule was profoundly unpopular.[^ch06-boston-revolt]
- **The consequence.** The Dominion concentrated grievances: it threatened land, self-government, and religious identity at once. When news of a change of monarchy arrived, those grievances had an immediate outlet.

### 3.2 The fall of Andros in Boston, 1689

- **The claim.** On April 18, 1689, a Boston uprising against Andros overthrew the Dominion and restored local government. It was the American face of England’s Glorious Revolution.
- **The event.** In November 1688 William of Orange invaded England and James II fell. When word reached Boston, a well-organized body of militia and citizens formed, arrested Andros and other dominion officials, and took members of the Church of England into custody. Neither side suffered casualties, and the leaders of the former Massachusetts Bay Colony reclaimed power.[^ch06-boston-revolt]
- **The wider revolts.** News of the revolution set off parallel uprisings. In New York the militia captain Jacob Leisler seized control in 1689; in Maryland a Protestant association took over the proprietary government; and the former charter colonies restored their governments. The English colonies had conducted their own revolution in the name of the one across the Atlantic.[^ch06-leisler][^ch06-mass-glorious]
- **The consequence.** The Dominion dissolved, and the colonies regained self-government — but on new terms. Massachusetts received a new royal charter in 1691 that joined it with Plymouth and widened the franchise while keeping a royal governor.

### 3.3 Leisler’s rebellion and the politics of the revolution

- **The claim.** In New York, the revolution became a bitter contest between factions that outlasted it. Leisler’s rebellion shows that “revolution” in America could mean an internal struggle over who should rule as much as resistance to the crown.
- **The seizure.** Jacob Leisler, a German-born merchant and militia captain, took control of the southern part of New York in 1689 and governed until 1691, after the 1689 Boston revolt had ended the Dominion. His support came largely from merchants, militia, and lesser colonists against the old elite.[^ch06-leisler]
- **The reversal.** Royal authority was not restored until 1691, when English troops and a new governor arrived. Leisler was arrested, tried, and convicted of treason, and executed. The colony was left split into Leislerian and anti-Leislerian factions that mapped loosely onto English Whig and Tory alignments.[^ch06-leisler]
- **The pattern.** Boston, New York, and Maryland each turned the fall of James II into a local reckoning. The revolution was celebrated as a defense of English liberties and used to settle scores that had nothing to do with England.
- **The consequence.** The 1689 revolutions confirmed that colonial politics now ran through empire: colonists claimed English rights while imperial officials claimed the authority to define them.

## 4. The Covenant Chain and the imperial wars

### 4.1 The making of the Covenant Chain

- **The claim.** In the same years that the New England wars ended, New York and the Haudenosaunee built an alliance — the Covenant Chain — that came to organize English diplomacy across the Northeast. It served both sides and bound neither completely.
- **The origin.** New York’s governor Sir Edmund Andros asked the Five Nations for help in ending the conflicts of 1675, and worked with the Onondaga leader Daniel Garacontié. The Mohawks attacked Metacom’s winter camp, denying him gunpowder, and their intervention helped end the war in New England.[^ch06-chenussio-covenant][^ch06-covenant-wiki]
- **The metaphor.** The Covenant Chain was imaged as a silver chain binding the English ship to the Haudenosaunee Tree of Peace; a three-link chain symbolized Peace, Friendship, and Respect, and the treaties used formulaic phrases — “as long as the sun shines, as long as the waters flow, as long as the grass grows green.” The metaphor was renewed, “polished,” and “brightened” at councils.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-onondaga-covenant]
- **The terms and the dispute.** Later English officials assumed the chain granted them sovereignty over the Haudenosaunee. The Haudenosaunee did not agree. At a covenant council in 1692 their leaders rejected the language of father and son and claimed to be brothers.[^ch06-richter-longhouse][^ch06-covenant-wiki]
- **The consequence.** The Covenant Chain gave New York a leading role in English Indian policy and gave the Haudenosaunee security on their eastern and southern flanks — in exchange for obligations neither party read the same way.

### 4.2 King William’s War and the northern frontier

- **The claim.** European dynastic war became colonial war on Indigenous ground. King William’s War (1689–1697) carried the conflict between England and France into New England, New York, and Canada, where Native nations fought for their own reasons.
- **The war’s opening.** The war in America began with frontier raids by New France and its Abenaki allies against English settlements, and with English and Haudenosaunee attacks to the north. The 1689 revolutions had already unsettled colonial governments, leaving them less able to defend their frontiers.[^ch06-king-william]
- **The Indigenous terms.** For the Haudenosaunee and their neighbors the European war was folded into older conflicts over hunting grounds, captives, and trade. The Covenant Chain obliged the Five Nations to fight beside New York, but their aims were their own.[^ch06-chenussio-covenant]
- **The consequence.** The war demonstrated that the eastern woodlands’ conflicts could not be separated from the empires’ quarrels, and it exhausted and divided Indigenous communities that had survived King Philip’s War.

### 4.3 Queen Anne’s War and the Peace of Utrecht

- **The claim.** Queen Anne’s War (1702–1713) transferred the European war to North America again, and the Peace of Utrecht that ended it rearranged imperial claims, gave Britain new territory and the slave-trade contract, and ignored the Indigenous nations whose lands were at stake.
- **The Deerfield raid.** On February 29, 1704, a French and Native force attacked Deerfield, Massachusetts, killing about 47 colonists and taking 112 captive on a march to Montreal; some captives were ransomed, and others were adopted into Mohawk families at Kahnawake. The raid became famous through the published captivity narrative of the town’s minister, John Williams, whose daughter Eunice was adopted and chose to remain among the Mohawks.[^ch06-deerfield]
- **The war’s conduct.** Queen Anne’s War was fought through frontier raids, ransoms, and captivity on both sides, from New England to the Carolina frontier, while the Haudenosaunee pursued a policy of neutrality between the French and English that the Covenant Chain had to accommodate.[^ch06-qaw][^ch06-richter-longhouse]
- **The settlement.** The Peace of Utrecht, signed between 1713 and 1715, ended the War of the Spanish Succession. France ceded claims to Hudson Bay, Acadia, and Newfoundland to Britain. Britain also won the asiento, the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-qaw]
- **The silences.** The treaty settlements did not consult the Indigenous nations whose homelands changed hands, and the Haudenosaunee continued to treat the Covenant Chain as an alliance among equals rather than a subjection. The diplomatic formula and the imperial assumption of sovereignty remained at odds.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-richter-longhouse]
- **The consequence.** By 1713 the English empire in North America had grown and its Native alliances had hardened into a system — but the system rested on agreements the Native parties read differently.

## 5. Coercion and the imperial slave system through 1713

### 5.1 The imperializing of enslaved labor

- **The claim.** In the same decades that empire expanded, coerced labor became more central, more racialized, and more legally complete. Indian and African slavery grew together, and the colonies’ slave codes multiplied.
- **The scale.** Native people continued to be enslaved after the war, from New England to the southern frontier, while the African slave trade expanded. New England courts and households held both Native and African enslaved people.[^ch06-commonplace-shoemaker][^ch06-newell-changing]
- **The overlapping categories.** “Indian,” “Negro,” and “mulatto” appeared in the same statutes and inventories, and the categories overlapped in people’s lives and in the law. The chapter should treat them as one developing labor regime, not two separate stories.[^ch06-brown-fisher][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]
- **The consequence.** The enslavement that began during the wars became a permanent feature of colonial society, and the codes that defined it hardened in the early eighteenth century.

### 5.2 The New York slave code and the revolt of 1712

- **The claim.** New York’s 1702 slave code and its harsher 1712 code show how colonial law responded to the fear of insurrection by stripping enslaved people of even the few protections they had.
- **The 1702 code.** “An Act for the Regulating of Slaves” barred free people from trading with the enslaved without permission, allowed enslavers to punish the enslaved as they saw fit short of mutilation or death, banned gatherings of three or more enslaved people off their owners’ property, and barred enslaved testimony except against other enslaved people.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]
- **The revolt.** On the night of April 6, 1712, a group of about twenty-five enslaved people gathered in New York City, set fire to a building, and attacked the white residents who came to put it out, killing nine and wounding several. Militia captured most of the rebels; six took their own lives. Of about seventy people taken into custody, about forty were tried and eighteen acquitted; roughly twenty-five were convicted and sentenced to death by hanging, burning, starvation, or breaking on the wheel.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes][^ch06-1712]
- **The consequence.** In December 1712 New York passed “An Act for Preventing, Suppressing and Punishing the Conspiracy and Insurrection of Negroes and Other Slaves,” further restricting the enslaved and punishing manumission. The rebellion and the code together show that enslaved people resisted and that the law answered resistance with more coercion.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]
- **The caution.** The number of rebels and the details of the trial vary across accounts; the chapter should give the figures as reported and identify its source.[^ch06-check-dating]

### 5.3 The asiento and the peace of 1713

- **The claim.** The Peace of Utrecht made enslaved African labor an instrument of European statecraft: Britain won the exclusive right to supply enslaved people to Spanish America, tying the slave trade to the imperial balance of power.
- **The contract.** The treaty texts approved “the Assiento adjusted with the company of England, for the importation of Negro Slaves into the Indies, for the term of thirty years.” The asiento gave British traders privileged access to Spanish American markets and became one of the most valuable prizes of the war.[^ch06-utrecht-doc][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]
- **The economic reading.** Historians note that the economic value of the asiento lay in its command of the slave trade to Spanish America, a commerce the British would exploit for decades and one that connected the imperial wars to the growth of the Atlantic slave system.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki]
- **The consequence.** By 1713 the English empire’s prosperity and its Native alliances and its African slave trade had become one system. The settlement that ended the war over the Spanish throne also fixed the terms of a labor regime that would deepen across the eighteenth century.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Metacom’s War was the most destructive war in seventeenth-century New England.** It killed more than 600 colonists and an estimated 3,000 Native people, destroyed about a dozen towns, and ended Wampanoag and Narragansett military power in the south.[^ch06-umassd][^ch06-great-swamp]
- **Native enslavement was central, not incidental.** New England shipped Native captives to the Caribbean and beyond and bound many more locally; Native people made up the majority of those enslaved in the Americas before 1700.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-fisher]
- **The Pueblo Revolt expelled Spanish rule for twelve years.** On August 10, 1680, the Pueblos rose nearly as one and drove the Spanish out until Vargas’s return in 1692.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]
- **The Glorious Revolution produced colonial uprisings.** Boston overthrew Andros on April 18, 1689, New York divided under Leisler, and Maryland’s government changed hands.[^ch06-boston-revolt][^ch06-leisler]
- **The Covenant Chain was a real and contested alliance.** It organized English diplomacy in the Northeast, and the Haudenosaunee claimed equality within it rather than subjection.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-richter-longhouse]
- **The Peace of Utrecht transferred territory and the asiento.** France ceded Hudson Bay, Acadia, and Newfoundland, and Britain won the slave-trade contract.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-doc]

### 6.2 What is open

- **How to weigh the causes of the wars.** Was Metacom’s War primarily a contest over sovereignty and land, a religious conflict, a consequence of imperial competition, or a civil war within a divided region? Each framework explains part of the record.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-great-swamp]
- **Whether the “middle ground” describes this region.** Richard White’s model of accommodation between Europeans and Indians took shape in the Great Lakes; how far it applies to New England, the Chesapeake, or the Southwest is contested.[^ch06-middle-ground-stanford][^ch06-lipman]
- **How much of Pueblo religion and autonomy survived the reconquest.** Recent scholarship stresses Pueblo agency in the return, but the balance between accommodation and coercion is debated.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest][^ch06-pueblo-ipcc]
- **The meaning of the 1689 revolutions.** Were they colonial versions of a metropolitan revolution, local power struggles draped in English rhetoric, or both?[^ch06-leisler][^ch06-mass-glorious]
- **The scale of Native enslavement.** The commonly repeated figure of more than 1,200 Native people enslaved in seventeenth-century New England is an estimate; the *Seaflower*’s count varies among sources, and the total traffic is not fully documented.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-seaflower]
- **What the archives cannot show.** The enslaved and the defeated left few records of their own. The 1680 revolt and the 1712 rebellion reach us chiefly through the documents of the people who suppressed them, and the chapter must not mistake the victors’ categories for the whole of the past.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which Indigenous nations made their own decisions, empires fought their own wars, and the two became inseparable. Metacom, Po’pay, the Haudenosaunee councilors, and the enslaved rebels of New York were actors, not symbols.
- **White’s contribution and limit.** *The Middle Ground* transformed the field by showing that Europeans and Indians constructed common meanings and accommodations rather than simply conquering and being conquered. Its concept, however, was place-specific: it described the *pays d’en haut* around the Great Lakes, and its admirers extended it to places where the balance of power ran differently.[^ch06-middle-ground-stanford]
- **The critics’ contribution.** Later historians argue that the “middle ground” was an incomplete characterization even on its home turf, and that in many regions Native and European worlds pulled apart rather than merging. In New England, the Chesapeake, and the Missouri Valley, the model explains less than it does in the Great Lakes.[^ch06-lipman]
- **Lepore’s contribution.** Lepore reads the war through the language its participants used, showing that the English fought as much over their own identity as over land. Her account of who was permitted to speak, and how, is a model for handling the era’s one-sided archives.[^ch06-lepore-name]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, the positions say that sovereignty and alliance were negotiated across the period, that the negotiations were real, and that they were conducted under conditions of growing coercion. The 1713 settlement did not end the argument; it reset its terms.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]
- **The lesson.** The best history refuses the single-cause story and the single-origin story alike, and it refuses to let either empire or victim stand in for the people who made the choices.

## Legacies

The 1713 settlement left an imperial order that was also a set of bargains with Indigenous nations. Britain had gained territory and the slave-trade contract, but its empire in North America rested on the Covenant Chain and on treaties the Haudenosaunee and their neighbors understood as alliances among equals. The empire prospered where the bargains held and faced war where they broke, as they did repeatedly over the next half-century.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-wiki]

The enslavement that began during the wars left a regime that outlived the empire that made it. New England’s Native captives, sold in the Caribbean or bound in local households, joined the African slaves who would make the eighteenth-century plantation economy. The 1712 New York revolt and the code that answered it were early signs of the coercion that would define the century, and of the resistance that answered coercion.[^ch06-newell-changing][^ch06-ny-slave-codes]

The memory of these years became a foundation of American identity. Metacom’s War was retold as the colonists’ heroic ordeal, and Metacom himself as a tragic or villainous foil; the Pueblo Revolt was largely forgotten outside the Southwest until Po’pay’s statue was given a place in the Capitol. The legacies are double: a nation built on conquest also kept alive the memory of those who resisted it.[^ch06-lepore-name][^ch06-pueblo-countryman]

## Optional threads

- **The imperial frame.** Boston, New York, Santa Fe, Albany, and Deerfield all sat inside one European competition for empire, but the colonies and Native nations pursued their own interests within it. The Covenant Chain, the Spanish reconquest, and the Peace of Utrecht were imperial events and Indigenous ones at once.[^ch06-richter-longhouse][^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- **Archives and silences.** The defeated appear in the victors’ records: Wampanoag grievances survive because a Quaker official wrote them down; the enslaved appear in inventories and court minutes; the Pueblos’ revolt is known mainly through Spanish accounts. The chapter should name these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch06-easton-relation][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]

## Summary

- Metacom’s War (1675–1676) began at Swansea and became the most destructive conflict in seventeenth-century New England, ending with Metacom’s death in August 1676 and fighting in the north until 1678.[^ch06-great-swamp][^ch06-congregational]
- The Great Swamp Fight of December 19, 1675 killed hundreds of Narragansett non-combatants and was one of the war’s most brutal engagements.[^ch06-great-swamp]
- New England shipped Native captives to the Caribbean and beyond; the *Seaflower* carried about 180 out of Boston in 1676, and the region enslaved more than an estimated 1,200 Native people in the seventeenth century.[^ch06-seaflower][^ch06-newell-changing]
- The Pueblos rose on August 10, 1680, killed some 400 Spaniards, and ended Spanish rule in New Mexico for twelve years.[^ch06-pueblo-countryman][^ch06-pueblo-wiki]
- Diego de Vargas reconquered New Mexico beginning in 1692 through negotiation and the exploitation of Pueblo divisions, and Spanish rule took years to consolidate.[^ch06-pueblo-reconquest]
- Boston overthrew Governor Andros and the Dominion of New England on April 18, 1689, in the American face of the Glorious Revolution.[^ch06-boston-revolt]
- Leisler’s rebellion divided New York from 1689 until Leisler’s execution in 1691, and the colony stayed split between rival factions.[^ch06-leisler]
- The Covenant Chain allied New York and the Haudenosaunee and gave the Five Nations standing that imperial officials read differently from the Haudenosaunee themselves.[^ch06-covenant-wiki][^ch06-richter-longhouse]
- King William’s War (1689–1697) and Queen Anne’s War (1702–1713) carried European conflict onto Indigenous ground; the 1704 Deerfield raid became the era’s most famous captivity story.[^ch06-king-william][^ch06-deerfield]
- The Peace of Utrecht transferred Hudson Bay, Acadia, and Newfoundland to Britain and won Britain the asiento, the slave-trade contract to Spanish America.[^ch06-utrecht-wiki][^ch06-utrecht-doc]
- New York’s slave codes of 1702 and 1712 answer the 1712 rebellion with harsher law, showing coercion and resistance advancing together.[^ch06-ny-slave-codes]
- Historians disagree about whether accommodation, conquest, language, or slavery best explains these decades, but they agree that Indigenous nations and enslaved people made choices that shaped the outcome.[^ch06-middle-ground-stanford][^ch06-lipman]

### War and captivity documents

- **John Easton, “True Relation of what I know and of Reports and My Understanding Concerning the Beginning and Progress of the War now Between the English and the Indians” (1675).** A Rhode Island official’s attempt to record the Wampanoag grievances as well as the English case; essential for the opening scene and the first epigraph. Use it to locate primary material and to name the silences.

### The Pueblo Revolt

- **Edward Countryman, “The Pueblo Revolt,” *History Now* (Gilder Lehrman Institute, 2011).** A concise scholarly essay on the revolt’s causes and consequences. Useful for framing; return to the Spanish and Franciscan records for detail.
- **Indian Pueblo Cultural Center, “Pueblo Revolt.”** A Pueblo institution’s account that treats the revolt as an act of resilience and survival. Use it for the Pueblo perspective and for the knotted-cord story.
- **GSHA, “1692 Reconquest.”** A short account of Diego de Vargas’s return and the erosion of Pueblo unity. A summary; verify its chronology against the documentary record.

### Empire, alliance, and war

- **“Covenant Chain,” Wikipedia.** A usable overview with citations to Richter and Jennings; treat as a guide to the scholarship, not as the scholarship. The 1692 quotation must be verified in Richter.
- **Onondaga Nation, “Polishing the Silver Covenant Chain.”** The Haudenosaunee account of the chain’s metaphors, including the 1710 visit of four diplomats to Queen Anne. The clearest Indigenous-authored description of the alliance.
- **“Peace of Utrecht,” Wikipedia, and the 1713 treaty text at PrimaryDocuments.ca.** The encyclopedia supplies context; the treaty text supplies the asiento clause and the peace articles. Read the actual instrument before quoting it.

### The debate

- **Richard White, *The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815* (2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2011).** The book that reframed European–Indigenous relations as accommodation. Consulted here through the publisher description; read it before stating its thesis as fact.
- **Jill Lepore, *The Name of War: King Philip’s War and the Origins of American Identity* (New York: Knopf, 1998).** The foundational study of how the war was written and remembered. Consulted through the publisher’s authorized first chapter; read it before quoting beyond that chapter.
- **Margaret Ellen Newell, *Brethren by Nature: New England Indians, Colonists, and the Origins of American Slavery* (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 2015).** The leading study of Native slavery in New England. Consulted through a published review and a university news account; read it before presenting its figures as settled.

> *A closing note.* The years from 1675 to 1713 made the English empire in North America and unmade the nations that had shaped it. At Swansea a dispute over who had the right to judge and punish became a war; at Santa Fe a coordinated revolt drove out an empire; at Boston, New York, and Albany colonists and Indigenous nations renegotiated who would rule and on what terms; and at Utrecht the empires divided a continent they did not fully control. Through it all, the enslavement of Native and African people deepened, and the people it held resisted. The chapter begins with a shooting in a Plymouth Colony town and ends with a treaty in a Dutch city that never consulted the nations whose homelands it reassigned.[^ch06-seaflower][^ch06-utrecht-doc]
