# Chapter 45. The Nineties: Globalization, Culture Wars, and the New Economy (1991–2000)

> “My fellow Americans — on this day, with high hopes and brave hearts, in massive numbers, the American people have voted to make a new beginning.”
>
> — Bill Clinton, address in Little Rock, Arkansas, accepting election as the 42nd president of the United States, November 3, 1992.[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]

> “If you’re paying $12, $13, $14 an hour for factory workers, and you can move your factory south of the border, pay $1 an hour for labor … there will be a giant sucking sound going south.”
>
> — H. Ross Perot, presidential debate at the University of Richmond, October 15, 1992.[^ch45-perot-debate]

> “What we may be witnessing is not just the end of the Cold War, or the passing of a particular period of postwar history, but the end of history as such: that is, the end point of mankind’s ideological evolution and the universalization of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government.”
>
> — Francis Fukuyama, “The End of History?,” *The National Interest*, Summer 1989.[^ch45-fukuyama-1989]

Three voices, three verdicts. Clinton named the election as a new beginning and promised a government that offered “a hand up not a hand out.” Perot named the same economic integration as a transfer of American jobs to lower-wage Mexico. Fukuyama named the end of the Cold War as the ideological victory of liberal democracy. They disagree about whether the decade was opportunity, loss, or triumph, and their disagreement frames the chapter’s question: who gained from the post-Cold War settlement, and what did the boom leave out?[^ch45-check-epigraphs]

On the night of November 3, 1992, Bill Clinton spoke to supporters in Little Rock, Arkansas. He turned to the result. “My fellow Americans,” he said, “on this day, with high hopes and brave hearts, in massive numbers, the American people have voted to make a new beginning.” He called the election “a clarion call for our country to face the challenges of the end of the Cold War and the beginning of the next century, to restore growth to our country and opportunity to our people.”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]

The speech described a bargain. Clinton said the victory belonged to “the people who are ready to compete and win in the global economy, but who need a government that offers a hand up not a hand out.” He promised to “offer those on welfare new opportunity and the challenge to move to work” and to “export American products, not American jobs.” He asked voters of every party to join in creating a “Reunited States,” and he closed with the line that had become the campaign’s emblem: “I still believe in a place called Hope.”[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992]

The returns measured a plurality, not a mandate. Clinton won 44,909,326 votes, or 43.0 percent, and 370 electoral votes. Bush won 39,103,882 votes, or 37.4 percent, and 168 electoral votes. Perot won 19,741,657 votes, or 18.9 percent, and no electoral votes. The total was 104,425,014 votes. Perot’s share was the highest for a candidate outside the two major parties since 1912. The returns recorded a three-candidate race in which no one won a majority. They did not settle what the new administration would do.[^ch45-app-1992]

The record establishes the date, the place, the text, and the returns. It does not establish what the crowd felt, how individual voters decided, or whether the speech caused any later outcome. On that night no one at the podium knew that the expansion would become the longest on record, that the North American Free Trade Agreement would pass a year later, that the Republicans would take Congress in 1994, that Clinton would be impeached, or that the Supreme Court would decide the next election. The question was already present in the room: who would gain from the peace and the boom, and who would be left out?[^ch45-clinton-victory-1992][^ch45-app-1992][^ch45-check-scene]

This chapter covers the years from the recession of 1990–91 through the disputed election of 2000 and the accession of George W. Bush. It asks who gained from the post-Cold War settlement and what the boom left out. Chapter 44 owns the end of the Cold War and the Gulf War; this chapter takes up the order that followed. It draws on presidential speeches, statutes and roll calls, federal data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Census Bureau, the Congressional Budget Office, and the Bureau of Justice Statistics, trade and investment records, court decisions, the records of the interventions, and the secondary literature. It treats “globalization,” “the new economy,” “the Third Way,” “welfare reform,” “culture wars,” “mass incarceration,” “the unipolar moment,” and “the end of history” as contested names that require definition, not neutral descriptions.[^ch45-check-scope][^ch45-check-dates-appD][^ch45-check-ch44]

The record over-represents presidents, officials, investors, and journalists. It under-represents the poor, the displaced, the incarcerated, the uninsured, and the people of Rwanda and the Balkans whose lives the decade’s politics touched. Where those voices do not survive, the silence is named rather than filled with a composite witness. Gross domestic product, median household income, and the top income share measure different things. Each figure below carries its series, dates, units, and denominators.[^ch45-check-silences][^ch45-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the recession, the expansion, the digital economy, the distribution of the gains, and the federal balance. §2 examines NAFTA, Chapter 11, the World Trade Organization, Seattle, and the evidence on jobs and wages. §3 examines deficit reduction, welfare reform, the crime act, health care, and rights. §4 examines the 1994 takeover, Oklahoma City, impeachment, and the 2000 election. §5 examines Somalia, Rwanda, Bosnia, Kosovo, and American predominance. §6 asks whether the decade’s growth was broadly shared and how historians have weighed its aggregate benefits against its distributional costs. The chapter begins in Little Rock on the night of November 3, 1992.[^ch45-check-scope]

## 1. The boom and its distribution (1991–2000)

### 1.1 The claim: the longest expansion on record, and a widening divide

**The claim.** The 1990s produced the longest peacetime expansion in American history, with low unemployment, low inflation, and a technology-led surge in productivity. The gains, however, were distributed unevenly, and the decade left a public debate organized around the difference between aggregate growth and median experience. The section holds the boom and the divide together, because neither is intelligible without the other.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-census-income-1999]

### 1.2 The 1990–91 recession and the long expansion

The recession began in July 1990 and ended in March 1991. The recovery was slow at first. Unemployment kept rising after the official trough and peaked at 7.8 percent in June 1992, fifteen months into the recovery. That lag is one reason the economy remained a central issue in the 1992 campaign. The business-cycle dates come from the National Bureau of Economic Research; the unemployment rate comes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Neither is a measure of how the recession felt to the people who lost jobs.[^ch45-nber-cycles][^ch45-bls-june-1992][^ch45-check-numbers]

The expansion that began in March 1991 ran until March 2001. Brookings’s retrospective on 1990s economic policy records that between 1993 and 2000 the United States had “the best economic performance of the past three decades.” In 2000, it notes, the expansion “surpassed in length the expansion of the 1960s, and thus became the longest on record.” During Clinton’s second term, real economic growth averaged about 4.5 percent per year. Unemployment fell to about 4 percent, inflation stayed low, and the federal budget recorded structural surpluses.[^ch45-brookings-1990s]

The causes are plural and disputed: monetary policy under Alan Greenspan, the fiscal tightening of 1993, the investment boom in information technology, and the productivity gains of the late 1990s. Economists still dispute how much of the productivity acceleration was lasting and how much was cyclical. The chapter presents these as overlapping claims, not a settled single cause.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 1.3 Median income and the distribution of the gains

Median household income was about $42,000 in 1999, according to the 2000 census. That was 7.7 percent more than in 1989 after adjusting for 29.8 percent inflation over the period. The median divides households into two equal groups, half above and half below. It is not an average, and it is not a measure of wealth.[^ch45-census-income-1999][^ch45-check-numbers]

The top of the distribution moved differently. The Congressional Budget Office found that between 1979 and 2007 income grew by about 275 percent for the top 1 percent of households, far faster than for the middle and the bottom. A Center on Budget and Policy Priorities analysis of the same data recorded that the average after-tax income of the top 1 percent tripled between 1979 and 2000. The endpoints matter: a figure ending in 2007 is not a 1990s measurement, and a figure beginning in 1979 includes the entire period before the decade under study.[^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-cbpp-2003]

The mechanisms moved together: tax changes altered after-tax incomes, the bull market raised asset values, trade and immigration changed employment in some sectors, and labor-market institutions affected the bottom. The chapter separates them rather than assigning the whole shift to one policy.[^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-check-debate-works]

There is counterevidence, and it belongs in the same account. In the tight labor market of the late 1990s, real wages rose across the distribution, including at the bottom. The poverty rate fell to 11.3 percent in 2000, representing about 31.1 million people, down from 12.7 percent in 1998. The top share widened while the bottom gained. These are not contradictory findings, and the distribution of the gain is a separate question from its existence.[^ch45-census-poverty-2000][^ch45-brookings-1990s]

### 1.4 Technology, the new economy, and the bubble

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 was the first major overhaul of American communications law in almost 62 years. The Federal Communications Commission described its goal as letting “anyone enter any communications business” and letting “any communications business compete in any market against any other.”[^ch45-telecom-act]

The investment boom was real. The Nasdaq Composite rose 86 percent in 1999 alone. It peaked at 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000. By October 2002 it had fallen by about 78 percent from that peak. The collapse came after the 2000 election, and it must be dated as such. “The new economy” named a claim: that information technology had raised the sustainable rate of productivity growth and reduced the trade-off between growth and inflation. The claim had evidence behind it, including the productivity gains of the late 1990s. It also had a speculative excess that the subsequent decline exposed. The chapter treats it as a period belief with a mixed record, not as a verdict.[^ch45-dotcom-goldman][^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-numbers]

### 1.5 The federal balance and Map 36

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 was signed on August 10, 1993. It raised the top federal income-tax rate from 31 percent to 39.6 percent, raised other taxes, and cut spending. It passed the House 218–216 and the Senate 51–50, with Vice President Al Gore breaking the tie. No Republican voted for it. The law is the fiscal hinge of the decade, and its effects are still debated.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-app-budget]

The federal balance moved from a fiscal 1992 deficit of $290.3 billion to a fiscal 1998 surplus of $69.3 billion and a fiscal 2000 surplus of $236.2 billion. Those are annual balances in current dollars, not the accumulated debt, and they do not show what would have happened without the 1993 law, the 1990 budget agreement, or the boom.[^ch45-app-budget][^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-numbers]

Map 36, “Where the boom landed, 1992–2000,” accompanies this section. It will show county-level population and employment growth, state its dates and geographic units, and distinguish measured growth from areas the data do not cover. The supporting series are County Business Patterns, the 2000 census, and Appendix B, Table 7.[^ch45-check-map36]

## 2. NAFTA, the WTO, and Seattle (1992–1999)

### 2.1 The claim: integration delivered aggregate gains and concentrated losses

**The claim.** The decade’s trade and investment agreements integrated the North American and global economies and produced aggregate gains in output and lower consumer prices. They also concentrated displacement in specific industries and communities and shifted authority over investment disputes to tribunals beyond the ordinary courts. The section holds the aggregate case and the distributional case together.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-epi-nafta]

### 2.2 NAFTA: negotiation, passage, and entry into force

The dates must be kept distinct. President George H. W. Bush signed NAFTA on December 17, 1992. Congress approved the implementing legislation on November 20, 1993. President Clinton signed the North American Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act, Public Law 103-182, on December 8, 1993. The agreement entered into force on January 1, 1994. The implementing act was H.R.3450, introduced on November 4, 1993. A single “NAFTA date” obscures four different decisions.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-nafta-impl-act]

The votes were close in the House and comfortable in the Senate. The House passed the implementing act 234–200 on November 17, 1993. The Senate passed it 61–38 on November 20. The winning coalition depended heavily on Republican votes, while Clinton supplied the Democratic votes he could. The result split the president’s party and exposed the divide between the administration’s market-oriented wing and organized labor.[^ch45-nafta-votes]

The opposition had a case. Perot’s “giant sucking sound” became the shorthand for critics who argued that the agreement would accelerate relocation to lower-wage, lower-standard jurisdictions and would not enforce its labor and environmental side agreements. Some plants did move and some workers did lose jobs; the dispute is over how many, why, and what else the agreement produced.[^ch45-perot-debate][^ch45-epi-nafta]

### 2.3 Chapter 11 and the In Focus

The investor-state provisions of NAFTA Chapter 11 permit an investor of one party to seek money damages, through arbitration, for measures of another party that allegedly violate the chapter’s investment obligations. The agreement’s investment chapter includes standards for national treatment, a minimum standard of treatment, and expropriation and compensation. NAFTA was among the first agreements to make investment-protection standards enforceable through investor-state arbitration, giving foreign investors direct access to binding international tribunals. It became a template for later trade pacts, and it moved a category of disputes beyond the reach of the voters the 1992 campaign had addressed.[^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-nafta-ch11-state][^ch45-check-scope]

**In Focus.** Article 1110 of NAFTA governs expropriation and compensation. It requires that compensation be “equivalent to the fair market value of the expropriated investment immediately before the expropriation took place,” and it says the compensation “shall not reflect any change in value occurring because the intended expropriation had become known earlier.” Article 1116 allows an investor of a party, on its own behalf, to submit a claim to arbitration that the other party has breached an obligation and that the investor has incurred loss or damage. Article 1120 provides the arbitration mechanisms. The words carry the dispute. “Measure tantamount to expropriation” can reach regulation that leaves title in place but destroys value. “Fair market value” requires a valuation at a hypothetical moment before the measure was known. A private investor can bring the claim without asking its own government to espouse it, and the tribunal, not a domestic court, decides. The provision is a legal mechanism, not a verdict on the decade; its meaning lies in the cases that applied it.[^ch45-nafta-ch11][^ch45-check-focus]

### 2.4 The WTO and the Seattle protests

The Marrakesh Agreement, signed on April 15, 1994, concluded the Uruguay Round of trade negotiations that ran from 1986 to 1994 and established the World Trade Organization. The WTO entered into force on January 1, 1995, replacing the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade as an institution. The change gave the trading system a formal organization, a dispute-settlement body, and a membership that extended beyond the original contracting parties.[^ch45-wto-marrakesh]

The Third WTO Ministerial Conference met in Seattle from November 30 to December 3, 1999. It drew tens of thousands of protesters and came to be called the “Battle of Seattle.” The demonstrations were the work of identifiable coalitions — labor unions, environmental groups, farm organizations, and consumer advocates — rather than a spontaneous national mood. Inside the hall, developing-country delegations disputed the agenda and the terms of a new round. The conference failed to launch one. The failure owed as much to disagreements among member governments as to the streets, and the protests mattered most for what they made visible: the connection between trade rules, labor standards, environmental protection, and the authority of international institutions.[^ch45-wto-seattle]

### 2.5 The distributional record

Defenders of the agreements argued that they lowered prices for consumers, expanded export markets for American producers and farmers, and anchored liberalization in Mexico and elsewhere. That case deserves its strongest form. The agreements did increase trade and investment flows, and consumers benefited from cheaper goods. The aggregate gains are real, and an accounting that counts only the lost jobs is incomplete.[^ch45-nafta-crs]

The critical estimates are also real. The Economic Policy Institute estimated that the rise in the United States trade deficit with Canada and Mexico through 2002 displaced production that supported 879,280 American jobs, 686,700 of them in manufacturing. The estimate uses employment multipliers and a counterfactual trade balance. Other economists argue that the job changes reflected broader technological and macroeconomic forces and cannot be attributed to the agreement alone. A 2026 Peterson Institute commentary on a later mortality study made the methodological point sharply: statistical association is not policy causation, and the absence of a safety net for displaced workers may matter more than the trade rule itself.[^ch45-epi-nafta][^ch45-piie-nafta]

No single net job number captures both the gains to consumers and exporters and the losses to displaced workers. The chapter separates the aggregate effect from the distributional one and marks the limits of each estimate.[^ch45-epi-nafta][^ch45-piie-nafta][^ch45-check-debate-works]

## 3. The Third Way, welfare, and crime (1993–1996)

### 3.1 The claim: a centrist program that reduced the safety net and expanded the punishing state

**The claim.** The Clinton administration’s domestic program combined deficit reduction and a claim to have ended “the era of big government” with a welfare law that ended the federal entitlement to cash assistance and a crime law that expanded federal punishment. The section holds those choices together as one governing strategy, not as a list of separate policies.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-sotu-1996][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-crime-bill]

### 3.2 Deficit reduction and the “Third Way”

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 raised the top marginal rate and other taxes and reduced spending. It passed without a single Republican vote in either chamber. The administration argued that the deficit reduction would lower interest rates and enable the later expansion. That argument is plausible and contested. The 1990s expansion had multiple causes, and the counterfactual without the 1993 law cannot be observed. The chapter presents the claim as a claim, not as a proven sequence.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-check-debate-works]

The governing formula came in the 1996 State of the Union. “The era of big government is over,” Clinton said, adding that the country could not “go back to the era of fending for yourself” and had to “go forward to the era of working together.” The phrase was not a description of a smaller federal government; federal spending did not shrink in absolute terms, and the federal role in policing expanded. It was a claim about what government should do: less direct cash assistance, more work requirements, more tax credits, more policing, and more market competition.[^ch45-sotu-1996][^ch45-obra-1993]

The fiscal measure belongs beside the slogan. The deficit fell from $290.3 billion in fiscal 1992 to $21.9 billion in fiscal 1997 and became a surplus of $69.3 billion in fiscal 1998. By fiscal 2000 the surplus was $236.2 billion. A surplus is a balance, not a level of debt or of spending, and the federal government could run one while spending more than it had in 1992 because revenues rose faster.[^ch45-app-budget][^ch45-check-numbers]

### 3.3 The 1994 crime act

The Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, H.R.3355, became Public Law 103-322 on September 13, 1994. It funded community policing, created the federal death-penalty expansion of the Federal Death Penalty Act of 1994, imposed mandatory life imprisonment for a third violent felony, and included a ban on certain semiautomatic assault weapons.[^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-justice-death-penalty]

The law expanded federal criminal jurisdiction and punishment. It was part of the longer rise of mass incarceration, a term that names the sustained growth of the prison and jail population and its concentration among Black and Latino men. At the end of 2000, 9.7 percent of Black men aged 25 to 29 were in prison, compared with 2.9 percent of Hispanic men and 1.1 percent of White men in the same age group. About one in every 109 American men was in a state or federal prison. Between 1990 and 2000, the number of male prisoners grew 77 percent and the number of female prisoners grew 108 percent. States added more than 528,000 prison beds, an increase of 81 percent.[^ch45-bjs-prisoners]

The crime rate fell over the same years. The Federal Bureau of Investigation’s Uniform Crime Reports put the violent crime rate at 758.2 per 100,000 people in 1991 and 506.5 in 2000. That is a substantial decline, and it is also a national aggregate that mixes very different local trends. The strongest case for the law is that violent crime imposed real harm on affected communities and that police funding and tougher penalties could reduce it. The case is strongest when it is specific about which provisions might have mattered.[^ch45-fbi-ucr][^ch45-crime-bill]

The causal question is open. The crime decline began before the 1994 act, continued through the decade, and occurred in cities with different policing strategies and imprisonment rates. The law cannot be singled out without a counterfactual the record does not supply. What can be said is that the law expanded the federal role, incarceration rose, and crime fell.[^ch45-fbi-ucr][^ch45-bjs-prisoners][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 Welfare reform

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, H.R.3734, became Public Law 104-193 on August 22, 1996. It replaced the Aid to Families with Dependent Children program with the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant. It imposed work requirements, set a five-year lifetime limit on federally funded assistance, and restricted benefits to some immigrants and to some families with additional children. The law recast the safety net as a time-limited program tied to employment.[^ch45-prwora]

The caseload fell sharply. In fiscal 1996, 4.5 million families received AFDC. By fiscal 2001, about 2.1 million families received TANF, a decline of 53 percent. The poverty rate also fell, to 11.3 percent in 2000. The contributions of the law, the strong labor market, the minimum-wage and tax-credit expansions, and state policy choices must be distinguished. The timing is suggestive but not decisive: the caseload began falling before the law took effect, and it fell fastest in states with strong economies and generous work supports.[^ch45-tanf-caseload][^ch45-census-poverty-2000]

The debate is about what the numbers mean. Supporters read falling caseloads and rising employment as proof that the law worked. Critics read them as the product of a strong economy and a reduced safety net, and they note that many families who left the rolls worked at low wages and that some lost income. Both readings can draw on the same data, and the chapter uses the poverty and employment record rather than a single caseload figure.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-tanf-caseload][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Rights, health care, and the uninsured

The administration’s record on gay and lesbian rights was double-sided. The “Don’t Ask, Don’t Tell” policy, announced on July 19, 1993, allowed gay and lesbian service members to serve only if they concealed their identity. The Defense of Marriage Act, signed on September 21, 1996, barred federal recognition of same-sex marriages and allowed states to refuse recognition of other states’ marriages. The Clinton Library’s own topic guide says the two policies “would continue to cause harm to gay and lesbian Americans for years.” The statutes and the administration’s stated reasons belong here, and the costs are plain.[^ch45-dadt-doma]

The health-care effort failed. The administration created the Task Force on National Health Care Reform on January 25, 1993, with Hillary Rodham Clinton as chair. Its Health Security Act ran to 1,342 pages. The insurance industry’s “Harry and Louise” advertisements attacked the plan, and opposition grew through 1994. On September 26, 1994, the bill’s sponsor, Senator George Mitchell, declared it dead after several attempts at compromise. The failure left the coverage system in place.[^ch45-health-care]

The number of uninsured Americans was a persistent feature of the decade. In 1999, 42.6 million people, or 15.5 percent of the population, were without health insurance for the entire year. That was a decline from 16.3 percent in 1998, the first decline since 1987. The improvement came with the strong labor market and employer coverage, not with a new universal program. The uninsured are under-represented in the political record because they are less organized and less visible than the insurers, employers, and providers who shape the debate, and the same administration that proposed universal coverage signed a welfare law that reduced cash assistance and a marriage law that wrote discrimination into federal statute.[^ch45-census-health-1999][^ch45-dadt-doma][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-check-silences]

## 4. Rights, organizing, impeachment, and the 2000 election (1994–2000)

### 4.1 The claim: a conservative revival, a personal scandal, and an election decided by a court

**The claim.** The decade’s political history runs from the Republican takeover of 1994 through the budget confrontations, the domestic-terror bombing of 1995, the impeachment of 1998, and the disputed election of 2000. In that election the presidency was decided by a divided Supreme Court and a margin of 537 votes in Florida. The section asks how a strong economy and a booming decade produced so unsettled a politics.[^ch45-contract-america][^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-app-2000]

### 4.2 The 1994 takeover and the budget confrontations

Republican House candidates signed the Contract with America on September 27, 1994. They pledged a set of reforms and floor votes in the first 100 days. In November the party won control of both chambers for the first time in four decades. The Republicans gained 52 House seats and held 230; they took 52 Senate seats. Newt Gingrich became Speaker. The takeover was the work of an organized conservative movement, not a spontaneous national mood.[^ch45-contract-america]

The cultural language was explicit. At the 1992 Republican convention, Patrick J. Buchanan said “there is a religious war going on in this country. It is a cultural war, as critical to the kind of nation we shall be as was the Cold War itself, for this war is for the soul of America.” The 1994 campaign translated that language into a program of welfare, crime, term limits, tax cuts, and a balanced budget. The program was not identical to the complaint, but the two reinforced each other. The conflicts over schooling, abortion, and family life gave the coalition its language, even when its legislative program was fiscal.[^ch45-buchanan-1992][^ch45-contract-america]

The new majority’s budget and welfare proposals produced confrontations with the president. The first government shutdown began on November 14, 1995, and lasted five days. The second began on December 16, 1995, and ended on January 6, 1996. More than 800,000 “nonessential” federal employees were furloughed. Public opinion blamed the Republicans, and the pressure helped bring them to the table. The episode established a pattern that later shutdowns would repeat: the party that forced the closure often paid a political price.[^ch45-miller-shutdown]

Clinton won reelection in 1996. He took 379 electoral votes and 49.2 percent of the popular vote. Bob Dole took 159 electoral votes and 40.7 percent, and Perot took 8.4 percent. The result ratified the president while the Republicans kept Congress. Divided government became the decade’s normal condition, and the budget fights continued.[^ch45-app-1996]

### 4.3 Oklahoma City and domestic terrorism

On the morning of April 19, 1995, Timothy McVeigh detonated a truck bomb outside the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in Oklahoma City. The attack killed 168 people, including 19 children, and injured several hundred more. The Federal Bureau of Investigation calls it “the worst act of homegrown terrorism in the nation’s history.” McVeigh was arrested about 90 minutes later, convicted, and executed.[^ch45-fbi-okc]

The bombing drew on the decade’s antigovernment and militia currents, and it exposed the danger of a politics that treated the federal government as an enemy. The evidence does not implicate a broader group, and the record of the trial is the place to establish the specific conspiracy. McVeigh acted with accomplices, but the plot was his and theirs, not a movement’s.[^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 The impeachment of 1998

The House adopted two articles of impeachment against Clinton in December 1998. The first charged perjury before a grand jury; the second charged obstruction of justice. Both arose from the independent counsel’s investigation of Clinton’s relationship with a White House intern, Monica Lewinsky, and from his testimony about it. The House approved the perjury article 228–206 on December 19 and the obstruction article 221–212; two other articles failed.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-starr-report]

The Senate acquitted the president on February 12, 1999. On the perjury article, the vote was 45–55 for conviction. On the obstruction article, it was 50–50. Both fell short of the two-thirds required by the Constitution. The votes were largely along party lines, with a handful of defections.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-impeachment-congress]

The episode was a constitutional confrontation as well as a political fight. The House majority argued that the president’s testimony and his efforts to conceal the relationship were the kinds of misconduct the impeachment clause was meant to reach. The president’s defenders argued that the charges were a partisan attempt to undo an election and that the conduct did not meet the standard of high crimes and misdemeanors. The Senate’s failure to convict did not settle the constitutional question.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-check-debate-works]

The political cost was real. The impeachment consumed the capital for a year, distracted from legislation, and deepened the partisan divide. It also coincided with a strong economy and high public approval for the president, a juxtaposition that defined the decade’s strangest politics.[^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-brookings-1990s]

### 4.5 The 2000 election and Bush v. Gore

The election of 2000 produced a popular-vote plurality for Al Gore and an Electoral College majority for George W. Bush. Bush won 271 electoral votes to Gore’s 266, with one District of Columbia elector abstaining. Gore won 50,992,335 votes, or 48.4 percent; Bush won 50,455,156, or 47.9 percent; Ralph Nader won 2,882,738, or 2.7 percent. The Electoral College and the popular vote pointed in opposite directions.[^ch45-app-2000][^ch45-archives-2000]

Florida decided the presidency. The initial count put Bush ahead by 1,784 votes. A machine recount reduced the margin to 327. After the litigation, the state certified Bush the winner by 537 votes out of 5,963,110 cast. Each margin belongs to a different stage: the first count, the machine recount, and the certified result. They are not interchangeable.[^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-constcenter-2000][^ch45-app-2000]

The recount dispute went to the Supreme Court. In *Bush v. Gore*, 531 U.S. 98 (2000), seven justices agreed that the manual recount’s varying standards violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Five justices concluded that no constitutionally valid recount could be completed within the federal deadline of December 12. The recount ended, and Bush remained the winner. The per curiam opinion limited its holding to the circumstances of the case; the dissenters argued that the majority had stopped a recount it could not lawfully stop.[^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-check-2000]

The holding and the political outcome are different things. The Court decided a legal question about how votes would be counted and whether a recount could be completed. The political outcome was that Bush became president. The chapter presents the reasoning and the dissent without treating either as the final historical verdict.[^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-constcenter-2000]

Bush’s accession is the immediate prehistory of Chapter 46. The chapter ends there. It does not narrate the attacks of September 11, 2001, and it does not give the new administration knowledge of them.[^ch45-check-ch46][^ch45-check-bridge]

## 5. Rwanda, the Balkans, and American predominance (1992–1999)

### 5.1 The claim: a superpower that intervened unevenly

**The claim.** In the decade after the Cold War, the United States intervened in Somalia, Bosnia, and Kosovo but did not intervene to stop the genocide in Rwanda. The contrast between the interventions and the non-intervention is the section’s problem. It asks what governed the choices and what they cost.[^ch45-state-somalia][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-dayton-state][^ch45-nato-kosovo]

### 5.2 Somalia

The United States began Operation Restore Hope in December 1992 under President George H. W. Bush, acting on a United Nations mandate. The operation transitioned to UNOSOM II in March 1993. On October 3, 1993, forces loyal to the warlord Muhammad Farah Aideed shot down two Black Hawk helicopters in a battle in Mogadishu. Eighteen American soldiers were killed, and the images of American bodies being dragged through the streets were broadcast around the world.[^ch45-state-somalia]

Clinton addressed the nation on October 7. He defended the mission, acknowledged the nation’s anger and horror at the desecration of the soldiers’ bodies, and set a date for withdrawal. The episode set the political limits of later interventions. It taught a generation of officials that a humanitarian mission could end in a firefight with no strategic payoff and that Congress and the public would ask why.[^ch45-clinton-somalia][^ch45-state-somalia]

### 5.3 Rwanda

On April 6, 1994, a plane carrying President Juvénal Habyarimana of Rwanda was shot down. Within hours, Rwandan military and militia forces began an organized slaughter that killed hundreds of thousands of people, by most estimates about one million, in roughly 100 days. The killers targeted Tutsi and moderate Hutu. The speed and the intimacy of the killing made the term “genocide” precise and inadequate at the same time.[^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]

The American response is documented in the National Security Archive’s collection, “The U.S. and the Genocide in Rwanda 1994: Evidence of Inaction.” The documents record that the United States discouraged a stronger United Nations response and avoided describing the killings as genocide while they continued. A policy review completed in May 1994 set restrictive conditions for American support of peace operations. The archive does not show a single decision by a single official; it shows a series of choices that together amounted to standing aside.[^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-check-rwanda]

The record requires distinguishing what officials knew from what they said publicly. The intelligence and diplomatic reporting were not silent, but the public language was careful, and the care had consequences: without the word “genocide,” the legal obligations of the Genocide Convention were easier to avoid.[^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]

On March 25, 1998, in Kigali, Clinton said: “We did not act quickly enough after the killing began. We should not have allowed the refugee camps to become safe havens for the killers. We did not immediately call these crimes by their rightful name: genocide.” He pledged American support for survivors. The apology was a later acknowledgment, not a policy the administration pursued in 1994. It belongs in the record as both a reckoning and a reminder that words came after the dead.[^ch45-clinton-kigali]

### 5.4 Bosnia and Kosovo

The war in Bosnia and Herzegovina ran from 1992 to 1995. It killed more than 200,000 people and displaced about two million. In July 1995, Bosnian Serb forces overran the United Nations “safe area” of Srebrenica and killed up to 8,000 Bosniak men and boys. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia and the International Court of Justice later ruled that the massacre was genocide.[^ch45-un-srebrenica][^ch45-dayton-state]

After Srebrenica and a NATO air campaign, the parties negotiated at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base near Dayton, Ohio. The talks opened on November 1, 1995, and the General Framework Agreement for Peace was signed in Paris on December 14, 1995. The agreement ended the war and deployed a NATO-led force, but it also froze a political settlement that preserved ethnic partitions and created a state structure too complex for easy governance.[^ch45-dayton-state]

Kosovo came next. NATO’s Operation Allied Force ran from March 24 to June 10, 1999, a 78-day air campaign of more than 38,000 sorties flown without a single Allied fatality. It began after the failure of talks at Rambouillet and Paris and ended after the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia accepted terms and the United Nations endorsed a political settlement. The campaign achieved its objective without a ground invasion and exposed the limits of high-altitude bombing.[^ch45-nato-kosovo]

The casualty figures and the legal findings must come from the tribunals and the United Nations, not from political rhetoric. The interventions were the product of decisions by many governments, not of American power alone. The United States led NATO, but the alliance’s members supplied bases, aircraft, and political cover. The chapter presents the interventions as collective choices with American weight, not as a unilateral act.[^ch45-un-srebrenica][^ch45-nato-kosovo][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 The unipolar moment

The decade left the United States without a rival great power. Charles Krauthammer named the condition in 1990 as “the unipolar moment.” He expected it to be brief: “the unipolar moment will be brief indeed (one decade, perhaps, rather than, say, three or four),” he wrote, if the American economy were run into the ground. Officials and commentators debated whether the resulting predominance should be used to enlarge democracy, to keep a narrow account of the national interest, or to withdraw.[^ch45-krauthammer-1990]

The interventions and the non-intervention are the evidence for the debate. Somalia showed the cost of a humanitarian mission that turned into a manhunt. Rwanda showed the cost of standing aside. Bosnia and Kosovo showed that air power and alliance politics could stop a war and force a settlement, at least in Europe. Primacy, selective engagement, and restraint each had serious advocates and serious objections.[^ch45-state-somalia][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-nato-kosovo]

The “unipolar moment” is not a settled description. It was a claim about a distribution of power that the decade’s own conflicts tested. The United States did not stop the Rwandan genocide, and it did not settle the Balkans without its allies. Its power was unmatched, and its ability to convert that power into outcomes was limited. That gap is the section’s conclusion.[^ch45-krauthammer-1990][^ch45-check-debate-works]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Clinton won 370 electoral votes and about 43.0 percent of the popular vote in 1992. Bush took 168 electoral votes and about 37.4 percent, and Perot about 18.9 percent. In 1996 Clinton won 379 electoral votes and 49.2 percent; Dole took 159 electoral votes and 40.7 percent, and Perot 8.4 percent.[^ch45-app-1992][^ch45-app-1996]

NAFTA was signed on December 17, 1992, approved on November 20, 1993, implemented on December 8, 1993, and entered into force on January 1, 1994; the House vote was 234–200 and the Senate 61–38. The World Trade Organization was established by the Marrakesh Agreement of April 15, 1994, and entered into force on January 1, 1995. The Seattle ministerial ran from November 30 to December 3, 1999.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-nafta-votes][^ch45-wto-marrakesh][^ch45-wto-seattle]

The 1994 crime act became law on September 13, 1994, and the 1996 welfare law on August 22, 1996; the welfare law replaced AFDC with TANF. The Republicans took Congress in 1994. The Oklahoma City bombing killed 168 people on April 19, 1995. The House impeached Clinton in December 1998, and the Senate acquitted him on February 12, 1999. The 2000 election turned on Florida’s 537-vote margin and *Bush v. Gore*.[^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-contract-america][^ch45-fbi-okc][^ch45-impeachment-aap][^ch45-bush-v-gore]

The economy had its best performance in three decades, the expansion became the longest on record, unemployment fell to about 4 percent, and the budget moved to surplus. Income growth at the top outpaced the rest after 1979, even as median income rose and poverty fell late in the decade.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-census-income-1999][^ch45-census-poverty-2000]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the decade’s growth broadly shared or top-heavy? Low unemployment, rising median income, and falling poverty coexisted with a rising top share. The “shared prosperity” reading emphasizes the tight labor market and the late-decade wage gains. The “winner-take-all” reading emphasizes the top 1 percent and the slow growth of the middle. Both describe the same distribution from different points on the curve.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-cbpp-2003]

Did trade cause the manufacturing losses? The Economic Policy Institute attributes a large net displacement to the trade deficit with Canada and Mexico. The Peterson Institute and other economists argue that the job changes cannot be attributed to the agreement alone, and they warn that statistical association is not proof of policy causation. The chapter compares their methods and years without settling either figure.[^ch45-epi-nafta][^ch45-piie-nafta][^ch45-check-debate-works]

Was welfare reform a success? Supporters read falling caseloads and rising employment as proof the law worked. Critics read them as the product of a strong economy and a reduced safety net. Poverty fell to 11.3 percent in 2000, but the decline began before the law and had multiple causes. The chapter states each at its strongest and uses the poverty and employment record.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-census-poverty-2000][^ch45-check-debate-works]

How much did the interventions matter? Interests, domestic politics, and military constraints each explain part of the pattern. The interest account emphasizes oil, alliances, and credibility. The domestic account emphasizes casualties, Congress, and public opinion. The constraints account emphasizes what air power could and could not do. None explains all of it.[^ch45-state-somalia][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-nato-kosovo]

Has history ended? Fukuyama’s 1989 proposition and its critics disagree about whether the decade confirmed the triumph of liberal democracy or exposed its limits. The genocides, ethnic wars, and inequality can be read against the proposition; so can the spread of elections, markets, and international institutions. The chapter treats it as a question about evidence, not a slogan.[^ch45-fukuyama-1989][^ch45-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the post-Cold War settlement produced real and widely enjoyed growth whose gains were nevertheless concentrated. Integration created aggregate benefits and localized losses. American power was used selectively, and its costs fell heavily on people with little voice in the record. Those are the same decade seen from different positions.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]

The outcomes were contingent. The 1993 budget passed the House by two votes and the Senate by one. NAFTA passed with a coalition that depended on Republicans. The 1996 welfare law followed a long partisan fight. The crime act, the intervention choices, and the 2000 election could have gone otherwise.[^ch45-obra-1993][^ch45-nafta-votes][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-bush-v-gore]

Structure and choice must be held together. The end of the Cold War, the technology boom, and the global integration of markets set the conditions. The choices made within them decided the form. Between 1991 and 2000 the United States enjoyed the longest expansion on record, ended its federal cash-assistance entitlement, expanded its prisons, acquitted an impeached president, decided a presidential election in court, and intervened in some humanitarian crises while standing aside from the worst. The chapter’s question is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-prwora][^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]

## Legacies

The decade left an economy integrated with its neighbors and the world and a politics organized around the difference between aggregate growth and median experience. The trade and investment agreements fixed a framework later governments would defend, revise, or abandon, and the distributional widening became a permanent feature of the political debate. The decade’s politics would be fought over who had benefited and who had been left behind, and the answer depended on which series and which community one looked at.[^ch45-nafta-crs][^ch45-cbo-distribution]

It also left a smaller cash-assistance safety net and a larger prison system. The welfare law of 1996 and the crime act of 1994 reset both debates for a generation. Caseloads fell, employment rose, and incarceration continued to grow; the debate over what those changes mean is not over. The states’ patchwork of benefits and the federal sentencing rules outlasted the debates that produced them.[^ch45-prwora][^ch45-crime-bill][^ch45-bjs-prisoners]

And it left the United States with an unmatched position in the world and an unsettled rule for when to use it. Somalia, Bosnia, Kosovo, and Rwanda left the question of American purpose open. Chapter 46 takes up the answer the following year forced; the next administration would inherit both the apparatus of selective intervention and the unresolved question of when to use it.[^ch45-dayton-state][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda][^ch45-check-ch46]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1991 and 2000 the United States enjoyed the longest expansion on record, integrated its economy with its neighbors and the world, ended its federal cash-assistance entitlement, expanded its prison system, impeached and acquitted a president, decided a presidential election in court, and intervened in some humanitarian crises while standing aside from the worst. The question — who gained from the post-Cold War settlement, and what did the boom leave out? — is answered by a decade in which growth and integration were real and their gains were uneven, and in which American power was both unmatched and used selectively. The boom was real, and so was the unevenness; the political system would spend the next decade arguing about which fact mattered more. The 2000 election decided who would govern. It did not decide what the decade meant.[^ch45-brookings-1990s][^ch45-cbo-distribution][^ch45-bush-v-gore][^ch45-nsarchive-rwanda]
