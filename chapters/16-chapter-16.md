Four voices, four verdicts. A retired founder heard the Missouri question as an alarm bell announcing the Union’s death. A president told voters that the rich were bending government to their own ends. A visiting Frenchman warned that the majority ruled opinion so absolutely that a dissenter would subside into silence. A modern historian described the age’s democratic energy as a class coalition against Eastern capital. They disagree about whether the widening electorate was a victory for the many, a new kind of conformity, or a contest that the economically powerful still won. The question is not which voice was right. It is who gained a political voice as mass democracy expanded, and who paid for it.[^ch16-check-epigraphs]

In the spring of 1820, at Monticello, Thomas Jefferson answered a letter from John Holmes, a congressman from Maine who had voted for the Missouri Compromise and sent Jefferson a copy of his letter to constituents defending it. Jefferson thanked him for the copy. He had long ago “ceased to read the newspapers,” he said, content to be “a passenger in our bark to the shore from which I am not distant.” Then the Missouri question broke in. It came, he wrote, “like a fire bell in the night,” and it “awakened and filled me with terror.” He called it “the knell of the Union,” hushed for the moment, a “reprieve only, not a final sentence.”[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]

The letter held the age’s contradiction in one hand. Jefferson called slavery a “heavy reproach” and wrote that “we have the wolf by the ear, and we can neither hold him, nor safely let him go. Justice is in one scale, and self-preservation in the other.” He insisted that the condition of persons within a state was that state’s exclusive right. He also argued that diffusing enslaved people over a greater surface would make them “individually happier” and make emancipation easier. A man who had helped build the republic now described its future in the language of terror, and in the same letter defended the system that caused the terror. He was a slaveholder. He took no public role in the crisis, and the letter did not free anyone.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]

Congress had drawn a line across the Louisiana Purchase at 36°30′ north latitude and paired Missouri’s admission as a slave state with Maine’s as a free one. The line was a settlement. It was not a solution. Jefferson predicted that a “geographical line, coinciding with a marked principle, moral and political,” would “never be obliterated,” and that every new irritation would mark it deeper. The compromise had done exactly what a settlement does: it changed the terms of the conflict without ending it. Who gained a political voice as mass democracy expanded?[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-jefferson-holmes][^ch16-check-scene]

This chapter covers the political and cultural history of the United States from 1815 to 1840. It draws on congressional debates, statutes, presidential messages, state constitutions, election returns, politicians’ correspondence, reform-society records, penny newspapers, and travelers’ accounts. It treats “democracy,” “the people,” “the party,” “reform,” and “the West” as contested terms that the chapter must define in use. Enslaved people, free Black men, Native nations, and women appear in the period’s records as property, as a disfranchised class, as wards, and as dependents. The chapter says so rather than letting the rhetoric of the common man stand as a description of everyone.[^ch16-check-scope][^ch16-check-names][^ch16-check-dates]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the Missouri crisis and the end of the Era of Good Feelings. §2 follows the widening White male electorate, the election of 1824, and the invention of the mass party. §3 takes up Jackson’s governing conflicts over office, nullification, the Bank, and Indian removal. §4 turns to the revivals and reform societies of the 1820s and 1830s. §5 reads the penny press and the popular culture of the age against the exclusions that bounded its public sphere. §6 asks how historians explain Jacksonian democracy and who it served. The chapter begins in the spring of 1820, when a retired founder heard the Missouri question as a fire bell in the night.[^ch16-check-scope]

## 1. Missouri and the Era of Good Feelings

### 1.1 The Era of Good Feelings and the appearance of consensus

The years after the War of 1812 produced a one-party nationalism that looked like consensus. The Federalist Party had collapsed after the Hartford Convention and the peace of Ghent. James Monroe won the presidency in 1816 and again in 1820 with little organized opposition. A Boston newspaper gave the period its name, the Era of Good Feelings. National policy turned toward a tariff, a national bank, and internal improvements, the program Henry Clay later called the American System.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]

The appearance was deceptive. The Panic of 1819, the young republic’s first nationwide depression, broke the mood. Unemployment rose, banks failed, mortgages were foreclosed, and agricultural prices fell by half. Debtors demanded stay laws and the abolition of debtors’ prisons. Others attacked banks and privileged corporations. The panic’s politics fed the demand for wider suffrage and the hostility to privilege that the 1820s carried into national elections.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri][^ch16-check-ch15]

The Era of Good Feelings was less a settled consensus than a pause between party systems. The Missouri crisis and the panic together ended it.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

### 1.2 The Tallmadge amendments and the sectional debate

Missouri’s application for statehood forced Congress to decide, for the first time since 1787, whether it could regulate slavery’s spread. On February 13, 1819, Representative James Tallmadge Jr., a first-term New York Republican, offered two amendments to the statehood bill. The first prohibited “the further introduction of slavery or involuntary servitude” into Missouri, except as punishment for crimes. The second freed every child born there after admission at the age of twenty-five.[^ch16-yawp-missouri][^ch16-house-tallmadge]

The House approved both amendments, voting along sectional lines. The Senate defeated them, and the 15th Congress ended without resolving Missouri’s statehood. Tallmadge rested his case on Article IV, Section 3 of the Constitution, which gave Congress power over the territories, and on the precedent that Congress had excluded slavery from the Northwest Territory in 1787. He said he was representing his constituents’ “hatred to slavery in every shape.” When southern members threatened disunion, he answered: “If a dissolution of the Union must take place, let it be so! If civil war, which gentlemen so much threaten, must come, I can only say, let it come!” He also conceded that his amendment did nothing about slavery where it already existed.[^ch16-house-tallmadge]

The debate exposed the fault line that would define the next 40 years of legislative conflict, even though the immediate bill failed. Tallmadge had asked whether Congress could put a boundary around slavery in the territories. The answer would be neither a clear yes nor a clear no.[^ch16-house-tallmadge][^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

### 1.3 The Compromise of 1820 and the line at 36°30′

Congress resolved the immediate crisis by pairing Missouri’s admission as a slave state with Maine’s as a free state, keeping the balance between slave and free states equal. Section 8 of the act declared that in the territory ceded by France as Louisiana lying “north of thirty-six degrees and thirty minutes north latitude,” not included in Missouri, “slavery and involuntary servitude … shall be, and is hereby, forever prohibited,” with a proviso allowing the recapture of fugitives. The act was approved March 6, 1820.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-yawp-missouri]

The sequel showed how fragile the settlement was. Missouri then adopted a constitution barring free Black settlement. Congress admitted the state in 1821 only after a second compromise requiring Missouri not to deprive any citizen of the privileges and immunities guaranteed by the Constitution. The line held until the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 repealed it, and the Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional in *Dred Scott v. Sandford* in 1857.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-check-dates]

The compromise preserved the sectional balance and postponed the conflict. It also established the principle that Congress could draw a boundary around slavery. The South accepted a limit it had denied Congress the power to impose. That contradiction would return every time the country acquired new territory.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

### 1.4 What the crisis exposed

The Missouri crisis ended the Era of Good Feelings on a note of foreboding. It revealed that expansion would keep returning the slavery question to national politics. Jefferson’s fire bell and his prediction that the geographical line would never be obliterated captured what many leaders feared: the Union contained a division that no compromise could close.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri][^ch16-jefferson-holmes]

The crisis also showed the price southerners would pay for victory. If the South was to defend its political power against an antislavery majority, it would have to forge new alliances with the North and West or acquire new territory in the Southwest. Either course would reignite northern opposition to slavery’s expansion. The compromise had bought time, not safety.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

The crisis did not create the sectional conflict. It made the conflict visible and gave it a line on the map. John Quincy Adams called it “the title page to a great tragic volume.” The chapter treats the compromise as a settlement that changed the terms of conflict without settling them. That is the book’s central claim about American settlements, and Missouri was its first modern test.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

## 2. Elections and parties

### 2.1 The widening White male electorate and its contractions

More White men could vote in the 1820s and 1830s than ever before. The expansion began before Jackson, advanced for reasons of party strategy as much as democratic conviction, and was accompanied by new exclusions. The most significant progress toward a more inclusive White male suffrage came well before the Jacksonian presidency. Eight of the first 11 new states, from Vermont in 1791 to Missouri, had no economic suffrage qualification. Historians’ estimates suggest that as many as 80 percent of adult White males were eligible to vote nationally by 1800. The Jacksonian era saw changes in party organization and campaign style more than in law.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]

The stragglers changed at different speeds. Maryland dropped its property qualification in 1802; New Jersey, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and New York switched to a taxpaying qualification by 1821; Rhode Island and Virginia were last, in 1842 and 1850. Party strategists usually drove the expansion, expecting a broader electorate to help them win.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]

As economic qualifications fell, other limits stiffened. New Jersey women lost the vote in 1807. New York’s 1821 convention expanded eligibility without addressing race, and an 1826 amendment eliminated voting by African Americans. Pennsylvania’s 1838 constitution confined the franchise to “white freemen.” Free Black men lost legal access to the ballot in nearly every state that widened White male suffrage. The same conventions that removed a property test often wrote a color line into the constitution.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-merritt-voting][^ch16-check-race]

### 2.2 The election of 1824 and the “corrupt bargain”

The election of 1824 was a political turning point in which the old rules failed. No candidate won an electoral majority, and the House of Representatives chose a president whom the plurality of voters had not chosen. For the first time the popular vote mattered broadly. Eighteen states chose electors by popular vote while six still left the choice to their legislatures. A new swath of regionally focused voters, enfranchised by the removal of property qualifications, made state conventions and assemblies, rather than congressional caucuses, the nominating machinery.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]

Four Democratic-Republicans ran. Andrew Jackson polled about 151,000 to 153,000 popular votes, roughly 41 percent, and 99 electoral votes. John Quincy Adams polled about 113,000 to 114,000, roughly 31 percent, and 84 electoral votes. William H. Crawford won 41 electoral votes and Henry Clay 37. Jackson led both the popular and the electoral count but fell short of an electoral majority.[^ch16-ucsb-1824][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain][^ch16-check-numbers]

Under the Twelfth Amendment the House chose among the top three, which eliminated Clay. As Speaker, Clay was the pivotal figure. In February 1825 Adams won with a one-vote margin as most of Clay’s supporters and several old Federalists swung to him. Adams then named Clay secretary of state. Jackson called the result a “corrupt bargain” and said Clay had sold his influence.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]

The available evidence suggests behind-the-scenes maneuvering by Clay, but also Clay’s genuine doubts about Jackson’s qualifications and his preference for Adams’s support of the American System. The defection of three states Jackson had won in the popular vote—Illinois, Maryland, and Louisiana—indicates that more was involved than one man’s maneuvering. The chapter presents the charge as Jackson’s claim and the record as ambiguous. Jackson resigned his Senate seat and rebuilt a grassroots campaign to defeat Adams in 1828. The grievance became the founding story of the new Democratic Party.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]

### 2.3 Party organization, the second party system, and Figure 3

Between 1824 and 1840 the mass party became the central institution of American politics. Under Jackson and Van Buren the Democrats perfected a pyramidal structure of local, state, and national committees, caucuses, and conventions. The structure supposedly reflected opinion at the grass roots, though its movements were often directed from Washington. The spoils system gave party ranks activity and discipline.[^ch16-feller-franchise]

Once Jackson left the scene, the Democrats and the newly formed Whigs were very nearly equal in their bases of popular support. Presidential elections through the 1840s were among the closest in history, and control of Congress passed back and forth. Close competition and near-universal White male suffrage turned campaigns into “a combination of spectator sport and participatory street theater,” with barbeques, rallies, and stump speeches.[^ch16-feller-franchise]

Turnout rose from about 26.9 percent of the voting-age population in 1824 to 57.6 percent in 1828, 55.4 in 1832, 57.8 in 1836, and 80.2 in 1840. The denominator excludes women and, in most states, free Black men. Figure 3 plots the series with those caveats: South Carolina chose electors by its legislature throughout the period, several other states did so in 1824, eligibility varied by state even among White men, and the 1840 spike reflects mobilization and counting as much as admission. Underlying returns and definitions belong in App. B, Table 2.[^ch16-infoplease-turnout][^ch16-ucsb-turnout][^ch16-check-figure][^ch16-check-numbers]

### 2.4 Campaigns as spectacle: 1828, 1832, 1836, and 1840

By 1840 the parties campaigned as never before, selling candidates as folk heroes and reaching ordinary voters through cheap print and mass rallies. The 1828 rematch, fought against Adams as “the people” against an elitist, drew more than twice the voters of 1824, and Jackson won decisively. Attacks on Jackson’s wife, Rachel, only enhanced his image as an authentic outsider. Jackson won the 1832 contest against Clay, fought over the Bank veto.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain][^ch16-jackson-domestic]

In 1836 the Whigs ran three regional candidates against Martin Van Buren rather than a single national ticket. Van Buren won 170 electoral votes to 113 for the three combined, but by a narrow popular margin. The contest showed that the opposition could compete only when it united.[^ch16-miller-vanburen][^ch16-ucsb-1836]

In 1840 the Whigs nominated William Henry Harrison, “Old Tippecanoe,” and ran a “log cabin and hard cider” campaign that painted the incumbent Van Buren as a wealthy sybarite while presenting the well-born Harrison as a man of the people. Charles Ogle’s three-day “Gold Spoon” speech attacked Van Buren’s supposed luxury. Harrison, breaking the custom that candidates should not campaign, appeared at enormous rallies, carried 19 of 26 states, and defeated Van Buren 234 to 60.[^ch16-nps-1840]

The imagery inverted the candidates’ origins. The log-cabin candidate was born to a prominent family and educated at Hampden-Sydney College and the University of Pennsylvania. Van Buren had been raised at his father’s tavern and farm. The new politics manufactured images as well as counted votes. Whether the second party system gave ordinary White men power or only the feeling of power is the chapter’s central question.[^ch16-nps-1840][^ch16-feller-franchise]

## 3. Jackson’s governing conflicts

### 3.1 Rotation in office and the spoils system

Jackson entered office promising reform of a corrupt bureaucracy and created instead the system of partisan patronage that his opponents named the spoils system. Charging the Adams bureaucracy with fraud, he initiated sweeping removals among high-ranking officials: Washington bureau chiefs, land and customs officers, and federal marshals and attorneys. He claimed to restore opportunity through “rotation in office.” In practice, many offices were rewards for political service, and his appointees included newspaper editors who had championed him. His most notorious appointee, Samuel Swartwout, collector of the New York customhouse, absconded in 1838 with more than $1 million.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

Senator William L. Marcy of New York defended the removals in 1832: in politics as in war, “to the victor belong the spoils of the enemy.” Jackson denied that political criteria motivated him, yet he treated support for Adams as evidence of unfitness. The spoils system gave parties discipline, but it tied office to faction and invited corruption.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-feller-franchise]

### 3.2 The Eaton affair and the “kitchen cabinet”

A Washington social scandal over the wife of the secretary of war entangled Jackson’s cabinet, estranged him from Vice President John C. Calhoun, and elevated Martin Van Buren. Jackson’s secretary of war, John Henry Eaton, married Margaret “Peggy” O’Neale Timberlake just before the inauguration, amid rumors that her first husband had died under scandalous circumstances. Cabinet wives, including Calhoun’s wife Floride, shunned her. Jackson saw in the snubbing the persecution he believed had hounded his own Rachel, and he came to see a plot by Calhoun to control his administration.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

The one cabinet officer who stood apart from the snubbing was the widower Van Buren, whose courtliness to Peggy Eaton and whose policy views drew Jackson closer. By 1831 the affair threatened to paralyze the administration. Eaton and Van Buren resigned to give Jackson an occasion to demand the other secretaries’ resignations and appoint a new cabinet. Calhoun cast the tie-breaking vote against Van Buren’s nomination as minister to Great Britain. Van Buren returned as a political martyr and Jackson’s choice for vice president.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-tocqueville-hanover]

The informal circle of advisers later called the kitchen cabinet showed how personal and factional the new politics had become. The breach widened into the constitutional struggle over the tariff.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

### 3.3 Nullification and the Union

South Carolina’s attempt to nullify the federal tariff forced Jackson to define the Union as indissoluble and the states as bound by federal law. In November 1832 a state convention declared the tariff acts of 1828 and 1832 “unauthorized by the constitution of the United States, and … null, void, and no law, nor binding upon this State.” The legislature moved to block the collection of federal customs at the state’s ports.[^ch16-loc-nullification]

On December 10, 1832, Jackson issued a Proclamation to the People of South Carolina disputing a state’s right to nullify federal law. Drafted largely by Secretary of State Edward Livingston, it dismissed the constitutional theory behind nullification and warned that “disunion by armed force is treason.” Jackson had already signaled his position at a political dinner in 1830, staring at Calhoun as he toasted, “Our federal Union: It must be preserved.”[^ch16-loc-nullification][^ch16-jackson-domestic]

Congress passed the Force Act in 1833 authorizing military force against a resisting state, and Henry Clay and Calhoun brokered a Compromise Tariff that lowered rates over nine years. South Carolina, claiming victory, rescinded its nullification but nullified the Force Act in a final gesture of defiance. The crisis ended tariff agitation until the 1840s. It also joined the defense of slavery to the doctrine of state sovereignty, which returned in the secession crisis that Chapter 20 owns.[^ch16-loc-nullification][^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-check-race]

### 3.4 The Bank War and the veto of 1832

Jackson’s war on the Second Bank of the United States destroyed the country’s central financial institution and made the Bank the defining issue of his second term. The Second Bank, chartered in 1816, was a quasi-public corporation that managed the federal government’s finances and provided a national currency. The government owned one-fifth of the stock and appointed one-fifth of the directors. The Bank had fallen into disrepute in the Panic of 1819 and been repaired by its president, Nicholas Biddle. Jackson attacked it in his first message to Congress in 1829 as unconstitutional and a threat to popular liberty.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

On the advice of Clay and Daniel Webster, Biddle sought recharter in 1832, calculating that Jackson would not dare veto before the election. Congress passed the bill, and on July 10, 1832, Jackson vetoed it. His message, intended for the public, attacked the special privilege enjoyed by private stockholders in a government-chartered corporation and declared that the “rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes.” His position was a suspicion of concentrated privilege, not a program of state enterprise.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

After his reelection, Jackson determined to withdraw the government’s deposits. When Treasury Secretary William John Duane refused, Jackson fired him and put Roger Taney in his place. The removal was largely complete by December 1833. Biddle retaliated by contracting the Bank’s lending to force a recharter, but the contraction discredited the Bank and vindicated Jackson’s charges.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

The Senate, in Whig hands, rejected Taney and adopted a resolution of censure against Jackson in March 1834; Jackson’s defenders expunged it in 1837. The state banks, cut loose and gorged with federal funds, fed the speculative boom that collapsed in the Panic of 1837. That economic sequel belongs to Chapter 15. The political sequel belongs here.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-check-ch15]

### 3.5 Removal’s national political role

Indian removal was not the work of one man. It was a national policy with broad consensus, and it became the only major legislation passed at Jackson’s behest in eight years. Congress passed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830, authorizing the president to grant lands west of the Mississippi in exchange for Indigenous homelands and to pay removal costs. The vote was close in the House, 102 to 97, and comfortable in the Senate, 28 to 19. Jackson regarded tribes within the states not as independent sovereign entities but as wards of the government and tenants-at-will. He called removal the “happy consummation” of a policy “pursued for nearly 30 years.”[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-ostler-removal]

The Cherokee Nation sued Georgia over its assertion of jurisdiction. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* in 1831 the Supreme Court called the Cherokee a “domestic dependent nation” without standing to challenge Georgia’s laws. In *Worcester v. Georgia* in 1832 it held Georgia’s laws undermining Cherokee sovereignty unconstitutional. The victories gave the Cherokee no practical means of resistance, and Georgia, tacitly encouraged by Jackson, ignored the rulings.[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-jackson-domestic]

Removal affected dozens of nations across the East and Midwest, not only the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek, and Seminole. About 88,000 people indigenous to the eastern United States were forcibly moved west in the 1830s and 1840s, and between 12,000 and 17,000 died in the roundups, detention, and westward journeys, a death toll of 14 to 19 percent. The routes, the treaties, and the consequences belong to Chapter 17.[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-check-ch17]

This chapter states the national political role: Jackson’s priority, the close 1830 vote, the Cherokee cases, and the consensus behind removal. It stops before the Treaty of New Echota of December 1835 and the removal of 1838–39, which Chapter 17 owns.[^ch16-check-ch17][^ch16-ostler-removal]

## 4. Revival and reform

### 4.1 The Second Great Awakening and the burned-over district

A wave of revivals in the 1820s and 1830s reshaped American religion, drew women and families into public religious work, and supplied the moral energy for reform. Charles Grandison Finney, a former lawyer turned evangelist, held a powerful revival in Rochester, New York, from September 1830 to March 1831. Daily preaching and revival services produced public conversions, often of leading community members. Women and families spread the revival through home visits and prayer groups, and Protestants traveled up to 100 miles to hear him. Converts sat on the anxious bench near his pulpit to be made the focus of public conversion.[^ch16-finney-rochester]

Rochester was a rapidly growing commercial and manufacturing town where the old mutuality of masters and apprentices had given way to sharper class stratification. It stood at the center of the Burned-Over District, the belt of intense revivals across upstate New York. According to the historian Paul Johnson, the revival attracted primarily the emerging middle class, who then evangelized the working class. Finney’s emphasis on perfectionism and free moral choice fed social reform, especially temperance. Halfway through the revival he invited the temperance activist Theodore Dwight Weld to organize temperance work in Rochester.[^ch16-finney-rochester]

The Second Great Awakening made religion a public and organized force in the North and gave women a legitimate, if contested, role in moral work that would widen into antislavery and women’s-rights activism.[^ch16-finney-rochester][^ch16-check-reform]

### 4.2 Temperance, common schools, and asylums

The revivals produced a cluster of reforms aimed at perfecting the individual and the society: temperance, public schooling, and humane treatment of the mentally ill. In 1825 the Presbyterian minister Lyman Beecher preached six sermons on intemperance, published the next year. The American Temperance Society was formed in 1826, and by the early 1830s thousands of similar societies had sprung up. Members first pledged to shun hard liquor; by 1836 leaders including Beecher advocated total abstinence, which split the movement and slowed it. A working-class phase began with the Washington Temperance Society in 1840, which enrolled as many as 600,000 pledge-takers by 1844. The early movement fit the emerging middle-class ethic of a sober workforce.[^ch16-lumen-temperance]

Horace Mann, appointed secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education in 1837, became the leading advocate of the common school: free, universal, non-sectarian, and public. He argued that it would create the virtuous republican citizenry needed to sustain self-government and the disciplined workforce needed for the economy. He edited *The Common School Journal*. His legacy is mixed. Some historians see a step toward a more open society; others see a tool of social control that universalized the values of the Protestant middle class. Deaf advocates have criticized his role in promoting oral over sign education.[^ch16-mann-common-school]

Dorothea Dix’s *Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts*, presented in January 1843, reported on her visits to jails and almshouses and demanded state provision for the pauper insane. She catalogued a woman “in a cage,” a person in a close stall for 17 years, and the “use of cages all but universal.” Her appeal, “I tell what I have seen,” helped drive the creation of state asylums.[^ch16-dix-memorial]

The reforms were genuine improvements and instruments of order at once. Temperance could free a family from a drunkard’s wages and discipline a worker; common schools could open opportunity and impose a Protestant moral consensus; asylums could shelter the suffering and confine the embarrassing. The chapter presents them as efforts to perfect a society the market was transforming, not as simple benevolence.[^ch16-lumen-temperance][^ch16-mann-common-school][^ch16-check-reform]

### 4.3 Abolitionism and the printed word

The 1830s saw the rise of immediate abolitionism, a movement that demanded an uncompensated end to slavery now and used the press to reach a national audience. William Lloyd Garrison, who had earlier advocated Black colonization and gradual abolition, changed his mind under the influence of Black northerners such as David Walker. He launched *The Liberator* in Boston on January 1, 1831. In its first issue he renounced gradual abolition as “timidity, injustice, and absurdity” and demanded the “immediate enfranchisement of our slave population.” He declared that he was in earnest and would “not retreat a single inch—AND I WILL BE HEARD.”[^ch16-garrison-liberator][^ch16-nps-liberator]

Garrison’s tenets included an immediate uncompensated end to slavery, attacks on the Church and the Constitution as tools of the oppressors, and change through peaceful moral suasion alone. He also championed women’s rights, pacifism, and the fight against capital punishment. The paper drew some of its strongest support from Boston’s free Black community. Black leaders endorsed it, a committee of Black women led fundraisers, and Black subscribers made up roughly 75 percent of subscribers in the early years. Garrison published writings by Black authors including Maria Stewart and William Cooper Nell.[^ch16-nps-liberator]

The movement provoked furious opposition. In 1835 abolitionists mailed thousands of antislavery tracts to southern clergy and officials. Southern postmasters and mobs intercepted many, and Jackson and Postmaster General Amos Kendall approved their action and recommended federal suppression of “incendiary publications.” Congress began adopting annual gag rules to keep abolition petitions off the floor. Abolitionism remained a minority movement, but it changed the terms of national debate. It forced the question of slavery into the mails, the petitions, and the floor of Congress, where the gag rule only proved its disruptive power.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-nps-liberator]

### 4.4 Women, moral reform, and the limits of “the people”

Women entered public reform through benevolence and antislavery work, and their participation sharpened the era’s central question about who belonged to the people. Angelina Grimké, a South Carolinian who became a Philadelphia abolitionist, published her *Appeal to the Christian Women of the South* in 1836. She grounded the case in the Declaration’s “self-evident truth that all men are created equal” and in Scripture. She answered the objection that women made no laws: “I know you do not make the laws, but I also know that you are the wives and mothers, the sisters and daughters of those who do.” She urged women to read, pray, speak, and act, and defended the doctrine that if a law commands one to sin, it should be broken.[^ch16-grimke-appeal]

Tocqueville, writing in 1835, described American democracy as separating the sexes into “two clearly distinct lines of action.” He held that American women never managed business or took part in political life. Angelina Grimké’s public activism was already straining that description.[^ch16-tocqueville-hanover]

In the same years artisans and journeymen organized. Philadelphia’s carpenters struck for a ten-hour day in 1827. Out of the failed strike William Heighton formed the Mechanics’ Union of Trade Associations, the nation’s first citywide coalition of local unions, and in 1828 the Working Men’s Party, the nation’s first labor party. Its platform demanded a ten-hour day, free public education, the abolition of imprisonment for debt, and a more equitable tax system. The party elected 20 of its 54 candidates in 1829 before collapsing. Pennsylvania abolished imprisonment for debt in 1833, passed the Free School Act in 1834, and granted city workers a ten-hour day in 1835. The Lowell mill operatives whose protests Chapter 15 follows belonged to the same wage-earning population.[^ch16-phila-workingmen][^ch16-dol-pessen][^ch16-check-ch15]

Seneca Falls and the organized campaign for women’s suffrage belong to 1848 and to later context. This chapter names the connection without absorbing it. The boundary is a forward pointer, not a subplot inside an 1840 endpoint.[^ch16-check-reform][^ch16-merritt-voting]

## 5. Print, popular culture, and exclusion

### 5.1 The penny press and a national audience

A new cheap daily press in the 1830s drew a mass urban readership and transformed how Americans learned about politics and the wider world. The *New York Sun*, founded in 1833, was among the inexpensive penny papers that began publication during the decade. It drew a large new readership with lower prices and a more narrative style of journalism than earlier newspapers. By selling for a penny, the penny papers built an audience that included the working class.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun]

The *Sun* made sensation into front-page material and in doing so helped invent the modern idea of the news. The new press extended the public sphere beyond the party organ and the mercantile paper. It also showed how easily a mass audience could be manipulated, a lesson the 1835 moon hoax delivered with a straight face.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-lindahall-locke]

### 5.2 In Focus: a *New York Sun* page from the moon hoax, 1835

**In Focus.** The page is the front page of the *New York Sun* for August 25, 1835, the first column of the six-day series headed “Great Astronomical Discoveries lately made by Sir John Herschel.” It announced that the English astronomer, working in South Africa with a telescope “of vast dimensions and an entirely new principle,” had found life on the moon. It attributed the account to a *Supplement* to the *Edinburgh Journal of Science*, borrowing credibility from a journal that had ceased publishing under that title in 1833. The telescope was described as 24 feet in diameter and able to magnify 42,000 times. Then came the creatures: unicorns, bipedal beavers, and the flying “man-bat,” *Vespertilio-Homo*. The page described “four successive flocks of large winged creatures” and beings who “were like human beings, for their wings had now disappeared.”[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-lindahall-locke]

The page shows the mechanics of borrowed authority and the entertainment economy of the penny press. Its author, the *Sun* writer Richard Adams Locke, meant to satirize popular belief in extraterrestrial life and to boost circulation. The series ran six days and was republished as pamphlets in several languages, but the *Sun* never publicly confessed, and Locke admitted the invention only in a private letter five years later. The often-reproduced “lunar animals” lithograph was sold separately and was not part of the columns. The page is evidence of how a mass audience was made and managed, not a curiosity.[^ch16-lindahall-locke][^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-check-focus]

### 5.3 The gag rule, the mails, and the boundaries of debate

The era’s widening public sphere had a hard boundary, and the boundary was slavery. Antislavery societies bombarded Congress with petitions against slavery and the slave trade. Southern defenders demanded that criticism of slavery be silenced in the interest of public safety. Congress adopted annual gag rules to keep abolition petitions off the floor.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

The 1835 postal campaign provoked violence. Southern postmasters and mobs intercepted antislavery tracts. Jackson and Kendall approved the interception and recommended federal suppression of “incendiary publications.”[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

The gag rule made the suppression of debate itself a political issue. It gave abolitionists a platform for arguing that the South was muzzling the North. The same public sphere that widened for White men narrowed around slavery.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-nps-liberator]

### 5.4 Racial exclusion and the narrowing republic

The age of the common man narrowed citizenship for free Black people and Native nations even as it widened it for White men. As White male suffrage expanded, free Black men lost the vote in state after state. New York’s 1821 convention expanded eligibility without addressing race, and an 1826 amendment eliminated voting by African Americans. Pennsylvania’s 1838 constitution confined the franchise to “white freemen.” During the Jacksonian era nearly every free state in the North and Midwest extended voting rights to all White men as economic qualifications were lowered or removed. State legislators remembered to confine the franchise explicitly to White men.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-merritt-voting]

In the South and Southwest, voting restrictions for White men persisted because enslavers wanted to limit the franchise to men who would defend slavery. Poll taxes, intimidation, and fraud held the electorate in check. The widening and the narrowing were one process.[^ch16-merritt-voting]

Removal and the cotton frontier’s dispossession of Native nations ran on the same national political consensus. Chapter 17 owns those routes. This chapter owns the political fact that the expanding democracy rested on the exclusion of the enslaved, the dispossessed, and the disfranchised.[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-check-ch17]

The chapter’s guiding question has its answer here. The political voice that widened for White men was purchased in part by the narrowing of the republic for everyone else. The common man was not a universal category. He was a racial and gendered one.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The evidence supports several conclusions. The electorate widened: more White men voted in the 1820s and 1830s than before, the mass party became the central institution of national politics, and turnout rose dramatically by 1840. The expansion preceded and outran Jackson. Historians’ estimates put White male eligibility as high as 80 percent by 1800, and the Jacksonian era saw changes in party organization and campaign style as much as in law.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-infoplease-turnout][^ch16-feller-franchise]

The widening was accompanied by exclusions. Free Black men lost the vote in state after state, women had no vote, and Native nations faced removal. The democracy expanded inside the category of White manhood. Jackson’s conflicts were real and consequential: the Bank veto, the nullification crisis, the Force Act, and the removal act defined his presidency and produced the Whig opposition. Revival and reform reshaped the North, and abolitionism used print and petition to force slavery back onto the national agenda.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-loc-nullification][^ch16-nps-liberator]

### 6.2 What is open

Five questions remain open. The first is whether the widening was democratic or anti-democratic: Democrats and historians have claimed Jackson as the champion of the common man, while others point to his hostility to abolitionism, his removal policy, and the racial limits of his democracy. The second is who made the expansion. Blumin argues that party strategists and demographic change drove it more than popular protest, which would make the era’s democracy as much a product of elite calculation as of popular demand.[^ch16-feller-franchise][^ch16-glencoe-historians][^ch16-blumin-suffrage]

The third is whether the parties gave voters power, since close competition made politics participatory and theatrical while the substance of party difference remains disputed. The fourth is how central slavery was to the age, with some historians reading the era through the market revolution and class and others arguing that the Democratic Party was above all a defender of slavery and White supremacy. The fifth is how to use Tocqueville, a foreign aristocrat who observed for nine months and wrote for a French audience; the chapter uses him as a source and a subject, not as an unqualified witness to equality.[^ch16-feller-franchise][^ch16-glencoe-historians][^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-tocqueville-hanover]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which Jacksonian democracy was genuinely expansive and genuinely exclusive. It mobilized White men into national politics and did so by narrowing the republic for the enslaved, the dispossessed, and the disfranchised. It cannot be told apart from either the market revolution that fed it or the slavery and removal it protected.[^ch16-check-sources]

The progressive historians of the early twentieth century, culminating in Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr.’s *The Age of Jackson* in 1945, saw Jacksonian democracy as an early battle against economic privilege. Schlesinger described a coalition of farmers and laborers, East, West, and South, against Eastern capital.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]

Richard Hofstadter’s 1948 essay read Jackson as the spokesman of rising entrepreneurs blocked by entrenched elites. Bray Hammond called the Jacksonian cause one of enterpriser against capitalist. Marvin Meyers’s *The Jacksonian Persuasion* in 1957 saw a nostalgic effort to restore an imagined agrarian past.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]

Lee Benson’s *The Concept of Jacksonian Democracy* in 1961 stressed religion and ethnicity over class in voting. Edward Pessen’s *Jacksonian America* in 1969 found rhetoric about equality disguising an increasingly stratified society. Sean Wilentz’s *Chants Democratic* in 1984 and *The Rise of American Democracy* in 2005 traced a working-class identity and a broadly democratizing politics. John Ashworth and Harry Watson read party conflict as a reflection of larger social change.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]

Michael Rogin’s *Fathers and Children* in 1975 and Alexander Saxton’s *The Rise and Fall of the White Republic* in 1990 foregrounded Jackson’s violence toward Native nations and the Democratic Party’s commitment to slavery and White supremacy. Daniel Walker Howe’s *What Hath God Wrought* in 2007 portrayed the Jacksonians as champions of White male supremacy and the Whigs as in some ways more truly democratic. Robert Remini’s biography still defends Jackson as, in his time, a genuine man of the people.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]

Read together, these positions show that the era’s democratic energy and its exclusions were one history. The chapter presents the debate as overlapping positions, not as teams, and tests each against the suffrage record, the removal vote, and the gag rule.[^ch16-glencoe-historians][^ch16-check-table]

## Legacies

The party system that took shape between 1824 and 1840 outlasted the men who built it. Mass parties, national conventions, patronage, and the campaign as spectacle became the permanent machinery of American politics. The twice-a-decade mobilization of a large White male electorate became a defining feature of the republic.[^ch16-feller-franchise]

The age left a divided inheritance. It widened participation, made ordinary White men a force in national politics, and created institutions through which they could demand attention. It did so while free Black men lost the vote in state after state, while women remained outside the formal political nation, and while removal cleared Native nations from the path of the expanding cotton frontier. The democracy rested on the enslavement of millions of people, and its parties organized White men without disturbing that foundation.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-check-race]

And the chapter’s central question endured. The Missouri line that Jefferson feared would never be erased stayed on the map until 1854. Nullification’s doctrine of state sovereignty returned in the secession crisis. The gag rule’s suppression of antislavery petitions foreshadowed the sectional conflict over slavery’s extension. The widening democracy and the deepening sectional conflict were two faces of the same expanding republic.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-check-ch17][^ch16-loc-nullification]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** Tocqueville observed the United States for a French audience comparing democracy with aristocracy. The British antislavery movement and the British and German enthusiasm for the moon hoax show that American print and reform circulated in an Atlantic world.[^ch16-tocqueville-hanover][^ch16-lindahall-locke]
- **Archives and silences.** The election returns, the party platforms, and the newspaper pages record the voices of White male voters most fully. The enslaved, the disfranchised, the removed, and women appear in those records as property, as exclusions, or as dependents. The chapter names that unevenness and seeks the excluded in petitions, narratives, and the records of the reform and removal debates.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal]
- **Distant connections.** The Missouri crisis, nullification, and the gag rule set up the sectional conflict of Chapters 18–20. The market revolution that fed the political crisis belongs to Chapter 15. The removal policy that this chapter treats politically becomes the substance of Chapter 17.[^ch16-check-ch15][^ch16-check-ch17]

## Summary

- The Missouri crisis ended the Era of Good Feelings. Tallmadge’s February 1819 amendments divided Congress, and the Compromise of 1820 paired Missouri and Maine and prohibited slavery north of 36°30′.[^ch16-house-tallmadge][^ch16-missouri-act]
- Jefferson heard the crisis as a “fire bell in the night” and predicted the geographical line would “never be obliterated,” while conceding he had no practicable remedy.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- White male suffrage widened mainly before Jackson—perhaps 80 percent of adult White males were eligible by 1800—and free Black men lost the vote in state after state as it widened.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-merritt-voting]
- In 1824 Jackson won the most popular and electoral votes but lost in the House, which chose Adams and prompted Jackson’s charge of a “corrupt bargain.”[^ch16-ucsb-1824][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- The mass party, with conventions, patronage, and spectacle, drove turnout from about 26.9 percent in 1824 to about 80.2 percent in 1840, winning the era its reputation as the age of the common man.[^ch16-infoplease-turnout][^ch16-ucsb-turnout]
- Jackson’s administration made the spoils system a national practice, survived the Eaton affair, faced down nullification with a proclamation and the Force Act, vetoed the Bank recharter in 1832, and pushed the Indian Removal Act of 1830 through a closely divided Congress.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-loc-nullification]
- The Second Great Awakening’s revivals fed temperance, common-school, and asylum reform, and immediate abolitionism used the press to force slavery onto the national agenda.[^ch16-finney-rochester][^ch16-garrison-liberator]
- The penny press built a mass audience in the 1830s, and the 1835 moon hoax showed how easily that audience could be manipulated.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-lindahall-locke]
- The gag rule, the interception of antislavery mail, and the disfranchisement of free Black men bounded the era’s public sphere even as it widened for White men.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- The debate over Jacksonian democracy turns on whether the widening electorate was a democratic victory, an elite-managed expansion, or a White male supremacy built on exclusion, and the honest answer holds several of these together.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* The age of the common man was real, and so was its price. Between 1815 and 1840 more White men voted than ever before, parties competed for their support, and a military hero from the Tennessee frontier could rise to the presidency. That same era drew a line across the continent at 36°30′, took the vote from free Black men in state after state, removed tens of thousands of Native people from their homelands, and silenced antislavery petitions in Congress. The chapter’s question is not whether the era was democratic. It is how a widening democracy and a hardening exclusion could be the same history, and who decided where the line between the two would fall.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-jackson-domestic]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at New Echota in December 1835, where a small faction of Cherokees signed the treaty that would lead to their removal. This chapter has explained why removal was a national political question: Jackson’s priority, the close vote of 1830, the Cherokee cases, and the consensus behind dispossession. Chapter 17 takes up the treaty, the routes, and the making of Indian Country.[^ch16-check-ch17][^ch16-ostler-removal]

[^ch16-check-reform]: Editorial check — the reform material must not present the benevolent societies as simple benevolence.
