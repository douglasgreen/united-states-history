# Chapter 23. Reconstruction and Its Betrayals (1865–1877)

> “Slavery is not abolished until the black man has the ballot. While the Legislatures of the South retain the right to pass laws making any discrimination between black and white, slavery still lives there.”[^ch23-douglass-ballot]
>
> — Frederick Douglass, “In What New Skin Will the Old Snake Come Forth?,” address to the American Anti-Slavery Society, New York, May 10, 1865.[^ch23-douglass-ballot]

> “In fact, the distinction of race and color is by the bill made to operate in favor of the colored and against the white race.”[^ch23-app-veto]
>
> — Andrew Johnson, veto message on the Civil Rights Act, Washington, D.C., March 27, 1866.[^ch23-app-veto]

> “The slave went free; stood a brief moment in the sun; then moved back again toward slavery.”[^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]
>
> — W. E. B. Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America*, 1935.[^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]

A Black abolitionist told the American Anti-Slavery Society in New York in May 1865 that emancipation without the ballot was a mockery. The president who inherited Reconstruction insisted that a law protecting one race was discrimination against another. A historian writing seventy years later compressed the era into a brief interval of freedom. The three disagree about whether freedom was incomplete, whether defending it was favoritism, or whether it had already been reversed.[^ch23-check-epigraphs]

Late in November 1865, the Mississippi legislature, elected under Andrew Johnson’s plan, turned to the status of the people the war had freed. Its members did not need to restore slavery to preserve the old labor order. The statutes they passed in Jackson answered the question of freedom with a precision that speeches rarely matched.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-mshistorynow]

The first was a vagrancy law. Section 2 provided that freedmen, free Negroes, and mulattoes over 18 found “with no lawful employment or business, or found unlawfully assembling themselves together” should “be deemed vagrants,” fined up to $150 rather than the $200 set for a White man, and imprisoned. Section 7 made failure to pay a tax “prima facie evidence of vagrancy” and required the sheriff to “hire … such delinquent taxpayer to anyone who will pay the said tax.”[^ch23-yawp-black-code]

The second statute was called “An Act to Confer Civil Rights on Freedmen.” Section 1 let freedpeople sue and be sued and acquire personal property, but it barred them from renting or leasing land outside incorporated towns. Section 7 authorized every civil officer, and any person, to arrest a worker “who shall have quit the service of his or her employer before the expiration of his or her term of service without good cause,” and to carry the worker back, with a $5 reward and 10 cents per mile charged against the worker’s wages.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]

The third re-enacted the antebellum laws “defining offenses and prescribing the mode of punishment for crimes and misdemeanors committed by slaves, free Negroes, or mulattoes,” and it allowed convicts who could not pay their fines to be hired out. It also barred freedmen from keeping or carrying “firearms of any kind, or any ammunition, dirk, or Bowie knife” without a license from the county board of police.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]

Read together, the statutes granted a narrow civil capacity and withheld what made it usable. A freedman could sue in court, own a mule, and sign a contract. He could not rent a farm outside a town, leave an employer without permission, carry arms, or vote for the men who wrote the law. The code did not restore slavery; it tried to make freedom serve the old labor discipline and left the freedpeople a narrow space in which to build something else. Whether the state would prevail depended on a federal government that had not yet decided what it owed them.[^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes][^ch23-check-scene]

How was freedom built, and how was its protection withdrawn?

The question guides this chapter through the twelve years that ended with the withdrawal of the last federal troops from the statehouses of Louisiana and South Carolina in 1877. Chapter 22 ended with slavery abolished and freedom’s terms unsettled. This chapter begins where those terms were being written into state law, and it follows them through presidential Reconstruction, the constitutional and statutory reconstruction that Congress built, the institutions freedpeople made, the white supremacist counterrevolution, the western connection, and the settlement of 1876–77.[^ch23-check-scope][^ch23-check-ch22]

It draws on state statutes and constitutional texts, congressional debates and votes, military and Bureau records, the hearings into Ku Klux Klan violence, the returning boards’ returns, and the words and institutions of the freedpeople themselves. It treats “freedom,” “Reconstruction,” “Radical,” “carpetbagger,” “scalawag,” and “Redemption” as terms that require definition rather than as neutral descriptions. It distinguishes what the law declared from what enforcement delivered. The amendments settled legal status and citizenship. They did not settle land, labor, protection, or the vote, and that distinction is the chapter’s argument.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-check-names][^ch23-check-amendments]

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 examines presidential Reconstruction and the Black Codes. §2 follows the constitutional and statutory reconstruction that Congress built between 1866 and 1871. §3 turns to the freedpeople’s families, labor, schools, churches, and officeholding. §4 examines the white supremacist counterrevolution and Redemption. §5 places the western citizenship and land question beside the southern one. §6 narrates the disputed election of 1876 and the settlement of 1877. §7 asks how historians explain what Reconstruction was. The chapter begins in Jackson, Mississippi, in late 1865, where a new legislature wrote the meaning of emancipation into its statute book.

## 1. Presidential Reconstruction

Andrew Johnson became president in April 1865 with a theory of the war and a theory of the South. The war, he believed, had settled secession. It had not settled the racial order, and he intended to leave that order to White southerners themselves.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-senate-impeachment]

### 1.1 Johnson’s assumptions and the restored states

In May 1865 Johnson issued guidelines modeled on Lincoln’s wartime plan. Amnesty went to those who took a loyalty oath, with individual pardons required of former high civil and military officers and of owners of property worth $20,000 or more in 1860. A state could be readmitted once 10 percent of its voters had sworn loyalty and formed a government. Johnson appointed provisional governors, including William L. Sharkey in Mississippi, to organize conventions and elections.[^ch23-mshistorynow]

The plan returned the former Confederate states to self-government quickly. The governments that formed under it were controlled largely by White southerners who had supported the Confederacy, and the new legislatures moved at once to define the freedpeople’s status. Johnson pardoned many of the wealthiest and most prominent Confederates, and he used his veto against the earliest federal protections.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-app-veto]

The result was a set of consequential choices made under real constraints. Johnson believed that the federal government had no warrant to remake southern society and that White southerners would govern themselves responsibly if the Union was restored. He did not invent the racial order he defended. He chose to leave it intact at the moment when the people it had enslaved were least able to protect themselves.[^ch23-senate-impeachment][^ch23-check-scope]

### 1.2 The Black Codes

The Black Codes sought to define emancipation as a racialized labor regime. They granted a narrow civil capacity while denying land, mobility, and political rights.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]

Mississippi’s code is the exemplary case. The vagrancy law treated unemployment and assembly as crimes and hired out those who could not pay. The “civil rights” act let freedpeople contract and own property but not rent land. The penal code re-enacted the slave code and allowed convicts who could not pay their fines to be hired out. South Carolina and other states adopted comparable laws.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]

The purpose and the effect were connected. The codes gave employers a criminal-law remedy for labor discipline and gave the state a means of controlling a population it would not enfranchise. They also produced a documentary record of the freedpeople’s refusal to accept the terms. Workers left plantations, sought land, and demanded schooling, and their movements made the codes necessary in the first place.[^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes][^ch23-mshistorynow]

The consequence reached Washington. The codes, and the violence that accompanied them, persuaded many northern Republicans that federal protection was necessary. The argument that the South could be trusted to secure freedom collapsed in the face of the statutes and the beatings that enforced them.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-memphis-massacre]

### 1.3 The break of 1866

The veto fights, the massacres of 1866, and the Fourteenth Amendment turned Reconstruction from a presidential project into a congressional one.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-archives-14th]

Congress had created the Freedmen’s Bureau on March 3, 1865, and in 1866 it extended the Bureau over Johnson’s veto. The Bureau’s wartime and one-year mandate, its supervision of contracts, and its relief work made it a target of the president and of White southerners. Its agents were the federal government’s most visible presence in many counties, and their records became the era’s richest archive of Black family life and labor.[^ch23-senate-freedmens-bureau][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]

Senator Lyman Trumbull’s civil rights bill declared all persons born in the United States, “excluding Indians not taxed,” citizens with the same contracting, property, and legal rights as White citizens. Congress overrode Johnson’s veto on April 9, 1866. The House vote was 122 to 41. It was the first time Congress legislated on civil rights, and Johnson’s veto message had argued that the bill made race “operate in favor of the colored and against the white race.”[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-constitutioncenter-civilrights1866][^ch23-app-veto]

Violence made the case for federal action. In May 1866, White police and mobs in Memphis attacked Black residents over three days. In July 1866, a White mob attacked a Black convention in New Orleans. The Memphis project documents the first large-scale racial massacre of the postwar South, and both events lent urgency to the demand for a constitutional guarantee that no state could withdraw.[^ch23-memphis-massacre][^ch23-mshistorynow]

Congress passed the Fourteenth Amendment on June 13, 1866, and submitted it to the states on June 16. It defined citizenship, guaranteed due process and equal protection, reduced representation where the male franchise was denied, and barred former Confederates from office. The states ratified it on July 9, 1868.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-check-amendments]

The Republican victory in the elections of 1866 gave Congress the votes to override Johnson and to build a statutory and constitutional reconstruction. The break of 1866 was a sequence, not a single decision: the president’s leniency, the codes, the violence, and the amendment together moved the center of gravity from the White House to Capitol Hill.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-house-civilrights1866]

## 2. Congressional Reconstruction

Congress did not take power in a single act. It built a structure piece by piece, and each piece answered a problem the previous one had exposed.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]

### 2.1 The Fourteenth Amendment and the terms of citizenship

The Fourteenth Amendment made national citizenship and equal protection constitutional. It transferred a measure of authority over rights from the states to the federal government, but its enforcement provisions and its representation formula left much to Congress and to the courts.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-foner-second-founding]

Section 1 defined citizenship and prohibited states from abridging the privileges or immunities of citizens, depriving any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, or denying any person the equal protection of the laws. Section 2 reduced a state’s representation when it denied the male franchise, and Section 5 gave Congress power to enforce the amendment by appropriate legislation.[^ch23-archives-14th]

The amendment’s representation formula counted “the whole number of persons” in each state, “excluding Indians not taxed.” That phrase left Native Americans within tribal structures outside the citizenship the amendment declared, a question taken up in §5. The exclusion was not an oversight in the text. It marked the boundary of the political community the amendment was creating.[^ch23-archives-14th]

The chronology matters. Passage on June 13, 1866, submission to the states, and ratification on July 9, 1868, must be kept distinct. The election of 1868 came after ratification, and readmission depended on a state’s acceptance of the amendment. Collapsing these dates obscures how the amendment became law and why its enforcement remained contested.[^ch23-check-amendments][^ch23-archives-14th]

### 2.2 The Reconstruction Acts of 1867

The Reconstruction Acts put the former Confederate states, except Tennessee, under military authority and made Black male suffrage and the Fourteenth Amendment conditions of readmission.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]

The first act, passed over Johnson’s veto on March 2, 1867, declared that no legal state governments or adequate protection for life or property existed in 10 named states. It divided them into five military districts and directed each commander to protect “all persons in their rights of person and property” and to suppress disorder. The supplementary acts of March 23 and July 19, 1867, required a registration of male citizens 21 and over under an oath of loyalty, called conventions to frame new constitutions, and made Congress’s approval of each constitution a condition of representation.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]

Map 19 plots the five military districts and the pattern of Black political participation. It also marks the counties where Klan violence was heaviest and the nine South Carolina counties placed under martial law in 1871. The map is a guide to where federal authority was asserted and where it was contested. It does not claim that geography decided the outcome.[^ch23-check-map]

The acts authorized force in principle, but they depended on the president and the army to enforce it. Johnson’s administration used removals and instructions to restrain the commanders, and the tension between congressional authority and executive will ran through the rest of the decade. Congress could pass a law. It could not make a president enforce it.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts][^ch23-senate-impeachment]

### 2.3 Conventions, constitutions, and Black participation

The Reconstruction conventions were the era’s most direct experiment in interracial self-government. Black delegates helped write constitutions that established public schooling and expanded rights. Northern-born Republicans, whom opponents called “carpetbaggers,” and White southern Republicans, whom they called “scalawags,” also served in the new governments, and the Radical Republicans in Congress pressed hardest for Black suffrage and federal enforcement.[^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders][^ch23-mshistorynow]

The Mississippi convention that opened on January 7, 1868, included 16 Black and 78 White delegates. It produced a constitution providing for Black male suffrage and a system of free public schools, among other provisions. The convention was a government in the making, and its work had to be submitted to the voters who had elected it.[^ch23-zinn-ms-convention]

The scale of Black officeholding was real. Historians’ tables count roughly 1,500 Black officeholders in the southern states during Reconstruction, plus about 185 federal officeholders; the same tables count 267 Black delegates to the constitutional conventions of 1867–69 and 795 Black state legislators. The figures vary by source and definition, and they should be presented as estimates rather than as a census.[^ch23-check-numbers][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]

The conventions created governments that depended on Black voters and on the willingness of the federal government to protect them. That dependence made those governments the target of the counterrevolution in §4. Black southerners were building the governments that white supremacists would destroy.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-mshistorynow]

### 2.4 The Fifteenth Amendment and the Grant years

The Fifteenth Amendment barred disfranchisement on account of race, and the election of Ulysses S. Grant in 1868 put the presidency behind Reconstruction. The amendment’s silence on gender and its enforcement gap limited its reach.[^ch23-archives-15th][^ch23-battlefields-1868]

Congress passed the amendment on February 26, 1869, and the states ratified it on February 3, 1870. It protected the vote against denial “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude” and gave Congress power to enforce the guarantee. It did not prohibit literacy tests, poll taxes, or violence, and it said nothing about gender.[^ch23-archives-15th]

The election of 1868 was the first presidential election in which newly freed Black men could vote in some states. Grant defeated Horatio Seymour with 214 electoral votes to 80. The result put a general who had commanded the Union armies in the White House and gave congressional Reconstruction an executive who would use the Enforcement Acts, at least for a time.[^ch23-battlefields-1868]

The amendment split the women’s suffrage movement. The word “male” in the Fourteenth Amendment and the Fifteenth’s silence on gender led Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony to oppose it, while others argued that this was, in the period’s phrase, “the Negro’s hour.” The split showed that the political community was being defined by inclusion and exclusion at once.[^ch23-nps-womens-suffrage-split]

The limits were built into the victory. The amendment prohibited racial disfranchisement but left poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence available, and the Court’s later reading of the enforcement power narrowed what Congress could do.[^ch23-archives-15th][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]

### 2.5 Impeachment and the limits of federal will

Johnson’s conflict with Congress culminated in impeachment, but his acquittal by one vote showed the limits of Reconstruction’s constitutional instruments and left enforcement dependent on the executive.[^ch23-senate-impeachment]

The House voted to impeach on February 24, 1868, over Johnson’s removal of Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act. The Senate trial began on March 5. On May 16, 1868, the Senate fell one vote short of the two-thirds needed, 35 guilty and 19 not guilty. Votes on further articles failed on May 26, and the court of impeachment adjourned.[^ch23-senate-impeachment][^ch23-check-dates]

The outcome reflected concern for the presidency as much as support for Johnson. Senators who disliked him could still believe that removing him for a policy dispute would make the office subordinate to Congress.[^ch23-senate-impeachment]

Johnson’s acquittal and the tenor of his administration showed that Reconstruction would rest on a fragile combination of congressional will and executive enforcement. That combination held through Grant’s first term and weakened afterward. It is the theme that runs through §§4 and 6.[^ch23-senate-impeachment][^ch23-check-scope]

## 3. Families, labor, schools, churches, and officeholding

The constitutional history of Reconstruction is the part that Congress wrote. The social history is the part that freedpeople wrote, often in the same months and often against the resistance of the officials who were supposed to help them.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-check-race]

### 3.1 Families and marriage

Freedpeople made the family and the marriage contract the first arena of freedom, and the Freedmen’s Bureau became the agency through which many of those claims were legalized.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]

Bureau agents legalized marriages entered into during slavery, helped reunite families separated by sale and wartime displacement, and provided transportation to freedpeople seeking relatives. The Bureau recorded these acts, and its files became a principal archive of Black family reconstruction. The records are bureaucratic and repetitive, and they are also a record of people insisting that their relationships were real.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]

By formalizing their marriages, freedpeople made claims on one another and on the state. They sought federal recognition of relationships the slave regime had refused to protect, and they used the Bureau’s authority to give those relationships standing that local courts might otherwise deny. The marriage certificate was a legal instrument and a statement about what freedom was for.[^ch23-gilder-families]

The record documents what the Bureau recorded. The interior lives and the separations that left no paperwork survive unevenly, and the chapter must name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]

### 3.2 Labor, contract, and sharecropping

Freedpeople entered freedom with their labor as their main asset. The contract system, the crop lien, and sharecropping converted legal freedom into economic dependence for many of them.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-gilder-families]

The Bureau negotiated written contracts between planters and freedpeople and supervised their terms. Many freedpeople resented the system and tried to avoid it. A contract fixed a wage and a term, but it also fixed a worker to a place and gave an employer a legal claim on the worker’s time. The Bureau’s approval could protect a worker from a fraudulent account. It could also make the agreement enforceable against the worker.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-gilder-families]

Land reform failed. Despite talk during and after the war of dividing Confederate plantations among freedpeople, most land returned to its former owners. Sharecropping became the dominant arrangement in much of the South. In Georgia, 32 percent of farms were operated by sharecroppers by 1880, and 37 percent of the state’s 291,027 farms by 1910. A worker who could offer only labor often received as little as one-third of the crop, and crop liens kept many families in debt.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-check-numbers]

The debt cycle was not uniform. Evidence shows substantial movement among arrangements from year to year. The measure of freedom’s economic limits is not one contract or one crop. It is the structure that made land, credit, and law work against the people who had the least of each.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-check-numbers]

### 3.3 In Focus: a Freedmen’s Bureau labor contract

**In Focus.** On August 28, 1865, an agent of the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands for Robeson County, North Carolina, witnessed and approved an agreement in Lumberton between Robert McKenzie and Truss B. Hall. The transcription names the agent as James Sinclair, with a query after the surname. McKenzie agreed to pay Hall $4.00 a month until December 25 “for and in sideration of the said Truss B. Hall rendering the Robert McKenzie true and faithful service and obey all lawful commands as he use to when a slave.” Hall signed by his mark, and the Bureau agent witnessed and approved the instrument.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract]

The contract is evidence of both the federal presence and its limits. It gave Hall a wage and a term, but its language carried over the vocabulary of bondage, and its enforcement depended on the same agent and the same planters who dominated local power. The agent’s signature was meant to be a federal guarantee, but it was administered in a county where the employer held the land and the courthouse.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract][^ch23-capitol-contract]

A second contract from Kentucky, commencing January 19, 1866, between the employer Abraham Bledsoe and the freedman Henry Bledsoe, sets conditions and penalties for both parties and shows the same form. The Bureau urged written contracts rather than the distribution of land, and the form itself shows what the Bureau thought freedom would look like: a wage relation, supervised by a federal agent.[^ch23-capitol-contract]

The Robeson County transcription is a single instrument in one county, and the chapter should not generalize from it about all Bureau contracts. It should present the language as the record gives it, including the original spelling.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract][^ch23-check-focus]

### 3.4 Schools and churches

Black schools and churches were the institutions freedpeople built first and kept, and they became the foundation of a distinct political and civic life.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches]

The Freedmen’s Bureau did not hire teachers or operate schools itself, but it assisted the aid societies and the freedpeople. In Georgia at least 8,000 students attended freedmen’s schools within a year of freedom, and nearly 20,000 within eight years. Freedpeople paid tuition, raised funds, and built schoolhouses, and adults were often a third of the students, attending night and Sabbath schools. Roughly 600 teachers worked in the state’s freedmen’s schools; more than one-fifth were native Georgians, and a quarter were African American.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education]

The schools were an assertion of citizenship. A people systematically denied schooling built institutions to teach their children and themselves, and they did it with their own money and labor. The Bureau’s support was real, but the initiative was not the Bureau’s.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education]

Black Baptist and Episcopal congregations became sites where freedpeople worked out the terms of political participation. Church meetings were among the few places where Black southerners could assemble without White supervision, and ministers became political leaders. The churches often excluded women from formal leadership, an outcome that reinforced the era’s male-centered franchise politics.[^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches]

These institutions outlasted Reconstruction. They carried the demand for rights into the long decades of Jim Crow, and they are the chapter’s forward connection to Chapter 26.[^ch23-check-ch26][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education]

### 3.5 Officeholding and political life

Black officeholding was real and consequential, and it was persistently exaggerated and vilified as “Negro rule” by those who sought to overturn it.[^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]

Hiram Revels of Mississippi was seated on February 25, 1870, after the Senate voted 48 to 8 to admit him, becoming the first African American to serve in Congress. The vote turned on whether the Fourteenth Amendment had reversed *Dred Scott*. Revels’s presence in the Senate was a constitutional argument as much as a political event.[^ch23-senate-revels]

Blanche K. Bruce, also of Mississippi, was elected to a full Senate term in 1874 and became the first African American to preside over the Senate in 1879. During Reconstruction, 17 African Americans served in Congress, 15 in the House and two in the Senate. Across the period, Black officeholders held federal, state, and local posts, including six lieutenant governors, nine secretaries of state, and one governor, P. B. S. Pinchback of Louisiana. Map 19 shows where Black political participation was concentrated. The counts differ by source and should be presented as estimates with their definitions.[^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders][^ch23-check-numbers][^ch23-check-map]

The falsity of “Negro rule” follows from the numbers. Black officeholders never held power proportionate to their numbers, and they governed in states where White violence and economic power remained concentrated. The caricature served the counterrevolution that §4 describes. It gave respectable language to a campaign to drive Black voters from political life.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-senate-revels]

## 4. White supremacist counterrevolution

The counterrevolution was organized, armed, and aimed at the political capacity of Black communities and their White allies. It was not a response to crime or disorder. It was a response to the sight of Black people voting, holding office, teaching school, and sitting on juries.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-senate-enforcement]

### 4.1 The Klan and paramilitary violence

The Ku Klux Klan was founded in December 1865 by former Confederate officers and grew as a secret vigilante force targeting voters, officeholders, teachers, and jurors. It was one of several armed groups, alongside White Leagues and rifle clubs. The costumes and rituals were theater. The violence was not.[^ch23-eji-martial-law]

Violence concentrated in counties where Black majorities were entering politics. Federal investigators found in York County, South Carolina, alone evidence of 11 murders and more than 600 whippings and assaults. Attorney General Amos Akerman described the counties as “under the domination of systematic and organized depravity.” The phrase was official, and it described a system rather than a series of isolated crimes.[^ch23-eji-martial-law]

Map 19 marks the areas of heaviest Klan violence and the counties placed under martial law in 1871. The geography is part of the argument. The violence followed the places where Reconstruction governments were most dependent on Black voters, and it aimed at the local officials, teachers, and ministers who made those governments work.[^ch23-check-map][^ch23-senate-enforcement]

The violence made the federal government’s enforcement power the decisive question of Reconstruction. If the national government could not protect a citizen from a masked rider, then the amendments were promises without a remedy.[^ch23-senate-enforcement][^ch23-house-kkk-act]

### 4.2 Enforcement and Grant’s response

Congress answered the violence with a series of Enforcement Acts, and Grant used them, briefly, with real effect, before the will to enforce them receded.[^ch23-senate-enforcement][^ch23-eji-martial-law]

The Enforcement Act of May 1870 targeted conspiracies to violate citizens’ rights, including going “in disguise upon the public highways.” The Second Force Act of February 1871 placed national elections under federal supervision. The Third Force Act, the Ku Klux Klan Act of April 20, 1871, made it a federal crime to deprive any person of the rights named in the Constitution and authorized the president to use the military and to suspend habeas corpus.[^ch23-senate-enforcement][^ch23-house-kkk-act]

After a warning on October 12, 1871, Grant declared martial law and suspended habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties on October 17. Roughly 600 Klansmen were jailed by December, more than 200 were indicted, 53 pleaded guilty, and five were convicted at trial. Klan terrorism in the state declined significantly.[^ch23-eji-martial-law]

The gains depended on federal prosecutions and on local juries and officials. The violence subsided but did not end, and the legal foundation for enforcement was about to be narrowed by the courts. Grant had shown that federal power could work. He had also shown how much of it depended on a president willing to use it.[^ch23-fjc-cruikshank][^ch23-senate-enforcement]

### 4.3 Colfax and the Supreme Court’s retreat

The Colfax Massacre of 1873 and the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Cruikshank* in 1876 showed that the constitutional guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment would not reach the private violence that was overturning Reconstruction.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]

On Easter Sunday, April 13, 1873, in Colfax, the seat of Grant Parish, Louisiana, about 140 heavily armed White men, led by former Confederate officers, attacked a roughly equal number of Black men defending a Republican slate of parish officials after Louisiana’s disputed elections of 1872. Most of the Black men were killed while fleeing or after being taken captive. The death toll is contested, with claims ranging from about 50 to 400 and many recent accounts giving about 150.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-jcwe-colfax-count]

Federal authorities secured convictions against three perpetrators in 1874 under the Enforcement Act, but the Supreme Court overturned the verdicts in *United States v. Cruikshank* on March 27, 1876. The Court held that the rights at issue were not enforceable against private individuals and that the Fourteenth Amendment applied only to state action.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]

The Court’s narrow reading of the Fourteenth Amendment had been anticipated in the *Slaughter-House Cases* of 1873, and it eviscerated the federal enforcement that Congress had built. The Civil Rights Act of 1875, passed on March 1, 1875, would be struck down in 1883, a decision that belongs to Chapter 26. After *Cruikshank*, the federal government could not easily prosecute the violence that was restoring White rule, and the counterrevolution accelerated.[^ch23-senate-civilrights1875][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank][^ch23-check-ch26][^ch23-jcwe-colfax-count]

### 4.4 The Mississippi Plan and Redemption

“Redemption” was the participants’ own term for the overthrow of the Reconstruction governments. It was not a return to lawful order. It was a campaign of intimidation, fraud, and violence that dismantled Black political power state by state.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

In Mississippi in 1875, Democrats organized armed clubs, disrupted Republican meetings, and drove Black voters from the polls. They elected a Democratic legislature and governor and effectively ended Reconstruction in the state. The pattern was repeated across the South. The methods varied with local conditions, but the aim was constant: to make Black majorities unable to govern and a White minority able to rule.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

“The Mississippi Plan” became the name for the techniques later formalized in disfranchising constitutions. The name is a period term, and it should be used with care. It describes a set of practices that were illegal, violent, and effective, not a policy that was debated and adopted in public.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-names]

By the mid-1870s the surviving Republican governments in the South depended on federal troops and on contested election returns. That dependence set up the crisis of 1876–77 in §6. It also exposed the central weakness of Reconstruction: the rights were national, but the enforcement was local, and the local power belonged to the people who opposed them.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-race]

## 5. Western citizenship and land

Reconstruction was a national renegotiation of citizenship and land, not a southern episode alone. The same years that produced the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments produced the end of treaty-making and the integration of the West into a national market.[^ch23-check-scope][^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy]

### 5.1 The bounded citizenship of the Fourteenth Amendment

The Fourteenth Amendment defined citizenship in a way that included the formerly enslaved while leaving many Native Americans outside it. The era’s redefinition of “the people” was both expansive and bounded.[^ch23-archives-14th]

Section 1 granted citizenship to all persons born or naturalized and subject to the jurisdiction of the United States. Section 2 apportioned representation by “the whole number of persons in each State, excluding Indians not taxed,” a phrase that left those within tribal structures outside the count and, for many, outside the citizenship the amendment declared. The amendment’s reach and its exclusions were settled later by statute and litigation, but the pairing of Black citizenship with Native non-citizenship was a defining feature of Reconstruction’s constitutional order.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-check-race]

The pairing was not a contradiction in the minds of the amendment’s authors. It was a boundary: the amendment extended citizenship to a people whose labor had been the basis of southern society while confirming that tribal nations were something else. That distinction shaped the next century of federal Indian policy.

### 5.2 Grant’s Peace Policy and the end of treaty-making

The same federal government that reconstructed the South also reorganized its relations with Native nations, substituting a “peace policy” and unilateral legislation for treaty-making as the framework of western policy.[^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]

Grant took office on March 4, 1869, and endorsed a plan advanced by Ely S. Parker, a Seneca leader and the first Native American Commissioner of Indian Affairs, and by the 1867 Indian Peace Commission. The plan reformed the Bureau of Indian Affairs and distributed the management of reservations among Christian denominations while supporting western settlement and railroads. Its advocates sought to end fraud and war. Its structure placed Native nations under the supervision of churches and the federal government at the same time that settlers and railroads were moving onto their lands.[^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy]

In 1871 Congress ended formal treaty-making. The act provided that no Indian nation or tribe “shall be acknowledged or recognized as an independent nation, tribe or power with whom the United States may contract by treaty.” Congress agreed to honor the roughly 368 treaties ratified between 1778 and 1868, but the diplomatic framework had changed. Native nations could still negotiate, but they could no longer do so as treaty partners.[^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]

The reform and the dispossession belong together. The Peace Policy’s reformers sought to end fraud and war, and they believed they were acting in the interest of Native people. The policy also accompanied and enabled the seizure of land and the destruction of Indigenous sovereignty that Chapter 25 narrates.[^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy][^ch23-check-ch25]

### 5.3 Railroads, land, and the western political economy

The postwar West was being integrated into a national market by railroads and federal land policy at the same time that the South was being reconstructed. The two projects shared a logic of federal subsidy and racialized belonging.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]

Railroad construction boomed after the war. 35,000 miles of new track were laid between 1866 and 1873, and the first transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869. Railroads were the nation’s largest non-agricultural employer and the object of federal land grants and loans. The same Congress that imposed conditions on the southern states subsidized and organized the western territories, and the same racial logic that bounded citizenship in the Fourteenth Amendment shaped land and treaty policy in the West.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873][^ch23-archives-14th]

The connection is one of political economy, not analogy. In the South, the federal government created the legal conditions for a free labor system and then withdrew from protecting the people in it. In the West, it created the conditions for extraction and settlement and treated Native nations as obstacles to be administered. Both distributed land and authority along racial lines.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]

### 5.4 The concise western frame

Two facts place the West beside the South. The West’s integration by railroad and subsidy, and the narrowing of Native sovereignty by the end of treaty-making, both belong to the same years as the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]

The chapter does not narrate the Plains wars, the boarding schools, or the Dawes Act. It states the connection and hands it to Chapter 25. The point is not that the West explains the South or the reverse. The point is that Reconstruction was a national settlement about who belonged, who could own land, and who could govern, and the West was part of that settlement.[^ch23-check-ch25][^ch23-check-scope]

## 6. The 1876–77 settlement

The election of 1876 ended Reconstruction not by a clear verdict but by a disputed count resolved through a commission and an informal understanding. The outcome was a settlement about the presidency that became a settlement about the South.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

### 6.1 The disputed election of 1876

Democrat Samuel J. Tilden ran against Republican Rutherford B. Hayes. The outcomes in South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana were unclear, and Republican-controlled returning boards in all three states threw out enough Democratic votes to award their electoral votes to Hayes. A dispute over an elector in Oregon added a fourth contested state.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

On December 6, 1876, conflicting electors met in Salem, Columbia, Tallahassee, and New Orleans. Tilden had 184 electoral votes and Hayes 165, with 20 disputed. Turnout was reported at about 82 percent, the highest in United States history. The numbers describe a nation closely divided and a result that no single institution could certify without a fight.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-numbers]

### 6.2 The Electoral Commission and the count

Congress resolved the crisis by creating an Electoral Commission whose composition and vote decided the presidency. The resolution was constitutional improvisation, not a rule that all parties accepted.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

The Electoral Commission Act of January 29, 1877, created a commission of five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices. The independent justice refused to serve and was replaced by a Republican, and the commission voted 8 to 7 to award the disputed votes to Hayes.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

After Democratic threats of filibuster, Speaker Samuel J. Randall forced the completion of the count in the early hours of March 2, 1877. Hayes was declared the winner, 185 to 184, two days before his inauguration. The margin was one electoral vote, and the commission’s vote had been one justice.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-dates]

The chapter must separate the disputed election, the commission’s vote, and the withdrawal of troops as three distinct decisions. They were connected in time and consequence, but they were not a single bargain, and confusing them is one reason the “Compromise of 1877” has been so hard to define.[^ch23-check-scope][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

### 6.3 The withdrawal of troops and the “compromise” question

Hayes withdrew the remaining federal troops from the statehouses of Louisiana and South Carolina after Democratic pledges to protect the rights of Black and White Republicans, and the pledges were not kept. Whether a formal “Compromise of 1877” existed is disputed.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

When Hayes took office, federal troops protecting Republican governments were limited to small areas around the statehouses in New Orleans and Columbia. After the Democrats pledged to uphold civil and voting rights, Hayes pulled the troops out. White southerners reduced Black voting through intimidation and fraud, and later through poll taxes and literacy tests.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

The historiographical caveat matters. The Miller Center judges it “doubtful that Hayes, his supporters, and Democrats reached any sort of deal beyond what Hayes promised to do in his letter of acceptance.” The chapter should present the “Compromise of 1877” as a name historians have given to a contested cluster of events, not as a documented agreement with named terms.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-scope]

The withdrawal removed the last federal shield from the surviving Reconstruction governments and made Redemption complete. It did not create the conditions that ended Reconstruction by itself. The violence, the Court’s retreat, the depression, and the northern turn away had already done most of that work.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

### 6.4 The Panic of 1873 and the national turn away

The long depression that began in 1873 turned northern attention from civil rights to economic distress and helped make the withdrawal of federal protection politically acceptable.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]

The banking firm Jay Cooke and Company, heavily invested in railroad construction, closed on September 18, 1873, and a major panic followed. By 1876, 89 of the country’s 364 railroads had collapsed, 18,000 businesses had failed in two years, and unemployment had risen to about 14 percent.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]

Workers and businesspeople fought over relief, Grant sided with eastern financial interests, and the North became, in the PBS account’s phrase, “less and less concerned with addressing racism in the South” as falling farm prices and labor strife dominated politics. The depression did not cause racism. It changed the price of acting against it.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]

The depression and the railroad strikes of 1877 frame Chapter 24’s account of industrial conflict and the standardization of time. The same nation that was abandoning the South’s freedpeople was reorganizing its economy around steel, railroads, and the corporation. Chapter 24 takes up that story.[^ch23-check-ch24][^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]

## 7. The Debate

The argument over what Reconstruction was began while it was happening and has never entirely ended. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-aaihs-dubois]

### 7.1 What is settled

Slavery was abolished and citizenship redefined. The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments settled slavery’s legal status, defined national citizenship and equal protection, and prohibited racial disfranchisement, whatever their later enforcement.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-archives-15th]

Black political participation was real. Hundreds of thousands voted, hundreds held office, and Black delegates helped write state constitutions. The “Negro rule” caricature is false.[^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]

The counterrevolution relied on organized violence. The Klan, White Leagues, and rifle clubs used terror and fraud to overturn Reconstruction governments, and federal enforcement was real but temporary.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-archives-colfax]

The constitutional guarantees were narrowed by the courts. *Cruikshank* and related decisions limited the reach of the Fourteenth Amendment and undercut federal enforcement.[^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]

Land and labor were not settled. The amendments did not distribute land or guarantee a livelihood, and sharecropping and the crop lien bound many freedpeople to dependence.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping]

### 7.2 What is open

Was Reconstruction a revolution, an occupation, a democratic experiment, or a counterrevolution? Historians disagree about the era’s essential character and about which term best captures it.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-aaihs-dubois][^ch23-bensel-yankee-leviathan]

Did a “Compromise of 1877” exist? The withdrawal of troops is certain. A formal bargain is not documented, and the chapter should say so.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

How much did federal will, as against southern violence and northern economics, decide the outcome? The relative weight of enforcement, terror, and the depression remains debated.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]

What did the era settle for the long run? The amendments’ later force, from the twentieth-century courts to the civil rights movement, is a legacy the era’s actors could not foresee.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-foner-second-founding]

Whose memory prevailed? The Lost Cause account of Reconstruction as misrule competed with the emancipationist memory, and the chapter must not treat either as inevitable.[^ch23-blight-race-reunion][^ch23-aaihs-dubois]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which Black people built freedom under impossible conditions, Congress created real constitutional guarantees, and a violent counterrevolution combined with northern retreat to withdraw their protection. The era was neither a simple failure nor a completed revolution.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-aaihs-dubois]

Du Bois argued that enslaved people had already struck for their freedom and that Reconstruction was a genuine democratic experiment, not the corrupt misrule of the Dunning School. His account restored Black agency and political economy to the center of the story. The “general strike” thesis and his account of Reconstruction governments have been qualified and debated, but the challenge to the Dunning School held.[^ch23-aaihs-dubois][^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]

Eric Foner describes Reconstruction as an unfinished revolution and the amendments as a “second founding” that remade the Constitution and the federal government. The position’s risk is to overstate the revolution’s completeness and understate the counterrevolution’s success.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-foner-reconstruction]

Accounts of Reconstruction as an occupation stress how few troops sustained it and how quickly northern will receded. State-building accounts such as Bensel’s emphasize the durable federal capacities the era created even as its southern reach was uneven. “Occupation” explains why enforcement receded, but it misdescribes a program also built by southern Unionists, Black voters, and elected state governments.[^ch23-bensel-yankee-leviathan][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

The Dunning School and its Lost Cause foundations treated Reconstruction as an error and Redemption as rescue. Modern scholarship, including Blight’s, shows how reunion purchased a White republic and suppressed the emancipationist memory. A determinist version of the thesis can understate Black political action and the amendments’ later force.[^ch23-blight-race-reunion][^ch23-foner-second-founding]

The synthesis is that freedom was built, constitutionalized, and then starved of protection. The positions overlap more than they compete; they are tools for explanation, not teams.[^ch23-check-table]

## Legacies

Reconstruction ended with slavery abolished, citizenship defined, and Black political participation demonstrated, yet with the guarantees of the amendments largely unenforced and the federal government withdrawing from the South. The freedpeople who had legalized their marriages, built schools, and sent delegates to state conventions had proved that they could make freedom real where they had power. What they lacked was protection from violence and a land base, and both were withheld.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping]

The amendments’ promise did not disappear when the troops left. The Fourteenth Amendment’s text, narrowed in the 1870s and 1880s, became the foundation on which twentieth-century courts and movements would build. The institutional life of Black churches and schools carried the demand for rights through the decades of Jim Crow. The memory of Reconstruction as a failure or a betrayal shaped American politics for a century.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches][^ch23-blight-race-reunion]

The era’s lasting legacy is a set of questions that outlived it: whether constitutional guarantees mean anything without enforcement, whether freedom requires land and a livelihood, and whether a nation that had abolished slavery would protect the people it had freed. Those questions passed to the industrial order that Chapter 24 describes, and to the Jim Crow order that Chapter 26 takes up.[^ch23-check-ch24][^ch23-check-ch26]

## Summary

- Presidential Reconstruction returned the former Confederate states to self-government without securing the freedpeople’s rights, and Mississippi’s late-1865 Black Code defined emancipation as a racialized labor regime.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-yawp-black-code]
- Congress overrode Johnson’s veto of the Civil Rights Act on April 9, 1866, and passed the Fourteenth Amendment on June 13, 1866; the amendment was ratified on July 9, 1868.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-archives-14th]
- The Reconstruction Acts of 1867 placed 10 former Confederate states under military authority and made Black male suffrage and the Fourteenth Amendment conditions of readmission.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]
- Black delegates helped write new state constitutions, roughly 1,500 Black officeholders served in the southern states, and Hiram Revels was seated in the Senate on February 25, 1870.[^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders][^ch23-senate-revels]
- The Freedmen’s Bureau legalized marriages and supervised labor contracts, and one Robeson County contract shows both the federal wage relation and the language of bondage that survived it.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-dpla-labor-contract]
- Black schools and churches were built by freedpeople themselves; in Georgia at least 8,000 students attended freedmen’s schools within a year of freedom and nearly 20,000 within eight years.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches]
- Sharecropping replaced land reform for many freedpeople; by 1880, 32 percent of Georgia farms were sharecropped, and the crop lien kept many families in debt.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping]
- The Klan and other armed groups used terror to overturn Reconstruction; Grant declared martial law in nine South Carolina counties on October 17, 1871, but *Cruikshank* undercut federal enforcement.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]
- The Colfax Massacre of April 13, 1873, killed a contested number of Black men, and the subsequent prosecution failed in the Supreme Court.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-jcwe-colfax-count]
- The disputed election of 1876 was resolved by an Electoral Commission that awarded the presidency to Hayes, 185 to 184; whether a formal “Compromise of 1877” existed is disputed.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Reconstruction gave the United States its first constitutional definition of a biracial citizenship and its first serious attempt at interracial democracy, and it withdrew the protection that made both possible. The amendments outlived the era. The land and the votes did not, or not for long, and the schools survived under constant pressure. What the freedpeople had built — families, churches, schools, and political organizations — proved more durable than the federal will to defend them.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

The chapter takes up the second industrial revolution, the corporation, immigration, and the labor conflicts that followed Reconstruction.
