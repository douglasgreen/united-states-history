# Introduction — A Nation in Question

Shortly before nine on the morning of July 4, 2026, a Chinese gong sounded nine times from the roof of Monticello. The Thomas Jefferson Foundation estimated that nearly 3,000 people attended. The West Lawn served as an outdoor courtroom. The ceremony was held beneath a sprawling tent equipped with fans, only the second tent in the event’s 64-year history. The forecast had called for 102 degrees in Central Virginia. The morning was kinder on the mountaintop.[^intro-monticello-official][^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]

The ceremony was Monticello’s 64th annual Independence Day Celebration and Naturalization Ceremony. John Charles Thomas, a retired justice of the Supreme Court of Virginia, read from the Declaration of Independence. Chief Judge Elizabeth K. Dillon of the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Virginia declared the court in session. Senior Judge Michael F. Urbanski administered the oath of citizenship to 74 people from 36 countries. They raised their hands for the oath and placed them over their hearts for the Pledge of Allegiance. More than 4,000 people have become citizens there since the ceremony began in 1963.[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir][^intro-monticello-2026-cville][^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]

Gov. Abigail Spanberger delivered the keynote. “You have chosen to become Americans,” she said. Jane Kamensky, president and CEO of the Thomas Jefferson Foundation, welcomed the crowd and quoted Simon and Garfunkel: “We’ve all come to look for America, and gosh darn it, you’ve found it.”[^intro-monticello-2026-cville][^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]

Several of the new citizens spoke. Ana Maria Silva Molina, born in Ecuador, said, “I am choosing to become a U.S. citizen because I want my voice to be heard, and I want it to count.” Omar Basel Bouz Alassal, 19, who was born in Syria and came to the United States five years earlier, said that no words could explain how proud he was to become an American citizen and “have the right to call the United States my home.” Frank Georg Biller, born in Switzerland, promised to be “a citizen that gives more to the community and to the country than I take.” A reporter for *The Daily Progress* wrote that the day’s biggest applause was reserved not for the politicians but for the immigrants.[^intro-monticello-2026-wvir][^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]

Four days earlier, the Supreme Court had rejected President Donald Trump’s executive order ending birthright citizenship. In *Trump v. Barbara*, the judgment was 6–3. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for five justices on the constitutional holding that children born in the United States to parents unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth. Justice Brett Kavanaugh concurred in the judgment but not in that constitutional reasoning.[^intro-scotus-barbara-slip][^intro-scotus-barbara-npr]

At the ceremony, Nazanin Ash, chief executive of Welcome.US, said, “What a week to be having this conversation.”[^intro-monticello-2026-daily-progress]

The people taking the oath were joining a country whose highest court had just reaffirmed birthright citizenship, and a republic whose first naturalization law had limited the privilege to free white persons. The contradiction was not abstract. It stood in the ground beneath the tent.[^intro-naturalization-1790]

Thomas Jefferson was the principal author of the Declaration of Independence. He also enslaved more than 610 people during his lifetime; 400 men, women, and children lived in bondage at Monticello. The Declaration he drafted says that “all men are created equal.” The naturalization law approved in 1802, while Jefferson was president, used the phrase “any alien, being a free white person.”[^intro-monticello-slavery][^intro-declaration][^intro-naturalization-1802]

The man who wrote the sentence had been dead for two hundred years. He did not know the people under the tent, the Court’s decision, or the 250th anniversary of the Declaration. The contradiction belongs to the reader, not to him.

How did a continent of Indigenous nations become a republic, a continental empire, and a global power, and who could claim membership, freedom, land, and authority within those changing arrangements?

This book takes up that question across the territory that became the United States, from the peopling of the Americas to evidence available through October 29, 2026. Its center is the fifty states, but it follows the Atlantic, Caribbean, hemispheric, and Pacific connections that shaped them. It uses archaeology, oral histories, archives, law, newspapers, census series, testimony, landscapes, and digital records. It does not pretend to a neutral view from nowhere. It states its claims, shows the evidence, and names what the record cannot establish. The result is neither a celebration nor an indictment. It is an argument about how a nation was made, and what that making cost.

The Introduction proceeds in nine movements. §1 states the question and the wager. §2 defines the unit of analysis. §3 sets out five through-lines and seven claims. §4 explains the periodization. §5 describes the evidence and its silences. §6 maps the historiography. §7 states the method. §8 sets the language and dates. §9 is the reader’s guide. Chapter 1 begins at Fajada Butte, not at Monticello.

## 1. Why this book

### 1.1 The question

The question is not “What is America?” It is more specific and more historical: how were political arrangements made, and who could claim membership, freedom, land, and authority within them? The question asks how particular arrangements were made, defended, contested, and changed. It does not ask for a national essence.

Four nouns organize the inquiry. *Membership* is legal status, social belonging, and political claim. *Freedom* is not the absence of restraint but a set of enforceable capacities and exemptions. *Land* is property, homeland, ecology, and jurisdiction. *Authority* is the power to make and enforce rules, including the power to decide who belongs. The four are entangled. A change in the law of land can change the meaning of freedom; a change in the boundaries of the people can change who has authority to make law.

The Monticello ceremony is a small recent instance. Seventy-four people took an oath that made them citizens of a state whose boundaries and terms were made through conquest, treaty, slavery, migration, and law. The scene raises the question; it does not answer it. The answer requires a history of the arrangements themselves: how they were built, who benefited, who resisted, and what survived.

The book is a history of the United States, not a history of everything in North America. It follows the state’s formation and the territory it claimed, while treating Indigenous nations, empires, and neighboring republics as actors with their own histories. The distinction matters. A history that treats the United States as the only actor turns the continent into scenery. A history that treats every part of the continent as equally central to the American story loses the shape of the state’s power.

The question also cannot be answered by a single cause. Capitalism, race, geography, culture, law, and contingency all matter. The book tests combinations of causes against evidence and against rival explanations. It does not begin with a verdict and look for illustrations.

### 1.2 The wager

The wager is that the United States was repeatedly constituted through settlements that changed the terms of conflict without settling them permanently. Independence was a settlement. The Constitution was a settlement. The defeat of the Confederacy and the Reconstruction amendments were settlements. The New Deal, the wartime alliance system, the civil rights legislation, and the conservative realignment of the 1980s were settlements too.

A settlement, in this sense, is not a compromise, a victory, or a consensus. It is a durable arrangement that redistributes power: a constitution, a treaty, a labor regime, a racial order, an imperial status, a welfare state. It lasts long enough to structure the next conflict. The Missouri Compromise was a settlement. So was the post-Reconstruction racial order. So was the New Deal’s welfare state, with its exclusions. Each created real possibilities and real exclusions. Each was contested from the moment it was made.

The title’s double meaning follows from this. The nation is in question because its terms are contested. The book also puts the nation in question by asking how it was made, rather than assuming its goodness or its bad faith. The book does not treat the United States as a promise that kept itself. It does not treat the country as a fraud that fooled everyone. It asks what the settlements were, whom they served, and what they left unfinished.

Four rival explanations test the wager. The first is progress: the nation as a long march toward inclusion. The second is betrayal: the nation as a promise always broken. The third is inevitability: the outcome as the product of geography, culture, or economic law. The fourth is exceptionalism: the United States as a category apart from other nations. Each explains part of the record. None explains all of it. The book uses them as foils, not as teams.

The test runs in both directions. A settlement that permanently closed conflict would count against the thesis. A turning point with no plausible alternative would count against contingency. The book looks for both. It asks whether an arrangement lasted, what it changed, and what alternatives were available to the people who made it.

### 1.3 Audience and contract

The audience is attentive general readers, students, and listeners. No prior knowledge is assumed. No condescension is allowed. The book explains terms at first use and keeps the prose concrete. It does not assume that the reader has already chosen a side in the historiography.

The book is not an encyclopedia. It selects, argues, and shows its evidence. It does not claim to cover every event or to settle every controversy. It gives the reader enough to disagree. A history that tries to include everything ends by explaining nothing.

The book also has a point of view. It states its commitments: to evidence, to the dignity of the people it describes, to the seriousness of the questions. It tests those commitments against the record. The reader should be able to see where the argument is going and why. That is different from pretending that the argument has no author.

The voice is plain, serious, sometimes wry, never sneering. The book is written to be read aloud. It should sound like a person thinking in public, not a textbook reciting a consensus. Before the argument can be tested, however, the book has to define its unit. What does “the United States” mean?

## 2. What “the United States” means

### 2.1 State, project, place, and people

“The United States” names four things at once: a legal state, a settler project, a geographic place, and a people. Confusing them produces bad history. The book keeps them distinct and shows how they interact.

The legal state is the Constitution of 1787, a federal union with limited powers, divided sovereignty, and a promise of republican government. The Fourteenth Amendment’s Citizenship Clause names “citizens of the United States and of the State wherein they reside,” a dual structure that persists.[^intro-fourteenth-amendment] The state’s authority was never uniform. Territories, Indian country, the District of Columbia, and overseas possessions have had different relationships to the Constitution. *Downes v. Bidwell* held that Puerto Rico was not “a part of the United States” for the Constitution’s uniformity clause. The Constitution did not follow the flag everywhere.[^intro-downes]

The settler project is the dispossession of Indigenous nations, the extraction of land and labor, and the settlement of migrants. This is not a moral add-on to the national story. It is a description of how the state acquired and organized its territory.[^intro-dunbar-ortiz] The project was never complete or uniform. Indigenous nations retained, reacquired, and co-managed territory. Migrants changed the project from within. But the project set the terms.

The place is the territory that became the fifty states. The book follows the Atlantic, Caribbean, hemispheric, and Pacific connections that shaped that territory. Mexico, Canada, the Caribbean, Europe, Africa, and Asia enter where their histories explain the center, not as scenery for American decisions. The geographic frame is a choice, not a natural fact.

The people are the fourth meaning. “We the People” was a claim before it was a fact. The boundaries of the people were set by birth, naturalization, race, gender, settlement, and conquest. The book treats those boundaries as historical problems, not as background. The four meanings are not separate. The state’s law creates the people; the project takes the land; the place shapes the project. The next question is how much of this history happened before there was a republic at all.

### 2.2 A continent before the republic

The republic is a late arrival on a continent with a deep human history. People were living in North America at least 23,000 years ago. Fossil human footprints at White Sands National Park in New Mexico were made between about 23,000 and 21,000 calibrated years ago; the published seed dates include 22,860 ±320 and 21,130 ±250 years ago.[^intro-white-sands] The date is a minimum, not a single arrival. The peopling of the Americas was a long process of movement, settlement, and adaptation, with several plausible routes and many pauses.

Indigenous peoples built cities, earthworks, agricultural systems, and trade networks. They managed fire, water, and game. They made war and peace. The republic entered a world that was already political. The book begins with the peopling of the Americas, not with 1607 or 1620. The deep past is not a prelude to the nation. It is the first chapter of a continental history in which the nation is one episode.

Indigenous nations were not “prehistory.” They were actors with territory, diplomacy, kinship, and law. The book uses nation-specific names where known and explains contested names. Chapter 1 owns the archaeological and oral evidence for the continent’s early societies. Chapter 3 owns the political worlds of the contact era. The point here is simpler: the United States was not created on an empty stage.

The archaeological record is uneven and constantly revised. The book states dates as estimates or minimums, not as settled facts. A date can be precise without being certain. That caution is not a concession to obscurity. It is part of the history.

### 2.3 Borders, territories, and the problem of the map

The map of the United States is a claim, not a fact. Borders were made by treaties, purchases, conquest, court decisions, and settlement. They were contested by Indigenous nations, neighboring states, and the people who lived on the ground. A line on a map can represent a treaty, a reservation, a military base, a border crossing, or a place where none of those things is settled.

The United States includes states, territories, the District of Columbia, and overseas possessions with different rights and representation. *Downes v. Bidwell* is one landmark in that uneven geography. The census is another. On April 1, 2020, the resident population of the fifty states and the District of Columbia was 331,449,281. Puerto Rico’s resident population was 3,285,874.[^intro-census-2020-total] The census counts residents, not citizens. It is a portrait of who was counted, not a referendum on who belongs.

The book follows the territory that became the fifty states, but it does not treat the current map as inevitable. It shows the acquisitions, the claims, and the people who were already there. It also shows the places that the map does not resolve. The map cannot show jurisdiction, sovereignty, or belonging. A single color can hide a treaty, a reservation, a military base, or a border crosser. If the map is a claim, so is the category of “the people.” Who counted as “the people” was a legal question before it was a demographic one.

### 2.4 Who is “the people”?

Citizenship is a legal status, a political claim, and a cultural boundary. The book treats all three. The legal status can be granted or withheld. The political claim can be asserted by people who lack the status. The cultural boundary can be policed by ordinary people who have no legal authority. The distinctions matter because a country can naturalize 74 people in the morning and still exclude millions of residents from the full practice of political membership.

The Introduction’s close reading is a pair of statutes. The first federal naturalization law, approved March 26, 1790, provided that “any alien, being a free white person, who shall have resided within the limits and under the jurisdiction of the United States for the term of two years, may be admitted to become a citizen thereof.”[^intro-naturalization-1790] The 1802 law, approved April 14, 1802, during Jefferson’s presidency, kept the racial limit: “any alien, being a free white person, may be admitted to become a citizen of the United States.”[^intro-naturalization-1802]

The 1802 law changed the mechanics. It required a declaration of intention three years before admission and five years of residence. It kept the boundary of whiteness. The law was both a machine for making citizens and a fence around the people it would make. That double operation is the close reading. The republic did not simply have a citizenship policy. It used citizenship to define the republic.

The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, made “all persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof” citizens of the United States and of the state where they resided.[^intro-fourteenth-amendment] The clause established a constitutional rule of birthright citizenship and made Black people citizens after the Civil War. It did not settle every boundary. Section 2 of the amendment referred to “Indians not taxed,” and the legal treatment of tribal nations meant that the Citizenship Clause did not by itself make all Indigenous people citizens.

*United States v. Wong Kim Ark* held in 1898 that a child born in the United States to Chinese parents domiciled here was a citizen under the clause.[^intro-wong-kim-ark] The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 declared “all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States” to be citizens, while preserving tribal property rights.[^intro-indian-citizenship-act] The act did not by itself settle voting rights or tribal sovereignty. State law and later litigation mattered.

Tribal sovereignty is a distinct legal and political order, not a waiting room for citizenship. *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* described tribes as “domestic dependent nations” in a “state of pupilage,” with the relation of “a ward to his guardian.”[^intro-cherokee-nation] The language treated tribes as political communities distinct from foreign states. It also justified tutelage. The book keeps both sides of that language in view.

The case at the opening, *Trump v. Barbara*, turned on the same clause. Chief Justice Roberts wrote: “Citizenship, then and now, was the right to have rights—to freely participate in our political community.” He added: “We keep that promise today.”[^intro-scotus-barbara-slip] The judgment was 6–3, but the constitutional holding rested on five votes. Justice Kavanaugh concurred in the judgment on statutory grounds.[^intro-scotus-barbara-npr] The boundary of the people is still being litigated.

Legal citizenship is not the same as social belonging. A citizen can be excluded from the polity by race, gender, poverty, or violence. A noncitizen can be a member of a community in every sense except the legal one. The book keeps the distinction visible. The boundaries of the people are one of five through-lines. The next section sets out all five and the seven claims they support.

## 3. Five through-lines and seven claims

### 3.1 The five through-lines

The five through-lines are not separate histories. They are lenses that make different parts of the same history visible. Each chapter usually carries two or three. The book does not assign one lens per chapter, and it does not use the lenses as a checklist.

The first is land and environment. Settlement, Indigenous land management, agriculture, extraction, infrastructure, and climate all belong here. The central tension is that land is both a relation and a commodity. The lens follows the conversion of homelands into property and the resistance that conversion provoked. It also follows the places where the conversion was incomplete. Chapters 1–3, 14–18, 25, 34, and 49–50 carry much of this history.

The second is liberty and unfreedom. Slavery, emancipation, labor, bodily freedom, punishment, and the limits of legal rights belong here. The central tension is that American freedom was defined through, not despite, coercion. The lens follows the legal and social arrangements that made some people free by making others unfree. Chapters 4–6, 19–23, 26, and 39–40 carry much of this history.

The third is pluralism and belonging. Citizenship, migration, religion, race, gender, and the changing boundaries of “the people” belong here. The central tension is that inclusion and exclusion are produced by the same legal and political order. The lens follows the making and remaking of the people. Chapters 2–5, 13–17, 29–32, and 45–49 carry much of this history.

The fourth is capitalism and inequality. Production, credit, public subsidy, work, consumption, and the distribution of power and wealth belong here. The central tension is that markets are political creations, and they distribute risk unequally. The lens follows the relationship between growth and inequality. Chapters 15, 24–28, 33–34, and 43–45 carry much of this history.

The fifth is power and the world. Indigenous diplomacy, European empires, continental expansion, overseas empire, and superpower commitments belong here. The central tension is that the United States was made by the world and then made the world in its image. The lens follows the projection of power and the constraints on it. Chapters 8, 11, 18, 27, 30–31, 35–37, 41, and 46–48 carry much of this history.

The lenses can become a checklist if they are used badly. The book uses them to ask questions, not to force every chapter into five parts. A chapter that is mostly about land may also be about labor, race, and empire. Each chapter says which lens is primary and why. The reader should be able to see the others in the background.

### 3.2 The seven claims

The seven claims are the five through-lines stated as arguable propositions, plus two methodological claims. They are the book’s wager in its most compact form.

1. **Claim 1 — Land and environment.** The republic’s wealth and power rested on the conversion of Indigenous homelands and ecosystems into property, infrastructure, and commodities; that conversion was extensive but never total, and its consequences remain material.
2. **Claim 2 — Liberty and unfreedom.** American liberty was defined through, not despite, slavery, coerced labor, and exclusion; emancipation and civil rights changed the legal order without dissolving the entanglement.
3. **Claim 3 — Pluralism and belonging.** The boundaries of “the people” were repeatedly redrawn through migration, birth, naturalization, and racial and gender rules; belonging was never a single legal status or a stable cultural fact.
4. **Claim 4 — Capitalism and inequality.** Economic growth depended on public authority, credit, expropriated land, and unequal labor regimes; markets distributed risk and reward unevenly, and booms and busts reshaped political coalitions.
5. **Claim 5 — Power and the world.** The United States became a continental empire and then a global power through conquest, treaty, basing, and trade; its external role shaped its domestic arrangements and was shaped by them.
6. **Claim 6 — Settlements remained contested.** Constitutional, territorial, labor, and racial settlements changed the terms of conflict without ending it; each settlement produced new claims and new arenas.
7. **Claim 7 — Outcomes were contingent.** At each turning point, plausible alternatives existed; later outcomes were made by choices, accidents, and resistance, not foreordained by geography, culture, or economic law.

Claims 1–5 are substantive. They say what kind of history this is. Claims 6–7 are methodological. They say how the book explains change. Claim 6 without Claim 7 becomes a story of eternal conflict. Claim 7 without Claim 6 becomes a story of random accidents. Together they say that settlements are real and revisable.

The claims do not say that every settlement was equally unjust, that every outcome was equally open, or that the United States is uniquely evil or uniquely good. They are propositions to argue, not verdicts to apply. Each claim must be falsifiable. The book should be able to say what evidence would count against it.

### 3.3 What would count against the claims

The claims are built to be argued with. If the conversion of Indigenous homelands into property had been total and one-way, Claim 1 would be false. The federal government also conserved land, and Indigenous nations retained, reacquired, and co-managed territory. The book must show both the conversion and its limits.

If American liberty had been nothing but a disguise for slavery, Claim 2 would be too strong. Antislavery and free-labor ideologies were genuine. Not every appeal to freedom was a mask for coercion. The book must show the entanglement without erasing the genuine commitments.

If belonging were only a story of exclusion, Claim 3 would be false. Naturalization, birthright citizenship, and the expansion of rights created real inclusion. The book must show inclusion and exclusion as co-produced, not as one or the other.

If inequality were the only result of economic growth, Claim 4 would be misleading. Markets also created mobility, lower prices, and new goods. The book must show distribution, not just growth. It must ask who gained, who lost, and on what terms.

If American power had been only unilateral, Claim 5 would be false. The United States built international institutions and alliances that constrained its own power. The book must show the constraints as well as the projections. The same is true of the domestic constraints on empire.

If every settlement had collapsed, Claim 6 would be false. Some settlements were durable: the Constitution’s amendment process, the post–Civil War amendments, the New Deal, the Civil Rights Act. The book must explain durability without equating it with finality.

If contingency meant that anything could happen, Claim 7 would be trivial. Not every alternative was viable. Structural constraints, path dependence, and the distribution of power limited the options. The book must specify the alternatives and the constraints. A claim survives if it explains the pattern better than its rival, not if it explains every case.

## 4. Periodization

### 4.1 Why periods are arguments

A period is not a neutral container. It selects events, implies causes, and silences alternatives. “The Jacksonian era” tells a different story from “the age of removal.” “The Cold War” tells a different story from “the American century.” The names are arguments about what mattered.

The book uses parts and chapters as interpretive groupings, not as date bins. The boundaries are chosen to make a set of changes visible: the consolidation of colonial societies, the break of independence, the re-founding of Reconstruction, the rise of industrial capitalism, the New Deal, the rights revolution, and the neoliberal turn. The dates matter, but the questions matter more.

Some chapters overlap on purpose. Chapters 23–28 revisit the same post–Civil War decades through Reconstruction, industry, the West, the South, empire, and culture. Chapters 37–43 revisit the Cold War through institutions, abundance, movements, legislation, foreign war, and realignment. The overlaps are intentional. Each chapter has a different job. The book uses cross-references to avoid repeating an earlier explanation.

Periodization can become teleology. The book resists by returning to contingency. At each boundary, it asks what else was possible. The point of a period is not to make the outcome look inevitable. It is to make the choices visible.

### 4.2 The spine

The spine is a sequence of periods and turning points, not a chapter list. The periods overlap at the seams, as the chapters do.

- Deep past to 1500 CE: peopling, agriculture, cities, earthworks, and regional political worlds.
- c. 1000–1551: Norse and Iberian landfalls, contact, conquest, exchange, and the Valladolid debate.
- 1500–1620: Indigenous political worlds, European footholds, and the first permanent English settlements.
- 1607–1763: colonial formation, slavery, empire, and the great war for empire.
- 1763–1801: imperial crisis, independence, constitution-making, and the new republic.
- 1800–1848: expansion, the market revolution, mass politics, removal, and the Mexican-American War.
- 1850–1877: the sectional crisis, the Civil War, emancipation, and Reconstruction.
- 1877–1900: industrial capitalism, the conquest of the West, the New South, empire, and culture.
- 1900–1945: progressive reform, the First World War, prosperity, depression, the New Deal, and the Second World War.
- 1945–1968: the Cold War, affluence, the civil rights revolution, the Great Society, and Vietnam.
- 1968–1991: unraveling, stagflation, the Reagan revolution, and the end of the Cold War.
- 1991–2008: globalization, culture wars, 9/11, and the war on terror.
- 2008–October 29, 2026: the Obama era, the populist turn, the first Trump presidency, the reckoning, and the 250th anniversary.

The boundaries are arguments, not natural joints. 1551 marks the Valladolid debate. 1620 marks the Mayflower. 1763 marks the Treaty of Paris. 1877 marks the end of Reconstruction. 1945 marks the end of the Second World War. 1968 marks a rupture in politics and culture. 2001 marks 9/11. 2026 marks the 250th anniversary and the book’s cutoff. Chapter 50 is a retrospective conclusion, not a fifty-first chapter. It weighs what the preceding 49 chapters established.

### 4.3 The 2026 cutoff

The narrative ends with evidence available through October 29, 2026. The 250th anniversary is a framing occasion, not permission to invent a ceremony or predict a result. The book can describe the Monticello ceremony because it happened and was reported.

The November 2026 election is after the cutoff. The book does not report it as completed history or predict its outcome. The Introduction can say that the election lay ahead at the cutoff. It does not say what it meant.[^intro-check-recent-cutoff]

Claims about 2025–2026 require fresh verification. Journalism is a source, not the final word. Official documents, court records, and transcripts may appear later. The book marks recent claims as provisional where the record is thin. The 250th anniversary is an occasion for public memory. It is not a conclusion about the nation’s meaning.

### 4.4 What this periodization excludes

The book is not organized presidency by presidency. Presidents matter, but they are not the only actors. The book is not organized war to war. Wars are turning points, but land, labor, migration, and law change in peacetime too. The book does not assume that the twentieth century was the nation’s culmination. It treats the “American century” as one period among others, not as the end of the story.

The book also does not assume that the nation is rising or falling. It asks how arrangements were made and changed. That choice has a cost. A periodization that foregrounds land, labor, and belonging will underplay some political and military events. The chapters on those events must justify their place, not assume it. The result is a history with a shape, not a history with a single plot.

## 5. Sources and silences

### 5.1 The evidentiary base

The book uses many kinds of evidence because no single archive contains the history. Archaeology, oral histories, archives, law, newspapers, census series, testimony, landscapes, and digital records each answer different questions. A landscape can show how people used a river. A statute can show what the state permitted. A letter can show what a person believed, or wanted a correspondent to believe.

Primary sources establish what a document said, what a record counted, or what a witness claimed. Scholarship supplies interpretation and context. A primary source can establish that someone made a claim without establishing that the claim was true. The book uses both, and it keeps the difference visible.

Every substantive claim has a named citation note with a precise locator and a “Used for” annotation. The note is part of the argument, not decoration. The reader should be able to follow the trail from the sentence to the source. The book prefers accessible, stable, document-specific sources. Where a source is offline or restricted, it identifies the edition and locator and states the limitation. It does not invent a URL, a page, or a verification result.

The book also distinguishes three states of evidence: discovered, read, and verified for the claim. A search result is a lead. A source that is merely discovered is not evidence. Only a source that has been read and checked supports a checked assertion. That rule slows the writing. It also keeps the book honest.

### 5.2 What the archive holds

The archive is rich, but it is not neutral. It was created by states, institutions, enslavers, courts, and officials. Its categories reflect their interests. The book uses the archive against the grain where it can, and it names the grain where it cannot.

The Thomas Jefferson Foundation’s Getting Word project records and preserves the family histories of the over 610 people enslaved by Jefferson.[^intro-getting-word] It is a model of institutional research and descendant collaboration. It is also a curated archive. The book distinguishes the foundation’s summary from the underlying records and oral histories.

SlaveVoyages documents more than 36,000 transatlantic voyages between 1514 and 1866 and more than 37,000 intra-American voyages between 1550 and 1887. Its home page describes the voyages as having relocated more than 12 million enslaved Africans.[^intro-slavevoyages] The database is a remarkable reconstruction, but it records what surviving sources document. It is not a census of the enslaved.

The Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center gathers records of the school and the thousands of students sent there.[^intro-carlisle] The archive is partial and shaped by the school’s own recordkeeping. The book uses it to recover names and experiences, and it names what the archive cannot show.

Official records anchor the book’s legal and statistical claims: the National Archives, court opinions, statutes, census releases, and presidential documents. They are authoritative for what they record, not for what they omit. Journalism is essential for recent events, including the Monticello ceremony. It is firsthand or near-firsthand reporting, but it is not a transcript. The book attributes quotations to the reporting and checks them against recordings where possible.[^intro-check-quote-transcription]

### 5.3 What the archive does not hold

Silence is evidence of power, not proof of absence. The archive’s gaps are part of the history the book tells. The book asks who had the authority to record, who was recorded, and who disappeared from the record.

Many enslaved people left no written record. Their voices survive in court testimony, bills of sale, letters, oral histories, and the records of the people who owned them. The book uses those traces without pretending to recover a full interior life. It does not invent thoughts to fill a silence.

Some Indigenous knowledge is not public, and some is protected by protocols of permission and reciprocity. The book does not treat a public archive as the whole of Indigenous history. It uses what communities and institutions have made available, and it names the limits.

Unpaid care work, household production, and reproductive labor are often missing from wage records and census categories. The book reads the sources with that absence in mind. People without legal status often avoid records or are recorded by the state in categories designed for enforcement. The book does not infer their lives from the state’s categories alone.

Records from 2025–2026 are incomplete. Archives may be closed, and officials may not have released documents. The book marks recent claims as provisional.[^intro-check-recent-cutoff] Where no voice survives, it names the silence. It does not ventriloquize, infer consensus from an absent record, or treat an archive’s exclusions as proof of nonexistence.

### 5.4 Numbers and their limits

Numbers are arguments. They carry definitions, denominators, observation years, and uncertainty. The book states them.

The 2020 Census counted 331,449,281 residents of the fifty states and the District of Columbia. The Post-Enumeration Survey estimated a 3.30% undercount of the Black population, a 4.99% undercount of the Hispanic or Latino population, a 5.64% undercount of American Indian or Alaska Native people living on reservations, and a 1.64% overcount of the non-Hispanic White population.[^intro-census-2020-total][^intro-census-2020-undercount] These are estimates with standard errors, not counts of missing individuals. The categories are census categories, not natural kinds.

The SlaveVoyages figure of more than 12 million is a count of people forced onto documented voyages, not a count of survivors or of all enslaved people in the Americas.[^intro-slavevoyages] The book does not convert it into a population estimate without saying so.

Every statistic names its source, observation year or period, units, population or denominator, and series or table locator. Rounding and calculations are stated. Inflation-adjusted money names the price index and base year. A number can be precise without being accurate. The book prefers a range with a stated uncertainty to a false precision.

## 6. Historiographical map

### 6.1 Why historiography is part of the argument

History is written from a position. The book’s synthesis is a wager, not a consensus. The reader should know the arguments the book is choosing among. Historiography is not a detour from the argument. It is part of the evidence that the argument is serious.

The map is not a parade of schools or a scorecard. It is a set of tools for asking better questions. Each position illuminates something the others miss. A label can become a stereotype. The book names positions to clarify differences, not to dismiss the people who hold them. The table’s “limitation” column is an invitation to argue, not a verdict.

### 6.2 From national narrative to professional history

Nineteenth-century historians wrote the nation as the fulfillment of liberty: Providence, progress, great men, and the march of settlement. The narrative was powerful and popular. It also pushed Indigenous nations, enslaved people, and conflict to the margins. The book uses that tradition as a foil, not as a set of arguments to attribute to every national historian.

The Progressive critique put land, class, and interest at the center. Frederick Jackson Turner argued that “the existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development.”[^intro-turner] Charles A. Beard argued that the Constitution reflected the economic interests of its framers and should be read as a document of political economy.[^intro-beard] Turner and Beard made conflict historical. They also tended to treat the frontier as empty and politics as a calculation of interest.

The consensus turn of the mid-twentieth century emphasized shared liberal premises and the stability of American politics. Richard Hofstadter’s *The American Political Tradition* traced a set of shared political ideas across the presidents. Two decades later, *The Progressive Historians* criticized Turner, Beard, and Parrington for imposing a conflict narrative on the past.[^intro-hofstadter] The consensus account explained stability. It underestimated slavery, dispossession, and coercion.

The social-history turn recovered the agency of workers, enslaved people, women, immigrants, and ordinary communities. Eric Foner’s *Reconstruction* treated emancipation as an unfinished revolution and placed freedpeople’s agency at the center.[^intro-foner] Jacquelyn Dowd Hall’s “long civil rights movement” argued that the movement should be understood as a longer struggle over the political uses of the past.[^intro-hall] Social history restored agency, violence, and the long arc of struggle. It sometimes fragmented the national story.

The sovereignty, race, gender, and environment turns made conquest, Indigenous political orders, gender, and ecology constitutive rather than peripheral. Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz’s *An Indigenous Peoples’ History of the United States* made settler colonialism a central frame.[^intro-dunbar-ortiz] Richard White’s *The Middle Ground* described a space of accommodation and brokerage between Indigenous nations and empires.[^intro-white-middle-ground] William Cronon’s *Nature’s Metropolis* showed that Chicago and its rural hinterland were mutually constituted through markets, transportation, and ecology.[^intro-cronon] Linda K. Kerber’s *No Constitutional Right to Be Ladies* made citizenship a relation of rights and obligations, not only a legal status.[^intro-kerber]

The capitalist turn and its critics added another layer. The New History of Capitalism argued that slavery and cotton were central to industrial capitalism.[^intro-new-history-capitalism] Alan L. Olmstead and Paul W. Rhode criticized its quantitative claims and called for more careful definitions and evidence.[^intro-olmstead-rhode] The debate is a useful case study in how a powerful interpretation can outrun its evidence.

### 6.3 Five positions

Five positions organize the map. The first is progressive political economy. Its core claim is that American democracy and the Constitution were shaped by westward expansion and conflicts over economic interest. Its contribution was to put land, class, and power at the center. Its limitation was to treat the frontier as empty and inevitable and to reduce politics to interest.

The second is consensus/liberal. Its core claim is that American politics has rested on a shared liberal framework, with conflict occurring within narrow bounds. Its contribution was to explain stability and the durability of institutions. Its limitation was to underestimate slavery, dispossession, and coercion, and to treat a national consensus as universal.

The third is conflict/social history. Its core claim is that history from below matters: race, labor, and grassroots movements drive change, and emancipation and civil rights are unfinished. Its contribution was to restore agency, violence, and the long arc of struggle. Its limitation was to fragment the national story and sometimes to let the state and political economy disappear.

The fourth is sovereignty, race, gender, and environment. Its core claim is that conquest, Indigenous political orders, gender, and ecology are constitutive, not peripheral. Its contribution was to reframe the nation as a settler-colonial and ecological project. Its limitation is that synthesis can flatten regional variation, and “settler colonialism” can become a single explanatory key.

The fifth is transnational/critical political economy. Its core claim is that slavery and cotton were central to industrial capitalism, and that the economy was built through global flows of capital, labor, and violence. Its contribution was to connect domestic inequality to global markets and coerced labor. Its limitation is the persistence of definitional and quantitative disputes, and the risk of reducing slavery to a factor of production.

### 6.4 What is settled, what is open, and what this book takes

What is settled, among the works this book relies on, is that the Constitution was a contested settlement, not a neutral framework. Slavery and dispossession were central, not marginal. Indigenous nations were political actors. The New Deal and the rights revolution changed the country. The recent past is not a simple story of progress or decline. These are not unanimous conclusions, but they are the ground on which the argument stands.

What is open is the relative weight of economic interest, ideology, race, and contingency. The degree to which the United States was exceptional is open. The periodization of the twentieth century is open. The meaning of the 2026 anniversary is open. The book does not pretend to close these questions.

What the book takes from the Progressive tradition is power and political economy. From the consensus tradition it takes the importance of durable institutions and shared frameworks. From social history it takes agency, violence, and the view from below. From the sovereignty, race, gender, and environment turns it takes conquest, ecology, and the politics of difference. From transnational and critical political economy it takes global flows and coerced labor.

What the book rejects is inevitability, consensus as a national fact, pure victimhood, pure agency, market fundamentalism, and the idea that the nation is either a promise or a fraud. The synthesis is a set of tested claims, not a blend of schools. The historiographical lesson is that the best history is not the one that picks a side and stops. It is the one that states its position, gives the strongest version of the rival, and shows what the evidence can bear.

## 7. Method

### 7.1 Scene and claim

Each chapter begins with a documented scene, states its claim, and then shows how the evidence supports it. The scene is not decoration. It poses the problem the chapter will explain. A scene can be a place, a person, an object, a document, or a day. It must be established by evidence.

If the record does not support a detail, the book omits it, qualifies it, or chooses another scene. It does not invent thoughts, dialogue, weather, or witnesses. A scene can be reconstructed from reporting, records, and testimony. It cannot be filled in from the historian’s imagination. The rule is simple and hard: the scene must be documented, and the documentation must be visible.

The chapter’s first subsection states the claim and the structure. The reader should know what the chapter argues before the narrative begins. The claim is not a thesis pasted onto the front. It is a proposition the chapter will test. Each section ends on a consequence or a new question, not a repetitive summary. The chapter’s last section, the Debate, states the strongest rival interpretation and answers it.

### 7.2 Evidence and counterevidence

The book distinguishes evidence, inference, and speculation. It gives the strongest version of the rival argument before answering it. That is not a courtesy. It is a test of whether the argument is any good.

Every substantive assertion, quotation, statistic, and caption has a named citation note with a precise locator and a “Used for” annotation. Reused notes support the same passage and qualification. The book names what each source cannot establish. A mandatory policy can be documented without proving that it was enforced. A speech can be documented without proving that the audience agreed.

The book uses “the record suggests,” “probably,” and “the surviving evidence does not show” when the evidence is inferential. It does not use “arguably” to avoid a judgment. The difference between a qualified claim and a vague one is the difference between honesty and evasion.

### 7.3 Causation and contingency

Historical explanation distinguishes structural conditions, precipitating events, and contingent choices. The book shows the mechanism, not just the correlation. If the book says that slavery helped cause the Civil War, it must show how the political system, the economy, and the moral conflict interacted. “In part” is not a causal explanation. It is a placeholder for one.

Counterfactuals are disciplined questions, not fiction. The book asks what alternatives were plausible for the actors, given their information and constraints. It does not invent a world in which everything else stays the same. Contingency is not randomness. The book shows that outcomes were made, not that they were arbitrary.

### 7.4 Scale and connection

The book moves between local, national, and global scales. The through-lines connect the scales without erasing their differences. A chapter may begin with a single place—a courthouse, a factory, a reservation, a kitchen—and then widen. The local scene grounds the argument in evidence.

The national scale follows the state’s formation, law, and politics, but it does not assume that the state is the only actor. The global scale follows empires, markets, migrations, and wars across borders. It does not treat the world as scenery for American decisions. The scales are not a ladder. A local event can change a national settlement, and a global war can change a kitchen.

Chapters overlap intentionally. When a later chapter needs an earlier explanation, it uses a cross-reference, not a repetition. The reader should not expect every chapter to be self-contained. The book is a sequence, not a set of encyclopedia entries.

### 7.5 Writing to be read aloud

This is prose for the ear. The sentences should have a cadence, a clear main idea, and a human voice. Most should stay under 25 words. The book prefers concrete nouns and active verbs. It cuts “it is important to note,” “arguably,” “of course,” “in many ways,” and “the fact that.” It defines technical terms at first use.

A representative page should be read aloud, or heard through text-to-speech, before it is called finished. The test is not whether the prose sounds impressive. It is whether a listener can follow the argument without rereading a sentence. Stacked clauses, repeated rhythm, and places where the reader stumbles are signs that the prose is doing too much at once.

Reading aloud can also encourage overstatement and false intimacy. The book should sound like a person thinking, not a performer seeking applause. The voice is a means of clarity, not a substitute for evidence.

## 8. Names, language, and dates

### 8.1 Names and groups

The book capitalizes Native American and Indigenous. It uses the specific nation and its preferred name where known: Haudenosaunee, Wampanoag, Cherokee, Diné. It uses “Indian” only in quotation or historical proper names such as the Indian Removal Act, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and Indian Territory. Alternate names are explained where they matter.

The book capitalizes Black and White when referring to people. It prefers “enslaved people” in its own prose and retains historical proper names and exact quotations. “Enslaver” is used where it is accurate. The choice is explained where a source uses a different term.

“Latino” is the general noun and adjective. “Latina” refers to a woman. “Hispanic” appears when citing census categories or a person’s self-identification. “Latinx” appears only when a source or a person uses it. The book states the convention once and then follows it.

“Immigrant” describes a person who moves to a country. “Refugee” describes a person who meets the legal definition or is described by a source as a refugee. “Migrant” describes a person who moves without specifying status. The book prefers “unauthorized immigrant” for a noncitizen who lacks lawful immigration status. It avoids “illegal” as a label for a person, because the term describes conduct rather than a person and can misstate a violation that is often civil rather than criminal. “Illegal” appears only in quotation or in an official program name. “Undocumented” remains available where it means absent from the documentary record.

For gender and sexuality, the book uses the terms people use for themselves where known. It explains terms at first use. It does not infer identity from behavior or from a source’s category. A name is part of the evidence. The book does not modernize a name silently. It explains the change in a note.

### 8.2 “America” and “the United States”

“America” can mean the hemisphere or the United States. The book uses “the United States” for the state and “the Americas” for the hemisphere. “American” is the adjective, with the context made clear. The choice is not a solution to the naming problem. It is a consistent practice.

The title, *A Nation in Question*, uses “nation” as a claim, not a neutral description. The book asks what kind of nation the United States is, and for whom. It does not resolve the question by fiat. It explains the choice and uses it consistently.

### 8.3 Dates and numbers

The book uses American date order: July 4, 1776. It gives full years before 1800 and at first mention. A standalone year is never abbreviated. It writes “the 1850s.” It uses CE and BCE for the deep past. When it uses “years before present,” it identifies the reference year; radiocarbon dates conventionally use 1950 CE.

Great Britain and its North American colonies adopted the Gregorian calendar in September 1752. The book uses Julian (Old Style) dates through September 2, 1752, and Gregorian (New Style) dates from September 14, 1752. It does not silently convert dates to the other system. Where a January–March date before 1752 is recorded under the old legal year, the book retains the source’s double year, as in “February 9, 1649/50.” Dates from Spanish, French, or other jurisdictions that used a different calendar are given as their records date them, with the system identified when it matters.

Ranges and coordinated compounds use en dashes: 1861–65, Chs. 5–13, North–South. A hyphen never means a range between numbers. Prose spells out one through nine and uses figures for 10 and above, except in proper names, dates, statistics, populations, money, and item numbers. Tables, data, and appendices use figures.

Every statistic states its source, observation year or period, units, population or denominator, and series or table locator. Rounding and calculations are stated. Inflation-adjusted money names the price index and base year. A date can be precise without being certain. The book uses “c.” and ranges where the evidence is uncertain.

### 8.4 Quotations, titles, and slurs

Quotations are exact. The book checks spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and omissions against the consulted edition. It uses curly quotation marks and apostrophes. Square brackets mark insertions. A spaced ellipsis marks an omission. If a passage is modernized or translated, the book says so and identifies the version or translator. It never silently repairs the source.

Books, journals, newspapers, ships, films, and court cases are italicized: *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*, *The New York Times*, USS *Arizona*, *Brown v. Board of Education*. Articles, essays, speeches, sermons, chapters, and titled short documents appear in quotation marks: “A Model of Christian Charity,” “I Have a Dream,” “Letter from Birmingham Jail.” Acts, treaties, amendments, and official documents are capitalized and roman: the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, the Fourteenth Amendment, the Emancipation Proclamation.

Slurs and degrading period language appear only in quotation, only when necessary to the historical argument, and with an explanation in the same paragraph of why they are reproduced. They are not used as section titles or marketing hooks. Quotation is not endorsement. The book can quote a document’s cruelty without adopting its voice.

## 9. Reader’s guide

### 9.1 How a chapter works

Each chapter has a repeatable architecture: a documented scene, a framing paragraph, a roadmap, numbered sections, an In Focus box, a Debate, Legacies, a Summary, Study Questions, Further Reading, a closing note, and, where there is a next chapter, a bridge. The Introduction is the exception. It has no epigraphs, no In Focus box, and no formal bridge.

The reader can read in order or follow a through-line. The chapters are designed to stand alone, but they assume the previous chapter’s argument. Cross-references point to the section that supplies the missing context. The book does not repeat an earlier explanation when a cross-reference will do.

The apparatus is part of the book. The notes are not decoration. The appendices are reference material: A, Documents; B, Tables and Data; C, Maps and Gazetteer; D, Chronology; E, Historiographical Essay and Further Reading. The book is written to be argued with. The Study Questions are prompts for analysis, not quizzes.

### 9.2 How to use the notes and appendices

The named footnotes take a claim to its source. The first citation of a work gives the full details. Later citations give the author, short title, and new locator. The notes also carry qualifications and source limitations. They are not a bibliography of everything the author read. They are the evidence for the claims in this book.

The appendices use the same evidence and typography rules as the chapters. Appendix A collects documents. Appendix B collects tables and data. Appendix C catalogs maps and places. Appendix D gives the chronology. Appendix E surveys the historiography and recommends further reading. Further Reading is a selective guide, not a citation ledger.

### 9.3 The route to Chapter 1

Chapter 1 begins at Fajada Butte, where people are believed to have marked the sun long before there was a United States. The Introduction’s Monticello scene is a recent frame, not the chronological start. The book moves from the deep past to the present, and the reader should feel the distance.

The hand-off is deliberate: the next chapter begins at Fajada Butte, not in 1776.[^intro-fajada] The Introduction does not answer its question. It gives the reader the terms, the evidence, and the stakes, and then sends them into the history.[^intro-check-chapter1-opening]

## Closing note

The tent on the West Lawn was temporary. The new citizens posed with their certificates. Monticello still stands above the Virginia piedmont, with its slave quarters, its kitchen, its nail factory, and its carefully written silences. The man who wrote “all men are created equal” enslaved more than 610 people, and the naturalization law approved while he was president used the phrase “any alien, being a free white person.” The country that naturalized 74 people that morning was the same country whose Supreme Court, four days earlier, had rejected an attempt to end birthright citizenship.[^intro-monticello-slavery][^intro-declaration][^intro-naturalization-1802][^intro-scotus-barbara-slip]

The book’s wager is that these facts belong together. The United States was not a promise that kept itself, and it was not a fraud that fooled everyone. It was a series of settlements that changed the terms of conflict without settling them permanently. The settlements were real: constitutions, treaties, emancipation, the New Deal, the rights revolution. They were also incomplete: land still taken, labor still coerced, belonging still policed. The nation is in question because the question was never closed.

The reader is not a spectator. The evidence is here, the arguments are stated, and the notes show the way back. The next chapter begins not in 1776 but at Fajada Butte, where a dagger of sunlight once crossed spiral petroglyphs long before anyone called this continent the United States.[^intro-fajada][^intro-check-chapter1-opening]
