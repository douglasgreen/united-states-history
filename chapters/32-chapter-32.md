## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On the evening of November 2, 1920, the Westinghouse station KDKA, in East Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, broadcast the Harding–Cox presidential election returns. The station had received its commercial broadcasting license from the Department of Commerce the previous month, in October 1920.[^ch32-pabook-kdka][^ch32-fcc-radio]
- **The announcer and the words.** KDKA’s first announcer, Leo Rosenberg, opened with a statement the station preserved: “This is KDKA of the Westinghouse Electric and Manufacturing Company in East Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. We shall now broadcast the election returns. We are receiving these returns through the cooperation and by special arrangement with the Pittsburgh Post and Sun.”[^ch32-pabook-kdka]
- **The scale.** The Wednesday-night audience was small. Accounts place the number of listeners at roughly 1,000, most of them amateur operators with receiving sets, though the figure is an estimate rather than a count.[^ch32-pabook-kdka]
- **The larger context of the day.** The broadcast came at the end of a year in which the Nineteenth Amendment had been ratified, the Red Scare had peaked, and the wartime state was being dismantled. It also came less than four years before roughly 600 stations would exist and radio would be a mass medium.[^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-earlyradio]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the station, the license month, the announcer, the opening words, and a rough audience estimate. It cannot show what the room looked like, what Rosenberg or the engineers felt, or what any listener thought, because no such account is cited here.[^ch32-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In November 1920 no one knew that radio would become the shared national medium of the decade, how many stations would be licensed, or that a station owner in Pittsburgh was beginning a cultural transformation. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch32-check-scene][^ch32-check-dates]

### The scene’s argument

- **A mass audience appears.** The scene’s claim is that the 1920s’ central novelty was not a single invention but the arrival of a shared mass audience — people in scattered homes hearing the same voice at the same moment for the first time. Radio made the audience audible to itself.[^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-earlyradio]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question. The same medium that could build a national audience could also carry advertising, propaganda, and a homogenizing popular culture; the same prosperity that built the audience rested on credit, on an agricultural depression, and on an exclusionary immigration regime. Who belonged to that audience, and on what terms?[^ch32-check-scope][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: who belonged to the new mass society?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the Pittsburgh studio to the assembly lines of Detroit, the wheat fields of the Plains, the speakeasies of New York, the Dayton courthouse, the Tulsa neighborhood of Greenwood, and the Harlem of the New Negro.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, room, or dialogue.** The date, the station, the license month, the announcer, the opening words, and the audience estimate are documented. The scene may not stage the room, the equipment, the sounds, or any exchange the record does not contain.[^ch32-check-scene]

## 1. Production, credit, agriculture, and Republican policy

### 1.1 The claim: a prosperity that was real and uneven

- **The claim.** The 1920s produced a genuine and broad increase in output and consumption, but its benefits were distributed unevenly across regions, sectors, and households, so the decade’s prosperity was a fact and a problem at the same time.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the boom as three connected stories: the mass-production industries that made new goods cheap; the credit system that let households buy them; and the agricultural and extractive sectors that did not share the gains. The three belong to one account rather than three separate ones.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]
- **What the record can show.** It can show output, prices, wages, and credit statistics. It cannot show that most households felt prosperous, and the chapter should treat “prosperity” as a claim to be tested, not a settled description.[^ch32-check-silences][^ch32-check-numbers]

### 1.2 Mass production and the automobile

- **The claim.** The automobile industry was the decade’s leading sector and its model of mass production, and its growth reshaped work, geography, and consumption.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- **The method.** Henry Ford’s moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12½ hours in 1912 to about 1½ hours in 1914, and the resulting fall in costs let Ford cut prices repeatedly; the price of a new Ford fell to roughly $290 by the mid-1920s, less than three months’ wages for an average worker. Ford also raised the minimum wage to $5 a day and shortened the workday, on the argument that mass production required mass purchasing power.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- **The market.** Alfred P. Sloan, at General Motors from 1923, shifted competition from price to styling, marketing, and status, introduced the annual model change, and set up a national consumer credit agency in 1919. The contrast between Ford’s standardized product and Sloan’s differentiated one should be named as a change in how goods were sold, not only how they were made.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- **The numbers.** In 1919 there were about 6.7 million cars on American roads; by 1929 there were more than 27 million, roughly one car per household, and one American in five owned a car. In 1929 the auto industry produced about 12.7 percent of all manufacturing output and employed roughly one worker in twelve. The chapter should give the totals with their observation years and note that ownership figures rest on registration and census series.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Consumer credit and its costs

- **The claim.** The boom rested on installment credit, which let households buy goods ahead of their incomes and tied consumption to debt.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The mechanism.** Manufacturers and banks extended installment plans for cars, furniture, phonographs, radios, and appliances; in 1929 about a quarter of American families bought a car, and roughly 60 percent of car buyers used credit, often at interest rates of 30 percent or more. By the decade’s end, over three-quarters of furniture, phonographs, and washing machines were bought on credit.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The infrastructure of abundance.** By 1930, about two-thirds of American households had electricity and about half had telephones, and the automobile boom stimulated steel, glass, rubber, road building, and the new roadside economy. The chapter should present these as the material setting of the mass audience.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- **What the record can show.** It can show credit sales, prices, and ownership. It cannot show that installment buying made households worse off on balance, and the chapter should present the debt as a structural feature that would matter after 1929 without asserting that it caused the Depression.[^ch32-check-ch33][^ch32-check-numbers]

### 1.4 The farm depression, tariffs, and Republican policy

- **The claim.** Republican policy at the federal level encouraged industry and finance while leaving agriculture in a prolonged depression, so the decade’s prosperity was built and defended in a way that deepened a regional and sectoral divide.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-coolidge-mellon]
- **The farm depression.** High wartime prices had led farmers to expand; wheat acreage rose from about 48 million acres to more than 75 million between 1914 and 1919, and Iowa farmland that sold for about $82 an acre in 1910 sold for about $200 in 1920. When European recovery cut demand, American farm exports fell from about $4.1 billion in 1919 to about $1.9 billion in 1922, commodity prices collapsed, and land values fell sharply. The chapter should call the 1920s farm economy a depression inside a boom.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]
- **The political economy.** The Fordney–McCumber Tariff of 1922 and the tax reductions shaped by Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon cut the top marginal income tax rate from 58 percent to 46 percent in 1924 and to 25 percent in 1926. President Calvin Coolidge vetoed the McNary–Haugen farm relief bills, arguing that they would amount to price-fixing and bureaucratic control. The chapter should present the tariffs and tax cuts as a policy choice about who would carry the adjustment, not merely as a neutral recovery.[^ch32-coolidge-mellon][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutory rates, the veto messages, prices, exports, and acreage. It cannot show that the tax cuts caused the boom or that the vetoes by themselves produced the farm depression, and the chapter should distinguish the policy’s intentions from its measured effects.[^ch32-check-numbers][^ch32-check-debate-works]

## 2. Radio, film, literature, jazz, and the Washington conference’s international context

### 2.1 Broadcasting becomes a mass medium

- **The claim.** Radio grew from a few licensed stations into a national medium within a few years, and that growth created both a shared audience and a new regulatory problem.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio]
- **The boom.** After the Department of Commerce formally established a broadcast service in December 1921, stations multiplied rapidly, and by the end of 1922 there were more than 500 broadcast stations across the country. Within about four years of the KDKA broadcast, roughly 600 stations existed. Radio receivers grew from tens of thousands in 1922 to millions by the decade’s end; the chapter should give the receiver and station figures with their sources and years because the series differ.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio]
- **The medium’s character.** Early programming drew on what audiences already knew in theaters and newspapers — opera, orchestral music, vaudeville, news, weather, market prices, and farm updates — and stations like WLS in Chicago, created by Sears in 1924, aimed variety programming at rural listeners. The chapter should present the new medium as a rearrangement of existing culture rather than an entirely new one.[^ch32-americainclass-radio]
- **The regulation.** The Radio Act of February 23, 1927, created the Federal Radio Commission and gave it power to grant and deny licenses and to assign frequencies and power levels, replacing a Commerce Department role that had lacked the authority to refuse licenses. The chapter should present regulation as a response to crowding and interference, not as a coherent design.[^ch32-loc-law-radio][^ch32-fcc-radio]

### 2.2 The In Focus: one dated 1924 radio schedule

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads a single dated 1924 newspaper radio schedule as an artifact of the new audience. A printed daily radio log is not a program guide in the modern sense; its columns of call letters, times, frequencies, and sponsors show how the medium was organized and how it reached into the home. Read closely, the schedule shows a national audience being assembled station by station, hour by hour, and it shows what a listener was expected to want. The box should transcribe the schedule’s date, station, columns, and a few entries exactly, and identify the newspaper, issue, and page from the actual scan. It should treat the page as a commercial and cultural document, citing its source and rights status, not as an illustration of a generic “radio age.” The box should end on the question the chapter asks of every new medium: who was addressed, and who was left out?[^ch32-check-focus][^ch32-check-radio-log][^ch32-check-image5]

The register requires the newspaper, issue, page, crop, and rights before production; the chapter must not claim a specific scan it has not verified.[^ch32-check-image5][^ch32-check-radio-log]

### 2.3 Film, jazz, and the New Negro arts

- **The claim.** Film and recorded music joined radio in creating a national popular culture, and Black artists were central to it, which makes the decade’s culture both homogeneous and Black-made.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The film industry.** Hollywood consolidated into a studio system and, with the premiere of *The Jazz Singer* on October 6, 1927, moved toward synchronized sound; within a few years most Hollywood films had sound. The chapter should present the transition as an industrial and artistic change with winners and losers, including the silent stars whose careers ended.[^ch32-check-films]
- **Jazz.** Jazz became the decade’s signature music, carried on records and radio and associated with the dance halls and nightclubs of the cities, and the chapter should name it as an African American creation that became a national and international idiom rather than as background noise.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The literature.** The New Negro movement produced poetry, fiction, and criticism that insisted on Black self-representation; Alain Locke described a “spiritual coming of age,” and Langston Hughes described an “expression of our individual dark-skinned selves.” The chapter should quote only from identified editions of the primary texts.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]

### 2.4 The Washington Naval Conference and the international context

- **The claim.** The United States did not withdraw from the world after rejecting the Treaty of Versailles; it pursued disarmament and Pacific security through the Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22, which is the decade’s international frame.[^ch32-state-naval][^ch32-nww2-naval]
- **The conference.** Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes invited nine nations to Washington; the talks produced the Five-Power Treaty, the Four-Power Treaty, and the Nine-Power Treaty. The Five-Power Treaty set capital-ship tonnage at 500,000 tons each for the United States and Britain, 300,000 for Japan, and 175,000 each for France and Italy — the 5:5:3 ratio — and called for scrapping older ships and halting new capital-ship construction.[^ch32-state-naval]
- **The limits.** The treaty left cruisers and other classes unrestricted, producing a new competition that led to later conferences, and its Article XIX froze base expansion in the Pacific, which some naval officers saw as a danger to Hawaii, Guam, and the Philippines. The Nine-Power Treaty internationalized the Open Door in China but had no enforcement mechanism. The chapter should present the agreements as a status quo settlement, not a lasting peace.[^ch32-state-naval][^ch32-nww2-naval]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the treaties, the tonnage ratios, and the negotiations. It cannot show that disarmament prevented war, and the chapter should distinguish the settlement’s intentions from its later failure.[^ch32-check-debate-works]

## 3. Prohibition

### 3.1 The amendment and the statute

- **The claim.** Prohibition was the decade’s most ambitious federal attempt to regulate private behavior, and it failed on its own terms, which makes it the clearest test of the limits of moral reform by statute.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]
- **The instruments.** The Eighteenth Amendment prohibited the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors; Congress sent it to the states in 1917, ratification was completed in January 1919, and the National Prohibition Act — the Volstead Act — passed over President Woodrow Wilson’s veto on October 28, 1919, to provide enforcement. Prohibition took effect in January 1920.[^ch32-archives-volstead]
- **The reform coalition.** Temperance societies, the Anti-Saloon League, the Women’s Christian Temperance Union, and Progressive reformers had built the movement over decades, and factory owners supported it as a way to discipline an industrial workforce. The chapter should present Prohibition as a Progressive reform, not an aberration.[^ch32-archives-volstead]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the amendment, the statute, and the coalition. It cannot show that the reformers were hypocritical or that drinkers were defiant by nature, and the chapter should avoid both caricatures.[^ch32-check-prohibition]

### 3.2 Enforcement, evasion, and organized crime

- **The claim.** Prohibition’s enforcement was underfunded and corrupt, and the resulting illicit market produced organized crime and a culture of evasion.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]
- **The effects.** Alcohol consumption fell at first — the Archives lesson reports a drop of about 30 percent in consumption, with hard liquor off about 50 percent — and arrests for drunkenness fell. But bootlegging became rampant, and federal and local authorities would not commit the resources to enforce the law everywhere; in New York City alone, by 1925, there were reported to be anywhere from 30,000 to 100,000 speakeasies. The chapter should present the range of speakeasy estimates as a range.[^ch32-archives-volstead]
- **The corruption.** Prohibition agents were poorly paid and poorly trained, and bribery and corruption were widespread; several major cities saw violent turf wars among organized criminal gangs over the liquor trade. The chapter should connect the corruption to the enforcement design rather than to individual vice alone.[^ch32-crs-prohibition][^ch32-archives-volstead]
- **What the record can show.** It can show consumption statistics, arrest figures, and enforcement budgets. It cannot show that Prohibition increased total drinking, and the chapter should report the contested claims as contested.[^ch32-check-numbers][^ch32-check-prohibition]

### 3.3 The Wickersham findings and the road to repeal

- **The claim.** The federal government’s own commission confirmed by 1931 that Prohibition had failed, and its findings helped set the stage for repeal, which belongs to the early 1930s and to Chapter 33.[^ch32-crs-prohibition]
- **The commission.** President Herbert Hoover appointed the Wickersham Commission, which reported in 1931 that settled habits and customs do not yield readily to legislative fiat, that Americans patronized speakeasies and exploited loopholes for medicinal and sacramental liquor, and that enforcement suffered from inadequate funds and corruption. Though the commission opposed repeal, some members dissented.[^ch32-crs-prohibition]
- **The boundary.** The chapter should note that repeal came only with the Twenty-First Amendment in 1933 and leave its politics to the next chapters. This chapter’s subject is the dry decade and its lessons about the reach of federal moral regulation.[^ch32-crs-prohibition][^ch32-check-ch33]

## 4. Immigration, the Klan, religion, and Native policy

### 4.1 The turn to restriction

- **The claim.** The 1920s ended the era of open European immigration and built a race- and nationality-based quota system that governed entry for four decades.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]
- **The background.** The Immigration Act of 1917 had imposed a literacy test and an “Asiatic Barred Zone,” and wartime hostility to southern and eastern Europeans fed nativism. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 set the first numerical limits, capping immigration at about 350,000 a year and setting quotas at 3 percent of the foreign-born population of each nationality in the 1910 census. President Wilson pocket-vetoed the restrictionist bill, and President Warren Harding called Congress back to pass it.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]
- **The 1924 act.** The Immigration Act of 1924 (the Johnson–Reed Act) lowered the quota to 2 percent and moved the base year to 1890, before the great wave of southern and eastern European immigration, which shifted the quotas toward Britain and northern Europe; it also excluded from entry any alien ineligible for citizenship by race or nationality, ending most Asian immigration. The chapter should distinguish the 1921 formula, the 1924 interim quota, and the later national-origins figures, which is what Table 1 is for.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration][^ch32-check-table1]
- **The consequences.** The State Department distributed visas through embassies, and in 1924 Congress created the Border Patrol in response to illegal entries and smuggling. The chapter should present restriction as a system with an inside and an outside, not merely a set of numbers.[^ch32-uscis-restriction][^ch32-nps-immigration][^ch32-check-immigration]

### 4.3 The second Ku Klux Klan

- **The claim.** The second Ku Klux Klan became a mass movement in the early 1920s, especially in the Midwest, and its rise and fall show that nativism and racial reaction were not confined to the South.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]
- **The movement.** The second Klan spread across the country after 1915, but its recruitment peaked in 1923–24; the state of Indiana was said to have the most powerful Klan organization in the nation, with about 250,000 members under Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson. The Klan’s appeal mixed racism, antisemitism, and, above all, anti-Catholicism, and in the Midwest it organized on a county-unit basis with political power as its goal.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson][^ch32-in-gov-klan]
- **The decline.** Membership fell sharply after 1925 for several connected reasons: the Klan failed to deliver good government, Prohibition failed, federal legislation restricted immigration, and prominent leaders were exposed as corrupt — above all the conviction of Stephenson in November 1925 for his role in the death of Madge Oberholtzer. The chapter should present the decline as the result of the Klan’s own conduct as much as outside opposition.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]
- **What the record can show.** It can show membership estimates, klavern locations, and the leadership scandal. It cannot give an exact national membership total, and the chapter should present the figures as estimates tied to the sources that compiled them.[^ch32-check-numbers][^ch32-check-race]

### 4.4 Religion, fundamentalism, and the Scopes trial

- **The claim.** The decade’s religious conflict was a struggle over who would define American culture, and the Scopes trial of 1925 made that struggle a national spectacle.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The statute and the case.** Tennessee’s Butler Act made it unlawful to teach any theory denying the biblical account of creation and to teach instead that humans descended from a lower order of animals. John T. Scopes, a young science teacher, agreed to stand as the defendant in a test case; Clarence Darrow joined the defense and William Jennings Bryan the prosecution. The trial ran from July 10 to July 21, 1925; Scopes was convicted and fined, and the state supreme court later set the conviction aside on a technicality.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-umn-darrow-scopes]
- **The stakes.** The trial became a contest between modernism and fundamentalism in the press as much as in the courtroom, and the chapter should present both sides’ arguments fairly; Bryan’s concerns included the use of Darwinian ideas to support eugenics, and the defense argued for academic and intellectual freedom. The chapter should not reduce the case to a simple verdict about science versus religion.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-check-debate-works]
- **Nativism and the courts.** The execution of Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti on August 23, 1927, after a trial widely believed to have been influenced by hostility to their immigrant and anarchist backgrounds, belongs in the same section as the evidence that the decade’s nativism ran through the legal system as well as the culture. The chapter should present the debate over their guilt as unresolved and describe the protest the case provoked.[^ch32-mass-sacco][^ch32-gilder-sacco]

### 4.5 Federal Native policy and citizenship

- **The claim.** Federal policy toward Native nations in the 1920s combined a formal grant of citizenship with the continuation of allotment and assimilation, and the decade’s own survey documented the damage that policy had done.[^ch32-narf-citizenship][^ch32-narf-meriam]
- **The citizenship act.** The Indian Citizenship Act, also called the Snyder Act, was signed by President Calvin Coolidge on June 2, 1924, and conferred United States citizenship on Native Americans born within the United States. It did not grant voting rights, which the states continued to deny on grounds of reservation residence, tribal enrollment, taxation, or claimed incompetency, and many Native people and nations saw the act as an assimilationist measure that did not diminish tribal sovereignty or the treaty relationship.[^ch32-narf-citizenship][^ch32-bia-snyder]
- **The Meriam report.** Completed in 1928, *The Problem of Indian Administration*, known as the Meriam Report, found that allotment had been a disaster, that the income and health of Native families were poor, and that government administration was underfunded and harmful. Its findings set the stage for the reform of the 1930s, which belongs to later chapters.[^ch32-narf-meriam][^ch32-loc-indiancitizenship]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, the report’s findings, and the states’ voting restrictions. It cannot show what Native communities uniformly wanted, and the chapter should name the diversity of tribal responses rather than speak for a single Native voice.[^ch32-check-native][^ch32-check-silences]

## 5. The Harlem Renaissance and Black politics

### 5.1 Harlem and the Great Migration

- **The claim.** The Great Migration made Harlem and other northern cities Black cultural capitals, and the Harlem Renaissance was both an artistic movement and a political assertion.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-archives-greatmigration]
- **The movement.** The Great Migration moved roughly six million Black people from the South to the North, Midwest, and West between 1910 and 1970; the first wave, from 1910 to 1940, carried about two million people to cities such as New York, Chicago, Detroit, and Pittsburgh. Harlem, covering a few square miles of Manhattan, drew nearly 175,000 African Americans and became the largest concentration of Black people in the world. The chapter should give the migration figures with their periods and note that the census series differ.[^ch32-archives-greatmigration][^ch32-census-greatmigration][^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]
- **Map 25.** The registered visual `ch32-map-01` (Map 25, “The Great Migration, 1910–1930”) belongs in this section. It must show origin regions, destination cities, and city growth for its stated dates, using the 1910 and 1930 censuses and Appendix B, Table 1, and it must distinguish the movement’s volume from any claim about migrants’ motives.[^ch32-check-map]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the census totals and destinations. It cannot show migrants’ reasons from statistics alone, and the chapter should turn to letters, oral histories, and the Black press for that, naming the silence where the record runs out.[^ch32-check-silences]

### 5.2 The New Negro and the Renaissance

- **The claim.** The Harlem Renaissance produced a self-conscious literature and art that challenged racist stereotypes and redefined Black identity for a national and international audience.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]
- **The writers and artists.** Among the movement’s central figures were the critics and intellectuals W. E. B. Du Bois, Alain Locke, and Marcus Garvey; the writers and poets Zora Neale Hurston, Langston Hughes, and Countee Cullen; the visual artists Aaron Douglas and Augusta Savage; and musicians including Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington. Hughes described the movement’s aim as an “expression of our individual dark-skinned selves,” and Locke called it a “spiritual coming of age.” The chapter should quote them from identified editions.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]
- **The reach.** Harlem’s publishing houses, newspapers, music companies, and nightclubs made the neighborhood the epicenter of American culture, and the movement’s art circulated nationally and abroad. The chapter should present the Renaissance as Black-made culture that changed the mainstream, not as a separate sphere.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the works, the institutions, and the audiences. It cannot show that the Renaissance reached or represented all Black Americans, and the chapter should note its class and urban character as a limitation, not a dismissal.[^ch32-check-race][^ch32-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 Garvey, Black politics, and the Tulsa massacre

- **The claim.** Black politics in the 1920s ranged from the NAACP’s legal and legislative work to Marcus Garvey’s mass nationalism, and the decade also brought one of the worst racial massacres in American history, which the movement’s optimism did not prevent.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-tulsahistory]
- **Garvey.** Marcus Garvey, a Jamaican-born organizer, founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association, which combined Black nationalism, economic self-help, and a back-to-Africa program and drew a mass following in the early 1920s. The chapter should present Garvey as a major political figure alongside the integrationist organizations, not as a curiosity.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-check-race]
- **Tulsa.** On May 31 and June 1, 1921, a white mob destroyed the Greenwood district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, the prosperous Black neighborhood known as “Black Wall Street.” The 2001 Race Riot Commission found that 35 city blocks were destroyed, that credible estimates of the dead range from about 100 to 300, that more than 800 people were treated for injuries, and that more than 6,000 Black residents were detained, and that no one was ever prosecuted. The chapter should present the death toll as a range and name the commission as the source.[^ch32-tulsahistory][^ch32-check-numbers]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the organizations and the massacre’s documented course. It cannot show exactly how many died, and the chapter should explain why the count is uncertain and why the event was long misnamed.[^ch32-tulsahistory][^ch32-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The prosperity was real and uneven.** Output, consumption, and ownership grew substantially, but agriculture remained depressed and the gains were concentrated in industry and the cities.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]
- **Mass communication created a national audience.** Radio grew from a few stations to hundreds within a few years, and film and recorded music joined it in a shared popular culture.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio]
- **Immigration restriction was racial and national.** The 1921 and 1924 acts built a quota system designed to preserve an imagined homogeneity and to exclude Asians.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]
- **The second Klan was a mass movement.** It spread across the North and Midwest, peaked in the early 1920s, and declined after corruption and scandal.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]
- **Black culture and politics changed the mainstream.** The Harlem Renaissance produced lasting art, and Black politics ranged from legal work to mass nationalism.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-hughes-negro-artist]
- **The decade ended in mounting instability.** The boom rested on credit and an unequal economy, and the collapse that followed belongs to the next chapter.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-check-ch33]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the decade a culture war or a political economy?** One position treats the 1920s as a contest between modernism and tradition; another treats it as a distinctive postwar period defined by reaction, restriction, and political economy. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-goldberg-discontented][^ch32-dumenil-modern-temper]
- **Did mass culture liberate or homogenize?** One account stresses the new freedoms and shared experiences of the mass audience; another stresses standardization, advertising, and the narrowing of local cultures. The chapter should set the evidence for both side by side.[^ch32-dumenil-modern-temper][^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- **How central is race to the decade?** One account treats race and nativism as central to the decade’s politics; another treats them as one theme among several. The chapter should mark the question as open and weigh the evidence.[^ch32-higham-strangers][^ch32-nmaahc-harlem]
- **Was the Harlem Renaissance a movement or a moment?** One position stresses its lasting artistic and political impact; another stresses its small, urban, and patronage-dependent base. The chapter should present both.[^ch32-huggins-harlem][^ch32-lewis-when-harlem]
- **Did prohibition fail, or did it change behavior?** One account stresses the law’s failure and the rise of organized crime; another stresses the real decline in consumption and the long temperance legacy. The chapter should report both.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the 1920s simultaneously created a national mass culture and deepened the exclusions that determined who could join it. The chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-dumenil-modern-temper]
- **Modernity and reaction together.** The chapter’s synthesis should refuse the choice between a “roaring” decade and a reactionary one: the same years that produced jazz, radio, and the New Negro produced the quota acts, the second Klan, and the Scopes trial. That coexistence is the chapter’s contribution to the book’s argument about settlements that change the terms of conflict without settling them.[^ch32-goldberg-discontented][^ch32-gilder-zeitz]
- **The audience and its exclusions.** The chapter’s synthesis should connect the mass audience to the immigration regime and the color line, because the new national audience was being defined at the same moment its boundaries were being policed. The “who belonged” question is answered partly by the cultural record and partly by the statute books.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-check-race]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that the decade’s deepest question was not whether the country was prosperous but who was included in the prosperity and the culture it produced, and that the record supports an answer of expanding audience and narrowing membership at once.[^ch32-gilder-zeitz][^ch32-check-table2]

## Legacies

The decade’s prosperity left a double legacy. Mass production and installment credit created a consumer economy and a national market, but they also tied household finances to debt and left agriculture behind, and the fragility they built would become visible after 1929.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]

The immigration regime outlasted the decade. The quota system of 1921 and 1924 governed American admissions for four decades, and it shaped the country’s demography and its relationship with Asia and Europe until the Immigration Act of 1965, which belongs to later chapters.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-nps-immigration]

The mass audience outlasted the decade. Radio, film, and recorded music built a shared national culture that would carry the New Deal’s fireside chats and the politics of the 1930s, and the federal broadcast regulation created in 1927 became the institutional foundation of that medium.[^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-loc-law-radio]

The Harlem Renaissance’s assertion outlasted the decade. The New Negro’s insistence on self-representation and the political organizing it fed became part of the foundation of the civil rights movement, even as the Tulsa massacre and the quota acts showed how violent and how official the resistance to full membership remained.[^ch32-nmaahc-harlem][^ch32-tulsahistory]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The Washington Naval Conference, the quota acts, the New Negro’s diaspora networks, and the Mexican and Caribbean migration that the quota system left unrestricted all connect the decade to a wider world. The chapter should place these connections in notes rather than claim that events abroad caused American decisions.[^ch32-state-naval][^ch32-edwards-diaspora]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents the prosperous, the broadcast, the official, and the documented and under-represents the rural poor, the immigrant, the migrant, and the Black southerners whose lives the decade remade. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch32-check-silences][^ch32-check-race]

## Summary

- The 1920s produced real growth in output and consumption, but the gains were uneven and agriculture remained depressed throughout the decade.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer][^ch32-coolidge-mcnary]
- Henry Ford’s assembly line cut car assembly time from about 12½ hours to about 1½ hours, and the number of cars on American roads rose from about 6.7 million in 1919 to more than 27 million in 1929.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- Installment credit let households buy cars and appliances ahead of their incomes, with about 60 percent of 1929 car buyers using credit at rates often above 30 percent.[^ch32-digitalhistory-consumer]
- Farm exports fell from about $4.1 billion in 1919 to about $1.9 billion in 1922; Coolidge vetoed the McNary–Haugen relief bills, and the Mellon tax programs cut the top income tax rate to 25 percent by 1926.[^ch32-coolidge-mcnary][^ch32-coolidge-mellon]
- Radio grew from a few licensed stations to more than 500 by the end of 1922 and about 600 within four years of the KDKA broadcast, and the Radio Act of 1927 created the Federal Radio Commission.[^ch32-earlyradio][^ch32-fcc-radio][^ch32-loc-law-radio]
- The Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22 produced the Five-Power, Four-Power, and Nine-Power Treaties, including the 5:5:3 capital-ship ratio.[^ch32-state-naval]
- Prohibition took effect in January 1920, consumption fell at first, but speakeasies, bootlegging, and corruption spread, and the Wickersham Commission confirmed in 1931 that the law had failed.[^ch32-archives-volstead][^ch32-crs-prohibition]
- The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Johnson–Reed Act of 1924 built a racial and national quota system, and Congress created the Border Patrol in 1924.[^ch32-state-immigration][^ch32-uscis-restriction]
- The second Ku Klux Klan peaked in 1923–24, with about 250,000 members in Indiana under D. C. Stephenson, and declined after his 1925 conviction.[^ch32-vcu-klan][^ch32-indy-stephenson]
- The Scopes trial of July 1925 made the fundamentalist–modernist conflict a national spectacle, and Sacco and Vanzetti were executed on August 23, 1927.[^ch32-famous-trials-scopes][^ch32-mass-sacco]
- The Indian Citizenship Act of June 2, 1924, granted Native Americans citizenship without guaranteeing the vote, and the 1928 Meriam Report documented the damage of allotment.[^ch32-narf-citizenship][^ch32-narf-meriam]
- The Great Migration carried about two million Black southerners north between 1910 and 1940, and the Tulsa massacre of May 31–June 1, 1921, destroyed Greenwood and killed an estimated 100 to 300 people.[^ch32-archives-greatmigration][^ch32-tulsahistory]

> *A closing note.* Between 1920 and 1929 the United States built a consumer economy and a national audience, restricted immigration by race and nationality, banned alcohol and failed to enforce the ban, put the second Ku Klux Klan into the mainstream and then discredited it, made a fundamentalist schoolteacher’s trial a national event, and produced a Black cultural movement that redrew the boundaries of American art.
