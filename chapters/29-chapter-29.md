## Epigraphs

> “I speak for neither the operators nor the miners but for the general public.”[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

— President Theodore Roosevelt, opening address to the anthracite coal conference, Washington, D.C., October 3, 1902, Theodore Roosevelt Papers, Library of Congress, as quoted in Jonathan Grossman, *The Coal Strike of 1902: Turning Point in U.S. Policy*, 1974.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

> “It is time to set the public welfare in the first place.”[^ch29-progressive-platform]

— Progressive Party Platform, adopted at Chicago, August 1912.[^ch29-progressive-platform]

> “The Progressive Era is more than a matter of nostalgia. It is the argument of this book that progressivism created much of our contemporary predicament.”[^ch29-mcgerr]

— Michael McGerr, *A Fierce Discontent: The Rise and Fall of the Progressive Movement in America, 1870–1920*, 2003, Preface.[^ch29-mcgerr]

- **Why these three.** A president claimed to speak for a third party above capital and labor. A new party claimed that the public welfare outranked private interest. A historian claimed that the reform impulse left a difficult inheritance. The three disagree about whom the enlarged authority actually served, which is the chapter’s central question.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-mcgerr]
- **The attribution rule.** Roosevelt’s sentence is quoted from the Department of Labor’s history, which cites the Roosevelt Papers; it must be collated against the opening-address text in the Library of Congress before quotation. The platform sentence must be checked against the printed proceedings of the 1912 convention. - **The historian’s voice.** McGerr supplies the required historian’s voice and frames the chapter’s debate about what the reform movement achieved and cost. Alternatives if his sentence cannot be verified are the “status anxiety” position of Richard Hofstadter’s *The Age of Reform* or the “organizational synthesis” of Robert Wiebe’s *The Search for Order*, both of which are named in the debate.[^ch29-mcgerr][^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe][^ch29-check-epigraphs]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the summons.** On Friday, October 3, 1902, Roosevelt convened operators and union officials at the temporary White House at 22 Lafayette Place in Washington, D.C. A strike in the anthracite coalfields of Pennsylvania, begun on May 12, 1902, threatened a coal famine. Roosevelt had no clear legal authority to intervene, but he sent telegrams summoning both sides.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- **The president’s condition.** Roosevelt, injured a month earlier when a carriage in which he was riding was struck by a trolley car, received the parties while seated in a wheelchair.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- **Who was present.** Roosevelt’s account names Attorney General Philander Knox, Commissioner of Labor Carroll D. Wright, and Secretary George B. Cortelyou. The union was represented by John Mitchell, president of the United Mine Workers of America; the operators by figures including George F. Baer.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- **The appeal and the refusal.** Roosevelt disclaimed any right to intervene but asked for an immediate resumption of mining to meet “the crying needs of the people” and appealed to the contestants’ patriotism. Mitchell, for whom the summons itself implied recognition, was conciliatory; the operators were, in Roosevelt’s words, “insolent,” berated Mitchell, and refused to deal with the union.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- **The deadlock and its resolution.** Roosevelt told Senator Marcus Hanna that evening that he had tried and failed. Secretary of War Elihu Root and the banker J. P. Morgan then brokered an arbitration formula, and the 163-day strike ended on October 23, 1902, with an Anthracite Coal Strike Commission. The commission awarded most miners a 10 percent increase and a nine-hour day. Of roughly 147,000 strikers, thousands left the region.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, place, participants, the text of Roosevelt’s address, the operators’ refusal, the arbitration, and the award. It cannot show the room’s full atmosphere, the exact words of unrecorded exchanges, or the private calculations of each participant.[^ch29-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In October 1902 no one knew the commission’s award, that the federal government would return as an arbiter in later disputes, or that the episode would be read as the birth of a reform presidency. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch29-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **The state as broker of a third interest.** The scene’s claim is that Roosevelt made the federal government a party to a private industrial conflict in the name of “the general public,” and that this widening of federal authority was real but did not make the government neutral between the parties.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-check-scene]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question. The presidents before Roosevelt had sent troops to break strikes; Roosevelt summoned both sides to his own house. The same authority that could arbitrate in the public interest could also segregate a federal office, bar an immigrant at Angel Island, or send troops to a Colorado coalfield.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-nps-ludlow]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: who benefited when reformers enlarged public authority?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the coal conference to the state houses, the magazines, the settlement houses, the forests, and the ballot box of the same years.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, room, or dialogue.** The date, place, and participants are documented, and some sentences survive in Roosevelt’s own account. The scene may not stage the room’s arrangement, the sound of the meeting, or any exchange the record does not contain.[^ch29-check-scene]
- **No interior thought.** The private reasoning of Mitchell, Baer, Root, or Morgan must not be staged beyond what their letters and testimony state.[^ch29-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the commission’s award, of the federal government’s later role in labor disputes, or of the reform era’s name.[^ch29-check-scene][^ch29-check-dates]

## 1. Reformers and state and local experiments

### 1.1 Who were the Progressives?

- **The claim.** “Progressive” named no single movement but a coalition of overlapping constituencies — a new professional middle class, urban and immigrant working people, organized women, and Black reformers — who agreed that government should address the dislocations of industrial society and disagreed about whom it should serve.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-yawp-progressive]
- **The middle-class core.** The most visible Progressives were educated professionals — lawyers, journalists, physicians, social workers, and academics — tied to the emerging national economy and drawn to expert administration. Historians have described this group, following Robert Wiebe, as a “new middle class” seeking to stabilize a disordered society by building national institutions.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe]
- **The working-class and urban wing.** Other historians have located much of the reform energy in working-class and immigrant communities and in urban political machines, whose members pressed for workmen’s compensation and wage-and-hour laws. The two readings disagree less about the facts than about who drove reform.[^ch29-glencoe-historians]
- **What the record can show.** It can show who joined which organization and what each demanded. It cannot reduce the coalition to a single motive, and the chapter should present the constituencies as overlapping rather than as one movement with one interest.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-check-silences]

### 1.2 The Wisconsin Idea and the machinery of direct democracy

- **The claim.** The most durable early Progressive achievement was procedural: reformers rewrote the rules of representation to weaken party machines and give voters more direct control, and in doing so they redefined who counted as a competent citizen.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots][^ch29-progressive-platform]
- **La Follette and the Wisconsin Idea.** Robert M. La Follette, elected governor of Wisconsin in 1900 and later a senator, built a program of state regulation staffed by university experts, the arrangement later called the Wisconsin Idea. He pressed for direct primaries, campaign-spending limits, and a state railroad commission.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots]
- **The instruments.** The direct primary let party members choose candidates rather than conventions (South Carolina for statewide elections in 1896; Florida for presidential nominations in 1901). The initiative let voters propose laws (South Dakota, 1898; 20 states by 1920). The referendum let them approve or reject laws. The recall let them remove officials (Oregon, 1910; 12 states by 1920).[^ch29-openstax-grassroots]
- **The Seventeenth Amendment.** The 1912 Progressive platform called for direct election of United States senators, and the Seventeenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, provided it. The chapter should present the amendment as the completion of a state-level campaign.[^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-check-dates]
- **The limit.** The same years that widened the franchise through these devices also saw the completion of Black disfranchisement in the South and the use of at-large and commission systems to dilute the votes of immigrant and working-class city dwellers. The chapter should hold the democratizing and the narrowing together rather than treat the machinery as simply inclusive.[^ch29-check-race][^ch29-check-scope]

### 1.3 Municipal reform, efficiency, and the managed city

- **The claim.** City government was a principal Progressive laboratory, and its reforms substituted trained administration for elected patronage — a change that promised efficiency and often reduced the influence of the very neighborhoods it served.[^ch29-check-race][^ch29-check-silences]
- **The forms.** After a hurricane flooded Galveston, Texas, in 1900, the city replaced its commission with a board of commissioners, and the commission form spread. Beginning with Staunton, Virginia, in 1908, cities appointed a professional city manager to run daily administration under an elected council.[^ch29-check-lochner]
- **The efficiency ideal.** Reformers argued that skilled administrators, not politicians, should make policy, and they borrowed the language of business management for city government. The chapter should present that ideal as a claim about expertise, not a neutral description, and should show what it displaced.[^ch29-check-silences]
- **The exclusion.** At-large elections and nonpartisan ballots, adopted with the manager and commission plans, weakened ward representation, and in the South they worked alongside disfranchisement. The chapter should name who lost influence as administration was insulated from politics.[^ch29-check-race]

### 1.4 The courts and the boundaries of the police power

- **The claim.** State and federal courts set the outer limit of reform, and their reasoning sorted workers by gender and by the kind of labor the law could touch.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-check-lochner]
- **Lochner and liberty of contract.** In *Lochner v. New York* (1905) the Supreme Court struck down a New York law limiting bakery workers to a ten-hour day, holding that the law interfered with the liberty of contract protected by the Fourteenth Amendment without a sufficient health justification. The decision set the pattern for the era’s labor cases.[^ch29-check-lochner]
- **Muller and protective legislation.** In *Muller v. Oregon* (1908) the Court upheld Oregon’s ten-hour law for women in laundries, reasoning that women’s “physical structure and the performance of maternal functions” placed them at a disadvantage and justified a difference in legislation. The decision protected some women workers by treating them as a class apart, a logic later used against sex-based equality.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-check-gender]
- **The Brandeis brief.** The Oregon case was argued partly through social and medical evidence compiled by Florence Kelley and Josephine Goldmark for the National Consumers League and filed by Louis Brandeis — the “Brandeis brief” — which presented the facts of women’s working lives rather than only legal precedent.[^ch29-check-brandeis]
- **The consequence.** The courts permitted protective laws for women and children while invalidating general hours laws for men, so reform advanced along the lines the judiciary allowed and hardened a gendered divide in labor law.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-check-gender]

## 2. Muckraking, settlements, and workplace safety

### 2.1 The magazines and the exposure of corruption

- **The claim.** The mass magazines made reform a national subject by treating corruption and corporate power as scandals, and their exposés moved readers and legislatures even when the reporting overstated its case.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-loc-muckrakers]
- **The magazine boom.** In October 1902 *McClure’s Magazine* began Lincoln Steffens’s series on municipal corruption, and in January 1903 it carried Steffens’s Minneapolis installment alongside the opening of Ida Tarbell’s series on Standard Oil. Circulation rose, and other publishers commissioned their own exposés.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]
- **The roster.** Tarbell’s *History of the Standard Oil Company* (1902–04) examined the monopoly; Steffens’s *The Shame of the Cities* (1902–03) dissected urban machines; Ray Stannard Baker’s *Following the Color Line* (1907–08) documented segregation; David Graham Phillips’s “The Treason of the Senate” (1906) attacked senators’ ties to corporations; and Samuel Hopkins Adams’s “The Great American Fraud” (1905–06) exposed patent medicines.[^ch29-loc-muckrakers]
- **The name.** Roosevelt coined “muckraker” in a speech of April 14, 1906, drawing on Bunyan’s man with the muck-rake; he meant it as a rebuke to those who saw only filth, but many journalists adopted it as a badge of honor.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-loc-muckrakers]
- **What the magazines can show.** They can show what editors chose to print and what readers bought. They cannot show that a given exposé caused a given law, and the chapter should present the magazines as an agenda-setting force without claiming they manufactured reform by themselves.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-check-silences]

### 2.2 *The Jungle*, pure food, and the meat inspection acts

- **The claim.** Upton Sinclair wrote *The Jungle* to build support for socialism and instead produced federal regulation of food, which shows how the era’s reform could serve ends its authors did not intend.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]
- **The book.** Sinclair, a socialist, investigated Chicago’s Packingtown for the *Appeal to Reason* in 1904 and published the novel on January 25, 1906. It followed a Lithuanian immigrant family through the stockyards and described unsanitary meat and exploited labor in detail.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-yawp-progressive]
- **Roosevelt’s investigation and the laws.** Roosevelt, at first annoyed by the book, ordered an investigation that confirmed unclean methods. After the Senate passed a pure-food bill, the House wrangled until Roosevelt released the report. He signed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act on June 30, 1906.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]
- **The wry outcome.** Sinclair later remarked that he had aimed at the public’s heart and by accident hit it in the stomach, a sentence that captures how reform reached consumers more readily than it reached workers.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-check-quotations]
- **The meaning for the chapter.** The episode shows that public authority could be enlisted against corporate abuse when voters were aroused as consumers, and that the same authority was slower to protect the workers who produced the goods.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-check-race]

### 2.3 Settlements, Hull-House, and social work

- **The claim.** The social settlements put reformers into the neighborhoods that industry had built, and the work they did — kindergartens, clinics, classes, and investigations — became the evidence base for later law.[^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-check-gender]
- **Hull-House.** In 1889 Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr moved into a residence on Chicago’s Near West Side and opened Hull-House, which grew into the country’s most influential settlement. They lived “with” rather than “for” their immigrant neighbors and built an employment bureau, an art gallery, libraries, English and citizenship classes, and a theater.[^ch29-hullhouse]
- **The network.** Hull-House residents such as Florence Kelley carried what they found into law through organizations like the National Consumers League and the factory committees of several states. Frances Perkins, who worked with the factory investigations in New York, later applied their methods as Secretary of Labor.[^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-dol-fic]
- **What the settlements can show.** They can show the conditions investigators documented and the programs they built. They cannot show that their neighbors shared the reformers’ goals, and the chapter should treat the settlement as an encounter between unequal parties, not a one-way gift.[^ch29-hullhouse][^ch29-check-silences]

### 2.4 The Triangle fire and the In Focus

- **The claim.** The Triangle Shirtwaist fire of March 25, 1911, killed the workers whose strike for safety had failed the year before, and the disaster turned a documented workplace danger into law.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-cornell-triangle]
- **The fire.** Fire broke out late on a Saturday afternoon in the workrooms of the Triangle Waist Company, which occupied the eighth, ninth, and tenth floors of the Asch Building near Washington Square. The exits were inadequate and the stairways and fire escape failed to hold the crowd; the fire department’s ladders reached only the seventh floor, and workers at the windows jumped.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-dol-fic]
- **The dead and the aftermath.** The Factory Investigating Commission’s preliminary report states that 145 employees, mainly women and girls, lost their lives; other accounts give 146. The owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris, were tried on manslaughter charges and acquitted.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-yawp-progressive]
- **The In Focus.** The chapter’s close reading is the fire escape itself, read through the Factory Investigating Commission’s report and the inspection testimony rather than through undocumented dimensions or actions, and it should state the death count as a documented range.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-triangle-testimony][^ch29-check-focus]
- **What the record can show and cannot.** The report and the testimony establish the building’s condition, the exits, and the failure of the escape. They do not establish what each worker saw or did, and the box must not supply those details.[^ch29-check-focus][^ch29-check-silences]

### 2.5 The Factory Investigating Commission and the safety laws

- **The claim.** The fire produced one of the most thorough state investigations of the era and a body of safety and health law that rebuilt the state’s labor code.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-fic-preliminary]
- **The commission.** The New York legislature created the Factory Investigating Commission by Chapter 561 of the Laws of 1911, signed June 30, 1911, and seated a commission that included Senator Robert F. Wagner, Assemblyman Alfred E. Smith, Samuel Gompers, and Mary E. Dreier. It was charged to investigate factory conditions and recommend remedial law, and it took testimony from fire officials and labor inspectors about the Asch Building.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-triangle-testimony]
- **The laws.** The commission’s work led the legislature to adopt workmen’s compensation and to revise much of the state’s occupational safety and health law. Its methods became a model for other states.[^ch29-dol-fic]
- **The limit.** The reforms that followed regulated the conditions investigators could document; they did not give the workers a share of authority over the workplace, and the owners were acquitted. The chapter should close the section on that gap.[^ch29-yawp-progressive][^ch29-check-silences]

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads the Triangle fire escape as a document of reform’s reach and its limits. The Factory Investigating Commission’s Preliminary Report states that the fire of March 25, 1911, killed 145 employees and that a superficial examination revealed inadequate fire-escape facilities and “conditions in factories and manufacturing establishments that constituted a daily menace to the lives of the thousands of working men, women and children.” The first public hearing, on October 10, 1911, took testimony from Fire Chief Edward F. Croker, Labor Department inspector G. I. Harmon, and Fire Marshal William L. Beers on the Asch Building and the escape. Read together, the report and the testimony let the box show what a fire escape was supposed to do, how this one failed, and how the failure became evidence in a legislative investigation. The box must quote the report and testimony at their exact locators, give the death count as a documented range, and treat the escape as a mechanism whose condition was inspected and recorded — not as a stage for reconstructed action.[^ch29-check-numbers] The box should end on the question the chapter asks of every reform: the investigation documented the danger, but who gained the power to prevent the next one?[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-triangle-testimony][^ch29-cornell-triangle][^ch29-check-focus]

## 3. Roosevelt’s Square Deal and conservation

### 3.1 The Square Deal and the coal strike

- **The claim.** Roosevelt’s handling of the 1902 coal strike made the federal government a broker in industrial disputes, and the “Square Deal” named his promise to treat capital, labor, and the public evenhandedly — a promise that enlarged presidential power.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]
- **The settlement.** After the October 3 conference failed, Root and Morgan arranged arbitration, and the strike ended on October 23 with a commission that awarded most miners a 10 percent increase and a nine-hour day while refusing to recognize the union. Mitchell claimed de facto recognition through the arbitration board the award created.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- **The new role.** The episode marked the federal government’s turn from strikebreaker to peacemaker, and Roosevelt argued that “the public” had an interest in the dispute. The chapter should present that interest as a claim the president asserted, not a settled fact.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]
- **The limit.** The operators kept their property and refused recognition; the government arbitrated terms but did not take the mines. The chapter should show how much of the “Square Deal” consisted of presidential persuasion rather than statutory power.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]

### 3.2 Trusts, railroads, and the reach of federal regulation

- **The claim.** Roosevelt used the antitrust law and railroad regulation to assert federal supervision over the corporations that the states could not control alone.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]
- **Northern Securities.** In 1902 the Justice Department sued the Northern Securities Company, a railroad combination assembled by J. P. Morgan and others, under the Sherman Antitrust Act, and in 1904 the Supreme Court ordered it dissolved — a signal that a Republican administration would regulate corporate combinations.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]
- **The railroad acts.** The Elkins Act of 1903 ended the rebate system by which large shippers won lower rates, and the Hepburn Act of 1906 strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission’s power to set rates. Together they gave the federal government a lasting instrument over interstate carriers.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-check-dates]
- **The bargain.** Roosevelt believed big business was a permanent feature of the economy and sought to regulate it rather than break it up, saying he acted to forestall socialism. The chapter should present regulation as both a curb on corporate power and a stabilization of it.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic]

### 3.3 Conservation and the public domain

- **The claim.** Roosevelt’s conservation program withdrew vast lands from private disposal and put them under federal management for sustained use — an enlargement of federal authority over the West and its resources.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-usbr-reclamation]
- **Reclamation.** The Reclamation Act of June 17, 1902, funded irrigation works from the sale of federal lands and required water users to repay construction costs; the Reclamation Service was created within the Geological Survey in July 1902. Its advocates argued that irrigation would make homes on family farms in the arid West.[^ch29-usbr-reclamation]
- **Forests and monuments.** Roosevelt created the present United States Forest Service and established 150 national forests, 51 federal bird reserves, four national game preserves, five national parks, and 18 national monuments. The Antiquities Act of June 8, 1906, let him designate monuments without Congress, which is how he protected the Grand Canyon. In all he set aside roughly 230 million acres.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation]
- **The idea.** Conservation meant managing resources for continued use, not leaving nature untouched; Roosevelt reserved the forests for use under federal supervision. The chapter should present the program as an exercise of state power over land and water.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation]

### 3.4 The cost of conservation

- **The claim.** The same program that preserved forests and canyons also displaced the people who lived on the reserved lands and reorganized the West around federal authority, so conservation must be judged by who gained and who lost access.[^ch29-check-conservation][^ch29-check-race]
- **The displacement.** Federal forest and park reservations often overlapped Native homelands and the grazing and hunting grounds of rural communities, and the new rules restricted their use. The chapter’s claim that conservation could coexist with displacement still needs its own citation.[^ch29-check-conservation]
- **The split within conservation.** Preservationists who wanted nature protected from use, such as John Muir, and conservationists who wanted it managed for use, such as Gifford Pinchot, disagreed about the purpose of the public lands, and their dispute would divide the movement in the years after Roosevelt left office.[^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-check-dates]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the acreage, the proclamations, and the rules. It cannot show that the people removed from the reserves consented or that the program’s benefits reached them, and the chapter should name that asymmetry.[^ch29-check-conservation][^ch29-check-silences]

## 4. Taft, 1912, and Wilson’s New Freedom

### 4.1 Taft and the rupture over conservation

- **The claim.** William Howard Taft, Roosevelt’s chosen successor, continued antitrust suits but alienated the conservationists, and the resulting split opened a path between the Republican factions.[^ch29-check-dates]
- **The Ballinger–Pinchot affair.** In 1909 Interior Secretary Richard Ballinger was accused by a subordinate, Louis Glavis, of opening Alaskan coal lands to private claimants; Gifford Pinchot, the chief forester, pressed the charge. Taft sided with Ballinger and dismissed Pinchot in January 1910, and the affair convinced Roosevelt’s allies that Taft had abandoned conservation.[^ch29-check-dates]
- **The tariff.** Taft signed the Payne–Aldrich tariff of 1909 after promising revision, and the high rates disappointed reformers in his own party. The chapter should present the tariff and the conservation fight together as the sources of the Republican split.[^ch29-check-dates]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the charges, the dismissal, and the tariff. The dispute over Ballinger’s intent remains contested, and the chapter should present the accusation as a charge rather than a settled fact.[^ch29-check-sources]

### 4.2 The Republican split and the Progressive Party

- **The claim.** Roosevelt returned to politics with a program of national regulation stronger than his earlier one, and when the Republicans renominated Taft in 1912 he left to lead a new party.[^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-check-dates]
- **The New Nationalism.** In a speech at Osawatomie, Kansas, in 1910, Roosevelt called for federal regulation of corporations and a stronger national government, the doctrine he named the New Nationalism. It extended his earlier trust regulation and separated him from Taft.[^ch29-check-dates]
- **The Progressive Party.** In August 1912 the new Progressive Party — the “Bull Moose” party — nominated Roosevelt and adopted a platform calling to “set the public welfare in the first place,” and pledging direct primaries, direct election of senators, the initiative, referendum, and recall, and equal suffrage.[^ch29-progressive-platform]
- **The fracture.** The Republican split divided the party’s reform wing from its conservative leadership and shaped the outcome of the election. The chapter should present the split as a contingent choice, not an inevitability.[^ch29-progressive-platform][^ch29-app-1912]

### 4.3 The election of 1912

- **The claim.** The election gave the Democrats the White House with less than half the popular vote because the Republicans were divided, and it measured how far reform sentiment had reshaped both parties.[^ch29-archives-1912][^ch29-app-1912]
- **The result.** Woodrow Wilson won 435 of 531 electoral votes with about 42 percent of the popular vote; Roosevelt took about 27 percent and 88 electoral votes; Taft about 23 percent and 8; and the Socialist Eugene V. Debs about 6 percent with no electoral votes. California split its electoral votes between Wilson and Roosevelt.[^ch29-archives-1912][^ch29-tah-1912][^ch29-app-1912]

- **How to read it.** Wilson’s plurality was real and his electoral margin decisive, but the combined Roosevelt and Taft vote shows that a majority of voters chose a Republican or a Progressive. The chapter should draw that comparison rather than treat the plurality as a mandate.[^ch29-app-1912][^ch29-tah-1912]

### 4.4 Wilson’s New Freedom

- **The claim.** Wilson’s program lowered the tariff, created a central bank, and put new federal agencies over business, extending the regulatory state his predecessor had begun.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]
- **The tariff and the income tax.** The Underwood–Simmons Act of 1913 achieved the largest tariff reductions since the Civil War, and it offset lost revenue with a graduated income tax authorized by the Sixteenth Amendment, adopted February 25, 1913. Wilson broke precedent by addressing Congress in person to present the program.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]
- **Banking.** The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 created twelve regional reserve banks under a presidentially appointed board, empowered to adjust interest rates and the money supply — the most important domestic achievement of the administration.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]
- **Business regulation.** The Federal Trade Commission Act and the Clayton Antitrust Act, both of 1914, created a board to investigate unfair practices and prohibited price-fixing and interlocking directorates while exempting labor and farm organizations from the antitrust law. Wilson also signed the Adamson Act of 1916 granting railroad workers an eight-hour day.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-check-dates]
- **The New Freedom idea.** Wilson framed his program as the “New Freedom,” a promise to remove special privilege and restore competition rather than to supervise monopoly through a strong state as Roosevelt proposed. The chapter should present the New Freedom and the New Nationalism as rivals with different theories of government.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]

### 4.5 The limits of the New Freedom

- **The claim.** Wilson’s reforms stopped at several boundaries, and the two most consequential were child labor and race.[^ch29-archives-keating-owen][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]
- **Child labor.** Congress passed the Keating–Owen Act in 1916, barring from interstate commerce goods produced by children under 14 or by children of 14 to 16 working more than eight hours a day or six days a week. In *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918) the Supreme Court held the act outside the commerce power, and the law fell.[^ch29-archives-keating-owen][^ch29-oyez-hammer][^ch29-check-dates]
- **Federal segregation.** Wilson, consistent with his Southern background, allowed his cabinet to introduce segregation into departments such as the Post Office and the Treasury, and many Black federal employees were downgraded or discharged. He defended the separation as a way to reduce friction.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-whitehouse-trotter]
- **The pattern.** Wilson won Black support in 1912 on the promise of social justice and then disappointed it, which shows that the New Freedom’s universal language did not include racial equality. The chapter should hold his reform achievements and his segregation together.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-check-race]

## 5. Suffrage, segregation, immigration, and reform’s limits

### 5.1 The suffrage movement and the winning plan

- **The claim.** Woman suffrage gained ground in the states before the national amendment, and its organizations regrouped in 1916 around a two-track strategy; the amendment itself belongs to Chapter 31.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-check-ch31]
- **The state gains.** Nine western states had adopted woman suffrage by 1912, and more followed. Suffragists also won partial forms, such as the vote in presidential elections, in other states.[^ch29-archives-19th]
- **The winning plan.** At the National American Woman Suffrage Association convention at Atlantic City in September 1916, Carrie Chapman Catt presented a “winning plan” that combined a federal amendment campaign with state-by-state work in promising states, and urged the organization to avoid “detours” into states that could not win. Alice Paul’s more confrontational National Woman’s Party pursued the federal amendment and picketing.[^ch29-check-suffrage-plan]
- **The limit.** By 1916 almost all major suffrage organizations backed a constitutional amendment, but the vote remained partial and uneven by state and by race, and Black women in the South faced disfranchisement along with Black men.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-check-race]

### 5.2 Map 22: woman suffrage by state, 1916

- **The claim.** A map of suffrage by state in 1916 shows how patchwork the American franchise was, and how much of the country still denied women any vote.[^ch29-gilder-suffrage-map][^ch29-archives-19th]
- **What the map must show.** The registered visual `ch29-map-01` (Map 22, “Woman suffrage by state, 1916”) should distinguish states with full suffrage, states with partial or presidential suffrage, and states with none, and it should state its date and the state laws behind each category.[^ch29-check-map]
- **The sources.** The map should be built from the state suffrage laws and the NAWSA record, with the Catt and Shuler material as a guide. The Gilder Lehrman interactive map is a ca. 1919 suffrage map and shows how suffragists used such maps, but it is not a 1916 source and cannot be reproduced as the 1916 map.[^ch29-gilder-suffrage-map][^ch29-check-map]
- **The textual reference.** The chapter should cite “Map 22” where it discusses the patchwork franchise, and reconcile the map with App. B and Appendix C.[^ch29-check-map]

### 5.3 Federal segregation and the Black response

- **The claim.** The same federal authority that Wilson used for reform he used for segregation, and Black reformers answered with organization and protest.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-naacp-history]
- **The organizations.** The Niagara Movement, founded in 1905 and led by W. E. B. Du Bois, demanded full civil rights. After a deadly race riot in Springfield, Illinois, in 1908, a group of Black and White reformers issued a call on February 12, 1909, that founded the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, and Du Bois began *The Crisis* in 1910.[^ch29-naacp-history]
- **The protest.** In 1914 William Monroe Trotter of the National Independent Political League and the Boston *Guardian* confronted Wilson at the White House over the segregation of Black federal employees and was removed after the president declared himself offended. Trotter then took the case to the press.[^ch29-whitehouse-trotter]
- **The litigation and the limit.** The NAACP won an early victory in *Guinn v. United States* (1915) against the Oklahoma grandfather clause, but the federal government’s own segregation continued. The chapter should present the Black response as part of the era’s reform and its sharpest indictment.[^ch29-naacp-history][^ch29-check-race]

### 5.4 Angel Island and the enforcement of exclusion

- **The claim.** On the Pacific coast, federal reform of immigration meant exclusion, and the Angel Island station, opened in 1910, was its instrument.[^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-plan-angel-island]
- **The station.** The Angel Island Immigration Station opened on January 21, 1910, and operated until 1940. The Bureau of Immigration chose the island for its isolation and designed the station to detain rather than welcome newcomers, separating arrivals by race, gender, and class.[^ch29-aiisf-history]
- **The exclusion regime.** Under the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the laws that followed, only a few classes of Chinese could enter legally, and after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake destroyed records the “paper son” system of purchased identities supplied false claims of dependent children. Detainees faced invasive medical inspections and long interrogations before the Board of Special Inquiry.[^ch29-aiisf-history]
- **The record.** The station’s file—the interrogations, the barracks poems discovered in 1970, and the reports—gives the immigration-exclusion side of the same years in which the federal government expanded its reach. The chapter should place Angel Island beside the arbitrated coal field and the segregated office as faces of the same enlarged authority.[^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-plan-angel-island][^ch29-check-race]

### 5.5 Industrial violence and the limits of reform

- **The claim.** Where arbitration failed and where the federal government chose not to act, the era’s labor conflict produced open warfare, and Ludlow exposed both the reach and the limits of reform.[^ch29-nps-ludlow]
- **The Colorado strike.** In September 1913 coal miners in southern Colorado struck for enforcement of the eight-hour day and the right to choose their own housing and doctors. The Colorado Fuel and Iron Company, controlled by the Rockefeller interests, rejected the demands and evicted the strikers from company housing, and the miners built a tent colony at Ludlow.[^ch29-nps-ludlow]
- **The massacre.** On the morning of April 20, 1914, National Guardsmen aligned with the company attacked the Ludlow tent colony, killing 21 people, including 11 children. The attack ignited ten days of fighting until Wilson sent federal troops to disarm both sides.[^ch29-nps-ludlow]
- **The lesson for the chapter.** Ludlow shows that the same federal authority that arbitrated at the White House could also stand aside while a state militia fought a private company’s war, and that reform advanced mainly where the public was aroused. The chapter should close the section on that uneven record.[^ch29-nps-ludlow][^ch29-check-silences]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Public authority grew.** Between 1900 and 1916 the federal and state governments regulated railroads, food, drugs, and banks; created new agencies; and asserted an interest in industrial disputes and public lands.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]
- **Reform was a coalition, not a movement.** The Progressives included a professional middle class, urban and immigrant workers, organized women, and Black reformers who agreed on the need for action and differed on its aims.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-yawp-progressive]
- **The reform was limited and exclusionary.** The same years completed Black disfranchisement, segregated the federal service, enforced immigration exclusion, and displaced people from conservation lands.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-conservation]
- **Women won ground but not equality.** Suffrage advanced in the states and reorganized nationally, but the amendment was not yet won and protective-labor law treated women as a class apart.[^ch29-archives-19th][^ch29-justia-muller]
- **The courts set the boundary.** The Supreme Court permitted protective laws for women and children while invalidating general hours laws, and it later struck down the federal child-labor law.[^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-oyez-hammer]
- **The reforms did not redistribute authority within the workplace.** The Triangle investigation produced safety law and workmen’s compensation but no share of control for the workers, and the owners were acquitted.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-yawp-progressive]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Who drove reform?** One position holds that reform came from a displaced or anxiously rising middle class seeking order; another holds that it came from working-class and immigrant communities and their urban allies; a third holds that business leaders themselves sought regulation to stabilize competition.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe]
- **Did regulation serve the public or the regulated?** Roosevelt and Wilson presented regulation as a curb on corporate power; Kolko and Sklar argued that the regulated industries shaped the regulation to their own advantage. The chapter should state the strongest version of each before judging particular measures.[^ch29-glencoe-historians]
- **Was it one movement or many?** Wiebe’s organizational synthesis treats reform as a single response to disorder; later work treats it as many movements with different constituencies. McGerr argues for a coherent middle-class crusade; Leonard argues that the shared commitments of the reformers included eugenics and exclusion.[^ch29-tyrrell-wiebe][^ch29-mcgerr][^ch29-check-debate-works]
- **Where did the ideas come from?** Rodgers locates American reform in a transatlantic traffic of social policy; other accounts stress domestic conditions and institutions. The chapter should present the exchange as a real influence without claiming that Europe caused American reform.[^ch29-check-debate-works][^ch29-check-sources]
- **What did the era leave?** One reading stresses the durable regulatory and welfare institutions; another stresses the era’s exclusions and the distrust of government that followed. The evidence supports holding both, and the chapter should not let the later achievements excuse the exclusions.[^ch29-mcgerr][^ch29-check-conservation]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which Progressive reform at once widened the public’s power over concentrated wealth and narrowed who counted as the public. The chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-mcgerr]
- **Access and authority.** A worker who won a nine-hour day, a woman who won a protective law, and a Black clerk who lost a federal post each encountered the same enlarging state differently. The chapter’s synthesis should hold the gains and the exclusions together without claiming that one caused the other.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-justia-muller][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The reformers built an administrative state that could arbitrate a strike, inspect a factory, and manage a forest, and they built it with the era’s racial and gender order inside it. The chapter’s synthesis should say that the state’s new capacity and its new exclusions were products of the same movement.[^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-table2]

## Legacies

The administrative state that the Progressives built outlasted them. The Federal Reserve, the Federal Trade Commission, the income tax, the direct election of senators, and the labor and safety codes of the states became durable parts of American government, and the conservation program left a permanent federal presence in the West.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-nps-tr-conservation]

The exclusions outlasted the reformers too. Federal segregation, the enforcement of Asian exclusion, and the disfranchisement of Black southerners continued after 1916, and the eugenic assumptions that ran through parts of the reform movement fed the immigration restriction and sterilization laws of the 1920s, which belong to Chapter 32.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-check-ch32]

The reform impulse also left a double legacy for later Americans: a confidence that expert government can correct concentrated power, and a skepticism bred by the movement’s moralism and its exclusions. McGerr argues that this ambivalence about politics and the state has roots in these decades, which is a claim the chapter should present as an interpretation.[^ch29-mcgerr]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** American reform drew on European social insurance, city planning, and labor law, and reformers traveled and translated across the Atlantic; the Progressive movement was national and international at once. The chapter should place the comparison in a note rather than claim that reform was imported.[^ch29-check-debate-works][^ch29-check-sources]

## Summary

- On October 3, 1902, Roosevelt summoned the anthracite operators and the United Mine Workers to the temporary White House, and though the conference failed, arbitration ended the 163-day strike with a 10 percent award and a nine-hour day.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike]
- “Progressive” named a coalition of a professional middle class, urban and immigrant workers, organized women, and Black reformers, not a single movement.[^ch29-glencoe-historians][^ch29-yawp-progressive]
- The states adopted the direct primary, the initiative, the referendum, and the recall, and the Seventeenth Amendment provided for the direct election of senators in 1913.[^ch29-openstax-grassroots][^ch29-progressive-platform]
- The muckraking magazines — *McClure’s* and its rivals — made corruption and corporate power national scandals, and Roosevelt coined the name “muckraker” in 1906.[^ch29-gilder-jungle][^ch29-loc-muckrakers]
- Sinclair’s *The Jungle* (1906) prompted the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act, signed on June 30, 1906, regulating food rather than the labor Sinclair had meant to expose.[^ch29-gilder-jungle]
- Hull-House, founded by Addams and Starr in 1889, became the model settlement and a base for the investigations that produced labor law.[^ch29-hullhouse]
- The Triangle Shirtwaist fire of March 25, 1911, killed 145 workers by the Factory Investigating Commission’s count, and the resulting investigation produced workmen’s compensation and a revised state safety code.[^ch29-fic-preliminary][^ch29-dol-fic]
- Roosevelt’s Square Deal included the Northern Securities suit, the Elkins and Hepburn Acts, the Reclamation Act of 1902, and the reservation of about 230 million acres under the Forest Service and the Antiquities Act.[^ch29-miller-tr-domestic][^ch29-nps-tr-conservation][^ch29-usbr-reclamation]
- The Ballinger–Pinchot affair and the tariff split the Republicans, and in 1912 Wilson won 435 electoral votes with about 42 percent of the popular vote while Roosevelt took 88 and Taft 8.[^ch29-archives-1912][^ch29-app-1912]
- Wilson’s New Freedom produced the Underwood–Simmons tariff, the Federal Reserve Act, the Federal Trade Commission and Clayton Acts, and the Adamson Act, while child labor and racial equality remained beyond it.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-archives-keating-owen][^ch29-oyez-hammer]
- Wilson allowed federal segregation, Trotter protested it at the White House in 1914, and the NAACP, founded in 1909, fought it through the courts and *The Crisis*.[^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-whitehouse-trotter][^ch29-naacp-history]
- The Angel Island Immigration Station, opened in 1910, enforced Asian exclusion through detention and interrogation, and the Ludlow Massacre of April 20, 1914, showed the violence that reform had not ended.[^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-nps-ludlow]

> *A closing note.* Between 1900 and 1916 the United States enlarged the authority of its government. A president arbitrated a coal strike in the name of the public; investigators wrote factory conditions into law; federal agencies took charge of the food supply, the currency, and the forests; and a new party wrote direct democracy into its platform. The same years segregated the federal service, barred and interrogated immigrants at Angel Island, completed the disfranchisement of Black southerners, and left workers to be shot in a Colorado tent colony. The chapter’s question — who benefited when reformers enlarged public authority? — is answered by a record in which the gains and the exclusions were built by the same hands.[^ch29-grossman-coalstrike][^ch29-dol-fic][^ch29-miller-wilson-domestic][^ch29-aiisf-history][^ch29-nps-ludlow]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in August 1914, when the steamship *Ancon* made the first transit of the Panama Canal, a decade after the United States helped create the country that the canal crossed. The reforming state that Roosevelt and Wilson had built at home would now be turned outward, into the Caribbean, the Pacific, and eventually the European war. Chapter 30 takes up the United States and the world, and asks what kind of power a nation exercises when it rules without annexing.[^ch29-check-ch30][^ch29-check-bridge]
