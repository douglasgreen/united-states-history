# Chapter 2. Contact, Catastrophe, and Exchange (c. 1000–1551)

> “There I found very many islands, filled with innumerable people, and I have taken possession of them all for their Highnesses, done by proclamation and with the royal standard unfurled, and no opposition was offered to me.”
>
> — Christopher Columbus, letter to Luis de Santángel, 1493, as printed in *Epistola Christofori Colom* (Rome, after April 29, 1493)[^ch02-columbus-letter-loc]

> “Into and among these gentle sheep … did creep the Spaniards, who no sooner had knowledge of these people than they became like fierce wolves and tigers and lions who have gone many days without food or nourishment.”
>
> — Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies*, written 1542, published 1552[^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]

> “Virgin soil epidemics are those in which the populations at risk have had no previous contact with the diseases that strike them and are therefore immunologically almost defenseless.”
>
> — Alfred W. Crosby, “Virgin Soil Epidemics as a Factor in the Aboriginal Depopulation in America,” 1976, p. 289[^ch02-crosby-virginsoil]

Three voices, three claims about the same crossing. Columbus speaks the language of possession. Las Casas speaks the language of predation. Crosby speaks the language of epidemiology, in which no conqueror has to be named. The chapter holds the three together because their disagreement is the subject.

The walls are low now. Turf has settled into the ground at the tip of Newfoundland’s Great Northern Peninsula, beside the shallow water of Islands Bay, once called Epaves Bay. A narrow terrace runs between a bog and a small brook. A high rocky ridge rises behind it, and the Strait of Belle Isle lies beyond. Eight timber-framed turf buildings stood here in four complexes. Two complexes held a large hall and a small hut. A third held a large hall, a hut, and a small house. The fourth was an iron-smelting hut built into the bank of the brook. The halls were built in the Icelandic style of the late tenth and early eleventh centuries. The sleeping places could hold between 70 and 90 people. Spindle whorls and a bone pin show that women were present.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia][^ch02-lam-unesco]

The occupation was short. The middens are small, the buildings were not rebuilt, and the deposits point to about a decade. This was a base for exploration and extraction, not a farming colony. There are no barns, no livestock structures, and no evidence of grazing. Butternuts and butternut wood, whose northern limit lies in eastern New Brunswick, show that the Norse traveled into the Gulf of St. Lawrence. The occupants may have called the wider region Vinland for its grapes.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]

Three pieces of wood, all modified with metal tools, fix the date. Tree-ring analysis, anchored by a cosmic-ray event in 993 CE, places the felling of all three in 1021 CE. Indigenous people of the region did not make metal tools at the time. The date is the only secure calendar date for a European presence across the Atlantic before Columbus.[^ch02-lam-1021]

The land was not empty. Five Indigenous groups used the site before the Norse, and one used it afterward. No group appears to have lived there during the Norse occupation, but hearths and tent rings from earlier camps would have been visible, and people of the Cow Head tradition lived in the larger vicinity. The Norse built in a landscape that already held other people’s traces.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]

No written record from the occupants survives. The Vinland sagas describe voyages and encounters with Indigenous peoples, but they were written down centuries later, they disagree with one another, and their scenes cannot be tied to these buildings. The site names no person, records no conversation, and preserves no weather observation.[^ch02-lam-1021]

The date is exact. The meaning is not. Why did one Atlantic encounter remain limited while another reorganized the hemisphere?

This chapter follows the Atlantic world from about 1000 to 1551. Its center is the Caribbean and the lands that became the United States, but its frame is oceanic: the North Atlantic of the Norse, the Iberian Atlantic of Columbus and Magellan, and the Indigenous Atlantic of the peoples who were already there. It begins with a base camp in Newfoundland, follows the voyages that divided the ocean on paper, examines the biological and material exchange, and ends with the Spanish conquest in the Caribbean and the debate at Valladolid. It uses archaeology, sagas written down centuries later, Columbus’s letters, Spanish chronicles, Nahua accounts compiled in the 1570s, royal laws, and the work of historians and demographers. It distinguishes a landing from a colony, a voyage from a conquest, and a biological exchange from a political one. It treats “contact,” “catastrophe,” and “exchange” as analytical terms, not neutral descriptions. It states mortality as ranges and regional estimates, names the silences in the record, and does not use a single continental percentage to stand for the whole hemisphere.[^ch02-check-population]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 sets out the Norse and Iberian landfalls and the difference between a base camp and a colony. §2 follows the voyages that divided the Atlantic on paper and the competing claims that divided it in practice. §3 examines the biological and material exchange, including the pathogens that caused catastrophic mortality. §4 turns to Hispaniola, the encomienda, the Taíno collapse, Las Casas, and the Valladolid debate. §5 follows Cabeza de Vaca, De Soto, Coronado, and Cartier into the interior and the St. Lawrence. §6 weighs the debate over virgin soil, coercion, and demographic change. The chapter begins at L’Anse aux Meadows, not at 1492. It ends with a world that had been remade, and with a question that had not been answered.

## 1. Norse and Iberian landfalls

### 1.1 A base camp, not a colony

The buildings at L’Anse aux Meadows answer a narrow question and refuse a larger one. Europeans crossed the Atlantic before Columbus. What kind of event that crossing was is harder to say.

The site held a forge, workshops, boat repair, and about 800 artifacts. That is a working camp, not a town. Greenland’s whole colony held only about 400 people, so the party here was a serious investment. The occupation lasted about a decade, and then it ended.[^ch02-lam-unesco][^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]

The purpose was extraction. The Norse wanted lumber, furs, and perhaps luxury foods, and the butternuts show travel into the Gulf of St. Lawrence. There are no barns or livestock structures. This was not a place to raise families.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]

The date is secure in a way that few early dates are. Three wooden items, all modified with metal tools, were felled in 1021 CE. The tree-ring sequence is anchored by a cosmic-ray event in 993 CE, and all three converge on the same year. The date fixes activity at the site, not the first or only landing, and it does not explain why the Norse came.[^ch02-lam-1021]

The sagas are the only written account, and they are not a transcript. The Vinland sagas describe Leif Eriksson, Þorfinnr Karlsefni, and encounters with Indigenous peoples. They were written centuries later, they disagree about the voyages, and their scenes have been conflated. They are evidence for how later Icelanders remembered the voyages, not a record of what happened here.[^ch02-lam-1021][^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]

The Indigenous context is part of the site, not a footnote to it. Five Indigenous groups used the place before the Norse, and one used it afterward. Hearths and tent rings would have been visible. The record does not show a meeting at the site, only that the Norse built among other people’s traces.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]

The Norse landing was real, dated, and brief. It was a base camp within an Indigenous landscape, not a founding moment. The chapter does not call it a “discovery,” and it does not treat it as a prelude that makes Columbus inevitable. The difference between the Norse and the Spanish is not virtue. It is scale, duration, purpose, and follow-up.

### 1.2 Columbus and the Caribbean landfall

Columbus’s first voyage changed the scale of the encounter, not because he was first, but because he was followed. His ships reached an island the Lucayan people called Guanahani and renamed San Salvador. The exact island remains uncertain. His letter claims possession for the Spanish crown with a proclamation and an unfurled royal standard.[^ch02-columbus-letter-loc][^ch02-columbus-settlement]

The Taíno were not a single people or polity. They were diverse Arawak-speaking communities with hierarchical religious, political, and social systems. They were skilled farmers and navigators, and they lived across the Greater Antilles. The Spanish chronicles and the archaeological record show dense settlements, caciques, and regional networks.[^ch02-columbus-taino-loc][^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-check-names]

In 1493 Columbus returned with seventeen ships and about 1,200 men. He found the fort of La Navidad destroyed and its occupants dead. Near its ruins he founded La Isabela, a short-lived town on the north coast of Hispaniola. The second voyage turned a landing into a settlement project.[^ch02-columbus-settlement]

The settlement project demanded labor. The encomienda granted a Spaniard the tribute and forced labor of Indigenous people in a defined territory. It became the legal and economic foundation of the early Caribbean colony.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]

The Spanish landing mattered because it was followed by ships, soldiers, settlers, priests, laws, and mines. The event and the system are not the same thing.

### 1.3 What the first encounters were and were not

“First contact” is a misleading phrase. It turns many encounters into one moment, and it makes the people who were already here into an audience. There were many encounters, in different places, with different scales, purposes, and consequences.

The Norse built a small base, stayed about a decade, extracted resources, and left. The Spanish built towns, granted encomiendas, mined gold, converted souls, and stayed. The difference is not a difference in virtue. It is a difference in scale, duration, purpose, and follow-up.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia][^ch02-columbus-settlement]

Indigenous people were not passive recipients of a European story. Taíno caciques negotiated and fought. Lucayan communities were enslaved and carried away. Indigenous people interpreted the newcomers, traded with them, resisted them, and used them for their own purposes.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]

The evidence is asymmetric. The Norse record is archaeological, and the sagas are late. The Spanish record was written by conquerors and their critics. The Taíno left no written account. The chapter names these asymmetries rather than pretending to a neutral view from nowhere.

Once the Spanish established a settlement system, the Atlantic became a zone of competing voyages and claims. §2 turns to the treaties and routes that tried to divide it.

## 2. Voyages and the division of the Atlantic

### 2.1 Tordesillas and the papal grant

The Treaty of Tordesillas divided an ocean with a line. The line was precise on parchment and unenforceable at sea.

On June 7, 1494, Spain and Portugal agreed to a boundary running from pole to pole, 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. Lands east of the line would belong to Portugal. Lands west of it would belong to Spain. The treaty shifted the boundary established by the papal bull *Inter caetera* in 1493 and allowed Portugal to claim the route to India and the land that became Brazil. It was a bilateral agreement about discovery, navigation, and trade, not a settlement of evangelization.[^ch02-tordesillas-unesco][^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

France, England, and later the Dutch Republic rejected the papal grant and the treaty. They argued that the pope’s decisions did not bind them and that the sea and the new lands were open to other crowns. The line became a source of dispute, not a settled border.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

The treaty created a legal fiction of two monopolies. In practice, the Atlantic became a zone of smuggling, fishing, privateering, and competing settlements. The map of 1494 did not match the map of 1551.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

### 2.2 Cabot, Cabral, and the routes

Crossing the Atlantic was not a Spanish achievement. English and Portuguese voyages turned the ocean into a zone of competing claims.

John Cabot sailed from Bristol in May 1497 in the *Matthew*. He coasted Newfoundland and Labrador and reported waters teeming with cod. The voyage became the basis of England’s claim to North America, although the surviving accounts are second-hand and the landfall is debated. The cod report mattered as much as the claim; the Grand Banks fishery became a major attraction for European ships.[^ch02-cabot]

Pedro Álvares Cabral left Lisbon in March 1500 with thirteen vessels and about 1,200 men, bound for India. He made landfall on the coast of present-day Bahia, erected a cross, and claimed the land for Portugal. Whether the landfall was accidental or informed by earlier knowledge is debated. The Portuguese crown soon treated Brazil as part of its domain.[^ch02-cabral][^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

By 1500, at least three crowns had claims in the Atlantic, and the Portuguese had a route around Africa and a landfall in South America. The Spanish Caribbean was one part of a wider competition.

### 2.3 Magellan and the global frame

Magellan’s expedition turned the Atlantic division into a global problem. The first circumnavigation showed that a western route to Asia lay across a vast ocean, that the anti-meridian could not be fixed by the Tordesillas line alone, and that the Americas were not the edge of Asia.

Five ships and about 270 men left Sanlúcar de Barrameda in September 1519. Magellan passed through the strait that bears his name, crossed the Pacific, reached the Philippines, and was killed at Mactan in April 1521. Juan Sebastián Elcano brought the *Victoria* back to Spain in September 1522 with eighteen survivors.[^ch02-magellan]

Spain and Portugal disputed the Moluccas because the anti-meridian of Tordesillas could not be determined. The Treaty of Saragossa in 1529 set a new line and recognized the Moluccas as Portuguese. The global division was an ongoing negotiation, not a single settlement.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

The Atlantic was not a closed Spanish lake. It was one theater in a global system of trade, conquest, and competing legal claims. The chapter keeps the global frame visible without losing the Caribbean center.

### 2.4 The limits of the line

The Tordesillas line was a European claim, not a description of political reality. It could not be fixed precisely, it ignored Indigenous sovereignty, and it was rejected by other European powers.

The treaty specified a distance west of Cape Verde but did not settle how to measure it or where the corresponding anti-meridian fell. The dispute over the Moluccas exposed the problem. The line was a map convention, not a surveyed boundary.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

Indigenous polities did not recognize the line, and their lands, alliances, and trade routes crossed it. The Spanish and Portuguese crowns could claim a monopoly, but they could not enforce one against Indigenous people or other Europeans without war, missionization, and settlement.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]

## 3. Biological and material exchange

### 3.1 The exchange framework

The Columbian exchange was a package, not a transaction. It moved pathogens, plants, animals, and people in both directions, and it transformed both sides of the Atlantic.

Alfred W. Crosby’s *The Columbian Exchange* (1972) gave the process its name. Crosby divided the exchange into diseases, animals, and plants. The term is useful as a shorthand, but it can obscure timing, region, and human agency.[^ch02-crosby-essay]

New World crops included maize, potatoes, manioc, peanuts, tomatoes, squash, pineapple, and avocado. Old World crops included rice, wheat, barley, oats, turnips, onions, cabbage, peaches, pears, and sugar. The lists were sharply different, with cotton as a possible exception: different species grew in both hemispheres.[^ch02-crosby-essay]

The animals differed even more sharply. The Americas had dogs, llamas, guinea pigs, and a few species of fowl. The Old World brought horses, donkeys, pigs, cattle, goats, sheep, and barnyard fowl.[^ch02-crosby-essay]

The exchange reshaped diets, landscapes, labor, and disease environments. It was a biological and political reorganization of two hemispheres, not a one-way transfer of European superiority.

### 3.2 Pathogens and the first epidemics

Old World pathogens did not need an army. They traveled in bodies, ships, and stores. They caused catastrophic mortality among Indigenous peoples, but the timing, scale, and causes varied by region. “Virgin soil” names a biological condition, not a complete explanation.

Smallpox, measles, influenza, malaria, and other Old World infections crossed the Atlantic. Indigenous communities had no prior exposure to many of them. Not all arrived at once, and not all were equally important in the first century. In populations not previously exposed, smallpox could infect almost everyone and kill 30 to 100 percent of the infected.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox]

By 1518–19, smallpox was spreading through the Caribbean and toward Yucatan. The precise route and the first carrier are not documented. The disease reached Tenochtitlan in 1520, during the Spanish war against the Mexica (Aztec), and contributed to the city’s fall in 1521.[^ch02-nlm-1518][^ch02-dumbarton-fall][^ch02-check-1518]

The scale was immense. Current estimates suggest a population loss of five to eight million people in Mesoamerica during the 1520 outbreak. Some historians estimate that the Indigenous population of the Americas fell by as much as 90 percent by the end of the sixteenth century. Others argue for a figure as high as 95 percent in the first century. These are estimates, not counts, and they do not agree.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox][^ch02-jones-virginsoils]

Mortality was worsened by warfare, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and the collapse of care. People too sick to move could not feed themselves or care for others. Famine followed epidemic. The outcome varied with the conditions the colonizers imposed.[^ch02-dumbarton-century][^ch02-newson-demographic]

Some populations declined for a century and then began to recover. Some, such as the Lucayans of the Bahamas, disappeared as distinct communities. Others, in different regions, grew after contact. The variation is the evidence that a single “virgin soil” story cannot explain the whole hemisphere.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils][^ch02-newson-demographic]

**In Focus.** The *Florentine Codex* is a detailed Nahua account of the 1520 epidemic. Book 12, folio 53r, remembers a sickness of pustules that “began in Tepeilhuitl,” covered people with agonizing sores from head to foot, and left the sick unable to move. “A great many died from this plague, and many others died of hunger. They could not get up to search for food, and everyone else was too sick to care for them, so they starved to death in their beds.” The codex was compiled in the 1570s, under the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún, with Nahua elders and students at the College of Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco; production ended in 1577. It is a collaboration shaped by a missionary project, written in Nahuatl and translated into Spanish, and illustrated by Nahua artists. It establishes that Nahua people remembered the epidemic as a social catastrophe as well as a bodily one: the failure of care, the hunger, and the isolation. It does not establish a clinical diagnosis, an exact death count, or the experience of every community. The modern identification of the disease as smallpox is a retrospective judgment, built from the Nahua terms *hueyzahuatl* and *totomonaliztli* and from the later history of the pandemic. No reproduction of the folio is planned; the box reads the text, not an image.[^ch02-dumbarton-florentine][^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox]

### 3.3 Plants, animals, and labor

The biological exchange was inseparable from labor and land. Crops and animals moved with people, seizure of land, and coercion of workers.

Horses, cattle, pigs, and sheep transformed Indigenous and colonial economies. Horses changed travel, hunting, and warfare. Cattle and pigs became new sources of food and property. The animals arrived within a regime of settlement and property, not as neutral tools.[^ch02-crosby-essay]

Wheat, sugar, and rice became staples of colonial agriculture. Maize, potatoes, and manioc became important foods in European, African, and Asian diets. The exchange changed the food base of the world, but the benefits were distributed unequally.[^ch02-crosby-essay]

Sugar, mining, and livestock required labor. The encomienda and the enslavement of Africans and Indigenous people supplied it. The biological exchange and the coercive labor regime were parts of the same history.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery]

The “exchange” was not a fair trade. It reorganized land, labor, and life, and its costs fell heavily on Indigenous and enslaved people.

### 3.4 What exchange explains and what it does not

The exchange explains vulnerability and speed. It does not explain why some communities recovered, why others disappeared, or why mortality varied so widely.

Old World pathogens could spread quickly through populations with no prior exposure. They could kill large numbers before any immune response developed. That is a real and important part of the story.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils]

The timing and scale of mortality depended on social conditions: war, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and the destruction of care. The same pathogen produced different outcomes in different places. A biological condition cannot by itself explain a political catastrophe.[^ch02-newson-demographic][^ch02-jones-virginsoils]

The Caribbean is the first place where the exchange, the labor regime, and the conquest came together. §4 turns to Hispaniola and the moral debate it provoked.

## 4. The Caribbean and the Valladolid debate

### 4.1 Hispaniola: encomienda, gold, and death

Hispaniola became the laboratory of Spanish conquest. The encomienda, gold mining, forced labor, African slavery, and epidemic disease produced a catastrophic decline and a durable colonial order.

Columbus founded La Isabela in 1493–94. The town was short-lived; the Spanish presence became permanent. The crown granted encomiendas: a Spaniard received the tribute and labor of the Indigenous people in a defined territory.[^ch02-columbus-settlement][^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]

Encomenderos demanded tribute in crops or money and labor in mines, forts, towns, and plantations. Their power was practically absolute; the records describe abuse and death. The crown issued regulations, but it did not abolish the system.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-newson-demographic]

In 1513 the crown issued the *Requerimiento*, a document read before conquest. It demanded submission to the pope and the king. If they refused, the Spaniards would “make war against” them, enslave them, and seize their goods. It was often read in Spanish to people who did not understand it.[^ch02-requerimiento-nlm]

One colonial estimate counted 60,000 Indigenous people on Hispaniola in 1508 and fewer than 20,000 ten years later. The numbers are not a census, but they show the direction and speed of the collapse. The causes included disease, forced labor, flight, and violence.[^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery][^ch02-newson-demographic]

As the Indigenous population fell, the Spanish imported enslaved Africans. From 1501 to 1518 the trade came largely through Iberia. In 1518 Charles V authorized a charter for 4,000 Africans from the Cape Verde Islands. The racial labor system of the Atlantic world was taking shape.[^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery]

Hispaniola was not a single tragedy of disease. It was a political and economic order that made disease, hunger, and overwork lethal.

### 4.2 The Taíno collapse and its sources

The sources do not support a single Taíno population figure or a single story of extinction. “Taíno” is a broad name for diverse communities, and the collapse must be described with ranges, regional variation, and named silences.

Las Casas claimed that Hispaniola had held three million souls and that only two hundred remained. Modern estimates vary widely. A survey table lists Caribbean estimates from 225,000 to more than six million in 1492, while others argue for several million on Hispaniola alone. The differences reflect assumptions about household size, agriculture, and colonial counts.[^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount][^ch02-newson-demographic][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]

“Taíno” is an umbrella for Arawak-speaking communities of the Greater Antilles, not a single nation or language. The communities had caciques, regional alliances, and different histories. The chapter uses the name while acknowledging its limits.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-check-names]

In the colonial record, the Taíno were close to extinction by 1550. Many had died from disease and violence. But Taíno descendants and cultural traditions survived. “Extinct” is a colonial classification, not a biological verdict.[^ch02-columbus-taino-loc][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]

No Taíno written account survives. The evidence comes from Spanish chronicles, encomienda records, archaeology, and descendant communities. The chapter names the silence rather than ventriloquizing a Taíno witness.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino]

### 4.3 Las Casas and the moral critique

Bartolomé de las Casas made the most influential critique of Spanish conquest in the sixteenth century. His critique was powerful, eyewitness, and limited by his own history and by the politics of reform.

Las Casas arrived in Hispaniola in 1502, became a landowner and enslaver, and was ordained a priest in 1510. After participating in the conquest of Cuba in 1513, he turned against the conquest. By 1515 he had renounced his encomienda, returned to Spain, and begun to petition the crown. He spent the rest of his life arguing for Indigenous rights.[^ch02-lascasas-origins]

In 1516 Las Casas proposed importing enslaved Africans to relieve the Indigenous population. He later repudiated the proposal and condemned the African slave trade. The episode shows that a critic of one form of coercion could be entangled in another.[^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery][^ch02-lascasas-origins]

*A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* was written in 1542 and published in 1552. Its title page frames the question as a dispute over whether the conquests were “lawful” or “tyrannies, unjust and iniquitous.” The book describes the Spaniards as “fierce wolves and tigers and lions” and claims that three million people had been reduced to two hundred. The numbers are polemical, but the account is an eyewitness indictment of a system he had once served.[^ch02-gilder-valladolid][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]

Las Casas’s lobbying contributed to the New Laws of 1542, which banned the enslavement of Indigenous people and regulated tribute and labor. The laws were widely resisted and unevenly enforced, but they marked a change in imperial policy.[^ch02-newson-demographic]

Las Casas did not end conquest. He made its moral cost visible and helped create the language of Indigenous rights later critics would use. He was a complicated reformer, not a saint.

### 4.4 Valladolid, 1550–51

The Valladolid debate made the legitimacy of conquest a public question. It did not answer it.

In April 1550 Charles V halted Spanish conquests in the Americas and convened a panel of theologians and officials at the Colegio de San Gregorio in Valladolid. The debate opened on August 15, 1550.[^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]

Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda argued from Aristotle that Indigenous peoples were “natural slaves,” incapable of self-government, and that war against them was a just means of Christianization. He claimed that they were as inferior to Spaniards as children to adults.[^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]

Las Casas replied for five days. He argued that Indigenous peoples were not natural slaves and that the conquest was unjust. He defended human sacrifice as a form of religious devotion, a move that shows how far his argument was from modern human rights language.[^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]

A second session followed in 1551. Both sides claimed victory, but the junta never issued a formal decision, and one judge did not deliver his opinion until 1557. Conquest and cruelty continued. In 1573 Philip II issued an ordinance that banned the word “conquest” and recommended “pacification,” a change in language rather than in power.[^ch02-kci-valladolid][^ch02-historytoday-valladolid]

Valladolid is important because it made the moral question public. It did not settle the question. The popular story that Las Casas won and that the empire reformed is not supported by the record.

## 5. De Soto, Coronado, Cabeza de Vaca, and Cartier

### 5.1 Cabeza de Vaca and the survivor’s account

Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca’s account of the Narváez expedition is a primary source, a literary construction, and a document that helped shape later conquest.

Cabeza de Vaca was treasurer of a 300-man expedition led by Pánfilo de Narváez. The expedition sailed into Tampa Bay in April 1528, marched overland, and tried to reach Mexico in makeshift boats. Cabeza de Vaca and three others survived: Dorantes, Castillo, and Esteban, an enslaved North African. They reached a Spanish settlement on the Sinaloa River in 1536.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate]

The *Relación* describes his years as a captive, a trader, and a healer among Indigenous peoples of the Southwest. It is the first European account of many of those communities, and it is shaped by his survival, his Christian frame, and his later political purposes.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate]

The route is disputed. Scholars have proposed at least five reconstructions. Any line drawn on a map is a hypothesis, not a fact.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate]

Cabeza de Vaca’s stories of wealthy cities to the north fed the expeditions of De Soto and Coronado. The survivor’s account became a guide for conquest, even as it described Indigenous worlds that the conquerors would destroy.

### 5.2 De Soto in the Southeast

Hernando de Soto’s expedition was a mobile campaign of violence and extraction, not a settlement. It left no colony, but it destabilized Indigenous societies across the Southeast.

De Soto landed in Florida in 1539 with about 700 participants. He marched through the Southeast, fought at Mabila, and reached the Mississippi River in 1541. He died of fever in 1542, probably in Arkansas or Louisiana. About 311 of the original 700 survived.[^ch02-desoto-nps]

At Mabila the Spaniards burned a fortified town and killed an estimated 2,000 to 6,000 Indigenous warriors. They lost about 200 dead and 150 wounded. The expedition took hostages and burned Mabila. The numbers come from the chronicles and are uncertain, but the pattern is clear.[^ch02-desoto-nps]

De Soto’s expedition did not find the wealth it sought. It spread violence and disruption, and it may have spread disease. Its failure did not make it harmless.

### 5.3 Coronado in the Southwest

Francisco Vázquez de Coronado’s expedition searched for wealth and converts. It found neither, but it transformed the Pueblo world through violence, demands, and the opening of a route.

Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza commissioned Coronado in January 1540. The expedition left Compostela in February with more than 300 Spanish soldiers, more than 1,200 Indigenous allies, and 1,500 stock animals. It reached Háwikuh, the first of the fabled Cities of Cibola, on July 7, 1540.[^ch02-coronado-nps]

The Spaniards attacked Háwikuh when negotiations failed. They wintered at Tiguex, where violations of hospitality turned the Pueblo people hostile. They killed the occupants of one pueblo and forced the abandonment of several others. They tortured and killed “The Turk,” the Plains guide who led them toward Quivira.[^ch02-coronado-nps]

Coronado reached Quivira, in present-day Kansas, in 1541 and found no gold. He returned to Mexico City in the spring of 1542, discredited but not powerless.[^ch02-coronado-nps]

The expedition did not establish a colony. It opened a route for later missions and settlement, and it left a trail of violence. The Pueblo world would remember it.

### 5.4 Cartier in the St. Lawrence

Jacques Cartier’s voyages were commercial and exploratory probes, not a conquest. They still used possession claims, kidnapping, and coercion, and they destabilized the St. Lawrence world.

Cartier left Saint-Malo in April 1534 with two ships and 61 men. He explored the Gulf of St. Lawrence, met Mi’kmaq people at Chaleur Bay, and erected a thirty-foot cross at Gaspé on July 24. Donnacona, the leader of Stadacona, objected; Cartier lied and called it an insignificant landmark. He took Donnacona’s sons to France.[^ch02-cartier-museum]

Cartier returned in 1535 with three ships. He sailed up the St. Lawrence, established headquarters at Stadacona, and traveled to Hochelaga, the fortified village on the island of Montreal. He arrived on October 2 and visited the village on October 3. Hochelaga had about fifty houses, a palisade, and cornfields.[^ch02-cartier-museum][^ch02-hochelaga]

In May 1536 Cartier seized about ten St. Lawrence Iroquoians, including Donnacona, and took them to France. Most died there. Cartier admitted Donnacona’s death but claimed the others had remained in France “where they were living as great lords.” The claim was a lie.[^ch02-cartier-museum]

In 1541 Cartier returned with five ships and about 1,500 people, part of a colonization scheme under Jean-François de La Roque de Roberval. The colonists settled at Charlesbourg Royal, but the venture failed. Cartier left in June 1542.[^ch02-cartier-museum]

When Samuel de Champlain traveled to the region in 1603, the village of Hochelaga and its inhabitants had disappeared. Disease, wars with other Indigenous groups, and control of trade routes are all possible. The evidence does not choose among them.[^ch02-hochelaga]

France did not establish a permanent colony in the St. Lawrence in this period. Cartier’s voyages still opened a route, charted the river, and left a record of societies the next century would transform.

### 5.5 The balance by 1551

By 1551 the Spanish had built a durable imperial system in the Caribbean, Mexico, and Peru. The Norse, English, and French had not.

Hispaniola, Cuba, Mexico, and Peru were governed through towns, encomiendas, mines, and imperial administration. Silver, gold, and forced labor connected the Americas to Europe. The system was contested and violent, but it endured.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-newson-demographic]

Cabot’s 1497 voyage gave England a claim, but no permanent English colony followed. Cartier’s voyages gave France a claim and a route, but the 1541–42 settlement failed. The Norse base had been abandoned five centuries earlier.[^ch02-cabot][^ch02-cartier-museum]

The Taíno of Hispaniola had been reduced to a remnant in the colonial records, but descendants survived. The St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages had disappeared by 1603. The Pueblo, Maya, Nahua, and many other peoples endured conquest, epidemic, and forced labor. The balance was not a simple story of European victory.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-hochelaga][^ch02-newson-demographic]

By 1551 the moral and political questions raised by conquest had become public. Map 2, panel B, places these expeditions in the wider Atlantic competition. §6 turns to the debate over how to explain the demographic catastrophe.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The Norse reached Newfoundland. The date 1021 is secure, and the site is a Norse base camp, not an Indigenous settlement or a later European village.[^ch02-lam-1021]

Columbus began a durable system. The 1492 landing was not the first European presence, but it was followed by settlement, encomienda, mining, and a continuing transatlantic exchange.[^ch02-columbus-settlement]

Old World pathogens caused catastrophic mortality. Smallpox, measles, influenza, and other diseases killed large numbers of Indigenous people who had no prior exposure.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox]

The mortality was regional and uneven. Some communities disappeared; some declined and recovered; some grew. The variation is documented across the hemisphere.[^ch02-newson-demographic]

Coercion was central. Violence, forced labor, hunger, and displacement shaped the catastrophe. Disease and conquest were not separate stories.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]

Valladolid did not settle the question. The debate ended without a formal verdict, and conquest continued.[^ch02-kci-valladolid]

### 6.2 What is open

The first landfall remains uncertain. The sources name Guanahani and San Salvador, but the identification of the island is disputed.[^ch02-columbus-settlement][^ch02-check-first-landfall]

The 1518 epidemic’s route and first carrier are not documented. The chapter does not invent a single infected person. It distinguishes the 1518 Caribbean outbreak from the 1520 Tenochtitlan epidemic.[^ch02-nlm-1518][^ch02-check-1518]

Pre-contact populations remain estimates. The figures for Hispaniola and for the hemisphere vary enormously, and the differences reflect assumptions as much as evidence.[^ch02-newson-demographic][^ch02-smithsonian-taino]

The relative roles remain disputed. Historians disagree about how much of the mortality should be attributed to pathogen exposure, and how much to violence, hunger, forced labor, and social disruption. The evidence supports a combined explanation, not a single cause.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils]

Recovery remains unexplained in detail. Why some communities recovered and others did not is still debated. The answers vary by region, ecology, and colonial policy.[^ch02-newson-demographic]

The St. Lawrence remains unresolved. The disappearance of the St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages is unexplained. Disease, war, and trade are all plausible; none is proved.[^ch02-hochelaga]

The expeditions’ routes remain contested. The routes of Cabeza de Vaca, De Soto, and Coronado are disputed, and the numbers in the chronicles are estimates.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate][^ch02-desoto-nps][^ch02-coronado-nps][^ch02-check-routes]

The category remains contested. “Taíno” is a broad label, and its use is debated. The chapter explains the term rather than treating it as a nation.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The choice is not between a biological explanation and a political one. The evidence shows an interaction between novel pathogens and the conditions of conquest.

Crosby’s 1976 definition of virgin soil epidemics is a useful starting point. But Crosby himself emphasized malnutrition and the social chaos of colonization, not a simple genetic weakness. Later writers often reduced his argument to “no immunity,” which is a slogan, not an explanation.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils]

David S. Jones argues that the specific contribution of genetic or developmental factors is probably unknowable, and that the fates of populations depended on their physical, economic, social, and political environments. Suzanne Alchon argues that Indigenous mortality rates were comparable to those of other populations exposed to new infections, and that colonialism, warfare, slavery, and forced migration explain why Indigenous populations failed to recover. Paul Kelton shows how the Indigenous slave trade spread disease in the Southeast. Andrés Reséndez recenters Indigenous enslavement in the colonial catastrophe.[^ch02-jones-virginsoils][^ch02-alchon-pest][^ch02-kelton-enslavement][^ch02-resendez-other]

Noble David Cook’s *Born to Die* remains a necessary counterweight to accounts that make pathogens incidental. Cook argues that epidemic disease was the principal agent of continental depopulation, while attending to regional variation. The chapter presents that position fairly and then explains why later historians found it insufficient by itself.[^ch02-cook-born]

Linda Newson’s survey shows considerable variation in depopulation and recovery. The Caribbean lost many communities entirely. Mexico and Central America recovered slowly in the seventeenth century. The Andes continued to decline. A single continental percentage cannot capture that pattern.[^ch02-newson-demographic]

The best history states its position, gives the strongest version of the rival, and shows what the evidence can bear. The chapter does not replace one myth with another. It says that pathogens were necessary but not sufficient, that coercion was central, and that the dead were not merely biological victims.

## Legacies

The contact era is not a prologue to the nation. It is the moment when the Atlantic world became a single system of ships, pathogens, crops, animals, and coerced labor. The evidence matters to later chapters because it establishes that the land was already inhabited, that American labor regimes were built in the sixteenth century, and that the catastrophe was both biological and political.

The “virgin soil” idea had consequences. If disease did the work, the conquerors can appear to be bystanders. But the evidence shows that mortality was worsened by violence, forced labor, hunger, and the destruction of care. The chapter states that connection without turning the past into a modern morality play. It also avoids the opposite error: treating disease as a minor factor because it complicates the story of conquest. Pathogens were real, and they killed.

The Taíno did not disappear. Their descendants live in the Caribbean, and their words, foods, and religious practices survive. The St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages did disappear from the valley, and the causes remain uncertain. These are not decorative details. They show that the outcomes of contact were different in different places, and that “extinction” is often a colonial classification rather than a biological fact.[^ch02-smithsonian-taino][^ch02-hochelaga]

The chapter’s legacies are therefore double. The Atlantic world was made by exchange, and that exchange created new possibilities. It was also made by conquest, and that conquest created durable inequalities. The chapter ends with the debate over how to explain the dead, not with a verdict about who deserved what.

## Optional threads

- The transnational frame. The Norse voyages crossed the North Atlantic; the Spanish crossed the Atlantic and the Pacific; Cartier crossed the Atlantic and entered the St. Lawrence. The book’s fifty-state frame is a political geography, not a cultural one. The chapter says so once and then returns to the Caribbean center.
- Archives and silences. The Taíno left no written account. The Norse sagas were written centuries later. The *Florentine Codex* was compiled decades after the epidemic it describes. The De Soto and Coronado chronicles were written by participants and partisans. The chapter names these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch02-dumbarton-florentine][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]
- Distant connections. Chapter 3 takes up Indigenous political worlds; Chapter 5, the legal consolidation of racial slavery; Chapter 25, conquest and the West; Chapter 49, the recent reckoning; Chapter 50, the book’s through-lines. This chapter establishes the Atlantic frame they presuppose.

## Summary

- The Norse reached Newfoundland by 1021 and built a small base camp, but they did not establish a lasting colony.[^ch02-lam-1021][^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia]
- Columbus’s 1492 landing began a durable transatlantic system of settlement, encomienda, mining, and exchange.[^ch02-columbus-settlement][^ch02-encomienda-nyhs]
- The Treaty of Tordesillas divided the Atlantic on paper, but other European powers and Indigenous polities did not accept the line.[^ch02-tordesillas-ehne]
- The Columbian exchange moved pathogens, plants, animals, and people in both directions and reshaped diets, landscapes, and labor.[^ch02-crosby-essay]
- Old World pathogens caused catastrophic mortality, but the timing and scale varied by region and depended on social conditions.[^ch02-dumbarton-smallpox][^ch02-newson-demographic]
- Hispaniola became the laboratory of Spanish conquest: encomienda, gold, forced labor, African slavery, and epidemic disease.[^ch02-encomienda-nyhs][^ch02-ldhi-african-slavery]
- Las Casas made a powerful critique of conquest, but he was a complicated reformer whose early proposal to import enslaved Africans exposes the limits of his sympathy.[^ch02-lascasas-origins][^ch02-lascasas-shortaccount]
- The Valladolid debate of 1550–51 made the moral question public but produced no formal verdict, and conquest continued.[^ch02-kci-valladolid]
- Cabeza de Vaca, De Soto, Coronado, and Cartier carried Spanish and French power, trade, and captivity into the interior; their routes are disputed, and the Spanish expeditions’ violence is documented.[^ch02-cabeza-texasstate][^ch02-desoto-nps][^ch02-coronado-nps][^ch02-cartier-museum]
- The debate over “virgin soil” is a debate about evidence, scale, and causation, not about whether Indigenous peoples were “close to nature.”[^ch02-jones-virginsoils][^ch02-alchon-pest]

## Further Reading

This is a selective guide, not a citation ledger. The groups move from primary voices and archaeological evidence to syntheses and specialized studies. Unless the annotation says otherwise, the works below are cited in the combined notes; several were consulted in excerpt, translation, or summary, as the entries indicate. Alchon, Cook, Kelton, and Reséndez were consulted through abstracts or reviews; their entries say so.

### Primary voices and archaeological evidence

- **Christopher Columbus, *Epistola Christofori Colom* (1493).** The promotional letter that announced the first voyage and claimed possession of “very many islands.” Read it as a claim-making document, not a neutral report.
- **Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (1552).** The most influential indictment of Spanish conquest. Its numbers are polemical, but its eyewitness detail and moral force remain essential.
- **The *Florentine Codex*, Book 12 (compiled 1570s, completed 1577).** The Nahua account of the conquest and the 1520 epidemic, compiled with Sahagún and Nahua scholars. Read it with attention to its later composition and missionary context; the codex itself was not consulted in facsimile for this chapter, and the summary relies on the Dumbarton Oaks exhibit.
- **Birgitta Wallace, “L’Anse aux Meadows,” *The Canadian Encyclopedia* (2006; updated 2025).** The clearest short synthesis of the excavation, the buildings, the butternuts, and the site’s brief occupation. It is an encyclopedia entry by a leading archaeologist, not a full site report.
- **National Park Service, “De Soto Expedition” and “Coronado National Memorial: Stories.”** Accessible summaries of the expeditions, their routes, and their violence. They are institutional syntheses, not primary chronicles.

### Surveys and syntheses

- **Alfred W. Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange* (1972; 30th anniversary ed. 2003).** The book that named the exchange and put diseases, plants, and animals at the center of Atlantic history. Read it with the later critiques of “virgin soil” in mind.
- **Linda A. Newson, “The Demographic Collapse of Native Peoples of the Americas, 1492–1650” (1993).** A concise, comparative survey of regional variation and recovery. It is the best short correction to a single hemispheric percentage.
- **David S. Jones, “Virgin Soils Revisited” (2003).** The strongest critique of the “no immunity” slogan and a careful review of the evidence for social and environmental causes. It is a synthetic article, not a new archival study.
- **Noble David Cook, *Born to Die* (1998).** A disease-centered account of the conquest that nonetheless attends to regional variation. It was consulted through abstracts and reviews for this chapter, so its page locators must be checked before quotation.

### Specialized studies

- **Suzanne Austin Alchon, *A Pest in the Land* (2003).** Argues that Indigenous mortality rates were comparable to those of other populations exposed to new infections, and that colonialism explains the failure to recover. It was consulted through the publisher’s abstract for this chapter.
- **Paul Kelton, *Epidemics and Enslavement* (2007).** Shows how the Indigenous slave trade spread disease in the Native Southeast. It is a regional study, not a hemispheric synthesis; it was consulted through a review for this chapter.
- **Andrés Reséndez, *The Other Slavery* (2016).** Recenters Indigenous enslavement in the colonial catastrophe. It was consulted through reviews for this chapter; its argument should be read in full before the chapter is checked.
- **Michel Gagné, “Hochelaga,” *The Canadian Encyclopedia* (2013; updated 2026).** A careful account of Cartier’s visit, the village, and the disappearance of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians. It states the uncertainty rather than solving it.

> *A closing note.* The two records that anchor the chapter do not speak in the same voice. At L’Anse aux Meadows, the past is low walls, a bog, a brook, and a date fixed by tree rings; the builders left no written account. In the *Florentine Codex*, it is a Nahua memory of pustules, hunger, and the failure of care, compiled decades later under a Franciscan’s direction. One record is silent; the other is mediated. Together they show that contact was not a single event and that the evidence is never neutral. The chapter ends with the dead and the debate over how to explain them, not with a verdict.[^ch02-lam-canadianencyclopedia][^ch02-dumbarton-florentine]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with the Two Row Wampum, a living diplomatic tradition whose association with the Dutch in 1613 must be assessed rather than assumed. Its dating and documentary associations belong to Chapter 3. This chapter ends with the Atlantic world in 1551; Chapter 3 turns to the Indigenous political worlds the newcomers did not erase.[^ch02-check-ch3]
