## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The object.** In February 1900 the Eastman Kodak Company launched the Brownie, a box camera five inches long, three inches high, and three inches wide, made of jute-board reinforced with wood and covered with imitation leather. It had a simple rotary shutter and an f/14 meniscus lens and made six 2 ¼-inch square negatives on what became 117 roll film. It sold for one dollar, and the company could make it for about fifty cents.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-gac]
- **The name and the market.** Eastman named the camera after the “Brownie” characters of the Canadian illustrator Palmer Cox, hoping to appeal to children; the company soon found that adults were buying the camera for themselves. The Brownie was advertised in the popular press, and more than 100,000 were sold within a year.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-franklin]
- **The system behind it.** The Brownie was not a single invention but the consumer end of a system Eastman had built since the 1880s: dry plates, roll film, and processing and printing by mail. The company’s slogan, “You press the button, we do the rest,” described a division of labor that left the chemistry to Kodak.[^ch28-brownie-acs][^ch28-brownie-neh]
- **The surviving object and its advertising.** The George Eastman Museum holds a 1900 Brownie (accession 1978.1657.0002, wood, leather, metal, and glass, 4 × 3 ⅛ × 5 in.) with its character box and accessory finder. A Kodak advertisement for the Brownie from 1900, credited to the George Eastman Museum and reproduced through Google Arts & Culture, states that the camera “could be made inexpensively for 50 cents and sold for $1.”[^ch28-brownie-object][^ch28-brownie-gac]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the launch date, the price, the materials, the film format, the name’s origin, the advertising claims, and the first-year sales. It cannot show the specific photographs any one owner took, the household budgets that did or did not stretch to a dollar plus film and processing, or how any buyer understood what she or he was doing.[^ch28-check-scene][^ch28-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In February 1900 no one knew that the Brownie would still be sold in the 1950s, that it would make snapshot photography a mass habit, or that a later generation would treat its images as historical evidence. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch28-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A shared form with a price.** The scene’s claim is that the Brownie made a formerly elite and technical practice into something almost anyone could try, and that this widening of access was real but bounded. A dollar was a day’s or more wages for many workers, film and processing cost more, and the camera was marketed to the families who could afford the whole system.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-acs][^ch28-check-scene]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question: the same years that produced a mass press, mass magazines, mass goods, and mass entertainment also produced new ranks and exclusions within that audience. The Brownie is the object that lets the chapter ask whether shared forms dissolved boundaries or reorganized them.[^ch28-check-scope][^ch28-check-scene]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: did a national audience dissolve social boundaries or reorganize them?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the camera to the newspapers, the catalogs, the colleges, the churches, and the laboratories of the same decades.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented child photographer.** The Stage 1 seed referred to an invented twelve-year-old and her six photographs; that scene is removed. The chapter may describe the object, its advertising, and its documented sales, but it may not stage a named or composite user, her thoughts, or the images she made.[^ch28-check-scene]
- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The launch date, price, materials, and sales are documented. The mood of a shop, the sound of a shutter, and the private reasoning of buyers or of Eastman must not be staged beyond what the record states.[^ch28-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** In 1900 no one knew that the Brownie would define amateur photography for half a century. The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of later outcomes.[^ch28-check-scene][^ch28-check-dates]

## 1. Press, telephone, and national audiences

### 1.1 The daily press and the making of a mass readership

- **The claim.** City newspapers became mass commodities in these decades, and their competition for readers produced both a wider public and a new style of sensational reporting whose influence on events was real but limited.[^ch28-yellow-journalism][^ch28-newspapers-pbs]
- **The market.** The number of daily papers grew through the 1880s and 1890s, and by 1900 most cities of moderate size had competing morning and evening papers. Aggregate circulation had grown and would keep growing into the 1940s, even as the number of titles began to fall.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs]
- **Pulitzer, Hearst, and yellow journalism.** Joseph Pulitzer’s New York *World* and William Randolph Hearst’s New York *Journal* fought for the same readers. The term “yellow journalism” came from their contest over Richard F. Outcault’s “Hogan’s Alley” cartoon and its “Yellow Kid,” and it then named the sensational style the two publishers used in their coverage of Cuba in 1896–98.[^ch28-yellow-journalism]
- **The limits of the press.** The State Department’s account holds that yellow journalism “helped to create a climate conducive” to war but “did not by itself cause the war,” and it notes that other factors played a greater role. The chapter should present the press as an agenda-setting power without adopting the legend that Hearst manufactured the war.[^ch28-yellow-journalism]
- **What the record can show.** It can show circulation, ownership, format, and content. It cannot show what every reader believed, which stories persuaded a given voter, or the private intentions of a publisher beyond the record’s evidence.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs][^ch28-check-silences]

### 1.2 The magazine revolution, the telephone, and rural delivery

- **The claim.** Two technologies and one price war reorganized the national audience in the 1890s: the cheap illustrated magazine, the telephone, and rural free delivery each stretched the reach of common information beyond the city and the neighborhood, though unevenly.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-telephones-pbs][^ch28-rfd-postal]
- **The magazine revolution.** Frank Munsey cut *Munsey’s* to ten cents in 1893 and its circulation rose from 40,000 to 500,000 in 1895 and 700,000 in 1897. *McClure’s*, founded in 1893, cut its price and reached about 250,000 by 1896, and the *Ladies’ Home Journal* passed half a million by 1900. These magazines carried national advertising and a mix of fiction, news, and departments that presupposed a general reader.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-mcclure-britannica]
- **A revolution in reading.** The American Antiquarian Society’s account, drawn from Frank Luther Mott’s 1954 study, calls the new ten-cent magazines “a revolution in magazine publishing and reading,” built on new engraving processes, new advertising, and hard times, and appealing to middle-class self-culture before it became a vehicle for the muckraking of the next decade.[^ch28-magazines-aas]
- **The telephone.** By 1900 the country had about 1.35 million telephone lines, roughly 18 lines for every thousand people, and almost 8 million calls a day, mostly for business. The telephone was not yet ordinary household equipment; that came by 1940. The chapter should give the 1900 figure and note that access was concentrated in cities and offices.[^ch28-telephones-pbs]
- **Rural free delivery.** Experimental rural free delivery began in 1896, and the service became an official part of the Post Office Department in 1902. It let farm families receive mail at home rather than travel to a country store, and it joined the catalog and the magazine in bringing the same goods and texts to isolated households.[^ch28-rfd-postal]
- **Figure 6.** The registered visual `ch28-figure-01` (Figure 6, “A national audience, 1870–1900”) belongs here. It should show newspaper circulation per person and telephone counts across the period, mark the missing years honestly rather than interpolate them, and state the observation years, units, and denominators. Its source targets are the Census volumes and Ayer directories, with App. B, Table 7 for the communications series; the exact series and a defensible per-person denominator remain to be fixed.[^ch28-check-figure][^ch28-check-numbers]

## 2. Catalogs, department stores, sport, vaudeville, and photography

### 2.1 The mail-order catalogue and the reach of goods

- **The claim.** The mail-order catalogue carried the department store’s selection to households that had no store, and it made price and choice the same in a Kansas farmhouse as in a city block.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-sears-archive]
- **The catalogue.** The 1897 Sears, Roebuck catalogue ran to hundreds of pages and sold everything from an eighteen-pound chest of tea for fifty-two cents to organs, pianos, firearms, sewing machines, and new “safety” bicycles at twenty-five to fifty-six dollars. Its size and ubiquity made it, in one reader’s phrase, a “cornucopia” of what Americans wanted and could imagine owning.[^ch28-sears-engine]
- **What the catalogue can show.** It can show prices, goods, and the claims of its advertisers. It cannot show what any household actually bought, on what credit, or what a reader made of the abundance on the page.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-check-silences]
- **The catalogue and the post.** The catalogue depended on the same postal system that rural free delivery extended, and it turned rural households into a national market. The chapter should connect §1’s delivery with §2’s goods rather than treat either alone.[^ch28-rfd-postal][^ch28-sears-engine]

### 2.2 The department store and the woman consumer

- **The claim.** The department store made shopping a pleasure and a public activity, and it recruited the same single women it addressed as customers, so that consumption and women’s work grew together.[^ch28-shopping-smithsonian]
- **The store as palace.** Marshall Field in Chicago, Wanamaker in Philadelphia, and Macy’s in New York built palatial stores that offered wide selections at set prices and encouraged shoppers to spend the day. Field added a bargain basement for less affluent buyers, and stores used pneumatic tubes and centralized cash offices to supervise money and to hire less skilled clerks.[^ch28-shopping-smithsonian]
- **The woman behind the counter and in front of it.** Department stores employed growing numbers of single women, who also became consumers, and owners sought middle- and upper-class customers with money to spend on impulse. Working-class people generally lacked the time to shop as a leisure activity, and the chapter should mark that limit rather than generalize the “shopper” to everyone.[^ch28-shopping-smithsonian][^ch28-check-gender]

### 2.3 Sport, vaudeville, and commercial amusements

- **The claim.** Sport and entertainment became organized, commercial, and national in these decades, and their promoters deliberately recast rowdy amusements as respectable middle-class leisure.[^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-vaudeville-arizona][^ch28-coney-parks]
- **Baseball.** The National League of Professional Base Ball Clubs was formed on February 2, 1876, by owners who separated management from play, restricted membership to one club per city, and raised the admission price to fifty cents to price out the rough crowd and attract a higher-income audience. The league’s own history shows a business built on monopoly and “conspicuous consumption” as much as on sport.[^ch28-baseball-sabr]
- **Vaudeville.** B. F. Keith worked to gentrify variety entertainment into “wholesome” family fare, opening a Boston dime museum in 1883 and a theater that drew on the “continuous” variety show. His career shows the industry’s bargain: cultural respectability sold tickets to the middle class even as it disciplined the performers and the audience.[^ch28-vaudeville-arizona]
- **Amusement parks.** Paul Boyton fenced and charged admission at Coney Island’s Sea Lion Park in 1895, and George C. Tilyou opened Steeplechase Park in 1897 with twenty-five attractions for twenty-five cents. Tilyou had introduced a Ferris wheel on the site in 1894, inspired by the wheel at Chicago’s 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition, and the park drew crowds estimated in the tens of thousands on hot days.[^ch28-coney-parks]
- **The Wild West show.** William F. “Buffalo Bill” Cody opened Buffalo Bill’s Wild West on May 19, 1883, at Omaha, an outdoor variety show of frontier vignettes that toured nationally and abroad. The chapter should name the Lakota and other Native performers as participants and as people whose participation was itself contested, and should leave the history of conquest to Chapter 25.[^ch28-buffalo-bill][^ch28-check-ch25]

### 2.4 Photography and the snapshot

- **The claim.** Photography passed in a single generation from a professional craft to an amateur habit, and the Brownie completed a system Eastman Kodak had assembled before it.[^ch28-brownie-acs][^ch28-brownie-smok]
- **The system.** Eastman introduced roll film from paper in 1885 and the transparent celluloid negative soon after, the handheld Kodak camera in 1888, and the Brownie in 1900. Each step lowered the skill and the cost required to take a picture and increased the demand for Kodak film and processing.[^ch28-brownie-acs]
- **The consequence.** By 1900, photography could be done by “anyone” in the company’s advertising, and millions of snapshots entered family life as a new kind of personal record. The chapter should treat the snapshot as both a widening of expression and a commercial form shaped by what the camera and the marketplace made easy to photograph.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-bicycle-smithsonian]

### 2.5 In Focus: the bicycle pages of the 1897 Sears catalogue

**In Focus.** The chapter’s close reading is a page or a spread of bicycle advertising in the 1897 Sears, Roebuck catalogue, the registered visual `ch28-image-01` (Image 4). The page does two things at once. It prices a new consumer technology precisely — the safety bicycles sold for a substantial twenty-five to fifty-six dollars — and it genders the purchase by showing men’s and women’s frames, clothing, and accessories. The bicycle was the decade’s great consumer good and its great symbol of mobility: the Smithsonian’s account notes that the safety bicycle gave women personal mobility outside the home, that the craze boosted the “rational clothing” movement, and that bicycles remained expensive, so most who took part were white and affluent. Read together, the catalogue page and the bicycle craze show the chapter’s pattern: a shared commodity that widened access while carrying the era’s prices, gender rules, and racial limits inside it. The box should quote the catalogue’s prices, note that it is a sales document and not a record of what was bought, and treat the pictured clothing and frames as evidence about how the market imagined its buyers, not as proof of how every reader lived.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-sears-archive][^ch28-bicycle-smithsonian][^ch28-check-focus]

The exact edition and page of the bicycle spread, and the scan credit, remain unresolved and must be fixed before the In Focus is drafted. ## 3. Realism, pragmatism, and religion

### 3.1 Realism and the truth of the present

- **The claim.** American writers turned from romance toward the close observation of ordinary life, and their realism was a claim about how the modern world should be described.[^ch28-howells-wsu][^ch28-check-literature]
- **Howells and the realists.** William Dean Howells, the era’s most influential editor and critic, defined realism as “the truthful treatment of material,” and the novels of the 1880s and 1890s — by Howells, Mark Twain, Henry James, and, more harshly, by Stephen Crane and Frank Norris — took contemporary society as their subject. The chapter should present realism as a contested program, not as the natural way to write.[^ch28-howells-wsu]
- **The naturalist turn.** By the 1890s a younger generation pushed realism toward naturalism, in which character was shaped by environment and heredity. The chapter should note the turn and its determinism without turning the literature into a history of ideas alone.[^ch28-howells-wsu]

### 3.2 Pragmatism and the American philosophical temper

- **The claim.** American philosophy in these decades abandoned the search for fixed foundations for a method that tested ideas by their consequences, and this pragmatism was both a technical philosophy and a popular attitude.[^ch28-james-will][^ch28-peirce-sep][^ch28-dewey-sep]
- **Peirce, James, and Dewey.** Charles Sanders Peirce stated the pragmatic maxim in 1878: the meaning of an idea lies in its practical consequences. William James carried it to a wide readership, and in “The Will to Believe” (1896) he argued that in some choices “our passional nature” must decide, since the evidence cannot. John Dewey built instrumentalism out of the same tradition in Chicago in the 1890s.[^ch28-peirce-sep][^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]
- **The quotation.** James wrote that not only do we find “our passional nature influencing us in our opinions,” but that “there are some options between opinions in which this influence must be regarded both as an inevitable and as a lawful determinant of our choice.” The chapter should quote the sentence from the 1896 edition and mark its argument as a defense of the right to believe, not a claim that all beliefs are true.[^ch28-james-will][^ch28-check-quotations]
- **The stakes.** Pragmatism in these years was a way of living without certainties, and the chapter should connect it to the era’s other uncertainties — in religion, in science, and in the economy — rather than present it as a school exercise.[^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]

### 3.3 The churches, Darwin, and the social gospel

- **The claim.** American Protestantism both absorbed and resisted the new science, and the same decades produced a socially engaged gospel and an organized opposition to Darwinism.[^ch28-rauschenbusch-yawp][^ch28-darwin-pew][^ch28-moody-moody]
- **Moody and mass revivalism.** Dwight L. Moody, a Chicago evangelist, perfected the mass revival and used the era’s business methods to reach vast audiences. By the end of the century he and other popular preachers were attacking Darwinism as a threat to biblical truth and public morality.[^ch28-moody-moody][^ch28-darwin-pew]
- **The social gospel.** Walter Rauschenbusch’s ministry in New York’s Hell’s Kitchen from 1886 to 1897 and his later *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) argued that Christianity must transform social relations, not merely individual souls. The book appeared after 1900, so the chapter should use it as the maturing statement of a movement that took shape in the 1890s, not as contemporary evidence of 1890.[^ch28-rauschenbusch-yawp][^ch28-check-dates]
- **The coming split.** From the 1890s to the 1930s the major Protestant denominations divided into modernist and evangelical camps, a schism driven by new science, new questions about the Bible, and new social theories. The chapter should present the split as beginning in these years and culminating later, and should not read the later fundamentalist-modernist controversy back into 1890.[^ch28-darwin-pew]
- **Vice and the moral border.** The Comstock Act of 1873 gave federal force to a private moral campaign: Anthony Comstock’s New York Society for the Suppression of Vice pressed Congress to bar “obscene” matter and contraceptives from the mail. The statute shows the era’s confidence that a shared moral community could be policed, and its vagueness shows how much that community disagreed with itself.[^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-comstock-pbs]

## 4. Research universities, medicine, and racial science

### 4.1 The research university and the doctorate

- **The claim.** The American university was remade in these decades around research and graduate training, and the new institutions concentrated the authority to define knowledge in a credentialed few.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hopkins-medicine]
- **Johns Hopkins and its imitators.** Johns Hopkins University opened in 1876 under Daniel Coit Gilman with 54 graduate students and combined the German research model with American features. It awarded its first PhD in 1878 and by 1889 had produced 151 doctorates, more than Harvard and Yale combined, and its graduates carried the model to other universities. Clark University opened in 1889 as the first all-graduate institution, and the University of Chicago opened in 1890 under William Rainey Harper.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond]
- **The widening and its limits.** Land-grant colleges and new state universities extended higher education, and Black institutions such as Hampton and Tuskegee built a separate system of practical training. Tuskegee opened on July 4, 1881, under Booker T. Washington and emphasized trades and self-help. The chapter should treat these as part of the same expansion of schooling and should leave the Jim Crow order to Chapter 26.[^ch28-tuskegee][^ch28-check-ch26]

### 4.2 Medicine and the professions

- **The claim.** Medicine reorganized itself around laboratory science and university training, and in doing so it raised standards and narrowed who could practice.[^ch28-hopkins-medicine][^ch28-flexner]
- **The Johns Hopkins model.** The Johns Hopkins Hospital opened in 1889 and the School of Medicine in 1893, recruiting William H. Welch, William Osler, William S. Halsted, and Howard Kelly, and requiring a college education and examinations in basic science for admission. It made bedside teaching and laboratory research central and created the clinical residency. Women were admitted on the same terms as men.[^ch28-hopkins-medicine]
- **The later reckoning.** The Carnegie Foundation’s Flexner Report of 1910 surveyed the medical schools and found most inadequate, holding up Johns Hopkins as the model. The report belongs to the next decade, and the chapter should name it as the consequence of reforms begun in the 1890s, not as a fact of them.[^ch28-flexner][^ch28-check-dates]

### 4.3 Racial science, eugenics, and classification

- **The claim.** The same decades that professionalized the sciences also produced a “scientific” ranking of peoples, and this racial science was not marginal to the era’s thought but central to it.[^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-eugenics-genome]
- **Eugenics and Social Darwinism.** Francis Galton coined the word “eugenics” in 1883 and defined it as the science of improving stock; the idea spread to the United States, where it was later institutionalized in Charles Davenport’s Eugenics Record Office (1910). Social Darwinism supplied a language for ranking races and classes, and eugenics supplied a program. Most of the American institutional apparatus came after 1900, and the chapter must label it as a consequence while showing its 1890s roots.[^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-eugenics-pbs][^ch28-darwin-pew]
- **Race Traits and its critics.** Frederick L. Hoffman’s *Race Traits and Tendencies of the American Negro* (1896) used census and insurance statistics to argue that Black Americans were degenerating and would die out. W. E. B. Du Bois reviewed it in 1897 and showed that Hoffman misused the statistical method, comparing incomparable groups and reading general conclusions from city death rates; Kelly Miller published a fuller critique the same year. The exchange is the chapter’s clearest evidence that racial science was contested from the start.[^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman]
- **How to handle it.** The chapter should present the racial science as a historical object to be explained and criticized, quoting its degrading claims only where the argument requires them and explaining in the same paragraph why they are reproduced. It should use Du Bois and Miller as the era’s own critics, and should not let the later discrediting of eugenics excuse the era’s scientists.[^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman][^ch28-check-race]

## 5. Women, education, suffrage, and household change

### 5.1 The women’s colleges and the “New Woman”

- **The claim.** Women entered higher education in numbers in these decades, and their entry was treated as a dangerous experiment that might unfit them for marriage and motherhood.[^ch28-womenscolleges][^ch28-gfwc]
- **The colleges.** Mount Holyoke (1837), Vassar (1865), Wellesley (1870), Smith (1871), and Bryn Mawr (1885) built a system of women’s higher education, and state universities became coeducational. Founders and critics alike feared that college might unhinge women, and the early campuses were designed to supervise them closely.[^ch28-womenscolleges]
- **The “New Woman” and the clubs.** The educated “New Woman” became a cultural type, and women’s clubs gave her a public life; the General Federation of Women’s Clubs was formed on April 24, 1890, when 63 clubs ratified a constitution. The clubs turned private reading and reform into organized public work.[^ch28-gfwc][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia]

### 5.2 Suffrage and the temperance alliance

- **The claim.** Suffrage was reorganized on a national footing in 1890, but the movement’s strategy and its coalition were contested, and its gains by 1900 were western and partial.[^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-willard-nwh]
- **NAWSA.** The National Woman Suffrage Association and the American Woman Suffrage Association merged in 1890 to form the National American Woman Suffrage Association, with Elizabeth Cady Stanton as president and Susan B. Anthony leading the work; Anthony served as president from 1892 to 1900 and trained Carrie Chapman Catt and Anna Howard Shaw as successors.[^ch28-nawsa]
- **The WCTU.** Frances Willard led the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union from 1879 until her death in 1898, making it the largest women’s organization in the country. She argued that women needed the vote to make and keep their towns dry, and she drew many women into public life who might not have joined a suffrage society. The WCTU linked temperance, suffrage, and a broad program of social reform.[^ch28-willard-nwh][^ch28-willard-dpla]
- **The gain and the limit.** By 1900 women had won the vote in several western states, but the national amendment and much of the South remained out of reach, and the Nineteenth Amendment is completed in Chapter 31. The chapter should state the partial result without reading 1920 back into 1900.[^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-check-ch31]

### 5.3 The household, contraception, and Black women’s clubs

- **The claim.** The household itself changed as women’s education and paid work expanded, and the era’s law and medicine restricted the knowledge women could get about controlling their own fertility.[^ch28-gilman][^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-nacw]
- **Gilman and the economics of the family.** Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s *Women and Economics* (1898) argued that women’s economic dependence on men stunted both women and society and called for women’s entry into productive work. The book is the era’s sharpest statement of the case that the household was an economic institution, not a private refuge.[^ch28-gilman]
- **The Comstock limit.** The Comstock Act barred contraceptives and information about them from the mail, and the law did not clearly define what “obscene” meant. Doctors and reformers who gave women information about pregnancy and contraception faced fines and prison, so the era’s widening of education stopped short of reproductive knowledge.[^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-comstock-pbs]
- **Black women’s clubs.** Because white suffrage organizations largely ignored the challenges Black women faced, Black women built their own federation. The National Association of Colored Women was founded in July 1896, with Mary Church Terrell as its first president, and it pursued a broad reform program alongside suffrage. The chapter should treat it as part of the era’s women’s movement and leave the southern racial order to Chapter 26.[^ch28-nacw][^ch28-check-ch26]
- **The connection.** The chapter should close §5 by connecting the “New Woman,” the store clerk, the temperance organizer, and the clubwoman as overlapping figures in a single transformation of women’s public lives, while stating who was excluded and how.[^ch28-check-gender][^ch28-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The audience widened.** The press, the magazines, the telephone, the mails, the catalogue, the store, the sport, and the amusement park reached more people in 1900 than in 1870, and the Brownie made a camera an ordinary household object.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-sears-engine]
- **The widening carried hierarchies.** The same forms that widened access carried new ranks and exclusions: fifty-cent and higher ticket prices, store departments for the wealthy and the basement for the less affluent, a camera that cost more than many could spare, and magazines that ignored Black life under Jim Crow and trivialized women.[^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-shopping-smithsonian][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia]
- **Culture stratified as well as spread.** The decades saw both a mass culture and the emergence of high and low categories, and Levine’s account of “sacralization” describes the second without denying the first.[^ch28-levine]
- **Science both advanced and ranked.** The research university and the reformed medical school raised standards, and the same era’s racial science ranked peoples and helped justify exclusion.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman]
- **The moral community was contested.** The Comstock Act shows a confident attempt to police a shared morality, and the ambiguity of “obscene” shows how little agreement lay beneath it.[^ch28-comstock-act]
- **Women’s public lives changed.** Women entered colleges, clubs, and paid work in growing numbers and reorganized the suffrage movement, though the vote remained partial in 1900.[^ch28-womenscolleges][^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-willard-nwh]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Democratization or hierarchy?** One position holds that the era’s commercial culture gave ordinary people more choices and more shared experience; another holds that it stratified taste, audiences, and authority into highbrow and lowbrow. Levine, Peiss, and Kasson disagree about how much agency popular audiences had, and the chapter should state each position at its strongest.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-peiss][^ch28-kasson]
- **Who made the new culture?** Some accounts stress the producers — publishers, promoters, universities, and advertisers; others stress the audiences and consumers, especially working-class women and immigrant and Black communities, who used commercial leisure for their own purposes. The record over-represents the producers.[^ch28-peiss][^ch28-ohmann][^ch28-check-silences]
- **Was the culture “incorporated”?** Alan Trachtenberg’s account folds culture into the corporate reorganization of American life; T. J. Jackson Lears’s account finds in antimodernism a therapeutic accommodation rather than a resistance. The two agree that culture and capitalism were entangled and disagree about what that meant for the people who lived it.[^ch28-trachtenberg][^ch28-lears]
- **How should the racial science be weighed?** One reading treats eugenics and scientific racism as a marginal pseudoscience that the era’s real scientists rejected; another treats them as mainstream products of the same institutions that professionalized science. The evidence of Du Bois’s and Miller’s early critiques supports the second reading and complicates the first.[^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman][^ch28-gould]
- **When did the mass culture begin?** Some accounts locate it in the 1890s magazine and catalogue revolution; others find its fuller form in the movies, radio, and advertising of the twentieth century. The chapter’s 1900 endpoint is a planning choice, and anything after it must be labeled as consequence.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-check-dates]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the era’s shared forms were at once democratizing and stratifying, and in which access is not the same as authority. The chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-peiss][^ch28-trachtenberg][^ch28-check-sources]
- **Access and authority.** A person might read the same magazine, ride the same bicycle, and sit in the same park as a wealthier neighbor and still not share the neighbor’s authority over what counted as culture, who was admitted, or who was classified as fit.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-hoffman-jstor]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The republic assembled a national audience in these decades and organized that audience at the same time, with a new cultural hierarchy above it and scientific classifications cutting through it. The chapter’s synthesis should hold the widening and the ranking together without claiming that one was the other’s hidden purpose.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-trachtenberg][^ch28-check-table]

## Legacies

The forms built in these decades outlasted the people who made them. The mass circulation magazine, the national advertisement, the mail-order catalogue, the department store, the amusement park, and the snapshot became ordinary parts of American life in the twentieth century, and the Brownie remained in production in new versions into the 1950s. The national audience that the 1890s assembled would be reassembled by the movies, the radio, and television.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-brownie-smok]

The cultural hierarchy that Levine describes did not vanish. The categories of highbrow and lowbrow persisted, and the argument over them returned in later debates over the canon, the curriculum, and public funding for the arts. The chapter should present that contested memory as part of the history, with the evidence on each side.[^ch28-levine]

The sciences of race left the heavier legacy. The eugenic program that grew from 1890s roots produced state sterilization laws and immigration restriction in the twentieth century, and its victims were counted in the tens of thousands before the movement was discredited. The university and the reformed medical school, meanwhile, became the model for American higher education and professional training, and the authority they concentrated in credentialed experts is part of the government and the culture that the following chapters describe.[^ch28-eugenics-pbs][^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-flexner]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The cheap magazine, the mass press, the bicycle, the department store, and the eugenic idea were not uniquely American; they circulated through the same Atlantic economy that carried goods and people. The chapter should place the comparison in a note rather than claim that the forms were identical or that one country caused the other.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Editors, publishers, professors, and manufacturers left abundant records, while the reading, listening, and buying of ordinary people, and especially of Black and immigrant audiences, survive unevenly. The catalogues and the magazines show what was offered, not what was taken. The chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-check-silences]

## Summary

- In February 1900 Eastman Kodak launched the Brownie, a one-dollar box camera that made six negatives on roll film and sold more than 100,000 in its first year, widening photography into a mass amateur practice.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-franklin]
- The daily press grew into a mass commodity, and the competition of Pulitzer’s *World* and Hearst’s *Journal* produced “yellow journalism,” which shaped but did not by itself cause the war with Spain.[^ch28-yellow-journalism][^ch28-newspapers-pbs]
- The ten-cent magazine revolution of the 1890s, led by *Munsey’s*, *McClure’s*, and the *Ladies’ Home Journal*, created a national reading public and national advertising; by 1900 there were about 1.35 million telephone lines, and rural free delivery, begun in 1896, carried mail to farm households.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-mcclure-britannica][^ch28-telephones-pbs][^ch28-rfd-postal]
- The 1897 Sears catalogue sold from an eighteen-pound chest of tea to safety bicycles at twenty-five to fifty-six dollars, and the department store made shopping a pleasure and employed the women it addressed as customers.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-shopping-smithsonian]
- The National League was formed in 1876 around forty-cent and fifty-cent admissions and business monopoly; B. F. Keith gentrified vaudeville for a family audience; and Coney Island’s Sea Lion Park (1895) and Steeplechase Park (1897) began the modern amusement park.[^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-vaudeville-arizona][^ch28-coney-parks]
- Howells defined realism as “the truthful treatment of material,” and a younger generation turned toward naturalism; Peirce, James, and Dewey built pragmatism and instrumentalism out of the test of consequences.[^ch28-howells-wsu][^ch28-peirce-sep][^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]
- Dwight L. Moody perfected mass revivalism and attacked Darwinism, while Walter Rauschenbusch’s social gospel argued that Christianity must transform social relations; from the 1890s the Protestant denominations began to divide into modernist and evangelical camps.[^ch28-moody-moody][^ch28-rauschenbusch-yawp][^ch28-darwin-pew]
- Johns Hopkins opened in 1876 as the first American research university and, with Clark and the University of Chicago, established the PhD and the research faculty; its hospital (1889) and medical school (1893) remade medical education, a reform the Flexner Report would codify in 1910.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hopkins-medicine][^ch28-flexner]
- Francis Galton coined “eugenics” in 1883, and the American movement that grew from these roots was institutionalized after 1900; Frederick Hoffman’s *Race Traits* (1896) used statistics to argue that Black Americans were degenerating, and Du Bois’s and Miller’s 1897 reviews showed the misuse of the method.[^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman]

> *A closing note.* In the last three decades of the nineteenth century the United States built a national audience and then organized it. The press, the magazine, the telephone, the mail, the catalogue, the store, the park, and the camera reached more people than ever before and gave them shared forms. But the same decades sorted those forms into high and low, priced some people out of the new pleasures, ignored Black life and trivialized women in the very pages that claimed to speak to everyone, and produced a science that ranked human beings as fit or unfit. The chapter’s question — did a national audience dissolve social boundaries or reorganize them? — is answered by a record in which the widening and the ranking happened together.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-hoffman-jstor]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at the White House on October 3, 1902, when Theodore Roosevelt summoned the operators and the owners of the anthracite coal mines to settle a strike that had paralyzed the winter’s fuel. The audience that the 1890s had assembled for goods and images would now be asked to follow a president into the reform of industrial society. Chapter 29 takes up the Progressive movement — its reformers, its experts, its regulations, and its exclusions — and asks who benefited when public authority was enlarged.[^ch28-check-ch29][^ch28-check-bridge]
