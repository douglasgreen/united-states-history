## Epigraphs

> “We, Willem Kieft, director general, and the council of New Netherland, having considered the petition of the Negroes named Paulo Angola, Big Manuel, Little Manuel, Manuel de Gerrit de Reus, Simon Congo, Antony Portuguese, Gracia, Piter Santomee, Jan Francisco, Little Antony and Jan Fort Orange, who have served the Company for 18 or 19 years, that they may be released from their servitude and be made free.”[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]

> “WHEREAS some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree, Be it therefore enacted and declared by this present grand assembly, that all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother.”[^ch05-ev-1662]

> “Barbados, the beautiful Caribbean island known today for its social amiability and political civility, was Britain’s colonial site of the first ‘black slave society’ — the most systemically violent, brutal and racially inhumane society of modernity.”[^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

- **Why these three.** The 1644 petition presents enslavement as a temporary service that ends in promised freedom; the 1662 statute makes bondage follow the mother and so makes it inheritable; Beckles locates the model of a hereditary, racialized slave society in the Caribbean, not the Chesapeake. The epigraphs disagree about where racial slavery came from and whether it was chosen, inherited, or “unthinking” — and that disagreement is the chapter’s problem.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. The 1644 line is an English translation of a Dutch council record; the wording and translation should be confirmed against the source before quotation in type. The 1662 act survives in printed session laws, not in a signed holograph. Beckles’s line is a historian’s retrospective judgment, from the preface to his study of Barbados.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom][^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-aaihs-beckles]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that every New Netherland African lived in “half-freedom,” that the 1662 act alone created racial slavery, or that mainland slavery was simply copied from Barbados. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and the people.** New Netherland’s enslaved men and women helped build the colony that became New York. The New Netherland Institute’s digital exhibition names Manuel de Gerrit de Reus, Sijmon Congo, and Paulo Angola among the first enslaved Africans to live in the colony, brought there only a year or two after the first European families and held as company slaves.[^ch05-nni-slavery]
- **The arrival dates are not precise.** The exhibit dates the first enslaved laborers “as early as 1625”; other accounts place first arrivals in 1626, the year the company organized Manhattan. The scene must say “by 1626” or “in the mid-1620s” rather than date a single landing, and must not stage a ship’s arrival the records do not describe.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-check-scene]
- **The work.** Under Director General Peter Stuyvesant, enslaved Africans labored as caulkers making boats watertight, blacksmiths, bricklayers, and masons, and on company farms that fed the colony and grew food for export. The company and private owners alike used enslaved labor in a colony that did not rest on a single cash crop.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- **The condition was unlike the later plantation South.** New Netherland’s enslaved population lived, worked, and worshipped beside free White settlers. Some earned wages, owned property, married and baptized children in the Dutch Reformed Church, obtained conditional freedom, and received farmland in Manhattan.[^ch05-nni-slavery]
- **The documentary turn.** On February 25, 1644, eleven enslaved men and their wives were granted “half-freedom” by the Dutch West India Company after petitioning the Council. They cited long service and broken promises and claimed the company had promised them their freedom. The grant imposed conditions: they would pay yearly dues, assist the company when needed, and their children — including those not yet born — would remain enslaved. Half-freedom was therefore neither freedom nor ordinary slavery.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]
- **The silence and the mediation.** The enslaved did not leave written records of their own; they survive in court records, land deeds, church records, and official correspondence kept by the people who owned them. The scene may read what those records say and what they cannot confirm; it may not invent thoughts, dialogue, or a tableau of petitioning.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A record, not a tableau.** The scene should open on the company’s resolution of February 25, 1644, and the men whose names it lists, not on a reenactment of a hearing. The document is the evidence and the story.
- **Enslavement was not yet hereditary.** The 1644 grant shows a labor regime in which bondage existed but was not fixed for life or across generations in the way it would be after the 1660s. That contrast is the chapter’s spine: the same decades that produced half-freedom in New Netherland produced partus sequitur ventrem in Virginia.
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: How did a labor regime become hereditary racial slavery?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that slavery was always about race or that it hardened by accident. Commercial demand supplied the workers; law and political choice fixed their children’s status and shrunk the paths out.
- **The move south and to the islands.** The scene sits in New Netherland in 1644. The framing paragraph follows immediately and sends the reader to the Caribbean sugar islands, whose codes were already writing the model the mainland would adopt.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles][^ch05-un-slavecode]

### What must not be invented

- No invented ship, dock, weather, clothing, dialogue, or ceremonial manumission. The 1644 grant is a council resolution, not a described event.[^ch05-check-scene]
- No claim that the earliest arrivals came on one identified date or ship. The surviving accounts do not fix the year precisely.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-check-scene]
- No claim that “half-freedom” meant liberty. Its conditions kept the men bound to company service and their children in slavery.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]
- No claim that New Netherland was free of racial hierarchy or that the Dutch treated the enslaved as equals.[^ch05-check-names]
- No claim that the mainland colonies copied Barbados directly and mechanically; the chapter argues connection, not a single origin.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

### Drafting target

Three hundred to four hundred words. Open on the 1644 resolution and the named men. State what the record shows about their condition — company slavery, wages, church membership, half-freedom — and what it cannot. Date the earliest arrivals loosely. Note that the grant’s conditions left their children enslaved. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows and supplies the years, scope, and limits.

## 1. New Netherland and New Sweden

### 1.1 A company colony and its enslaved laborers

- **The claim.** New Netherland was a trading colony, not a plantation colony, but slavery was present from its first years and its enslaved people built the settlement’s infrastructure. Their condition in the Dutch period was real bondage with more room to bargain than the law would later allow.
- **The founding.** The Dutch West India Company (WIC) organized the colony, founded New Amsterdam on Manhattan, and made it the seat of government. Peter Minuit, the colony’s third director, is associated with the 1626 purchase of Manhattan from its Native inhabitants.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- **The first Africans.** The WIC introduced enslaved Africans into New Netherland soon after the first European families; the New Netherland Institute dates the first enslaved laborers as early as 1625 and names Manuel de Gerrit de Reus, Sijmon Congo, and Paulo Angola among them. Most came through the intercolonial or transatlantic slave trade.[^ch05-nni-slavery]
- **The labor.** The colony’s economy did not rely on a cash crop, but the enslaved were valuable to its growth. They worked as caulkers, blacksmiths, bricklayers, and masons, cleared land, and grew food on company and private farms for the local market and export.[^ch05-nni-slavery]
- **The law and half-freedom.** New Netherland had no comprehensive slave code. On February 25, 1644, eleven enslaved men and their wives won half-freedom after petitioning the Council; their children remained enslaved. Some Africans earned wages, held property, and married in the Dutch Reformed Church. The contrast with the Caribbean and Chesapeake codes is the chapter’s point of departure, not evidence that Dutch slavery was mild.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]
- **Stuyvesant’s role.** Petrus (Peter) Stuyvesant served as director general until the English seized the colony in 1664. He made New Amsterdam more orderly and economically successful and oversaw major efforts to expand the colony’s role in the Atlantic trade in enslaved Africans, while sometimes compromising with leading residents and Indigenous powers.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- **The consequence.** In New Netherland, enslavement existed without a hereditary, racialized code. That is the baseline the rest of the chapter measures against.

### 1.2 The Swedes, the Dutch, and the Delaware valley

- **The claim.** Sweden planted a short-lived colony on the Delaware in 1638, and the Dutch absorbed it in 1655. The lower Delaware valley then passed through Dutch and English hands, carrying slavery with it.
- **The founding.** The New Sweden Company was formed in 1637 by Dutch, German, and Swedish investors, and Peter Minuit — the former Dutch director — led its first expedition. Minuit and his settlers sailed from Gothenburg in December 1637 and landed on the Delaware in the spring of 1638, where they bought from five Lenape chiefs sixty-seven miles of riverfront centering on the Christina River and built Fort Christina.[^ch05-psu-newsweden]
- **The colony.** New Sweden was a small trading and farming colony of Swedish and Finnish settlers along the Delaware, commercially modest and diplomatically dependent on the Lenape and the Dutch. It never rivaled New Netherland in wealth or population.[^ch05-psu-newsweden]
- **The end and the succession.** The Dutch under Stuyvesant conquered New Sweden in 1655 and absorbed it into New Netherland. In 1664 the English took New Netherland, and the Delaware valley passed to English proprietors. The region’s European governments changed; its dependence on unfree labor did not.[^ch05-psu-newsweden][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- **The consequence.** The Middle Colonies were a patchwork of charters and conquests, and slavery existed in all of them. That variety, not an absence of slavery, sets up §4’s comparison.

### 1.3 The English conquest and the persistence of slavery

- **The claim.** The English conquest of 1664 made New Netherland into New York and kept its enslaved population in bondage. The English did not free the Dutch colony’s slaves; they inherited and continued the labor system.
- **The conquest.** In 1664 the English seized New Amsterdam, which was renamed New York after the Duke of York, and the Dutch surrendered the colony. The city changed hands without a battle.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- **The continuity.** The English took over an enslaved population and a port already involved in the Atlantic trade in enslaved Africans. The institution passed from Dutch to English rule.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- **The consequence.** By the 1660s the Middle Colonies held enslaved people under several European flags, and the legal hardening to come would reach them all. The chapter should present the Middle Colonies as participants in the same Atlantic story as the Chesapeake and the islands, even though they lacked a plantation staple.[^ch05-check-names]

## 2. Barbados, Jamaica, and the Atlantic trade

### 2.1 The sugar revolution in Barbados

- **The claim.** Barbados converted from tobacco and White indentured labor to sugar and enslaved African labor in the middle decades of the seventeenth century, becoming the model of a racialized slave society that the mainland colonies borrowed from and traded with.
- **The settlement.** English settlement began in 1627 with private capital and the Crown’s blessing. Early tobacco profits, earned before Virginia’s large-scale production depressed European prices, helped finance the shift to sugar.[^ch05-bbc-barbados]
- **The turn to sugar.** English planters began growing sugarcane in Barbados in the 1640s; Dutch traders introduced sugarcane and windmill technology for processing it. As sugar production intensified in the 1640s and 1650s, planters replaced White indentured servants with enslaved Africans, and small landholdings gave way to large plantations.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-ldhi-barbados]
- **The population shift.** On the eve of the sugar boom, Barbadian “Negro slaves” numbered in the low thousands; within a generation the island’s enslaved population had grown to the tens of thousands while the White population, free and indentured, stagnated or fell. The island became what Beckles calls Britain’s colonial site of the first “black slave society.”[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-aaihs-beckles]
- **The consequence.** Barbados demonstrated that sugar grown by enslaved Africans could produce enormous wealth. That demonstration, and the planters it enriched, shaped every later colony the chapter discusses.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

### 2.2 The Barbados slave code of 1661

- **The claim.** Barbados passed a comprehensive slave code in 1661, only thirty-four years after settlement. It defined Africans as property and out of the protections of English law, and it became a template for mainland statutes.
- **The code.** The Barbados Assembly’s 1661 code was the first comprehensive slave law in the English colonies. It defined Africans as “heathens,” tied their bondage to their descent and status rather than their religion, and treated enslaved people as chattel property subject to their owners’ absolute authority. It passed less than half the time it took Virginia to adopt comparable law.[^ch05-rugemer][^ch05-un-slavecode]
- **The model.** Mainland colonies drew on the Barbadian example. Carolina’s leaders modeled their slave code almost verbatim on the laws of Barbados, and the code’s logic — hereditary, racial, and total — traveled with Barbadian planters who settled on the mainland.[^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]
- **The caution.** The chapter should not claim that one island’s code alone created mainland slavery. Barbados supplies the clearest early model and the strongest evidence that the law was made deliberately, not by accident.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles][^ch05-rugemer]
- **The consequence.** By 1661 the English Atlantic already had a comprehensive racial slave code. The Virginia and Maryland statutes of the 1660s are part of that legal movement, not isolated inventions.[^ch05-un-slavecode]

### 2.3 Jamaica, the Royal African Company, and the Atlantic circuit

- **The claim.** England’s capture of Jamaica in 1655 and the chartering of the Royal African Company in 1672 made enslaved African labor the center of England’s Atlantic empire and tied the mainland colonies to a single provisioning system.
- **Jamaica.** In 1655, under Cromwell’s Western Design, England seized Jamaica from Spain. Spanish colonists fled; some of the enslaved escaped into the mountains, and their communities became the basis of the Jamaican Maroons. Jamaica developed into a major sugar and slavery colony and a base for English privateering.[^ch05-rac-jamaica]
- **The company.** Charles II chartered the Royal African Company in 1672 and gave it a monopoly on English trade in West Africa, including the slave trade. Its earlier corporate predecessor, the Company of Royal Adventurers, had already realized that the money lay in supplying enslaved people to the West Indies and Virginia: by 1668 more than a quarter of the new company’s profits came from the slave trade.[^ch05-nps-rac]
- **The scale.** Between 1673 and 1688, during the years of its monopoly, the Royal African Company transported an estimated 90,000 to 100,000 enslaved Africans. Virginia and the Chesapeake received Africans through the company and through intercolonial traders.[^ch05-nps-rac]
- **The circuit.** The English Atlantic ran on enslaved labor: African captives were carried to the Caribbean and the mainland; sugar, tobacco, and provisions moved among the islands, the mainland, and England; and mainland colonies traded food and timber to the islands in return for sugar and cash. Map 5 shows this circuit.[^ch05-check-map]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the Caribbean and the mainland as one connected economy and one connected legal project, however differently their societies developed.

## 3. Chesapeake labor and Bacon’s Rebellion

### 3.1 The turn from servants to enslaved Africans

- **The claim.** In the later seventeenth century the Chesapeake shifted from White indentured servants to enslaved Africans. The shift was driven by the declining supply of English servants, the rising availability of enslaved Africans, the tobacco economy’s demand for labor, and planters’ pursuit of lifetime, inheritable workers.
- **The servant supply.** Indentured servitude had supplied most Chesapeake labor through the middle of the century. As English conditions improved and emigration fell, planters turned to the more expensive but permanent alternative taken from Africa.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-tcu-morgan]
- **The race line in law.** Virginia’s statutes increasingly distinguished servants by race: laws separated “Negroes” from normal legal protections, declared that Christian baptism did not free the enslaved (1667), and established a racial distinction between servants and slaves (1682). These acts are the Chesapeake’s version of the legal hardening under way in the islands.[^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]
- **The consequence.** By the 1680s the Chesapeake was becoming a slave society, not merely a society with enslaved people. The next subsection turns to the crisis that historians connect to that change.

### 3.2 Bacon’s Rebellion, 1676–1677

- **The claim.** Bacon’s Rebellion began as a dispute over Indian policy and became a civil war in Virginia. Disaffected planters, poor Whites, and enslaved Africans united under Nathaniel Bacon against Governor Sir William Berkeley, and the revolt unsettled the colony’s elite.
- **The origins.** The rebellion traces to 1675, when a trading dispute between the Doeg and the planter Thomas Mathew escalated into violence. Virginia militia pursued the Doeg into Maryland and attacked Susquehannock hunters, igniting the Susquehannock War. Virginians divided sharply over how to fight it.[^ch05-ev-bacon]
- **The leader.** Nathaniel Bacon, an ambitious recent arrival appointed to Berkeley’s Council, led unauthorized militia attacks against Native nations, including allies of the colony’s leadership, and then against Berkeley’s government. His followers included tobacco planters, poor Whites, and enslaved Africans.[^ch05-ev-bacon][^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]
- **The climax.** Berkeley abandoned Jamestown as Bacon’s forces besieged it; Bacon’s men occupied and burned the capital in September 1676. Bacon died of illness on October 26, 1676, and the rebellion collapsed, though fighting continued into 1677.[^ch05-ev-bacon][^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]
- **The aftermath.** Contemporary accounts disagreed about Bacon, seeing him either as the colonists’ guardian or as a self-serving demagogue. Later historians made the rebellion a turning point in the history of race.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]
- **The consequence.** The rebellion is both a documented event and a layered memory. The chapter must keep the event and the historiography apart.

### 3.3 The aftermath and the labor question

- **The claim.** Edmund S. Morgan argued that after Bacon’s Rebellion Virginia’s elite turned decisively toward racial slavery to divide poor Whites from Africans. The chronology is contested, but the law and the labor market moved together.
- **Morgan’s thesis.** Morgan argued that American slavery and American freedom emerged in the same place at the same time in colonial Virginia, and that the rebellion exposed the danger of an alliance between poor Whites and the enslaved. Elites responded by building a racial order that made Whiteness a badge of freedom and African descent a badge of bondage.[^ch05-tcu-morgan]
- **The qualifications.** The rebellion’s importance is debated, and the turn to enslaved Africans had economic causes as well: the declining supply of servants and the rising availability of African captives. The chapter should present Morgan’s thesis as a powerful argument, not a settled fact, and weigh the demographic and economic evidence beside it.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-check-dating]
- **The law after 1676.** Virginia restricted the vote to property holders and freeholders in 1670–71 and later sharpened the racial line among servants and the enslaved. These measures narrowed the political community as the labor regime hardened.[^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws][^ch05-tcu-morgan]
- **The consequence.** The Chesapeake is the mainland case where the legal question left open in Chapter 4 was answered. §5 reads the statutes that did it.

## 4. Maryland, Carolina, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware

### 4.1 Maryland’s first slave law

- **The claim.** Maryland practiced slavery from the 1640s but gave it legislative sanction only in 1664, when a growing African population, a tobacco economy hungry for labor, and questions about the status of mixed-race children forced the issue.
- **The timing.** In 1640 Maryland had about twenty Black people among roughly 600 colonists; by 1660 there were about 760 Black people among 8,500 — a shift from one in thirty to one in ten. The colony’s commitment to tobacco demanded cheap labor, and the incorporation of the Royal African Company in 1662 seemed to guarantee a supply of enslaved Africans.[^ch05-msa-maryland]
- **The 1664 law.** Maryland’s 1664 statute (“An Act concerning Negroes and other Slaves”) declared that all “negroes and other slaves” already in the province, and those later imported, should serve for life, and it addressed the status of children and the claims of baptized Black people. It has been described as the colonies’ first anti-amalgamation law.[^ch05-msa-maryland][^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]
- **The consequence.** Maryland’s path ran parallel to Virginia’s and used similar legal devices. The chapter should compare them rather than treat either as the single origin of American slavery.

### 4.2 Carolina and the Barbadian model

- **The claim.** Carolina was designed from the start around the West Indian model of enslaved African labor, and its proprietors recruited Barbadian planters and borrowed Barbadian law.
- **The founding.** Charles II granted Carolina to eight Lords Proprietors in 1663 (chartered again in 1665). Before the first successful settlement at Charles Town in 1670, the proprietors studied Barbados’s sugar plantation system and recruited White settlers from the island to launch the colony.[^ch05-ldhi-barbados]
- **The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, 1669.** The proprietors commissioned a framework of government drafted with the involvement of John Locke, secretary to the Earl of Shaftesbury. It provided that “every freeman of Carolina shall have absolute power and authority over his negro slaves, of what opinion or religion soever.” It went through several versions between 1669 and 1698.[^ch05-avalon-carolina][^ch05-slp-carolina]
- **The incentives and the code.** The proprietors offered settlers twenty acres for each enslaved Black male and ten for each enslaved Black female, and Carolina’s leaders modeled the colony’s slave code almost verbatim on Barbados’s laws. Barbadian families such as the Middletons, Draytons, and Gibbes became prominent Carolina planters.[^ch05-ldhi-barbadians][^ch05-ldhi-barbados]
- **The economy.** Carolina’s planters first tried and failed at olives, grapes, mulberry, and English grains; they traded furs, produced tar and pitch for naval stores, and raised livestock. By 1690 they had applied the West Indian model to a new staple and begun commercial rice cultivation.[^ch05-ldhi-barbados]
- **The caution.** The Fundamental Constitutions were a plan, not a functioning government, and Locke’s precise responsibility for its slavery clauses is debated. The chapter should not overstate either his authorship or the document’s immediate effect.[^ch05-slp-carolina][^ch05-avalon-carolina]
- **The consequence.** Carolina shows the Caribbean and the mainland as a single migration of people, capital, and law.

### 4.3 Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware

- **The claim.** Pennsylvania, founded as a haven of religious freedom, also practiced slavery; New Jersey and Delaware placed slavery in their law. The same colony that tolerated Quakers produced the first formal antislavery protest in British North America.
- **Pennsylvania.** Charles II granted William Penn the colony in 1681. Penn guaranteed religious freedom to settlers and drew Quakers and other persecuted Europeans, but he and other Quakers enslaved Africans, and Penn owned enslaved people who worked at his estate. By the 1760s more than 4,000 enslaved people lived in Pennsylvania.[^ch05-pah-penn][^ch05-pah-penn-slaves]
- **The Germantown Protest, 1688.** On April 18, 1688, four German Quakers in Germantown — Garret Hendericks, Francis Daniel Pastorius, and the brothers Derick and Abraham op de Graeff, cousins to William Penn — petitioned the Germantown Monthly Meeting to protest Quaker slaveholding. It was the first protest of its kind in the British North American colonies. The petition asked Quakers to practice what they preached: “we shall doe to all men like as we will be done ourselves.”[^ch05-nps-germantown]
- **The Quaker response.** The petition reached the Yearly Meeting at Burlington, New Jersey, which voted not to take a stand for or against slavery. The protest was recorded and set aside; antislavery would not become Quaker policy for decades.[^ch05-nps-germantown]
- **New Jersey and Delaware.** New Jersey and Delaware were small proprietary and former Dutch and Swedish jurisdictions where slavery took root in law and economy alongside the larger colonies. The chapter should specify their legal instruments and demographics rather than fold them into Pennsylvania.[^ch05-pah-penn]
- **The consequence.** Pennsylvania exposes the gap between a promise of religious freedom and the practice of slavery, and it produces the era’s clearest antislavery argument. The chapter should hold both facts together.

## 5. Hereditary status in law

### 5.1 The statutes that made status heritable

- **The claim.** A cluster of statutes passed between 1661 and 1705 converted a labor system into a hereditary racial order. The most important provisions made bondage follow the mother, made baptism irrelevant to freedom, and made servitude permanent for Africans.
- **Barbados, 1661.** The Barbados code fixed Africans’ legal status as hereditary chattel and excluded them from the protections of English law, providing the earliest comprehensive model.[^ch05-un-slavecode][^ch05-rugemer]
- **Virginia, 1662.** Virginia’s act “Negro womens children to serve according to the condition of the mother” reversed the common-law presumption that a child’s status followed the father. It declared that “all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother,” and it doubled the fine for a Christian who committed fornication with a “negro man or woman.” The act enabled enslavers to reproduce their own labor force.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-gl-1662]
- **Maryland, 1664.** Maryland made “negroes and other slaves” serve for life and addressed the children of enslaved parents and the claims of the baptized.[^ch05-msa-maryland]
- **Virginia, 1667.** The act “declaring that baptisme of slaves doth not exempt them from bondage” closed the most common path to freedom under English practice, ensuring that converting to Christianity would not free an enslaved person.[^ch05-ev-1667]
- **Virginia, 1668 and 1682.** Later acts taxed free Black women as laborers and fixed a racial distinction between servants and slaves, narrowing the status of free Black people as well.[^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]
- **The consequence.** Each act closed a door: to birth-status freedom, to baptismal freedom, and to the blurred line between servant and slave. By the 1680s the legal category existed before the word “slave” was fully settled.

### 5.2 In Focus: Virginia’s 1662 maternal-status statute

**In Focus.** Virginia’s December 1662 act is short and decisive. It begins from doubt: “WHEREAS some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree.” The puzzle was real. Under English common law, a child’s status usually followed the father, so the children of an enslaved woman and a free father could claim freedom. The assembly cut through the doubt by reversing the rule: “all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother.” The same act doubled the penalty for a Christian who committed fornication with a “negro man or woman,” enforcing the boundary the heredity rule drew. Read closely, the act is a labor measure as much as a moral one. By making bondage pass through the mother, it guaranteed enslavers a self-reproducing workforce and ended the debate over mixed-status children. The statute did not use the phrase “hereditary racial slavery,” but it built one of its essential mechanisms. The chapter should quote the act from the printed session laws (Hening’s *Statutes at Large*, volume 2, page 170) and should note that Barbados had adopted a comprehensive code the year before, while New Netherland’s enslaved children remained in a different, contested condition.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-gl-1662][^ch05-un-slavecode][^ch05-check-focus]

### 5.3 The consequences of the legal regime

- **The claim.** By 1705 Virginia had gathered its scattered acts into a comprehensive code. The law now defined slavery by race and descent, made it lifelong and inheritable, and stripped the enslaved and free Black people of legal protections in ways that closely resembled the Barbados model.
- **The 1705 code.** “An act concerning Servants and Slaves” (1705) consolidated the earlier statutes and regulated the treatment, punishment, and legal status of enslaved people, including restrictions on testimony and movement.[^ch05-ev-1705]
- **Race in law.** The statutes increasingly defined slavery by African descent rather than by religion or birthplace. Race became the legal shorthand for a set of disabilities that had once been justified by “heathenism.”[^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws][^ch05-ev-1667]
- **The contrast with New Netherland.** Half-freedom, wage earning, and property holding had existed in the Dutch colony; the English and mainland codes narrowed those possibilities. The comparison shows that the outcome was chosen, not inevitable.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]
- **The consequence.** The chapter’s central claim — that a labor regime became hereditary racial slavery through law and political choice — is established by the statutes themselves. §6 weighs the competing explanations for why that happened.[^ch05-check-1662]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Racial slavery was made in law, not born fully formed.** The statutes of 1661–1705 created hereditary, racialized bondage; it did not exist as a complete system in 1619 or 1626.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-msa-maryland]
- **The Caribbean led.** Barbados passed a comprehensive slave code in 1661 and became the model that Carolina copied and the mainland drew on.[^ch05-un-slavecode][^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]
- **Sugar and the slave trade transformed the English Atlantic.** The sugar revolution, the capture of Jamaica, and the Royal African Company made enslaved African labor central to England’s empire.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-nps-rac]
- **The Chesapeake shifted from servants to enslaved Africans in the later seventeenth century.** Declining servant supply, rising African imports, and tobacco’s labor demand moved together.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]
- **Bacon’s Rebellion was real and consequential.** It became a civil war in Virginia, killed its leader in October 1676, and unsettled the colony’s elite.[^ch05-ev-bacon][^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]
- **Pennsylvania practiced slavery and produced the first antislavery protest.** The 1688 Germantown petition is the earliest formal protest of its kind in British North America.[^ch05-nps-germantown][^ch05-pah-penn]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Why the turn to racial slavery happened.** Morgan emphasizes elite political choice after Bacon’s Rebellion; Jordan emphasizes deep cultural attitudes toward Africans; economic historians emphasize the supply of servants and captives. The relative weight is not settled.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-check-dating]
- **When race prejudice began.** Whether anti-Black attitudes preceded and shaped slavery or emerged from it is a central disagreement between Jordan’s “unthinking decision” and later work stressing law and class.[^ch05-jordan][^ch05-handlin]
- **The importance of Bacon’s Rebellion.** Many historians treat it as a turning point; others question whether the chronology of the shift to enslaved Africans matches Morgan’s account.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory][^ch05-tcu-morgan]
- **The place of gender.** Brown argues that gender was constitutive of racial slavery, not incidental; others treat gender as one factor among several. The 1662 statute’s focus on mothers supports Brown’s emphasis.[^ch05-brown][^ch05-ev-1662]
- **The role of the Caribbean in mainland law.** Barbados clearly supplied a model; whether mainland statutes were primarily borrowed or independently developed is debated.[^ch05-rugemer][^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]
- **What the archives cannot show.** The enslaved left few records of their own; their experience reaches us through the people who owned them, and the chapter must not mistake the enslavers’ categories for the whole of the past.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-check-names]
- **Who the early Africans were.** Ira Berlin argues that the Africans of the seventeenth-century “charter generations” were often Atlantic Creoles — culturally and linguistically plural people with prior experience of European colonialism — and that their world differed from that of later plantation slaves. Whether the earliest mainland Africans are best understood this way, and how far it applies to the Caribbean, is open.[^ch05-berlin]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The choice is not between race and class, or between culture and economics. The evidence supports a history in which commercial demand supplied the workers, cultural attitudes made them available for racialized bondage, and law and political choice fixed the status and made it inheritable.
- **Morgan’s contribution and limit.** Morgan connected the Chesapeake’s freedom to its unfreedom and gave the turn to racial slavery a political motive. His chronology and his emphasis on elite choice have been questioned, and his Virginia-centered argument underplays the Caribbean and the Middle Colonies.[^ch05-tcu-morgan]
- **Jordan’s contribution and limit.** Jordan’s *White Over Black* took seriously the cultural and psychological dimensions of English attitudes toward Africans and Blackness and named the “unthinking decision.” Later scholars charged that his framework understated law, labor, and the deliberate making of status.[^ch05-jordan]
- **Brown’s contribution.** Brown put gender and patriarchy at the center of the making of racial slavery and read the laws — including the maternal-status acts — as instruments of a gendered order as well as a racial one.[^ch05-brown]
- **The gradualist corrective.** The Handlins argued that racial slavery emerged gradually from the decay of the servant system rather than from deliberate design; their chronology has been largely rejected, but their attention to the blurry boundary between servant and slave remains a useful check on over-tidy turning-point stories.[^ch05-handlin][^ch05-billings]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, the positions say that racial slavery was made, not discovered, and that it was made in several places at once — Barbados, Virginia, Maryland, Carolina — through choices that can be traced in statutes and court records. New Netherland’s half-freedom shows that the outcome was contingent.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom][^ch05-check-sources]
- **The lesson.** The best history refuses the single-cause story and the single-origin story alike. The chapter’s job is to show the choices, the alternatives, and their cost.

## Legacies

The statutes of the 1660s left a regime that outlived the English Atlantic empire. By making bondage heritable through the mother and independent of religion or birthplace, Virginia, Maryland, Carolina, and Barbados created a racial order that the eighteenth-century plantation economy would deepen and that the new republic would inherit. Slaveholders had not invented the practice of enslaving people, but they invented a law that reproduced it across generations by design.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-msa-maryland]

The Caribbean’s legacy reached the mainland two ways. Barbadian planters, capital, and law moved to Carolina; and the sugar colonies supplied the model of a society openly organized around the ownership of human beings as chattel. What mainland colonists often called a “peculiar” institution was, in its origins, a Caribbean export as much as a Chesapeake development.[^ch05-ldhi-barbadians][^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

Bacon’s Rebellion became a theme in American memory. Invoked as evidence that racial slavery was deliberately chosen to divide the poor, it has been read and reread for three centuries, its meaning shifting with each generation’s politics. The rebellion’s afterlife is as revealing as the event.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]

Pennsylvania’s Germantown Protest left a quieter legacy. The Quakers who wrote in 1688 had no power to end slavery, and the Yearly Meeting set their petition aside. But the argument they made — do to others as you would have them do to you — gave antislavery a text and a precedent that later generations would recover.[^ch05-nps-germantown]

The legacies are double, as in earlier chapters. The same decades that made hereditary slavery into law produced the first formal protest against it, and the same Atlantic economy that enriched planters impoverished the people it enslaved. The chapter should end with both possibilities intact, not with a verdict about which better represents the era.

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** New Netherland, New Sweden, Barbados, Jamaica, and the mainland all sat inside one English and Dutch Atlantic economy of sugar, tobacco, provision crops, and enslaved people. The Royal African Company supplied Virginia and the islands; Dutch traders introduced sugar technology and enslaved Africans to Barbados; Barbadian planters carried their law and laborers to Carolina.[^ch05-nps-rac][^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]
- **Archives and silences.** The enslaved appear in the records of the people who owned them — inventories, deeds, court minutes, church registers. The 1644 petitioners are known by the names their enslavers recorded; the children who remained enslaved are mostly numbers. The chapter should name these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom][^ch05-nni-slavery]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 4 supplies the 1619 arrival and Virginia’s labor question; Chapter 6 owns King Philip’s War, the Pueblo Revolt, and the imperial turn; Chapter 7 owns Stono and colonial society; later chapters carry the regime built here into the revolution, the republic, and the Civil War. This chapter’s job is to explain how a labor system became hereditary racial slavery in law.[^ch05-check-ch4][^ch05-check-ch6]

## Summary

- New Netherland, a Dutch company colony, held enslaved Africans from the mid-1620s; eleven men and their wives won half-freedom in 1644, but their children remained enslaved.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-nni-half-freedom]
- New Sweden, planted on the Delaware in 1638, was absorbed by the Dutch in 1655; the region passed to English rule in 1664 with slavery intact.[^ch05-psu-newsweden][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- Barbados shifted from tobacco and White servants to sugar and enslaved Africans in the 1640s and 1650s and became the model of a racialized slave society.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-ldhi-barbados]
- The Barbados slave code of 1661 was the first comprehensive slave code in the English colonies and a template for mainland law.[^ch05-un-slavecode][^ch05-rugemer]
- England captured Jamaica in 1655, and the Royal African Company, chartered in 1672, transported an estimated 90,000 to 100,000 enslaved Africans during its monopoly years.[^ch05-rac-jamaica][^ch05-nps-rac]
- The Chesapeake shifted from indentured servants to enslaved Africans in the later seventeenth century, and Virginia’s statutes increasingly distinguished servants by race.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]
- Bacon’s Rebellion (1676–1677) became a civil war in Virginia; Bacon burned Jamestown in September 1676 and died that October.[^ch05-ev-bacon][^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]
- Maryland gave slavery legislative sanction in 1664, making “negroes and other slaves” serve for life.[^ch05-msa-maryland]
- The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina (1669) granted freemen “absolute power and authority over his negro slaves,” and Carolina’s code was modeled on Barbados.[^ch05-avalon-carolina][^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]
- Pennsylvania, founded as a haven of religious freedom, also practiced slavery; its 1688 Germantown Protest was the first formal antislavery protest in British North America.[^ch05-pah-penn][^ch05-nps-germantown]
- Virginia’s 1662 act made bondage follow the mother; its 1667 act made baptism irrelevant to freedom; the 1705 code consolidated a racial slave regime.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-ev-1667][^ch05-ev-1705]
- Historians disagree about whether class, culture, gender, or gradual legal change best explains the origins of racial slavery, but they agree that law made the status heritable.[^ch05-check-table][^ch05-check-sources]

> *A closing note.* The chapter’s subject is a set of short statutes that look technical and were not. In 1662 Virginia’s assembly answered a doubt about the children of enslaved mothers by making bondage pass down the maternal line; in 1667 it declared that baptism changed nothing; in 1664 Maryland made Africans serve for life; in 1661 Barbados had already written the model. None of these acts invented human bondage, and none used the modern phrase “racial slavery.” Together they built a machine that reproduced itself: a status that would be inherited, justified by descent, and defended as natural. The chapter begins in New Netherland, where an enslaved man could win “half-freedom” and where the law had not yet closed around his children, and it ends with the statutes that closed it.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-ev-1667][^ch05-msa-maryland]
