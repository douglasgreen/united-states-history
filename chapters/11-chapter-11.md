# Chapter 11. The War for Independence (1775–1783)

> “The disturbances in America give great trouble to all our Nations.”[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]
>
> — Thayendanegea (Joseph Brant), statement of March 14, 1776, as quoted by the Museum of the American Revolution[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]

> “I do hereby farther declare all indented servants, Negroes, or others (appertaining to rebels) free, that are able and willing to bear arms, they joining his Majesty’s troops, as soon as may be…”[^ch11-gilder-dunmore]
>
> — John Murray, Earl of Dunmore, “A Proclamation,” Williamsburg, November 7, 1775, printed in the *Pennsylvania Journal*, December 6, 1775[^ch11-gilder-dunmore]

> “We wish that our conduct, in maintaining inviolate our loyalty to our Sovereign, against the strong tide of oppression and tyranny, which has almost overwhelmed this Land…”[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration]
>
> — “The Loyalist Declaration of Dependence,” subscribed by 547 inhabitants of the City and County of New York, November 1776[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration]

> “This was quite simply a civil war — and routinely described as such by contemporaries on both sides of the Atlantic.”[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]
>
> — Maya Jasanoff, *Liberty’s Exiles: American Loyalists in the Revolutionary World* (2011), as excerpted by the Museum of the American Revolution[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]

The four quotations disagree about whose war this was. For Thayendanegea, a Mohawk leader, the disturbances gave “great trouble to all our Nations,” because independence would be decided on Native land. For Dunmore, Virginia’s royal governor, the war was a chance to preserve the empire by offering freedom to enslaved people who would bear arms for the king. The proclamation used the racial language of its time; the chapter quotes it as printed and uses “enslaved people” in its own prose. For the 547 New Yorkers who signed the Loyalist declaration, it was a war against “the strong tide of oppression and tyranny.” For Jasanoff, looking back, it was “quite simply a civil war.” The chapter keeps all four in view, because each explains something the others leave out.[^ch11-check-epigraphs]

The army was pinned against the water. On the night of August 29, 1776, some 9,000 Continental soldiers waited on Brooklyn Heights with the British army in front of them and the East River behind. Washington had brought them there. After the British evacuated Boston in March, he marched about 19,000 soldiers into Lower Manhattan, guessing that the next blow would fall on the port, and built forts on Brooklyn Heights across the East River. In early July a British fleet of some 400 ships carrying about 32,000 men under General William Howe arrived at Staten Island. One American thought the fleet looked like “all London afloat.”[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-usni-glover]

Howe moved on the night of August 26. His columns worked through the unguarded Jamaica Pass, and on August 27 they broke the American line. The British took General John Sullivan prisoner. Some 400 Maryland troops made a stand at the Old Stone House before the British took General William Alexander, Lord Stirling. Howe halted the fighting that afternoon and began digging in rather than storming the American works. Washington, who had crossed from Manhattan and watched the battle from a redoubt on Cobble Hill, chose to save the army rather than risk it.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn]

The crossing began on the night of August 29 and continued toward dawn. The work fell to the seamen of Brigadier General John Glover’s Marblehead regiment, the 14th Continental Regiment, fishermen and sailors from a Massachusetts fishing port. The regiment was one of the army’s first integrated units; roughly one-third of its ranks were recorded as of “dark complexion.” A contrary wind kept British warships from closing the East River, and darkness covered the boats.[^ch11-armyhistory-glover][^ch11-usni-glover]

According to eyewitnesses, a fog descended as the sun came up and the last men crossed. Washington was the last man to leave Brooklyn. The sources do not agree on the size of the armies or the cost. The Mount Vernon account lists about 300 Americans killed, 650 wounded, and 1,100 captured on Long Island, against 63 British killed and 314 wounded; the American Battlefield Trust counts roughly 30,000 engaged in all, about 10,000 Americans against 20,000 British, puts the evacuated force at 9,000, and gives about 2,000 American casualties. The range is part of the record.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-check-numbers][^ch11-check-scene]

The army was saved. New York was not. The city stayed in British hands until the end of the war, and the escape across the river bought the rebellion another chance rather than a victory. How did a weak rebellion survive and win a settlement?[^ch11-abt-brooklyn]

This chapter follows the war from the British campaign around New York in the summer of 1776 to the Treaty of Paris in 1783. The war had begun at Lexington and Concord in April 1775; this chapter takes up the fighting after independence was declared. It draws on the printed treaties and declarations, Loyalist petitions and confiscation records, the British evacuation records, including the register known as the Book of Negroes, the maps and returns of the campaigns, and the scholarship that has worked through the correspondence and journals of American, British, French, and German participants. It treats “independence,” “allegiance,” “loyalty,” “Native sovereignty,” “emancipation,” and “peace” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter establishes in use. It uses scholarship for what the records cannot show and names the silences left by archives that recorded enslaved and Indigenous people mainly through the records of officials, officers, and planters. It does not present the outcome as foreordained in 1776. Its dates are kept precise and reconciled with Appendix D (Chronology).[^ch11-check-scope][^ch11-check-names][^ch11-check-dates][^ch11-check-ch10]

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 weighs the resources of the two armies and the people who fought and followed them. §2 follows the loss of New York and the survival of the Continental Army through Trenton, Princeton, and Valley Forge. §3 turns to Saratoga and the French alliance. §4 examines the frontier war, the division of the Haudenosaunee, and the Loyalist war at home. §5 follows the fighting in the South, from Charleston and Camden to Cowpens and Guilford Courthouse. §6 reaches Yorktown and the peace of 1783. §7 asks how historians explain the war and what it settled and left open. The chapter begins on Brooklyn Heights, where an army escaped across the East River in the dark.

## 1. Resources and armies

### 1.1 The two armies and the German auxiliaries

The war began with an asymmetry that shaped every campaign. Britain began the campaign of 1776 with a professional army, the Royal Navy, and soldiers hired from German princes. After evacuating Boston in March, it assembled around New York a fleet of 10 ships-of-the-line, 20 frigates, and nearly 300 transports, and landed about 32,000 soldiers on Staten Island. The navy could move troops along the coast and close the approaches to any port it chose to attack. The army took New York, and the British held the city for the rest of the war.[^ch11-usni-glover][^ch11-abt-brooklyn]

Between 1776 and 1783 Britain hired an estimated 30,000 German soldiers under subsidy treaties with six territories in the Holy Roman Empire, chiefly Hessen-Kassel and Hessen-Hanau. Contemporaries called them all “Hessians,” a label that flattens a diverse body of men who kept their own records and included accompanying civilians and some Black recruits. The chapter uses the term only as the eighteenth-century shorthand it was.[^ch11-amrev-hessians][^ch11-ari-hessians]

The Americans fielded a small regular army and short-term militia, and a Congress with almost no power to tax or supply. That asymmetry shaped how the war was fought. The historian Andrew Jackson O’Shaughnessy argues that Britain fought “an army of conquest not of occupation,” which let the rebellion lose battles and still keep an army in the field by retreating, regrouping, and recruiting again. Neither side could end the war quickly. The American army’s survival, not its battlefield wins, became the measure of the rebellion.[^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy][^ch11-abt-trenton]

### 1.2 Supply, pay, and the crisis of the army

The Continental Army was chronically short of food, clothing, and pay, and its soldiers repeatedly had to choose between service and survival. The army drew provisions through state requisitions and a weak commissary. Paper money depreciated, pay fell in real value, and the crisis broke into mutiny: in May 1780 two Connecticut regiments at Morristown refused duty after days without meat, and on January 1, 1781, the Pennsylvania Line rose in one of the war’s largest mutinies. Congress’s inability to supply the army was the wartime face of the same weakness that Chapter 12 will examine in the Confederation.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge][^ch11-mutinies][^ch11-check-ch12]

Valley Forge is the best-documented test. Washington’s army marched into winter quarters there on December 19, 1777: more than 12,000 soldiers with about 400 women and children, who built some 1,500 to 2,000 log huts against a winter of thaw, rain, and shortage. Army records show an average daily ration of about half a pound of beef per man in January 1778, a food crisis in February when the men went days without meat, and 2,898 men listed unfit for duty for lack of clothing in early March. The park service cautions that the winter was neither the coldest nor the snowiest of the war, and that the romantic image of a helpless army in rags obscures the soldiers’ own work. The suffering was real, and it was measured.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

Baron Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge in February 1778 and drilled the army into a more cohesive force. The park service treats the encampment as the place where the army “emerged under Washington’s leadership as a cohesive and disciplined fighting force,” a judgment about the army’s later performance rather than proof that the winter itself won battles. Disease killed nearly 2,000 people during the encampment. The mutinies marked the limit of the army’s patience. Supply and pay were political problems, and they point forward to the debt that Chapter 12 will treat.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge][^ch11-mutinies][^ch11-check-ch12]

### 1.3 Who fought and who followed

Between about 5,000 and 8,000 people of African descent served with the Continental Army, and thousands more in state militias and at sea, while roughly 15,000 to 20,000 supported the British, the German corps, or the Loyalists. Washington at first barred their enlistment and tried to expel those already serving, then allowed free Black men already in service to remain. Enslaved men were largely excluded, though some states let owners free substitutes at enlistment, and Rhode Island for a time in 1778 offered freedom to enslaved men who enlisted. The 1st Rhode Island Regiment was for a time made up entirely of soldiers of African and Native American ancestry under white officers.[^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers]

The British opened that door the year before independence. On November 7, 1775, Virginia’s royal governor, John Murray, Earl of Dunmore, proclaimed martial law and offered freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants who would bear arms for the king. Within a month about 300 Black men had joined his “Royal Ethiopian Regiment,” which grew to some 800. The proclamation inspired thousands of enslaved people to seek freedom behind British lines, though it applied only to Virginia and offered freedom only to those who could bear arms. Estimates of how many reached the British range from about 20,000 to as many as 80,000 or 100,000, out of roughly 450,000 enslaved people in the thirteen colonies at the war’s start. Not all who reached British lines served as soldiers; many labored for the army, and others sought freedom without taking up arms. The higher figures are estimates rather than a counted total, and the chapter presents them as such.[^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers]

More than 250,000 Native Americans lived east of the Mississippi in 1775, in more than 80 nations. Both sides sought their support, and nations made different choices. The British had trade goods, a fleet, and some limits on colonial settlement, while an independent United States seemed to many nations the greater threat.[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]

Women and children followed and supplied the army. At Valley Forge hundreds of women and children lived in the encampment and took up labor the army needed. They were part of the army’s survival, not an afterthought.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

## 2. New York and survival

### 2.1 The fall of New York, 1776

In the second half of 1776 the British took New York and nearly destroyed the Continental Army, driving it across New Jersey into Pennsylvania. After the Brooklyn battle, Howe moved against Manhattan in mid-September. On November 16 a combined British and Hessian force of about 8,000 stormed Fort Washington, the last American stronghold on Manhattan, against about 3,000 defenders. The fort surrendered with about 2,800 men taken prisoner. Defeats at White Plains on October 28 and at Forts Washington and Lee in November forced Washington to retreat across New Jersey and into Pennsylvania.[^ch11-abt-fort-washington][^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn]

His force shrank to a few thousand, in threadbare clothing and many without shoes, and enlistments were set to expire. The occupation of New York became a Loyalist stronghold and a base for the rest of the war; the British held the city until 1783. The loss showed that the Americans could not hold a major port against a professional army with sea power.[^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-abt-fort-washington][^ch11-gotham-black-loyalists]

Map 9, whose northern panel is shared with Chapter 10, places New York in the campaigns that ran from Boston to Yorktown; its southern and alliance panel follows the war toward the Chesapeake and the Carolinas.[^ch11-check-map]

### 2.2 Trenton, Princeton, and the fight to keep an army

On Christmas night, December 25, 1776, Washington led a force of about 2,400 across the ice-choked Delaware River in a storm and marched some ten miles to surprise a Hessian garrison of about 1,500 at Trenton. A plan for three converging attacks failed when two columns could not cross; only the main body, with Washington, made it.[^ch11-abt-trenton]

The Americans captured the garrison and its stores with few casualties, then fought a delaying action and won a second victory at Princeton on January 3, 1777. The army took food and clothing it badly needed, and morale rose.[^ch11-abt-trenton]

The counterstroke did not win the war. It kept the rebellion’s army and its cause alive. Washington’s decision to fight after retreating, and to keep asking his men to stay, made survival a choice renewed under pressure, not a gift of geography or weather.[^ch11-abt-trenton]

### 2.3 The Philadelphia campaign and Valley Forge

In 1777 the British took Philadelphia, the American capital, but failed to destroy the army. Howe moved on Philadelphia by sea and beat Washington in the field; the British occupied the capital in September 1777. Washington’s army fought and then withdrew to winter at Valley Forge, about a day’s march away, rather than risk destruction.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

The encampment tested the army’s supply and endurance, as §1.2 describes, and von Steuben’s drills rebuilt its discipline.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

The British held a capital and a port but could not end the war. The Americans kept an army in being and, at Saratoga in the same year, won the victory that brought France in. The two campaigns of 1777, the one near Philadelphia and the one in the north, belong to a single strategic story.[^ch11-abt-saratoga][^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

## 3. Saratoga and alliance

### 3.1 Burgoyne’s campaign and the northern army

In 1777 Britain tried a three-part attack to occupy the Hudson River valley and isolate New England. Three armies were to converge near Albany. The northern prong under General John Burgoyne moved south from Canada with about 7,500 men and the Hudson valley as his route. By August he had taken Fort Ticonderoga and occupied Fort Edward, and after a detachment was beaten at Bennington he pushed on toward Saratoga.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]

General Horatio Gates commanded the Northern Department with about 8,500 men, supported by Benedict Arnold and Daniel Morgan’s riflemen, behind fortifications on Bemis Heights overlooking the Hudson. The armies fought at Freeman’s Farm on September 19; the British held the field at heavy cost. Burgoyne attacked again at Bemis Heights on October 7 and was repulsed, with Arnold prominent in the fighting. With no relief in sight, Burgoyne surrendered to Gates on October 17.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]

### 3.2 The Convention and its meaning

The surrender, known as the Convention of Saratoga, took Burgoyne’s army out of the war. The defeat of an entire British army lifted patriot morale and made the case that the Americans could beat a British field army.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]

The victory was a political event whose consequences outran its military size. Gates became the hero of Saratoga and later lost the southern command; Arnold was hailed for his bravery and later defected. No single general was the author of the outcome.[^ch11-abt-saratoga][^ch11-abt-camden]

### 3.3 The French alliance and the widening war

Saratoga brought France into the war as an ally, and the American conflict became a global war that Britain could not win on its own terms. On February 6, 1778, Benjamin Franklin, Arthur Lee, and Silas Deane signed a Treaty of Alliance and a Treaty of Amity and Commerce with France. The alliance barred either country from making a separate peace and left the door open for other European powers to join; the commerce treaty recognized the United States as an independent nation.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-founders-alliance]

The French foreign minister, the Comte de Vergennes, had wavered while the Americans lost in New York. Word of Burgoyne’s surrender in December 1777, and reports of British peace overtures to Franklin, led him to ally openly and not to wait for Spain. France had begun secret loans earlier.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance]

Spain entered the war against Britain on June 21, 1779, and the Netherlands came in as well, while France provided money, troops, and the fleet that made Yorktown possible. The alliance shifted the war’s scale; it was not merely a subsidy.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-abt-yorktown]

## 4. Frontier and civil wars

### 4.1 The Haudenosaunee confederacy divides

The war split the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee, turning a confederacy built on unity into a civil war in which nations fought on opposite sides. The Mohawk, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca generally sided with Britain, while the Oneida and Tuscarora allied with the United States. Many individuals and whole communities tried to stay neutral and were pulled in as the war reached their homelands. The Oneida’s missionary and adviser, the Reverend Samuel Kirkland, was among the influences drawing them toward the Americans.[^ch11-nps-oneida][^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

On August 6, 1777, members of different nations fought one another at the Battle of Oriskany, near Fort Stanwix. The park service calls the battle the event that “upended the Great Tree of Peace,” the confederacy’s founding covenant. Native nations were not a single actor in the war but several, with different goals and different fates.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-nps-oneida]

Thayendanegea, or Joseph Brant, a Mohawk leader, chose the British side early and became one of the war’s best-known Native commanders. His 1776 worry that “the disturbances in America give great trouble to all our Nations” states the dilemma the chapter keeps in view.[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]

### 4.2 The frontier war and the Sullivan–Clinton campaign

The war on the New York and Pennsylvania frontier was a war of raids, reprisals, and the deliberate destruction of Native homelands. Destructive raids by British-allied Native warriors and Loyalists on the frontiers of New York and Pennsylvania in 1778 crippled the Continental Army’s access to food and manpower and depopulated settlements. In 1779 Washington committed a large part of the army to breaking the Six Nations’ ability to fight.[^ch11-nps-sullivan]

General John Sullivan and General James Clinton led about 4,469 Continentals, with riflemen, militia, and Oneida allies, into the heart of Haudenosaunee country to destroy the villages and food supplies of the Cayuga and Seneca. Washington’s orders of May 31, 1779, called for “the total destruction and devastation of their settlements and the capture of as many prisoners of every age and sex as possible,” and directed that the country “may not be merely overrun but destroyed.” Sullivan reported burning more than 40 settlements and great stores of crops and food.[^ch11-nps-sullivan][^ch11-amrev-memory-wars]

As many as 5,000 people fled westward to the Niagara region; contemporary counts reported thousands of refugees at Fort Niagara by September 1779. The campaign did not end the frontier war and did not produce a Native peace. It cleared Native land for postwar settlement and opened the way for the land cessions and westward expansion that followed.[^ch11-amrev-memory-wars][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

The destruction of Haudenosaunee towns and food was, by modern standards, a war crime, and it worked as an act of conquest dressed as retaliation. The Oneida and Tuscarora alliance with the United States and the British alliances of the other nations were distinct decisions, not one Indigenous side.[^ch11-nps-sullivan][^ch11-amrev-memory-wars]

### 4.3 Loyalists, confiscation, and the war at home

The Revolution was also a war among Americans, in which Loyalists were organized, punished, dispossessed, and finally driven out. In late November 1776, 547 inhabitants of the City and County of New York signed a declaration to Admiral Richard Howe and General William Howe, pledging their loyalty “against the strong tide of oppression and tyranny” and asking for the restoration of peace within the empire. Loyalism was not one ideology: motives ranged from principle to habit to the belief that the colonies were better off in the empire, and the signatures show organization as well as conviction.[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]

New York built one of the harshest confiscation regimes. Provisional Committees of Sequestration in 1777 were followed by an October 1779 forfeiture act that declared listed Loyalists to have forfeited their property and banished them from the state. Confiscation laws criminalized dissent and raised revenue by redistributing Loyalist property. They made the Revolution, in the New York Public Library’s words, “a civil war” whose coercive measures muddled the later transition to government by consent.[^ch11-nypl-confiscation]

After defeat, about 60,000 Loyalists left the United States. Roughly 8,000 whites and 5,000 free Black people went to Britain; most of the rest went to Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Quebec; about 6,000 went to Jamaica and the Bahamas carrying some 15,000 enslaved people; and nearly 1,200 Black Loyalists went to Sierra Leone. Loyalists landed across the British Empire, and their departure reshaped Canada and the Caribbean.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]

The Loyalist experience shows that the war’s social result was not only expanded freedom but also exclusion and expulsion. The chapter keeps the Loyalist war and the Native war beside the better-known story of the thirteen colonies’ victory.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nypl-confiscation]

## 5. The southern war

### 5.1 The British southern strategy and the fall of Charleston

Britain shifted the war to the South after 1778, believing that Loyalists there would rally to the Crown. London expected the southern colonies’ cash crops and its supposed body of Loyalists to sustain a campaign, and British forces moved south. Freeing the enslaved workers of southern planters, and intimidating or alienating the backcountry population, undercut the Loyalist support the strategy assumed.[^ch11-abt-cowpens]

British forces captured Charleston in May 1780 and then established a post at Camden. In July General Horatio Gates marched into South Carolina; on August 16, 1780, Cornwallis routed him at Camden, where the militia fled, the Maryland and Delaware Continentals under Johann de Kalb fought on, and de Kalb was mortally wounded. The defeat cleared South Carolina of organized American resistance and opened the way into North Carolina.[^ch11-abt-camden]

The southern strategy drew Britain deeper into a region where the population was divided, and it produced a civil war among neighbors as much as a war between armies.[^ch11-abt-cowpens][^ch11-abt-camden]

### 5.2 The partisan war in the backcountry

Between the regular armies, the southern backcountry fought a brutal partisan war in which militia and irregulars on both sides raided, captured, and killed. The South Carolina backcountry was split between patriots and Loyalists, “neighbor pitted against neighbor,” with both sides organizing militia and mounting raids and reprisals. The American Battlefield Trust calls it a “civil war within a civil war,” a phrase that captures the neighbor-against-neighbor violence.[^ch11-abt-cowpens]

Patriot partisan leaders and their bands harried British posts and supply lines, and Loyalist militia answered in kind. The fighting produced massacres and reprisals on both sides that the chapter does not soften or sensationalize.[^ch11-abt-camden][^ch11-abt-cowpens]

British operations in the low country disrupted the labor force of southern planters, and enslaved people crossed to British lines seeking freedom under Dunmore’s and later proclamations. Their choices altered the war’s manpower and its meaning, and the chapter presents them as actors rather than as property in motion.[^ch11-abt-cowpens][^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-gotham-black-loyalists]

### 5.3 Greene, Cowpens, and Guilford Courthouse

After Camden, Nathanael Greene took command and fought a war of attrition that wore the British down without requiring a decisive battle. Greene replaced Gates about two months after Camden and split his force, sending Daniel Morgan across the Catawba to cut British supply lines.[^ch11-abt-camden][^ch11-abt-cowpens]

On January 17, 1781, Morgan’s mixed force of Continentals and militia defeated and nearly destroyed a British and Loyalist detachment under Banastre Tarleton at Cowpens. The victory was built on Morgan’s use of his militia and his knowledge of his men.[^ch11-abt-cowpens]

On March 15, 1781, Greene formed about 4,500 men in three lines to meet Cornwallis’s roughly 2,100 at Guilford Courthouse. Cornwallis won the field but lost about a quarter of his men and could not pursue. He withdrew to Wilmington and then shifted his operations to Virginia, a move that helped lead to Yorktown.[^ch11-abt-guilford]

Greene’s campaign shows the chapter’s larger point. The Americans did not need to win the big battles if they could keep an army and a cause alive, and the British could not replace what they lost.[^ch11-abt-guilford][^ch11-abt-cowpens]

## 6. Yorktown and peace

### 6.1 The war moves to Virginia

After Guilford Courthouse, Cornwallis took his army into Virginia, where geography, a French fleet, and a combined American and French army closed a trap. With his army unfit to chase Greene, Cornwallis moved to Virginia in the spring of 1781 and eventually occupied Yorktown, on a peninsula, to refit and await supplies and reinforcements from the Royal Navy.[^ch11-abt-guilford][^ch11-abt-yorktown]

Washington and the French commander, Jean-Baptiste Donatien de Vimeur, Comte de Rochambeau, chose to strike south rather than against New York because a French fleet under François Joseph Paul de Grasse, Comte de Grasse, would be available in the Chesapeake. Their combined armies marched hundreds of miles from north of New York City to Virginia.[^ch11-abt-yorktown]

Cornwallis’s position depended entirely on sea supply. Once the French controlled the bay, his army was isolated.[^ch11-abt-yorktown]

### 6.2 The siege and the surrender

On September 5, 1781, the French fleet kept the Royal Navy out of the Chesapeake at the Battle of the Capes, abandoning Cornwallis’s army. Allied forces reached Yorktown on September 28 and began siege works with French engineers; the bombardment began on October 9.[^ch11-abt-yorktown]

Cornwallis raised a flag of truce on October 17 and negotiated over two days. The British and Hessian garrison surrendered on October 19. Sources give Cornwallis’s force as about 9,000, with allied engaged totals near 28,900 counting American, French, and militia. The figures vary with what is counted, and the chapter gives them with their definitions.[^ch11-abt-yorktown][^ch11-check-numbers]

**In Focus.** The terms of surrender, the “Articles of Capitulation settled at Yorktown,” were negotiated between Washington, Rochambeau, and de Grasse on one side and Cornwallis and the naval commander Thomas Symonds on the other. Read closely, the articles show what each side cared about. Article I divides the prisoners: the land troops remain prisoners to the United States, the navy to the French. Article II delivers the artillery, arms, and public stores unimpaired. Article III fixes the ritual: the garrison marches out at two o’clock “with shouldered arms, colors cased, and drums beating a British or German march,” then grounds its arms. Article IV lets officers keep their side-arms and both officers and soldiers keep their private property, with the qualification that property belonging to inhabitants of the states “shall be subject to be reclaimed.” A separate dispute over the treatment of Loyalists ran through the negotiation; Washington allowed Cornwallis latitude to let some Loyalists depart. The document is a compact about honor, property, and allegiance as much as a record of defeat. It also exposes the position of Loyalists who had sided with the Crown: they asked the British to protect them, and the articles left their property open to reclamation by the states.[^ch11-mountvernon-capitulation][^ch11-gilder-yorktown][^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration][^ch11-check-focus]

Yorktown did not formally end the war, but it broke the British will to continue in America and set the terms for peace.[^ch11-abt-yorktown][^ch11-dos-treaty-paris]

### 6.3 The peace of Paris and the unfinished settlement

Formal negotiations began on September 27, 1782. Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, and John Jay reached preliminary articles signed in Paris on November 30, 1782, which took effect once Britain and France concluded terms. The definitive Treaty of Paris was signed on September 3, 1783.[^ch11-dos-treaty-paris][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris]

The treaty recognized United States independence and defined boundaries that opened western expansion. It provided for British evacuation of posts and access to the Mississippi, for payment of prewar debts, and for an end to retaliatory measures against Loyalists and their property. John Adams privately judged the definitive treaty “a Simple Repetition” of the provisional one, and understood that Britain, still the stronger power, would not grant more.[^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-masshist-treaty]

The treaty contained no terms for Britain’s Native allies, who had to negotiate their own peace with the United States. The first postwar treaty, at Fort Stanwix in 1784, imposed major land cessions on the four nations that had sided with Britain and left Oneida and Tuscarora lands insecure despite their alliance with the Americans. Enslaved people who had escaped to the British were not returned, and the British evacuated thousands of Black Loyalists from New York in 1783, recording them in the register called the Book of Negroes. The treaty’s article against carrying away “Negroes, or other Property of the American Inhabitants” was worked around by issuing certificates of freedom. Article V’s promise of restitution for Loyalist property was largely unenforced, and disputes over debts, posts, and Loyalists went unresolved until the Jay Treaty of 1794.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-masshist-treaty]

The peace ended the war without settling who belonged to the new republic or what would happen to the people the war had displaced. That unresolved settlement is the bridge to Chapter 12.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-check-ch12]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

The rebellion survived by keeping an army in being. The Americans lost New York and Philadelphia but preserved a force that could retreat, regroup, and recruit, while Britain could not destroy it.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy]

The win was international. American independence was secured as part of a world war, and the French fleet and army were essential at Yorktown.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-abt-yorktown]

The war divided Americans and nations. Loyalists were organized, dispossessed, and exiled, and the Haudenosaunee confederacy split and was destroyed as a power in its homeland.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

Enslaved people shaped the outcome. Self-emancipation and Black service on both sides changed the war’s manpower and enlarged the question of who the Revolution was for.[^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers]

The peace was incomplete. The treaty left Native nations, Loyalists, and enslaved people outside its settlement and generated disputes that outlasted 1783.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-masshist-treaty]

### 7.2 What is open

What won the war is open. Was the outcome produced by American resilience, by British strategy’s contradictions, by French intervention, by Native and Loyalist choices, or by some combination? The sources weight these differently.[^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy][^ch11-gaf-mackesy]

How much the war was a revolution is open. Did the war transform American society, or did it mainly secure independence for a society that changed more slowly? The answer depends on whether the measure is political structure, slavery, land, or ordinary people’s lives.[^ch11-kirkus-royster][^ch11-kirkus-taylor]

What the archives can show is open. Enslaved and Indigenous people appear in these records mainly through the documents of officials, officers, and planters. The Book of Negroes is a rare register compiled by the British and is a record of inspection as much as of freedom, and the chapter names those mediations.[^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-check-names]

What the numbers were is open. The strengths, casualties, and refugee counts vary by source: British strength near New York is given as 20,000 or 32,000; Long Island casualties differ; the number of enslaved people who escaped to the British is an estimate.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-check-numbers]

How to weigh the Loyalist and Native wars is open. Whether to treat these as parts of one Revolution or as distinct civil and imperial wars is a live interpretive choice.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the war was at once a revolution, a civil war, and a world war, and in which the political settlement must be read alongside its consequences for enslaved people, Loyalists, and Native nations.[^ch11-check-sources]

Charles Royster’s *A Revolutionary People at War* (1979) argues that Americans’ commitment to “virtue” and “freedom” collided with the demands of a standing army, and that only a minority served. The revolution’s ideals and its military practice pulled against each other. John Shy’s *A People Numerous and Armed* (1976) insists that war and society shaped each other and that “no other nation has had its official origin and constitutional preservation so clearly linked to warfare.” Shy’s Harmon Memorial lecture framed the war as a problem for the study of military and social history at once.[^ch11-kirkus-royster][^ch11-shy-review][^ch11-shy-harmon]

Maya Jasanoff’s *Liberty’s Exiles* (2011) treats the conflict as a civil war and follows the 60,000 Loyalists who left. Jasanoff calls it the longest war Americans fought before Vietnam and the bloodiest until the Civil War of 1861–1865. The New York confiscation records show how revolutionary governments coerced allegiance and redistributed property. This position restores the losers and the internally divided to the center.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nypl-confiscation]

Piers Mackesy’s *The War for America, 1775–1783* (1964) reconceives the conflict as a global imperial war seen from London. The National Archives likewise calls it “actually a world conflict” involving France, Spain, and the Netherlands. Andrew Jackson O’Shaughnessy’s *The Men Who Lost America* (2013) argues that Britain’s commanders were able rather than incompetent and that Britain fought “an army of conquest not of occupation,” which the rebellion could outlast.[^ch11-gaf-mackesy][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy]

Colin Calloway’s work on the Revolution in Indian country and Alan Taylor’s *American Revolutions* (2016) place Native nations, slavery, and westward expansion at the center, arguing that the Revolution’s liberty for some rested on the dispossession of others. Taylor’s reviewers note that his continental frame both widens the story and shifts attention away from the thirteen colonies’ politics.[^ch11-kirkus-taylor][^ch11-claremont-taylor][^ch11-check-sources]

Read together, these positions say that the war’s outcome was not one thing: independence was won in a global war, at the cost of a civil war at home and the destruction of Native power in the North, and it left slavery’s contradiction unresolved. The chapter presents the debate as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch11-shy-review][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris]

## Legacies

The war’s settlement was as consequential as its battles. The United States gained independence and a western boundary that opened the continent to settlement, but the peace imposed no terms on Britain’s Native allies, and the 1784 Treaty of Fort Stanwix demanded vast land cessions from the Haudenosaunee nations that had fought for Britain and left the American-allied Oneida and Tuscarora insecure.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

It left a contradiction at the heart of the republic. Enslaved people had seized the war as a chance at freedom, some 5,000 to 8,000 people of African descent had served the American cause, and the states north of Maryland began to abolish slavery, yet the institution survived in the South and expanded in the decades that followed. The Black Loyalist exodus, recorded in the Book of Negroes, carried the war’s promise of freedom across the British Empire.[^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers][^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-gilder-dunmore]

And it left a debt and an unfinished settlement. The Continental Army’s supply crisis, the mutinies, the unpaid soldiers, and the unresolved disputes over Loyalist property and British debts fed the crisis of the Confederation and the movement for a stronger national government that Chapter 12 follows.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge][^ch11-mutinies][^ch11-masshist-treaty][^ch11-check-ch12]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The war became a global conflict among Britain, France, Spain, and the Netherlands, and the French alliance was the single most important diplomatic success of the rebellion. The chapter places the American war inside the Atlantic imperial struggle, not outside it.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris]
- **Archives and silences.** Enslaved and Indigenous people appear in these sources mainly through the documents of officers, officials, and planters. The Book of Negroes is a rare register of Black lives compiled by the British, and it is evidence of inspection as much as of freedom; the chapter names the archive’s angle of vision.[^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-check-names]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 10 carries the road to independence; Chapter 12 carries the constitutional settlement; later chapters return to the dispossession of Native nations (Chs. 14 and 17) and to slavery (Chs. 16 and 20). The questions this chapter opens about who belonged to the republic run through the book.[^ch11-check-ch10][^ch11-check-ch12][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

## Summary

- Britain carried the war to New York in 1776 with a fleet and a professional army, supported over the war by about 30,000 hired German soldiers, against a Continental Army that Congress could barely supply.[^ch11-usni-glover][^ch11-amrev-hessians]
- The Americans lost New York and Philadelphia but kept an army in being through Trenton, Princeton, and Valley Forge.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-nps-valley-forge]
- Black soldiers served on both sides, and Dunmore’s 1775 proclamation and later British offers drew thousands of enslaved people toward British lines.[^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers][^ch11-gilder-dunmore]
- Burgoyne’s surrender at Saratoga on October 17, 1777, brought France into the war through the treaties of February 6, 1778, and the conflict became global.[^ch11-abt-saratoga][^ch11-dos-french-alliance]
- The Haudenosaunee confederacy split, and Washington’s 1779 Sullivan–Clinton campaign destroyed more than 40 villages and displaced as many as 5,000 people.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-amrev-memory-wars]
- Loyalists were organized, dispossessed under confiscation laws, and exiled; about 60,000 left the United States after the war.[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration][^ch11-nypl-confiscation][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]
- After Camden, Nathanael Greene fought a war of attrition and Morgan’s victory at Cowpens helped wear down Cornwallis, who won the field at Guilford Courthouse but lost a quarter of his men and then moved to Virginia.[^ch11-abt-camden][^ch11-abt-cowpens][^ch11-abt-guilford]
- The French fleet won the Battle of the Capes, and the allies besieged Yorktown until Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781, on articles that divided prisoners, delivered the stores, and preserved private property.[^ch11-abt-yorktown][^ch11-mountvernon-capitulation]
- The Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783, recognized independence and a western boundary but left Native nations, Loyalists, and enslaved people outside its settlement.[^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-masshist-treaty]

> *A closing note.* The War for Independence was won, but not in the way the word “victory” usually means. The Americans lost their largest port and their capital and rarely beat a British army in the open field. They survived because they kept an army in being, because Britain could not occupy a continent, and because France, then Spain, turned a colonial rebellion into a world war. The cost was borne unequally: Loyalists were dispossessed and driven out, the Haudenosaunee confederacy was broken and its homelands destroyed, and enslaved people who seized the war’s promise of freedom often had to leave the country to keep it. The peace of 1783 recognized a new nation and drew its boundaries, but it left the republic’s deepest questions, about slavery, about Native nations, and about who belonged, still open. That is why the chapter calls the American victory a settlement as much as a triumph.[^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]
