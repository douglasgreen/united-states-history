# A Nation in Question: A History of the United States

This is the canonical outline and production plan for a history written for attentive general readers, students, and listeners. The book contains an unnumbered Introduction, **exactly 50 numbered chapters, including the conclusion**, and Appendices A–E. The target is approximately 1,000 typeset pages, including notes, sources, illustrations, and reference material.

The title and publication metadata live in [metadata.yaml](metadata.yaml). The writing process, chapter architecture, evidence standards, and house style live in [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md). This README replaces the former separate outline. Appendix C will present the reader’s illustration catalog; it does not independently assign numbers or authorize additions.

## The book’s question and argument

**Guiding question:** How did a continent of Indigenous nations become a republic, a continental empire, and a global power, and who could claim membership, freedom, land, and authority within those changing arrangements?

**Provisional thesis:** The United States was repeatedly constituted through settlements that changed the terms of conflict without settling them permanently. Independence, emancipation, industrial growth, the New Deal, and the rights revolution created real possibilities and real exclusions. Neither progress nor betrayal alone explains the record. The book tests that interpretation against rival explanations and the choices available to people at the time.

Five through-lines connect the chapters:

1. **Land and environment:** settlement, Indigenous land management, agriculture, extraction, infrastructure, and climate.
2. **Liberty and unfreedom:** slavery, emancipation, labor, bodily freedom, punishment, and the limits of legal rights.
3. **Pluralism and belonging:** citizenship, migration, religion, race, gender, and the changing boundaries of “the people”.
4. **Capitalism and inequality:** production, credit, public subsidy, work, consumption, and the distribution of power and wealth.
5. **Power and the world:** Indigenous diplomacy, European empires, continental expansion, overseas empire, and superpower commitments.

The Introduction adds two methodological claims: settlements remained contested, and outcomes were contingent. These are propositions to argue, not conclusions to impose on every episode. The conclusion weighs what the preceding 49 chapters established.

### Scope, cutoff, and intentional overlaps

The narrative begins with the peopling of the Americas and ends with evidence available through **October 29, 2026**. The anniversary is a framing occasion, not permission to invent a ceremony or predict a result. Claims about 2025–2026 in the first draft require fresh verification; this planning revision does not certify them. Events scheduled after the cutoff, including the November 2026 election, must not appear as completed history.

The geographic center is the territory that became the United States, understood through Indigenous homelands and Atlantic, Caribbean, hemispheric, and Pacific connections. Mexico, Canada, the Caribbean, Europe, Africa, and Asia enter where their histories explain that center; they are not merely scenery for American decisions.

Parts are interpretive groupings, not mutually exclusive date bins. Chapters 1–3 distinguish deep history, transatlantic contact, and Indigenous political worlds. Chapters 4–8 separate colonial formation, society, and imperial rivalry. Chapters 14–18 separate expansion, economic change, mass politics, removal, and war. Chapters 23–28 revisit the same postwar decades through Reconstruction, industry, the West, the South, empire, and culture. Chapters 37–43 distinguish Cold War institutions, domestic abundance, movements, legislation, foreign war, and political realignment. Later entries identify ownership of shared events so these overlaps do not become duplicate narratives.

## How to use the chapter outline

The entries below are the Stage 1 plan, now copied into the seeded files; they are not completed researched outlines. Named historians identify questions to investigate; their presence is not a claim that this revision checked every work. In Stage 3, expand each entry in its own chapter file, read the sources, and attach named citation references to the supported claims. Put citation definitions, source details, “Used for” annotations, and other footnotes together in one list at the end of the file, following AGENTS.md §4; do not add a separate Sources list. Research citations must distinguish discovery from reading and verification.

Planned section numbers are the Stage 1 assignments. Where scope or a scene changes, update the chapter and affected cross-references during its next revision; this README does not silently alter manuscript prose. Each debate retains the four subsections specified in AGENTS.md. Every chapter also retains the agreed Legacies, Summary, Study Questions, Further Reading, closing note, and combined notes/citations. Optional threads remain optional.

Each entry names its incoming subject and its outgoing scene. These are the agreed hand-offs for Stage 3 and drafting; where a copied entry corrects an earlier planning scene, the correction governs. A scene is a documentary research target, not authorization to invent participants or details. Visual IDs resolve to the complete register later in this README; table assignments include the chapter’s debate table even when not repeated in its short visual bullet.
