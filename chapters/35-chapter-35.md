> — Franklin D. Roosevelt, “Quarantine Speech,” Chicago, October 5, 1937.[^ch35-quarantine-speech]

>
> — Charles A. Lindbergh, “Who Are the War Agitators?,” Des Moines, Iowa, September 11, 1941.[^ch35-lindbergh-text]

> “Each [nation] stepped through a series of escalating moves that provoked but failed to restrain the other, all the while lifting the level of confrontation to ever-riskier heights.”[^ch35-kennedy][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]
>
> — David M. Kennedy, *Freedom from Fear*, 1999.[^ch35-kennedy][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]

A president told a country wary of foreign wars that the world’s disorder would reach it. The most famous aviator of the age told an America First rally that a hidden effort was dragging the country toward war. A historian described two states climbing a staircase neither controlled. The three disagree about whether the danger was aggression abroad, manipulation at home, or an escalation dynamic. That disagreement frames the chapter’s question: how did neutrality give way to intervention, and how much of that outcome was chosen?[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-lindbergh-text][^ch35-kennedy][^ch35-check-epigraphs]

On October 5, 1937, Franklin D. Roosevelt spoke in Chicago at the dedication of a public-works project. The Roosevelt Library files the address as the Outer Link Bridge dedication. The transcript records him calling the occasion “this important project of civic betterment.” He said he had chosen “this great inland city and this gala occasion to speak to you on a subject of definite national importance.”[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-quarantine-speech-file]

The world he described was not at peace in any ordinary sense. Treaties were being broken, civilians were being bombed, and ships were being sunk without warning. “It seems to be unfortunately true that the epidemic of world lawlessness is spreading,” Roosevelt said. War, he added, “is a contagion, whether it be declared or undeclared.” The image that survived was medical. “When an epidemic of physical disease starts to spread, the community approves and joins in a quarantine of the patients in order to protect the health of the community against the spread of the disease.”[^ch35-quarantine-speech]

He proposed no sanctions, no alliance, and no rearmament program in the address. He asked his audience to see the world’s disorder as their own problem, and he closed with a sentence that promised activity without specifying it: “There must be positive endeavors to preserve peace. America hates war. America hopes for peace. Therefore, America actively engages in the search for peace.”[^ch35-quarantine-speech]

The record establishes the date, the venue, the text, and the fact that the speech was delivered. It does not establish how the audience heard it, what Roosevelt intended beyond the words, or how the country responded. In October 1937 no one knew that a European war would begin in 1939, that France would fall in 1940, or that the United States would be at war in 1941. The speech named a problem his government could not yet solve.[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-check-scene][^ch35-check-scene-reaction]

The chapter covers the years from 1937 to 1941. It asks how a legal regime designed to keep the United States out of other nations’ wars became a set of commitments that made entry difficult to avoid. It draws on statutes and treaty documents, presidential addresses and executive messages, the published diplomatic record, the records of the interventionist and isolationist organizations, and the secondary literature. It treats “neutrality,” “isolationism,” “intervention,” “aid short of war,” “quarantine,” “cash-and-carry,” “arsenal of democracy,” and “America First” as contested period terms. By “neutrality” the chapter means the legal position of a state that refuses to take sides in a war. By “isolationism,” the political disposition that opposed American entry into Europe’s wars, though its adherents often preferred “noninterventionism.” By “intervention,” the willingness to use American power, including aid, to affect the outcome of a foreign war. “Aid short of war” was the period’s phrase for measures that stopped before belligerency. “Quarantine” was Roosevelt’s metaphor for collective action against aggressors, and it never became a defined policy. “Cash-and-carry” required belligerents to pay in cash and carry goods in their own ships. “Arsenal of democracy” was Roosevelt’s name for an America that armed the Allies without sending its own armies. “America First” was the name of the organization that fought those measures.[^ch35-check-scope][^ch35-check-names]

The chapter also states a limit at the start. The record over-represents the organized and the official: presidents, cabinet officers, committee leaders, and major newspapers. It under-represents the unorganized, the poor, and the isolationism of people who left no archive. The available sources do not include a consistent polling series for 1939–41, so the chapter’s statements about public opinion are its least well measured. The Japanese government’s internal reasoning is also under-represented in the sources consulted. Those asymmetries are part of the evidence, not gaps to be filled by invention.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-check-public-opinion][^ch35-check-japan]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the neutrality acts and the world they assumed. §2 follows the European war and the fall of France. §3 turns to the interventionist and isolationist organizations, and reads Lindbergh’s Des Moines address in the In Focus. §4 examines destroyers for bases, Lend-Lease, the Four Freedoms, the Atlantic Charter, and the undeclared naval war. §5 traces the sanctions crisis with Japan, Pearl Harbor, and the declarations of December 1941. §6 asks how historians have explained the American entry into the war. The sections are thematic and overlap in time: §§3 and 4 both run through 1941. The chapter begins on October 5, 1937, when Roosevelt told a Chicago audience that the world’s lawlessness was spreading.[^ch35-check-scope]

The date falls inside the years Chapter 34 covered, but its subject is foreign policy. The domestic New Deal and the approach of war ran on separate clocks, and the country would soon be living on both.[^ch35-check-ch34]

## 1. The neutrality inheritance

### 1.1 A legislative inheritance that constrained the president

When war came to Europe in September 1939, the United States was bound by a body of neutrality law built in the mid-1930s to keep it out of another European war. The law constrained what Roosevelt could do even as he tried to help the Allies. It was not a single design. It was an accumulation: an arms embargo, a loan ban, travel restrictions, shipping restrictions, and finally cash-and-carry. Each provision had its own coalition behind it, and their cumulative effect was the real constraint.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]

The statutes answered a memory. Many Americans believed that bankers and arms makers had drawn the country into the First World War. The Senate’s munitions inquiry, chaired by Gerald P. Nye of North Dakota, gave that belief a hearing. The committee held 93 hearings between September 1934 and February 1936 and questioned more than 200 witnesses. It found ample evidence that the armaments industry had profited handsomely from the earlier war, but it found little support for the charge that the industry had conspired to bring the country into it. The distinction between profit and conspiracy mattered less in public memory than in the record. The hearings gave the charge a national audience, and the neutrality bills were written in its shadow.[^ch35-nye-committee][^ch35-neutrality-acts]

The record can show the statutes, their dates, and their terms. It cannot show that most Americans approved of every provision. Congressional action and public opinion were related but not identical, and the chapter keeps them separate.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 1.2 The neutrality statutes, 1935–1937

Congress passed the first Neutrality Act in 1935, renewed and extended it in 1936, and broadened it in 1937. On August 31, 1935, Congress prohibited the export of “arms, ammunition, and implements of war” to nations at war and required arms makers to obtain export licenses. Roosevelt opposed the bill but signed it. The 1936 renewal continued the act and barred loans to belligerents. The 1937 act forbade Americans to travel on belligerent ships, banned American ships from carrying arms to belligerents, allowed the president to bar belligerent ships from American waters and to extend the embargo, and covered civil wars.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]

Each step tightened the restrictions, but the sequence was not a single march. Roosevelt signed bills he disliked because Congress would have passed them anyway, and he accepted restrictions in order to keep worse ones out. The 1937 act also contained a concession he had engineered, the cash-and-carry provision that §1.3 examines.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]

The record can show the statutes and their sequence. It cannot show why each provision commanded its majority. A Congress that voted for an arms embargo in 1935 was not the same Congress that repealed it in 1939, and the chapter does not attribute a single motive to a divided legislature.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-neutrality-acts]

### 1.3 Cash-and-carry and the Neutrality Act of 1939

The 1937 act contained a concession Roosevelt had engineered. Belligerents could buy non-arms goods from the United States if they paid cash and used their own ships. The provision favored Britain and France, the only belligerents with the currency and the merchant shipping to use it. It let Roosevelt help the democracies without asking Congress for loans or putting American ships in the war zone.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]

The test came in 1939. Germany occupied Czechoslovakia in March, and on September 1 it invaded Poland. Roosevelt called Congress into special session on September 21 to revise the neutrality law. After weeks of debate, the House repealed the arms embargo on November 2 by a vote of 243 to 181, and Roosevelt signed the measure on November 4. The new law put all trade with belligerents on a cash-and-carry basis. It reopened arms sales to Britain and France while keeping American ships and loans out of the war.[^ch35-house-neutrality-1939][^ch35-neutrality-acts]

The vote was not a mandate for intervention. It was a narrow decision that aid could be sold without convoys, credits, or American crews. The margin showed how carefully Roosevelt had to move, and it showed that the isolationist bloc could be beaten when the issue was framed as a way to stay out. The revision did not save the Allies by itself. It made American factories available to them on terms that kept the American government a seller, not a belligerent.[^ch35-house-neutrality-1939]

### 1.4 The isolationist constituency

Behind the statutes stood a durable body of opinion, often called isolationist. It drew on the memory of the First World War, the findings of the Senate munitions inquiry, and the belief that bankers and arms makers had drawn the country into that war. It was never monolithic. It included pacifists who opposed all war, nationalists who distrusted foreign commitments, German- and Irish-American communities with grievances of their own, and conservatives who distrusted Roosevelt for domestic reasons. They could vote together without agreeing on why.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-nye-committee]

The term itself needs care. Its targets sometimes preferred “noninterventionist,” and the chapter uses “isolationist” as a period label rather than a description of a coherent ideology. The movement’s strength lay in its ability to unite people who wanted different things from American foreign policy. Its weakness lay in the same fact: a coalition assembled against entry did not have to agree on what should replace it.[^ch35-check-names][^ch35-afc-cfr]

The record can show organizations, members, and publications. It cannot easily show the views of the unaffiliated. The chapter names that gap rather than treating a rally’s crowd or a newspaper’s mail as a representative sample of the country.[^ch35-check-silences]

### 1.5 What neutrality law could not control

The neutrality acts governed the American state’s dealings with belligerents. They could not govern the actions of Germany, Italy, and Japan. The initiative in world affairs lay partly abroad, and the chapter keeps that fact in view. A law that told Americans not to sell arms to belligerents could not stop a German army from marching into Poland or a Japanese army from marching into China.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth]

The acts were written for a world of settled great-power war. They proved poorly suited to a world of aggression against weak states, civil wars, and undeclared conflict. By 1939 the gap between the law’s assumptions and the world’s realities was already wide. The outbreak of war in September tested the inheritance immediately, which is where the next section begins.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

## 2. European war and the fall of France

### 2.1 The war arrives

On September 1, 1939, German forces invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war. In a fireside chat, Roosevelt promised that the United States would remain neutral in law. He then added that he could “not ask that every American remain neutral in thought as well.” The sentence marked the distance between the legal position and the president’s sympathy.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

The first months of the war were quiet in the west. Then, in the spring of 1940, German forces struck. The campaigns matter here less than what the war did to American assumptions. The most important fact was not a battle but a collapse: France, the army that was supposed to hold the western front, was defeated in six weeks.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

The record can show the diplomatic and military chronology and the terms of American aid. It cannot isolate the effect of any single event on the public. The chapter treats that effect as an open question rather than a measured one.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 2.2 The fall of France and Britain alone

In May and June 1940 German forces defeated France. Britain became the last major power resisting Germany in Europe. The defeat reordered American strategic assumptions. American planners began to consider a world in which Britain might fall and the Western Hemisphere would have to be defended alone.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

The military view was not uniformly interventionist. General George C. Marshall, the Army chief of staff, anticipated that Britain might surrender after France and argued for reserving supplies for hemispheric defense. Aid to Britain was not the obvious military choice. It was a bet that American security depended on a British survival that the Army could not guarantee. Marshall’s position shows how contested the aid decision was among people who were not isolationists.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

The fall of France also changed the language of American debate. Aid to Britain could now be described as defense of the Western Hemisphere rather than as a favor to a European ally. That framing was partly strategic and partly political. It allowed Roosevelt to move without asking Americans to accept the word “intervention.”[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-destroyers-message]

### 2.3 American opinion and the 1940 election

The 1940 campaign turned on a distinction that Roosevelt treated as decisive: helping Britain was not the same as joining the war. He had to persuade a public that wanted Britain to survive that aid was the safest way to stay out. Wendell Willkie, the Republican nominee, charged that a third term meant war. Roosevelt answered that he would not send Americans to fight in “any foreign war.” He won an unprecedented third term with about 55 percent of the popular vote and the electoral votes of 38 states; Willkie carried 10.[^ch35-election-1940][^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

The result did not settle the aid question. It ratified a candidate who promised both help and distance. The election measured the constraint rather than removing it. Roosevelt could read the returns as permission to aid Britain and as a warning against asking for more.[^ch35-election-1940][^ch35-check-public-opinion]

The chapter describes the shift without precise figures because no consistent series with stable question wording was available. That is a limitation of the evidence, not a finding about the public.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 2.4 The strategic alarm and the turn to aid

The fall of France turned the question from whether to help Britain to how to help it without entering the war. That question organizes §4. It also explains why the aid measures of 1940–41 were framed as hemispheric defense. The bases, the destroyers, and the Lend-Lease Act were presented as ways to keep the war away from the Americas. The Atlantic Charter gave the aid a set of stated aims. Each moved the United States closer to the fighting.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-destroyers-message]

That problem took public form in the contest §3 covers: two campaigns arguing about whether aid was a substitute for war or a road to it.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-check-scope]

## 3. Interventionists and opponents

### 3.1 A contest of organized opinion

Between 1940 and 1941 the question of aid to Britain was fought out by two mass organizations. The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies and the America First Committee were rivals with real arguments, not a patriotic camp and a fringe. The outcome of their contest shaped what Roosevelt could do. It also showed how much of American foreign policy was made in public argument rather than in confidential memoranda.[^ch35-afc-cfr]

The chapter presents the two organizations in their own terms before it judges them. That is not neutrality between their positions. It is a condition of understanding why the aid question was so hard. Each side could describe its policy as the best way to keep the country out of war.[^ch35-afc-cfr]

### 3.2 The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies

The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies formed in May 1940. It was led by William Allen White, the Kansas newspaper publisher. Its argument was counterintuitive: sending aid to Britain would reduce, not increase, the chance that the United States would be drawn into the war. A British defeat would put Germany in control of the Atlantic and leave the United States alone. Aid was therefore a form of prevention.[^ch35-afc-cfr]

Its supporters did not necessarily want the United States to enter the war. They believed that a British defeat would imperil American security, which put them directly against the America First argument. The committee’s strength was its respectability. It could appeal to internationalist Republicans and to Democrats who trusted Roosevelt without endorsing everything he did.[^ch35-afc-cfr]

The record can show the committee’s formation, leadership, and public positions. It cannot measure how many Americans it persuaded. The chapter marks that limit rather than converting a speaker’s audience into a movement’s mandate.[^ch35-check-public-opinion]

### 3.3 The America First Committee

The America First Committee launched on September 4, 1940. It became the most powerful isolationist organization in the country. Its argument was that the war in Europe did not threaten American security and that aid to Britain would lead to war. Lindbergh’s celebrity after his 1927 solo flight across the Atlantic gave the committee a spokesman the press could not ignore.[^ch35-lindbergh-cfr] Its chairman was General Robert E. Wood, the head of Sears, Roebuck.[^ch35-afc-cfr] Its prominent supporters included Charles A. Lindbergh, Alice Roosevelt Longworth, the University of Chicago president Robert Hutchins, and the actress Lillian Gish. Among its younger supporters were Gerald Ford, Potter Stewart, Gore Vidal, and Kingman Brewster.[^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]

The roster shows the movement’s reach. It does not show that every member shared Lindbergh’s views, and it does not show that the younger members understood the committee as their later careers might suggest. The chapter names them as evidence of breadth, not as evidence of a single doctrine. Their later lives are not evidence about what the committee meant in 1940.[^ch35-check-names][^ch35-check-silences]

The record can show the organization, its leaders, and its materials. It cannot show the motives of every member. The chapter does not treat the whole membership as sharing Lindbergh’s antisemitism, and it does not use the membership’s respectability to soften the speech that made him famous.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]

### 3.4 The In Focus: Lindbergh at Des Moines

**In Focus.** On the evening of September 11, 1941, Charles A. Lindbergh spoke to an America First rally at the Des Moines Coliseum. Roosevelt’s fireside chat on German attacks in the Atlantic was broadcast over the hall’s loudspeakers before Lindbergh took the stage. Lindbergh titled his address “Who Are the War Agitators?” and began by claiming that “there has been an ever-increasing effort to force the United States into the conflict.” He promised to argue from “facts and issues.” Then he named the culprits: “The three most important groups who have been pressing this country toward war are the British, the Jewish, and the Roosevelt administration.” The sentence is reproduced because the argument requires it. It takes a religious minority and turns it into a foreign interest steering the nation toward war, which is the core of the antisemitic conspiracy theory the speech advanced. Lindbergh added that he was not attacking the Jewish or British people, but the disclaimer did not undo the charge. It gave the charge a veneer of reasonableness. The address was widely condemned, and the America First Committee defended him, then stopped inviting him to speak. The episode shows how a celebrated aviator used his credibility to circulate a dangerous lie, and how a public argument about war could become a politics of suspicion.[^ch35-lindbergh-text][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm][^ch35-check-focus]

### 3.5 The collapse of the opposition

The attack on Pearl Harbor ended the isolationist contest almost overnight. On December 11 the House voted 393–0 against Germany and 399–0 against Italy. Frances Bolton of Ohio, who had earlier opposed intervention, told colleagues that “all that is passed. We are at war.” The near-unanimity measured the depth of the attack’s effect, not the depth of every member’s conversion. A movement that had rested on the belief that the country could stay out had lost its premise in a morning.[^ch35-house-declarations]

The record can show the votes and the statements. It cannot recover the private reasoning of every member who changed position. Some converts may have been persuaded by the attack; others may have concluded that further opposition was futile. The chapter marks that silence rather than assigning a single motive to a sudden shift.[^ch35-check-silences]

## 4. Destroyers, Lend-Lease, the Atlantic Charter, and undeclared naval war

### 4.1 Aid without war

Roosevelt advanced American support for Britain through a series of measures that stopped short of war in form but moved steadily toward it in effect. The bases exchange, Lend-Lease, and the Atlantic naval patrols were justified as defense of the Western Hemisphere rather than as intervention in Europe. The Atlantic Charter gave the aid a set of stated aims. Together the measures moved the United States further into the war’s political and military orbit, even as each step was contested.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-destroyers-message]

The pattern was escalation without declaration. It was not a secret plan, and it was not an accident. It was a series of decisions taken under pressure, each defended as the minimum necessary to keep the country out of a war that was coming closer. No step was uncontested.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

### 4.2 Destroyers for bases, September 1940

On September 2, 1940, the United States and Britain exchanged notes. The United States transferred 50 over-age destroyers. Britain granted 99-year leases on naval and air bases in Newfoundland, Bermuda, the Bahamas, Jamaica, St. Lucia, Trinidad, and Antigua, and in British Guiana. Roosevelt made the arrangement by executive agreement rather than treaty, and he sent Congress the attorney general’s opinion on his authority to do so.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-destroyers-message]

Roosevelt told Congress that the rights in Newfoundland and Bermuda were gifts. The others were acquired “in exchange for 50 of our over-age destroyers.” The deal was, he said, “the most important action in the reinforcement of our national defense that has been taken since the Louisiana Purchase.” The language was defensive, but the transaction was not merely defensive. It put American bases on British territory and American ships in British hands without a treaty or a declaration.[^ch35-destroyers-message]

The legal precedent outlasted the ships. The exchange became a model for executive agreements in foreign policy, and Congress would contest their scope for decades. It was a precedent for presidential power, not merely a swap of obsolete ships for real estate.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-beard]

The record can show the notes and the message. It cannot show that the destroyers changed the outcome of the Battle of the Atlantic. The chapter treats that claim as open and does not convert a useful transfer into a decisive weapon.[^ch35-check-numbers]

### 4.3 Lend-Lease, March 1941

By December 1940 Churchill had warned that Britain could no longer pay for supplies. On December 17 Roosevelt proposed that the United States lend or lease war material to nations whose defense it deemed vital. In a fireside chat on December 29 he said, “We must be the great arsenal of democracy.” Congress passed the Lend-Lease Act on March 11, 1941.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-arsenal-democracy][^ch35-lend-lease-act]

The statute authorized the president to sell, transfer, exchange, lease, or lend “defense articles.” The recipient could be any government “whose defense the President deems vital to the defense of the United States.” It also barred him from using the act to authorize convoys or to send American vessels into a combat area in violation of the Neutrality Act of 1939. The restriction was a measure of how far the statute stopped short of war. It also showed that Congress meant to keep the decision to fight separate from the decision to supply.[^ch35-lend-lease-act]

Over the war the United States contracted Lend-Lease agreements with more than 30 countries and dispensed about $50 billion in assistance. Article VII tied repayment to a liberalized postwar economic order. The aid was necessary to Britain’s survival and not sufficient to explain it. Lend-Lease was a condition of Allied resistance, not a single cause of victory.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-check-numbers]

### 4.4 The Four Freedoms and the Atlantic Charter

Roosevelt gave the aid program a language of purpose in his annual message to Congress on January 6, 1941. He called for a world founded on “four essential human freedoms.” They were freedom of speech and expression, freedom of worship, freedom from want, and freedom from fear, each “everywhere in the world.” The Roosevelt Library records that the passage appeared only in the fourth of seven drafts and that the president dictated the peroration to his advisers.[^ch35-four-freedoms][^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms]

The Four Freedoms were a statement of aims, not a program. They named the kind of world the aid was meant to defend, and they gave Americans a vocabulary that outlasted the war. The text did not settle whether the freedoms were promises to be enforced or hopes to be announced. The answer would depend on what the postwar governments did.[^ch35-four-freedoms][^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms]

Roosevelt and Churchill met aboard naval vessels at Placentia Bay, off Newfoundland, from August 9 to 12, 1941. On August 14 they issued the Atlantic Charter, eight common principles. The charter called for no territorial aggrandizement, no territorial changes against the freely expressed wishes of the peoples concerned, and the right of peoples to choose their government. It also called for equal access to trade and raw materials, economic collaboration, a peace of “freedom from fear and want,” freedom of the seas, and the abandonment of force. It was a joint statement, not a treaty, issued by one nation technically neutral and one at war.[^ch35-atlantic-charter][^ch35-fdrlibrary-atlantic]

The charter’s principles did not bind later Allied policy. They gave the war a set of stated aims that postwar politics would contest. The charter was a declaration of principles whose meaning remained open, not a legal commitment that later leaders were obliged to honor.[^ch35-fdrlibrary-four-freedoms][^ch35-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The undeclared naval war in the Atlantic

In the fall of 1941 the United States became a participant in the Battle of the Atlantic without a declaration of war. American destroyers escorted convoys, and German submarines attacked them. The Greer incident in September, the torpedoing of the Kearny in October, and the sinking of the Reuben James on October 31, 1941, while it escorted Convoy HX-156, marked the escalation. The Reuben James was the first American warship lost in the war, and most of its crew died.[^ch35-reuben-james][^ch35-lend-lease-oth]

On September 11 Roosevelt delivered a fireside chat on the German attacks. That same evening Lindbergh spoke in Des Moines. The simultaneity is the chapter’s image of a country arguing with itself while its ships were already in the fight. Roosevelt described a German campaign to control the seas; Lindbergh described a campaign to drag the country into a European war. Both were describing the same autumn.[^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm][^ch35-reuben-james]

In October and November Congress allowed American merchant ships to be armed and repealed the ban on American ships entering belligerent ports and combat zones. The United States was now fighting an undeclared war in the Atlantic while it remained legally neutral. The distinction between neutrality and belligerency had become a lawyer’s line, and the line was moving.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]

The record can show the incidents and the votes. It cannot reconstruct the private orders or the submarine commanders’ intentions from the evidence consulted. The chapter marks that limit rather than turning every incident into a deliberate step toward war.[^ch35-check-numbers][^ch35-check-silences]

## 5. Japan, sanctions, Pearl Harbor, and declarations

### 5.1 Two crises converge

The American entry into the war came from the Pacific, but the Pacific crisis was entangled with the Atlantic one. The same American aid that supported Britain also shaped the confrontation with Japan, and the Axis alliance linked the two theaters. Japan’s expansion in China and its alliance with Germany and Italy turned a regional war into a global alignment.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth][^ch35-hitler-declaration]

The Pacific and Atlantic sequences converged; they were not separate wars. The Tripartite Pact is the hinge. It meant that a war with Japan might become a war with Germany and Italy, and that American action in the Atlantic might have consequences in the Pacific.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]

The record can show American policy and, in part, Japanese policy. It cannot fully reconstruct the Japanese government’s internal debates from the sources consulted. The chapter names that gap rather than treating American intelligence estimates as a portrait of Japanese intentions.[^ch35-check-japan]

### 5.2 Japan’s war in China and the breakdown of the Washington system

Japan’s expansion in China after 1931, and especially after the Marco Polo Bridge incident of July 7, 1937, destroyed the framework of cooperation the United States had hoped to preserve in the Pacific. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and installed a puppet government there, which the United States refused to recognize under the Stimson Doctrine. Full-scale war with China began in 1937. Japanese forces bombed the USS *Panay* in December 1937. Japan announced a “Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere” in August 1940, and it signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy on September 27, 1940.[^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]

The Washington system was the treaty framework the United States had hoped would manage competition in the Pacific. It did not survive these moves. The United States refused to recognize Manchukuo, but it did not use force to reverse the conquest. American companies continued to supply Japan with steel and petroleum long after the war in China had become a full-scale conflict. The gap between American principles and American trade was one of the contradictions that the sanctions of 1940–41 began to close.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-pearl-harbor-oth]

The record can show the incidents and the American response. It cannot present Japanese motives as settled. The chapter avoids reducing Japan’s decision to a single cause, whether resource dependence, military factionalism, or the example of European empire.[^ch35-check-japan][^ch35-barnhart]

### 5.3 Sanctions, the freeze, and the Hull note

American economic pressure tightened in stages. In July 1940 Roosevelt cut off shipments of scrap iron, steel, and aviation fuel to Japan while allowing oil to continue. Japan moved into French Indochina. In July 1941, after Japan’s further move into southern Indochina, Roosevelt froze Japanese assets in the United States, which effectively cut off Japan’s access to American oil. On November 26, 1941, Secretary of State Cordell Hull delivered a ten-point American note that restated the American position. It became known as the Hull note.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-hull-note]

The sequence was economic pressure, not a declaration of war. It narrowed Japan’s choices without determining which one Japan would take. The freeze turned a slow squeeze into a deadline: without oil, Japan’s navy could not sustain a long war. The Hull note restated the American demand for withdrawal from China and Indochina. Whether that demand was a negotiating position or a condition that Japan could not accept is one of the questions the debate section takes up.[^ch35-hull-note][^ch35-check-japan]

The record can show the measures and the note. It cannot show that the embargo made war inevitable. The sanctions were a contributing cause among several, not a single switch that turned on the Pacific war.[^ch35-check-numbers][^ch35-check-japan]

### 5.4 Pearl Harbor, December 7, 1941

On the morning of Sunday, December 7, 1941, Japanese naval and air forces attacked the American base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. The plan was devised by Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, who had studied at Harvard and served as Japan’s naval attaché in Washington. The attack came in two waves, lasted less than two hours, and crippled the Pacific Fleet. The National Park Service records 2,403 service members and civilians killed and 1,178 injured. The battleships USS *Arizona* and USS *Utah* were permanently sunk, and 188 aircraft were destroyed. The American aircraft carriers were at sea and survived.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-pearl-harbor-nps][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]

The attack was one of the greatest military surprises in the history of warfare. It was also one of several attacks that day. Japanese forces struck the Philippines, Guam, Midway, Wake Island, Malaya, and Hong Kong. Within days, Japan was master of much of the western Pacific. The American loss at Pearl Harbor was therefore both a single catastrophe and the opening of a wider war.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-path-pearl-harbor]

The record can show the attack, the losses, and the official American response. It cannot answer the counterfactual questions about warning and readiness, which belong to the intelligence debates outside this chapter’s scope. The attack does not prove that entry was inevitable, and the survival of the carriers does not prove that the outcome was lucky.[^ch35-check-numbers][^ch35-check-scope]

### 5.5 Declarations and the end of neutrality

On December 8 Roosevelt addressed a joint session of Congress. He said that December 7 would be “a date which will live in infamy.” The Senate voted unanimously for war. In the House, Jeannette Rankin of Montana cast the sole dissent. On December 11 Germany and Italy declared war on the United States. The House approved a declaration against Germany 393–0 and against Italy 399–0; Rankin answered “Present.” Hitler’s Reichstag speech invoked the Tripartite Pact and repeated antisemitic conspiracy claims.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-house-declarations][^ch35-hitler-declaration]

The votes ended the neutrality era and the isolationist contest. They did not tell Americans what kind of war they had entered. The country that had argued for four years about its place in the war was now at war with the Axis, and the scale of the fighting was still unknown. The moment ended in uncertainty, not in knowledge of the outcome.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-check-dates-appD]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The neutrality statutes constrained Roosevelt and were progressively loosened. From the arms embargo of 1935 to the repeal of neutrality provisions in late 1941, the legal framework moved from prohibition to aid to undeclared naval conflict. The direction of the movement is not in dispute.[^ch35-neutrality-acts][^ch35-house-neutrality-1939]

Aid to Britain was substantial and contested. The destroyers-for-bases exchange, Lend-Lease, and the Atlantic Charter marked a deliberate American commitment short of war. They were not the acts of a government that had decided to remain neutral in the ordinary sense.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-lend-lease-act][^ch35-atlantic-charter]

The Pacific crisis contributed directly to entry. American economic pressure and Japan’s decision for war produced the attack of December 7, 1941. Germany’s declaration made the European war automatic: Hitler’s December 11 declaration brought the United States into the European war without a separate debate.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-hull-note][^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-house-declarations]

Isolationism collapsed after the attack. The near-unanimous war votes showed how quickly the opposition dissolved. The collapse does not prove that the opposition had been shallow, but it does show how much of it depended on the belief that the country could stay out.[^ch35-house-declarations]

### 6.2 What is open

How much did Roosevelt intend entry? One position holds that he maneuvered the country to war behind the appearance of neutrality. Another holds that he responded to Axis aggression and moved only as far as politics allowed. Each position has a strong version. Beard’s revisionist account takes the gap between Roosevelt’s public posture and his private steps seriously. Langer and Gleason’s orthodox account integrates diplomacy and strategy and can make Roosevelt’s choices appear the only responsible ones. The evidence supports a middle judgment: Roosevelt moved toward intervention, but he moved under constraints he did not control and often did not master.[^ch35-beard][^ch35-langer-gleason][^ch35-divine]

How much did public opinion constrain or enable him? One account treats opinion as a real brake; another treats Roosevelt as a leader who shaped opinion. No consistent polling series is available, so the election returns, the press, and the organized campaigns are the evidence, and the gap is marked. Divine’s reluctant-belligerent account foregrounds the constraint. Cole’s political-contest account restores the isolationists as serious actors. Both are needed to explain why Roosevelt moved in steps rather than in a single speech.[^ch35-check-public-opinion][^ch35-divine][^ch35-cole]

Did the oil embargo make war in the Pacific inevitable? Barnhart’s economic-conflict thesis treats Japan’s resource dependence and the American embargo as the decisive combination. The escalation account treats the embargo as one move in a series that neither side controlled. The difference is partly about evidence and partly about the counterfactual: a Japan with oil might still have gone to war, and a Japan without it might have chosen differently.[^ch35-barnhart][^ch35-kennedy]

Was the outcome a contest of politics or a problem of strategy? Cole restores the American political contest. Heinrichs presents Roosevelt as a global strategic manager balancing Europe-first priorities and the Pacific crisis. Both matter. A president who managed strategy also had to manage a coalition, and a coalition that limited his options also shaped the strategy he could pursue.[^ch35-cole][^ch35-heinrichs]

Where did the initiative lie? The question is how much the outcome turned on American choices and how much on German and Japanese decisions that Washington did not control. Hitler’s declaration, Japan’s decision for war, and Britain’s survival were not American choices. The American decisions set the conditions in which those foreign choices were made.[^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-barnhart][^ch35-check-japan]

Ideology cut across these questions. Lindbergh’s charge that the British, the Jewish, and the Roosevelt administration were pushing the country toward war was a conspiracy theory, but it was also a claim about who belonged to the nation and who did not. The interventionists’ anti-Nazi arguments were claims about what the United States should defend. The debate over entry was never only a debate about strategy.[^ch35-lindbergh-text][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the United States moved from legal neutrality to war through a series of contingent decisions that narrowed options without making entry inevitable from 1937. Both American choices and foreign decisions mattered. The synthesis is not a compromise between the positions in Table 1. It is a claim about how the positions fit together.[^ch35-quarantine-speech][^ch35-hitler-declaration]

Aid short of war, the Atlantic incidents, the freeze, and the Hull note were choices taken by people who could have chosen otherwise. None of them predetermined December 1941. The counterfactuals are not decorations. They identify where the evidence would have to be strongest to support a claim of inevitability.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-hull-note]

Structure and choice belong together. Britain’s need, Japan’s resource dependence, and the Axis alliance set the pressures. The choices made within those pressures determined the timing and the form of entry. A history that emphasizes only structure cannot explain why the United States moved in steps. A history that emphasizes only choice cannot explain why the steps kept pointing in the same direction.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth][^ch35-barnhart]

The synthesis for this chapter is that the United States entered the war through a Pacific attack and a German declaration, but the ground for entry had been prepared by four years of American decisions and arguments that the isolationists lost. The attack and the declaration were foreign decisions. The preparation was American.[^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-check-table1]

## Legacies

The years between 1937 and 1941 left a lasting legacy in American government. The destroyers-for-bases exchange and Lend-Lease established that a president could commit the country to far-reaching foreign obligations through executive agreement and statute. Later administrations used the precedent, and Congress contested it for decades. The argument over how much power a president may wield abroad without a declaration of war has never been settled.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-lend-lease-act]

The mobilization that began before entry became a permanent national security establishment. The agencies, military programs, and global commitments built to fight the Axis fed into the Cold War institutions that Chapter 37 describes. The country that entered the war in December 1941 did not demobilize its new capacities when the fighting ended; it reorganized them for a longer confrontation.[^ch35-lend-lease-act][^ch35-check-ch36]

The isolationist tradition did not vanish with the December votes. It survived in later arguments about alliances, foreign aid, and the terms of American engagement, and the America First name returned to American politics in a different era. The recurrence is worth noting, but later movements are not the same as the 1940–41 organization. A name can be reused without carrying the same argument.[^ch35-afc-cfr][^ch35-check-silences]

The chapter’s harder legacy is evidentiary. The fight over entry produced a documentary record built by the state and by organized campaigns. The arguments of powerful and connected people survived in detail, while the reasoning of ordinary Americans left fewer traces. That asymmetry should shape how any history uses polling, newspapers, and memoirs. A silence in the archive is not evidence of consensus.[^ch35-check-silences][^ch35-check-public-opinion]

## Summary

- Congress passed neutrality acts in 1935, 1936, and 1937 that embargoed arms to belligerents, banned loans, and restricted American travel and shipping; the 1937 act also contained Roosevelt’s cash-and-carry concession.[^ch35-neutrality-acts]
- After Germany invaded Poland, Roosevelt called a special session on September 21, 1939; the House repealed the arms embargo on November 2 by 243 to 181, and he signed the cash-and-carry revision on November 4.[^ch35-house-neutrality-1939]
- Roosevelt declared American neutrality in law in September 1939 while saying he could not ask Americans to be neutral in thought; France fell to Germany in June 1940, leaving Britain alone.[^ch35-lend-lease-oth]
- The Committee to Defend America by Aiding the Allies, led by William Allen White, and the America First Committee, launched on September 4, 1940, under General Robert E. Wood, fought the aid question in public.[^ch35-afc-cfr]
- On September 11, 1941, Lindbergh told a Des Moines rally that “the British, the Jewish, and the Roosevelt administration” were pressing the country toward war, an antisemitic charge that discredited him and his cause.[^ch35-lindbergh-text][^ch35-lindbergh-ushmm]
- On September 2, 1940, Roosevelt exchanged 50 over-age destroyers for 99-year leases on British bases by executive agreement, and on March 11, 1941, he signed the Lend-Lease Act, which authorized aid to nations whose defense he deemed vital.[^ch35-destroyers-message][^ch35-lend-lease-act]
- Roosevelt’s Four Freedoms message of January 6, 1941, and the Atlantic Charter of August 14, 1941, stated American war aims; the charter was a joint statement, not a treaty.[^ch35-four-freedoms][^ch35-atlantic-charter]
- American destroyers fought an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic in 1941; the USS *Reuben James* was torpedoed on October 31, 1941, while escorting Convoy HX-156, the first American warship lost.[^ch35-reuben-james]
- After Japanese expansion in China and French Indochina, the United States froze Japanese assets in July 1941 and delivered the Hull note on November 26, 1941; Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, killing 2,403 people and wounding 1,178.[^ch35-path-pearl-harbor][^ch35-hull-note][^ch35-pearl-harbor-nps]
- Congress declared war on Japan on December 8, 1941, and on Germany and Italy on December 11, after Hitler declared war; the near-unanimous votes ended the isolationist contest.[^ch35-day-of-infamy][^ch35-house-declarations][^ch35-hitler-declaration]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1937 and 1941 the United States moved from a legal neutrality meant to keep it out of Europe’s wars to a set of commitments. Bases, Lend-Lease, a charter of war aims, and an undeclared naval war made conflict difficult to avoid. The final decisions were not American: Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, and Germany declared war four days later. But the ground had been prepared by four years of American argument and policy, and the isolationists who had fought every step saw their cause collapse in a week. The chapter’s question — how did neutrality give way to intervention, and how much of that outcome was chosen? — is answered by that double record of foreign decision and American preparation.[^ch35-lend-lease-act][^ch35-hitler-declaration][^ch35-house-declarations]

Chapter 36 takes up the Second World War itself and asks how coalition warfare remade American power and belonging. That story begins where this one ends, and the two chapters are meant to be read together.[^ch35-check-ch36]
