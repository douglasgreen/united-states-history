## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On the evening of Sunday, March 12, 1933, Roosevelt spoke to the nation by radio — the first of what became known as fireside chats — to explain what his administration had done to the banks. He had taken office on March 4; the banks had been closed under a national holiday he proclaimed on March 6; and Congress had passed the Emergency Banking Act on March 9.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **What he said.** Roosevelt explained that banks invest deposits, that a rush to convert deposits into currency had made even sound banks unable to pay, and that the government had closed and was now reopening them in stages. He assured listeners that reopening would proceed through the twelve Federal Reserve Bank cities first and then elsewhere, and he closed with a plea for public confidence.[^ch34-fireside-chat]
- **The documented lines.** “It is safer to keep your money in a reopened bank than under the mattress”; “there is an element in the readjustment of our financial system more important than currency, more important than gold, and that is the confidence of the people”; and “It is your problem no less than it is mine. Together we cannot fail.”[^ch34-fireside-chat]
- **The result.** The FDR Library reports that banks reopened on March 13 and that depositors lined up to return their money; Roosevelt’s adviser Raymond Moley later recalled that “Capitalism was saved in eight days.”[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the medium, the text, the banking chronology, the reopening schedule, and the reported return of deposits. It cannot show how many listeners heard the address, what each person in the country thought, or the precise size of the queues, because no such account is used here.[^ch34-check-scene][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In March 1933 no one knew how the New Deal would turn out, whether deposit insurance would pass, or that the phrase “New Deal” would name an era. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch34-check-scene][^ch34-check-dates]

### The scene’s argument

- **A broadcast becomes an instrument of policy.** The scene’s claim is not that a speech reopened the banks but that Roosevelt made the federal government’s explanation of finance part of governing. He addressed “the overwhelming majority who use banks for the making of deposits and the drawing of checks,” not the few who understood banking.[^ch34-fireside-chat]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question. Reopening the banks required more than a statute; it required restoring the belief that a bank’s doors would stay open. That belief was political as well as financial, and the New Deal’s later programs would rest on the same kind of appeal.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: what kind of security did the New Deal create, and for whom?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the fireside to the Hundred Days, to the cotton fields and wheat country, to the factory gates of Flint and Chicago, and to the relief offices of the states and cities.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented room, weather, or dialogue.** The date, the medium, the text, the banking chronology, and the reopening are documented. The scene may not stage the furniture of the White House study, the microphone’s placement, the weather, or any exchange the record does not contain.[^ch34-check-scene]
- **No interior thought.** The private reflections of Roosevelt, his advisers, or listeners must not be staged beyond what documents state.[^ch34-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the New Deal’s programs, the 1937 recession, or the durability of deposit insurance.[^ch34-check-scene][^ch34-check-dates]

## 1. Banking and the Hundred Days

### 1.1 The claim: a rescue that stabilized finance and set a template

- **The claim.** The New Deal began as a banking rescue, and its success in reopening the banks under federal supervision established the pattern that later programs followed: federal action, explained to the public, to make an essential system work again.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-frh-eba]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present three connected stories: the emergency of March 1933; the institutional rebuilding through deposit insurance and securities regulation; and the first relief and public-works programs that carried the same promise of federal responsibility. The three belong to one account rather than three separate ones.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-hopkins-relief]
- **What the record can show.** It can show statutes, dates, bank counts, and deposit figures. It cannot show that the rescue alone ended the banking crisis or that confidence returned evenly, and the chapter should treat causation as a question for §6, not a premise.[^ch34-check-numbers][^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 1.2 The bank holiday and the Emergency Banking Act

- **The claim.** Roosevelt used the banking emergency to reopen the banks under federal examination, and Congress passed a bill so quickly that many members voted without reading it.[^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **The sequence.** Roosevelt called a special session of Congress the day after his inauguration and declared a banking holiday that shut down the banking system, including the Federal Reserve. The Emergency Banking Act, signed on March 9, 1933, provided for reopening banks as examiners found them sound, broadened presidential authority during a banking crisis, and was divided into five titles.[^ch34-frh-eba]
- **The speed.** The bill had been prepared by Treasury staff during Herbert Hoover’s administration, was introduced on March 9, and passed that evening amid a chaotic scene; many members did not have an opportunity to read it before the vote. The chapter should present the speed as a decision with consequences for both recovery and accountability.[^ch34-frh-eba]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the proclamation, the statute, the titles, and the reopening schedule. It cannot show what individual members understood they were voting for, and the chapter should mark that gap.[^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-check-silences]

### 1.3 The fireside chat and the return of deposits

- **The claim.** The March 12 broadcast converted a legal reopening into a public act of confidence, and the following morning depositors began returning money to the banks.[^ch34-fireside-chat][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **The argument of the address.** Roosevelt explained banking in plain terms, insisted that the new currency was “backed by actual, good assets,” promised that reopening would be phased so that sound banks could be identified, and warned that a bank opening later was no less sound than one opening first.[^ch34-fireside-chat]
- **The result and its limits.** The FDR Library reports that the crisis eased and that Moley later said “Capitalism was saved in eight days.” The chapter should present that phrase as a participant’s retrospective judgment, not as a finding.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-check-scene]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the text and the reported return of deposits. It cannot prove that the speech caused the change, and the chapter should avoid attributing a measurable effect to a single broadcast.[^ch34-check-debate-works][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]

### 1.4 Glass-Steagall, the FDIC, and securities regulation

- **The claim.** The emergency rescue was followed by permanent institutions: federal deposit insurance, a separation of commercial and investment banking, and federal regulation of securities.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- **The Banking Act of 1933.** The Glass-Steagall Banking Act created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation and separated commercial from investment banking. The FDR Library reports that bank failures fell from more than 4,000 in 1933 to 61 in 1934, and that Congress dismantled the separation in 1999; the chapter should date each change and attribute the counts.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-check-numbers]
- **Securities regulation.** The Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created federal disclosure requirements and the Securities and Exchange Commission. The chapter should present these as the financial counterpart of the banking rescue, and should cite the statutes directly.[^ch34-check-hundred-days][^ch34-check-numbers]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the agency’s creation, and the failure counts. It cannot show that deposit insurance alone explains the decline in failures, and the chapter should treat the causal claim as open.[^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 1.5 The Hundred Days’ relief and public works, and Map 26

- **The claim.** The Hundred Days also created the first large federal experiments in work relief and regional development, which made Washington a direct employer and planner in ways it had not been before.[^ch34-hopkins-relief][^ch34-ccc][^ch34-tva-act]
- **The programs.** The Civilian Conservation Corps was signed into law on March 31, 1933; the Federal Emergency Relief Administration was created in May 1933 under Harry Hopkins; the Civil Works Administration followed in the winter of 1933–34; the Tennessee Valley Authority Act was signed on May 18, 1933; and the Works Progress Administration was created in 1935. The chapter should date each and note what each did.[^ch34-ccc][^ch34-hopkins-relief][^ch34-tva-act]
- **The scale and its costs.** The CCC eventually employed more than 2.5 million men in more than 4,500 camps and planted over 3 billion trees. The TVA built dams for flood control, navigation, and electric power, and its construction displaced families and sometimes entire towns. The chapter should present the benefits and the costs together.[^ch34-ccc][^ch34-tva-act]
- **Figure and data.** The registered visual `ch34-map-01` (Map 26, “New Deal landscape, 1933–1939”) belongs to this section and to §3.5. It should plot public-works and conservation locations, TVA and CCC sites, and Dust Bowl migration routes and destinations, with counts and dates, and it should draw its data from Appendix B, Table 8, as narrowed to land use, public works, and settlement. The caption and source line must state the definitions, years, and units.[^ch34-check-map26][^ch34-dustbowl][^ch34-ccc]
- **What the record can show.** It can show program dates, counts, and locations. It cannot show how every participant experienced relief, and the chapter should note that the records over-represent the enrolled and under-represent those the programs did not reach.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-hopkins-relief]

## 2. Agricultural and industrial recovery and the courts

### 2.1 The claim: recovery by organized scarcity, and its judicial unmaking

- **The claim.** The first New Deal tried to raise prices by organizing production — cutting farm acreage and letting industries write codes — and the Supreme Court struck down the core of both efforts, forcing the administration toward regulation that could survive judicial review.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler]
- **The structure.** The chapter should present the two programs and the two decisions together, because the judicial defeats set the terms for the “Second New Deal” of 1935.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-nira]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the codes, the taxes, and the opinions. It cannot show that the programs would have ended the Depression if upheld, and the chapter should mark the counterfactual as open.[^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 2.2 The AAA, the farm program, and the Dust Bowl

- **The claim.** The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 sought to raise farm prices by paying farmers to reduce acreage, financed by a tax on processors, and it produced both recovery and injustice.[^ch34-butler][^ch34-dustbowl]
- **The mechanism.** The act, approved on May 12, 1933, aimed to restore farm purchasing power by reducing surpluses; the cotton plow-up campaign of 1933 eliminated a large share of the growing crop. The chapter should state the parity concept, the processing tax, and the acreage-reduction contracts with their source and units.[^ch34-butler][^ch34-check-numbers]
- **Who bore the cost.** Tenant farmers and sharecroppers often received little of the payments, and landlords sometimes used the contracts to displace them. The chapter should present the distributional effect as central to the program’s meaning, not as an afterthought.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-dustbowl]
- **The Dust Bowl.** Drought and erosion drove an estimated 300,000 to 400,000 Oklahomans, Texans, Arkansans, and Missourians toward California in the 1930s, most visibly after 1935. The chapter should present the migration as one episode of displacement among many, not as the emblem of the decade, and should note that the “Okie” story became a racialized national parable.[^ch34-dustbowl][^ch34-check-map26]

### 2.3 The NIRA, the NRA, and Section 7(a)

- **The claim.** The National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 suspended antitrust law to let industries write codes of fair competition and guaranteed workers the right to organize, but the codes proved hard to administer and the organizing right proved the more durable legacy.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-nlra]
- **The statute.** The NIRA was signed on June 16, 1933, and was to remain in effect for two years. Its Title I created the National Recovery Administration and authorized codes covering prices, wages, hours, and conditions; its Title II provided public works. Section 7(a) protected collective bargaining and became the most enduring part of the law.[^ch34-nira]
- **The NRA in practice.** The “Blue Eagle” campaign enrolled employers who agreed to the codes, but the codes could raise prices, restrain competition, and burden small firms, and compliance was uneven. The chapter should present the NRA as a genuine attempt at planning whose administration exposed its contradictions.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-schechter]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, the codes, and the administrative record. It cannot show that the codes raised employment, and the chapter should treat the recovery claim as contested.[^ch34-check-debate-works][^ch34-nira]

### 2.4 Butler and Schechter: the courts strike down the recovery state

- **The claim.** In 1935 and 1936 the Supreme Court unanimously struck down the NIRA’s code authority and, by a 6–3 vote, invalidated the AAA’s processing tax, holding that Congress had delegated too much and regulated matters reserved to the states.[^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler]
- **Schechter.** On May 27, 1935, the Court held that §3 of the NIRA was an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power to the president and that the act, as applied to a poultry business, exceeded the commerce power. The decision came less than three weeks before the act would have expired.[^ch34-schechter][^ch34-nira]
- **Butler.** In *United States v. Butler* (1936), the Court held that the AAA’s processing tax was “but means to an unconstitutional end,” because it used the taxing power to control agricultural production reserved to the states. Justices Stone, Brandeis, and Cardozo dissented.[^ch34-butler]
- **The turn.** In 1937 the Court upheld the National Labor Relations Act in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, and observers later debated whether Justice Owen Roberts’s shift was a response to Roosevelt’s court-packing plan or a reconciliation with his earlier jurisprudence. The chapter should present the “switch in time” as a contested explanation.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the holdings, the votes, and the dates. It cannot settle the judges’ motives, and the chapter should not attribute a single cause to the shift.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]

### 2.5 What survived

- **The claim.** The judicial defeats ended the first New Deal’s planning experiments without ending federal recovery efforts; public works, the TVA, and the regulatory approach of the Second New Deal survived.[^ch34-tva-act][^ch34-nlra]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the shift from production controls to spending, public works, and legal protection of organizing as a response to judicial constraint as well as to political calculation.[^ch34-nira][^ch34-frh-1937]
- **The link to §3.** The administrative defeats left the Social Security Act as the New Deal’s most durable domestic achievement, which makes the question of its coverage the more pressing.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

## 3. Social Security and the two-track welfare state

### 3.1 The claim: security for some, assistance for others

- **The claim.** The Social Security Act of 1935 created a national system of old-age and unemployment insurance for workers in commerce and industry, a separate and less generous system of means-tested assistance for others, and thereby fixed a two-track welfare state that distributed protection unequally.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-gordon-welfare]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the act’s insurance titles, its assistance titles, and its exclusions as one design, not as a compromise imposed from outside.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-ssa-section-811]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, the definitions, and the coverage figures. It cannot show the drafters’ motives from the text alone, and the chapter should set the competing explanations in §6.[^ch34-check-debate-works][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

### 3.2 The Committee on Economic Security and the 1935 Act

- **The claim.** The act was the product of a deliberate planning process led by Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins, and it separated social insurance, financed by payroll taxes and earned as a right, from assistance, financed from general revenues and conditioned on a means test.[^ch34-perkins][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]
- **The setting.** Roosevelt created the Committee on Economic Security in 1934, and Perkins, the first woman to serve in a presidential cabinet and Secretary of Labor from March 1933 to July 1945, guided the proposal. The act was signed on August 14, 1935.[^ch34-perkins][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]
- **The titles.** The act provided grants to states for old-age assistance (Title I), federal old-age benefits (Title II), unemployment compensation administration (Title III), aid to dependent children (Title IV), and maternal and child welfare (Title V), and created the Social Security Board (Title VII).[^ch34-ssa-1935-act]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the process, the officials, and the titles. It cannot show that the design was inevitable, and the chapter should present the split as a choice with alternatives.[^ch34-perkins][^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 3.3 The In Focus: Section 811’s employment exclusions

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads the definition of “employment” in §811(b) of the Social Security Act of 1935 as a single legal artifact. The definition begins by covering “any service, of whatever nature, performed within the United States by an employee for his employer” and then lists exceptions: agricultural labor; domestic service in a private home; casual labor not in the course of the employer’s trade or business; service by a person who has attained the age of 65; service as an officer or crew member of a vessel; service for the United States or a state; and service for religious, charitable, scientific, literary, or educational nonprofit organizations. Section 811(a) also capped taxable wages at $3,000 a year. Read closely, the definition is the administrative boundary of the new social insurance: it decides who was inside the program and who was left to the states, to charity, or to nothing. The box should transcribe the exceptions exactly, identify the title and section, and connect the words to the people they excluded — a large share of farm and domestic workers, among them a disproportionate number of Black workers. It should treat the section as a legal and social document and end on the chapter’s question: what kind of security did the act create, and for whom?[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

- **The registered status.** No image is assigned to this chapter, so the section is planned as a textual close reading. Reproducing the page would require a new register entry, with its own caption, credit, and rights, before it could be added.[^ch34-check-image][^ch34-check-focus]
- **The exact text.** The exceptions are quoted from the Social Security Administration’s transcription of Title VIII, §811(b). The box should also note the parallel definition of “employment” in the benefit titles and the $3,000 cap in §811(a), and should cite the section rather than a later amended compilation.[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-check-ssa-exclusions]
- **What must not be done.** The box must not treat the exclusions as an accident of drafting without saying so, must not claim a single motive as established, and must not let the coverage statistics stand in for the words of the section. It should present the dispute over motive as a dispute.[^ch34-check-focus][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

### 3.4 Insurance and assistance: the two tracks

- **The claim.** The act’s two tracks gave workers in covered employment an earned, contributory benefit and gave others a stigmatized, means-tested payment, and the difference shaped American welfare politics for generations.[^ch34-gordon-welfare][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]
- **Old-age insurance.** Title II provided federal old-age benefits financed by payroll taxes on covered workers and employers, with taxes scheduled to begin in 1937. The chapter should present the contributory principle as the source of the program’s political durability.[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]
- **Assistance.** Titles I, IV, and X provided federal matching grants for old-age assistance, aid to dependent children, and aid to the blind, administered by the states and conditioned on need. The chapter should present these as the second track, less generous and more discretionary.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-gordon-welfare]
- **The two-track thesis.** Linda Gordon and Theda Skocpol trace the stratified structure to the maternalist and bureaucratic compromises of the Progressive Era and the 1930s, in which single mothers received “welfare” while (mostly male) workers received “social insurance.” The chapter should present the thesis and its evidence.[^ch34-gordon-welfare][^ch34-skocpol]

### 3.5 Who was covered, who was left out

- **The claim.** The act covered about half the jobs in the economy, and the excluded occupations — farm labor, domestic service, and much government and nonprofit work — were disproportionately held by African Americans and women, especially in the South.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]
- **The racial-exclusion thesis.** Ira Katznelson argues that southern Democrats shaped the New Deal’s occupational exclusions to protect the region’s racial order, so that federal social provision entrenched a racial gap. The chapter should state this argument in its strongest form and check it against the record.[^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]
- **The competing explanation.** Larry DeWitt, writing for the Social Security Administration, argues that the racial-bias thesis is flawed and unsupported and that the exclusions reflected the administrative difficulty of collecting payroll taxes from scattered farm and domestic workers. The chapter should present both positions fairly and reserve judgment for §6.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-check-debate-works]
- **The map.** Map 26’s second placement belongs here, showing the geography of program reach and migration together, and citing Appendix B, Table 8 for its data.[^ch34-check-map26][^ch34-dustbowl]
- **What the record can show.** It can show coverage shares and occupational distributions. It cannot fully separate intent from effect, and the chapter should name that limit.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

## 4. The CIO and workplace conflict

### 4.1 The claim: a labor regime remade from below and by law

- **The claim.** Between 1935 and 1937 the federal government protected the right to organize, industrial unions organized the mass-production industries, and workers used the sit-down strike to win recognition — a transformation that depended on law and mobilization together.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-cohen][^ch34-lichtenstein]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should connect the Wagner Act, the founding of the CIO, the Flint sit-down, and the violence at Little Steel as one story about who would control the workplace.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-memorial-day]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, the elections, the strike dates, and the casualty figures. It cannot show what every worker intended, and the chapter should avoid reducing a diverse movement to a single motive.[^ch34-check-silences][^ch34-cohen]

### 4.2 The Wagner Act and the National Labor Relations Board

- **The claim.** The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 made the federal government an enforcer of collective bargaining, not merely a mediator, and created a board to run representation elections and police unfair labor practices.[^ch34-nlra]
- **The statute.** Senator Robert F. Wagner introduced the bill in February 1935; it passed the Senate in May and the House in June and was signed on July 5, 1935. Section 7 guaranteed employees the right to organize and bargain collectively, and the act required employers to bargain with unions chosen by a majority of employees in an appropriate unit.[^ch34-nlra]
- **The constitutional test.** In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* (1937) the Supreme Court upheld the act as a valid exercise of the commerce power, the decision that anchors the “switch in time” debate. The chapter should date it and present the debate.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the text, the votes, and the board’s record. It cannot show that the law alone produced the organizing wave, and the chapter should credit workers’ own action.[^ch34-cohen][^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 The CIO and the sit-down strikes

- **The claim.** The Congress of Industrial Organizations, founded in 1935 under John L. Lewis, organized entire industries rather than single crafts, and the sit-down strike at General Motors in Flint, Michigan, in the winter of 1936–37 forced the company to recognize the United Auto Workers.[^ch34-lichtenstein][^ch34-cohen]
- **The CIO.** Originally a committee within the American Federation of Labor, the CIO was created in November 1935 by Lewis and other union leaders to organize workers in mass-production industries. The chapter should give the founding date, the leaders, and the industrial-union strategy.[^ch34-lichtenstein]
- **Flint.** The Flint sit-down strike lasted into February 1937 and ended with General Motors’ recognition of the union. The chapter should present the sit-down as a tactic that prevented strikebreaking and that raised the question of who controlled company property.[^ch34-cohen][^ch34-check-labor]
- **U.S. Steel.** In March 1937 the United States Steel Corporation recognized the Steel Workers Organizing Committee, a settlement that the “Little Steel” companies refused to match.[^ch34-memorial-day][^ch34-lichtenstein]

### 4.4 Little Steel, the Memorial Day Massacre, and the limits of the settlement

- **The claim.** The organizing wave met organized resistance, and on May 30, 1937, Chicago police shot and beat Republic Steel demonstrators, killing ten people and wounding scores — a defeat that showed the state’s force could fall on the side of employers.[^ch34-memorial-day]
- **The event.** On Memorial Day 1937, several hundred white, Black, and Latino men, women, and children demonstrated at Republic Steel on Chicago’s far Southeast Side. Company-purchased tear gas and police clubs and gunfire produced ten dead, 40 shot, and more than 90 wounded. The chapter should give the counts with their source and note the participation of Mexican American workers, including the injured volunteer Lupe Marshall.[^ch34-memorial-day]
- **The aftermath.** The La Follette Committee’s investigations documented employer espionage and labor practices. The chapter should present the massacre as a limit on the labor settlement, not a footnote.[^ch34-memorial-day][^ch34-check-labor]
- **Wages and hours.** The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set minimum wages and maximum hours and banned child labor in interstate commerce, but it too excluded many workers, including agricultural and domestic workers. The chapter should date it and connect its exclusions to those of the Social Security Act. [^ch34-check-labor][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

### 4.5 What the labor settlement did not include

- **The claim.** The new labor regime reached the organized core of mass-production industry and left much of the workforce — farmworkers, domestic workers, and many service and public employees — outside its protection.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-lichtenstein]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should connect the Wagner Act and the FLSA exclusions to the Social Security exclusions, showing a consistent boundary around the workers the system protected.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]
- **What the record can show.** It can show union membership, contract terms, and coverage exclusions. It cannot show what non-union workers thought about a movement that did not include them, and the chapter should name that silence.[^ch34-check-silences]

## 5. The 1937–38 recession and the conservative coalition

### 5.1 The claim: the New Deal’s high tide and ebb

- **The claim.** The New Deal reached its political peak with the 1936 landslide and then lost momentum through the court-packing fight, the recession of 1937–38, and the elections of 1938, which left a conservative coalition able to block further reform.[^ch34-app-1936][^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-e1938]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the triumph and the reversal as one story about the limits of the New Deal coalition.[^ch34-check-recession][^ch34-e1938]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the returns, the votes, and the economic series. It cannot show that a different strategy would have preserved momentum, and the chapter should mark that counterfactual as open.[^ch34-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The 1936 landslide and the court-packing plan

- **The claim.** Roosevelt won a second term in one of the most lopsided elections in American history, and then squandered some of that authority on a plan to enlarge the Supreme Court.[^ch34-app-1936][^ch34-fjc-courtp]
- **The result.** Roosevelt carried 523 electoral votes and about 60.8 percent of the popular vote, against Alfred M. Landon’s 8 electoral votes and about 36.5 percent. The chapter should give the totals with their source and note that compilations differ slightly.[^ch34-app-1936]
- **The court plan.** On February 5, 1937, Roosevelt proposed adding one justice for each sitting justice over the age of 70, up to six additional justices, to change the Court’s ideological balance. The bill failed, and the controversy cost him political support. The chapter should date the proposal and the defeat.[^ch34-fjc-courtp]
- **The “switch in time.”** The Court upheld the National Labor Relations Act in 1937, and some called it “the switch in time that saved nine.” Legal scholars have disputed whether Justice Roberts responded to the plan or reconciled his jurisprudence. The chapter should present the dispute rather than choose a side.[^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-check-court]

### 5.3 The 1937–38 recession

- **The claim.** In the middle of recovery the economy contracted sharply again, and the causes combined fiscal contraction, monetary tightening, and the removal of relief spending.[^ch34-frh-1937]
- **The downturn.** The contraction ran from May 1937 to June 1938. Real gross domestic product fell 10 percent, unemployment reached 20 percent, and industrial production fell 32 percent. The chapter should give the series and their definitions.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-check-numbers]
- **The causes.** Federal Reserve policymakers doubled reserve requirements in 1936 to absorb excess reserves, and the Treasury began sterilizing gold inflows in late 1936; the combined effect reduced the growth of the money stock and then turned it negative. Federal spending and relief also fell. The chapter should present these as interacting causes, not one master cause.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-check-recession]
- **The turn to spending.** The recession strengthened the argument for deficit spending and contributed to the administration’s later embrace of compensatory fiscal policy, a shift that Alan Brinkley identifies with the end of structural reform.[^ch34-brinkley][^ch34-frh-1937]

### 5.4 The 1938 purge and the conservative coalition

- **The claim.** Roosevelt tried and failed to defeat conservative Democrats in the 1938 primaries, and the elections left Republicans and southern Democrats able to block the New Deal’s remaining agenda.[^ch34-e1938]
- **The purge.** Roosevelt campaigned against conservative Democrats opposed to his programs beginning in the summer of 1938, and the effort largely failed. The chapter should date the campaign and name the targets.[^ch34-e1938]
- **The coalition.** The House’s own history records that the 1938 midterms helped cement the conservative coalition of Republicans and southern Democrats that would shape Congress for a generation. The chapter should present the coalition as an institutional constraint on the New Deal’s later reach.[^ch34-e1938][^ch34-check-recession]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the election results and the coalition’s roll-call behavior. It cannot show that the coalition alone ended reform, and the chapter should hold it together with the recession and the war’s approach.[^ch34-e1938][^ch34-check-dates-appD]

### 5.5 The shape of the New Deal state by 1939

- **The claim.** By 1939 the New Deal had built durable federal institutions — deposit insurance, securities regulation, social insurance, and legal protection for unions — while leaving large parts of the workforce outside them.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]
- **The balance sheet.** The chapter should close the section with the settlement’s gains and its boundaries, preparing the debate over what the New Deal did and for whom.[^ch34-check-table1][^ch34-katznelson]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the institutions and their coverage. It cannot show how Americans experienced the New Deal in general, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch34-check-silences]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The New Deal expanded federal responsibility.** Banking supervision, deposit insurance, securities regulation, social insurance, and labor law all made the federal government a permanent participant in the economy.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-nlra]
- **It created lasting institutions.** The FDIC, the SEC, the Social Security Board, and the National Labor Relations Board survived the New Deal’s political reversals.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]
- **It distributed protection unequally.** The act covered about half the jobs in the economy, and the labor and wage laws left farm and domestic workers outside their protection.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-nlra]
- **It faced real judicial and political limits.** The Court struck down the NIRA’s code authority and the AAA’s processing tax, and the conservative coalition checked further reform after 1938.[^ch34-schechter][^ch34-butler][^ch34-e1938]
- **The recession of 1937–38 was severe.** Real output, employment, and industrial production all fell sharply in the middle of recovery.[^ch34-frh-1937]

### 6.2 What is open

- **How much did the New Deal end the Depression?** One position credits federal spending and relief; another argues that recovery was incomplete until the war and that the New Deal’s own policies, including labor costs and taxes, slowed it. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-check-debate-works]
- **Did the exclusions reflect racial design or administrative necessity?** Katznelson and others argue for racial design through southern power; DeWitt argues for administrative feasibility and finds no racial motive. The chapter should weigh the evidence for both.[^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]
- **Was the New Deal a revolution or a rescue?** Leuchtenburg sees an incomplete revolution in federal responsibility; Bernstein sees conservative achievements that preserved capitalism. The chapter should set the evidence side by side.[^ch34-leuchtenburg][^ch34-bernstein]
- **Who drove the labor settlement?** One account credits federal law and the Wagner Act; another credits workers’ own mobilization and institutions. The chapter should present both mechanisms.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-cohen]
- **Did the New Deal’s two-track welfare state reflect maternalist choices or later racial politics?** Gordon and Skocpol locate the split in earlier maternalist policy; others emphasize southern power in 1935. The chapter should mark the question open.[^ch34-gordon-welfare][^ch34-skocpol][^ch34-katznelson]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the New Deal genuinely transformed the federal government’s role while building its protections around boundaries of occupation, race, gender, and region that the same legislation helped to fix.[^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-katznelson]
- **Contingency.** The New Deal’s shape was the result of decisions taken by people who could have chosen differently — the exclusions in the 1935 act, the court-packing gamble, the spending cutbacks of 1937 — not an inevitable design.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-frh-1937]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold the structural constraints — southern power in Congress, the administrative reach of the tax system, the Court, a weak recovery — together with the contingent choices made within them.[^ch34-katznelson][^ch34-e1938]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that the New Deal created a new kind of security, real and durable, and that it defined that security by who was inside the program and who was left outside.[^ch34-check-table1][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]

## Legacies

The New Deal left a durable legacy of federal responsibility. It made the federal government the guarantor of bank deposits, the regulator of securities markets, the administrator of old-age and unemployment insurance, and the protector of the right to organize. Americans would argue for generations about the size and shape of that responsibility, but the argument itself assumed the federal role the New Deal created.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-nlra]

The legacy was also exclusionary. Because social insurance and labor law reached workers in commerce and industry, the workers left outside — farmworkers, domestic workers, and many service and public employees, disproportionately Black and female — built their later claims against a program that had not originally included them, a story that runs into the civil rights era and Chapter 39.[^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-katznelson]

The institutional legacy outlasted the coalition. The Social Security Board became the Social Security Administration, the National Labor Relations Board endured, and deposit insurance survived the later separation of commercial and investment banking in 1999; the New Deal’s agencies carried the settlement into decades when its politics had changed.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-1935-act]

The political legacy was contested from the start. The conservative coalition that formed after 1938 set the terms of a long argument over the New Deal’s reach, an argument that continues in later chapters and in the present.[^ch34-e1938][^ch34-check-dates-appD]

- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents the insured, the enrolled, and the organized — covered workers, CCC recruits, union members — and under-represents tenant farmers, domestic workers, migrant laborers, and women without independent claims to work. ## Summary

- Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, proclaimed a national bank holiday on March 6, and signed the Emergency Banking Act on March 9; he explained the banking rescue in his first fireside chat on March 12.[^ch34-frh-eba][^ch34-fireside-chat]
- Banks reopened beginning March 13, and the Glass-Steagall Act created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation and separated commercial from investment banking; bank failures fell from more than 4,000 in 1933 to 61 in 1934.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking]
- The Hundred Days created the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Federal Emergency Relief Administration, the Tennessee Valley Authority, and other programs; the CCC employed more than 2.5 million men, and the TVA displaced families and towns as it built its dams.[^ch34-ccc][^ch34-tva-act]
- The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 paid farmers to cut acreage through a processing tax, and the Supreme Court struck the tax down in *United States v. Butler* (1936).[^ch34-butler]
- The National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 created the National Recovery Administration and protected collective bargaining in Section 7(a); the Supreme Court struck down its code authority in *Schechter* (1935).[^ch34-nira][^ch34-schechter]
- The Social Security Act of 1935 created old-age and unemployment insurance for covered workers and means-tested assistance for others; its §811(b) excluded agricultural labor, domestic service, and other categories, and its coverage reached about half the jobs in the economy.[^ch34-ssa-1935-act][^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions]
- The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 protected the right to organize and created the National Labor Relations Board, which the Supreme Court upheld in 1937; the CIO organized mass-production industries, and the Flint sit-down forced General Motors to recognize the United Auto Workers.[^ch34-nlra][^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-lichtenstein]
- On May 30, 1937, Chicago police killed ten Republic Steel demonstrators and wounded scores more in the Memorial Day Massacre.[^ch34-memorial-day]
- Roosevelt won 523 electoral votes and about 60.8 percent of the popular vote in 1936, then lost the court-packing fight; the recession of 1937–38 cut real output by 10 percent and pushed unemployment to 20 percent, and the 1938 elections cemented the conservative coalition.[^ch34-app-1936][^ch34-fjc-courtp][^ch34-frh-1937][^ch34-e1938]

### The debate

> *A closing note.* Between 1933 and 1939 the United States built a new relationship between the federal government and the economy. A banking rescue became deposit insurance and securities regulation; a farm program and an industrial code became, after the Court struck them down, a turn to social insurance and public works; a guarantee of the right to organize became the CIO and the sit-down strikes; and a recession and a failed purge became the conservative coalition. The New Deal created real security for millions, and it drew the boundaries of that security around occupation, race, gender, and region. The chapter’s question — what kind of security did the New Deal create, and for whom? — is answered by that double record.[^ch34-fdrlibrary-banking][^ch34-ssa-section-811][^ch34-dewitt-exclusions][^ch34-e1938]

That story’s roots run back through the same years this chapter has covered, and the two chapters are meant to be read together.[^ch34-check-bridge][^ch34-check-ch35]
