# Chapter 41. Vietnam and the Cold War World (1953–1968)

> “Nothing is more precious than independence and freedom.”[^ch41-hcm-quote]
>
> — Hồ Chí Minh, appeal to the nation, July 1966.[^ch41-hcm-quote]

> “We are not about to send American boys 9 or 10,000 miles away from home to do what Asian boys ought to be doing for themselves.”[^ch41-lbj-1964-quote]
>
> — Lyndon B. Johnson, remarks at the University of Akron, October 21, 1964.[^ch41-lbj-1964-quote]

> “The title is meant quite simply to convey that there was a choice, that American leaders had options besides escalation in 1964–65. There was nothing preordained about the war.”[^ch41-logevall]
>
> — Fredrik Logevall, interview on *Choosing War*, Woodrow Wilson International Center for Scholars, October 4, 1999.[^ch41-logevall]

Three voices, three verdicts. Hồ Chí Minh stated the Vietnamese claim in the period’s own words: independence and freedom, in that order. Johnson promised that the fighting would be left to Asian soldiers, a promise he abandoned within a year. Logevall argues that the escalation was a choice among alternatives, not a tragedy without authors. They disagree about what the war was for, who should fight it, and whether it had to happen. Their disagreement frames the chapter’s question: why did a strategy of containment produce an escalating war it could not resolve?[^ch41-hcm-quote][^ch41-lbj-1964-quote][^ch41-logevall][^ch41-check-epigraphs]

On the night of August 4, 1964, two United States destroyers, the USS *Maddox* and the USS *C. Turner Joy*, reported that North Vietnamese torpedo boats had attacked them in the Gulf of Tonkin, in international waters off the coast of North Vietnam. Two nights earlier, on August 2, North Vietnamese boats had attacked the *Maddox*, which was conducting an electronic-eavesdropping patrol in support of South Vietnamese commando raids on the North. The August 4 reports were different: confused, urgent, and, in the judgment of the patrol commander, uncertain. Captain John J. Herrick, aboard the *Maddox*, first reported a torpedo attack. He then cabled that “freak weather effects on radar and overeager sonarmen may have accounted for many reports.” Secretary of Defense Robert S. McNamara did not pass the commander’s doubts to President Lyndon B. Johnson.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

Johnson ordered retaliatory air strikes on North Vietnamese targets and, that evening, addressed the nation on television. He described the attacks as unprovoked aggression and asked Congress for a resolution of support. On August 7, Congress passed the joint resolution, 98 to 2 in the Senate, with Wayne Morse of Oregon and Ernest Gruening of Alaska dissenting, and unanimously in the House. Johnson signed it on August 10 as Public Law 88-408, 78 Stat. 384. It authorized him “to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression.”[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

Only later did the record show how shaky the August 4 report had been. In 2005 and 2006 the National Security Agency released more than 140 formerly classified documents, including a study by its historian Robert J. Hanyok. Hanyok concluded that the August 2 attack occurred, that the August 4 attack did not, and that the agency’s reporting had been “skewed” toward the administration’s claim. “The overwhelming body of reports, if used, would have told the story that no attack occurred,” he wrote.[^ch41-nsa-tonkin][^ch41-hanyok]

The record can show the reports, the doubts, the strikes, the votes, and the later finding. It cannot show what Johnson privately believed that night, what McNamara intended by withholding the commander’s cable, or that the resolution alone caused the escalation that followed. On August 4, 1964, nothing in the record shows that anyone in Washington knew the second attack had not happened, that American troop strength would reach 536,000 by 1968, or that Congress would repeal the resolution in 1971.[^ch41-check-scene][^ch41-check-silences]

Three days separated a contested naval report from a near-declaration of war. Why did a strategy of containment produce an escalating war it could not resolve?

This chapter covers the years from 1953 to 1968 and asks why a strategy of containment produced an escalating war it could not resolve. It treats the Cold War in the Third World as the frame for the Vietnam War, not as a backdrop: the instruments the United States used in Iran, Guatemala, Suez, Cuba, and the Congo — covert action, client armies, economic pressure, and the threat of force — were the instruments it carried into Indochina. It does not re-narrate the Great Society and its fiscal constraints (Ch. 40), the civil rights movement (Ch. 39), the 1968 election and Chicago (Ch. 42), or the end of the war after 1968 (Chs. 42–43). It draws on the *Foreign Relations of the United States* series, presidential speeches and recordings, signals-intelligence releases, congressional records, military histories, casualty data, opinion surveys, antiwar movement materials, and the secondary literature.[^ch41-check-scope][^ch41-check-ch40][^ch41-check-ch42][^ch41-check-dates-appD]

The chapter uses several contested names. “Containment” was a strategy of limiting Soviet and Chinese influence, but its instruments and its costs were disputed even in the period. “Credibility” described the belief that allies and adversaries would judge American resolve by what happened in Vietnam; it was a claim about reputation, not a measurable object. “Escalation” describes a series of decisions, not a single plan. “Client state” describes a government dependent on American aid and advice without being a colony. “Viet Cong” was the term for the communist insurgency in the South, which also included northern soldiers and cadres. “Body count” was a metric of enemy dead, not a measure of political success. “The Tet offensive” refers to the coordinated attacks of 1968, not a single battle.[^ch41-check-names]

The available record over-represents American presidents, generals, and reporters and under-represents Vietnamese peasants, soldiers, and officials and the Americans who resisted the draft or were drafted against their will. Troop, casualty, and opinion figures often measure different things; they are given with their definitions, dates, and denominators. Where the archive is thin, the chapter names the silence rather than filling it with a composite witness.[^ch41-check-silences][^ch41-check-numbers]

The chapter proceeds in six movements, organized thematically around questions rather than as a single strict chronology, because the covert, diplomatic, and client-state stories overlap in time. §1 examines the “cheap” Cold War of covert action and crisis in Iran, Guatemala, Suez, Cuba, and the Congo. §2 turns to Geneva and the first Vietnam war. §3 follows the client state under Ngô Đình Diệm and the 1963 coup. §4 examines the Gulf of Tonkin resolution, the Americanization of the war, the draft, and the supply war, reads Map 32, and closes with the resolution in the In Focus. §5 follows the Tet offensive, My Lai, the antiwar movement, and the decision of March 31, 1968. §6 asks how historians have weighed containment, credibility, Vietnamese nationalism, bureaucratic failure, and the alternatives. The chapter begins on the night of August 4, 1964, in the Gulf of Tonkin.[^ch41-check-scope]

## 1. The “cheap” Cold War: Iran, Guatemala, Suez, Cuba, and the Congo (1953–1965)

### 1.1 The claim: containment practiced on the cheap

**The claim.** Faced with a Soviet nuclear arsenal, and unwilling to risk a general war to match it, the Eisenhower and Kennedy administrations pursued containment through covert action, covertly armed clients, and regional crises rather than direct great-power war. These interventions were “cheap” in American lives and dollars and expensive in the sovereignty of the countries they touched. They set the pattern that Vietnam would follow and finally break.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive][^ch41-guatemala-frus][^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller]

The section runs from the 1953 Iran coup through the 1954 Guatemala operation, the 1956 Suez crisis, the Cuban crises of 1961–62, and the Congo from 1960 to 1965. Each case is a bounded episode; together they show the instruments — covert action, surrogate armies, economic pressure, and the threat of force — that the United States carried into Indochina. The cases were not identical, and together they establish a repertoire, not a template.[^ch41-suez-milestone][^ch41-congo-milestone]

### 1.2 Iran, 1953

In August 1953, the United States and Britain backed the overthrow of Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddeq of Iran, whose government had nationalized the British-owned oil industry. The covert operation was code-named TPAJAX, known as Operation Ajax. The CIA’s internal history, *The Battle for Iran*, written in the mid-1970s, states that the agency planned and helped carry out the coup; the agency’s first formal public acknowledgment of its role came with the release of documents in 2013.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive]

The record is incomplete. The National Security Archive notes that fundamental questions — who hatched the plot, who carried it out, who supported it inside Iran, and how it succeeded — remain in dispute, and that most CIA records on the coup were reportedly lost or destroyed in the early 1960s. The coup was a joint Anglo-American operation with significant Iranian participation, not an American act alone. The shah returned from exile, and the episode became a template for covert regime change.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive]

### 1.3 Guatemala, 1954

In June 1954, a CIA-organized operation code-named PBSUCCESS overthrew the government of President Jacobo Árbenz of Guatemala, whose land-reform program had threatened the holdings of the United Fruit Company. The operation combined psychological warfare, a small exile force, and the threat of American military force.[^ch41-guatemala-frus]

The State Department’s documentary history records that the plan of operations called for cutting off military aid to Guatemala, increasing aid to its neighbors, and exerting diplomatic and economic pressure. The retrospective FRUS volume and the CIA’s declassified account are the principal published records; the documents establish the plan and leave parts of the operation’s course in dispute.[^ch41-guatemala-frus]

### 1.4 Suez, 1956

On July 26, 1956, Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company, the British-French enterprise that had operated the canal since 1869. In October, Israeli forces invaded the Sinai, and Britain and France landed troops under the pretext of protecting the canal. The Eisenhower administration, worried about Soviet intervention and about being associated with European colonialism, pressured its allies into a cease-fire on November 6 and supported United Nations resolutions condemning the invasion.[^ch41-suez-milestone]

The episode ended Britain’s and France’s ability to act independently in the region and prompted the Eisenhower Doctrine, which extended American power in the Middle East. It also showed the United States using pressure on allies rather than force on the adversary, another version of the “cheap” approach.[^ch41-suez-milestone]

### 1.5 Cuba: the Bay of Pigs and the missile crisis, 1961–1962

In April 1961, President John F. Kennedy carried out a CIA plan inherited from the Eisenhower administration and altered it; about 1,500 anti-Castro Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on April 17 and were quickly defeated by Castro’s forces. The cover story collapsed, and the United States was exposed as the sponsor. The administration continued to press for Castro’s removal through the CIA-backed Operation Mongoose.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller]

In October 1962, American reconnaissance detected Soviet ballistic missiles and nuclear forces in Cuba, and Kennedy imposed a naval “quarantine,” backing it with the threat of force. After several days at the brink, the Soviets agreed to withdraw the missiles; the United States promised not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to withdraw missiles based in Turkey. The blockade was lifted on November 20, 1962.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller]

Both crises raised the value of “credibility” in American planning and deepened the belief that a small commitment, if abandoned, would invite larger challenges. That belief would be carried into Southeast Asia.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-debate-works]

### 1.6 The Congo, 1960–1965

The former Belgian Congo gained independence on June 30, 1960. Within days the army mutinied, Belgium sent troops, Katanga province seceded, and the United Nations created an intervention force. The United States, fearing Soviet influence, moved to remove Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba. He was arrested in September 1960 and killed on January 17, 1961; the State Department’s account records that plans to assassinate Lumumba were developed, and that Colonel Joseph Mobutu, later backed by American funds and support, took power.[^ch41-congo-milestone]

The Congo showed the same repertoire — covert action, client building, and the subordination of a newly independent state’s politics to Cold War categories — that would recur in Vietnam. The Congo establishes the recurring logic, not an identity among the cases.[^ch41-congo-milestone][^ch41-check-debate-works]

## 2. Geneva and the first Vietnam war (1945–1956)

### 2.1 The claim: a colonial war becomes an American commitment

**The claim.** The first Vietnam war was a Vietnamese war of independence against France, but by 1954 the United States was paying a large share of its cost and had begun to treat Vietnam as a Cold War test. The Geneva settlement of July 1954 partitioned the country and scheduled elections, and the United States supported the southern regime’s refusal to hold them.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-army-aid-france]

The section runs from Hồ Chí Minh’s 1945 declaration of independence from France through the French war, the siege of Dien Bien Phu in 1954, the Geneva Conference, and the cancellation of the 1956 reunification elections.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 2.2 The French war and Dien Bien Phu

After World War II, Hồ Chí Minh declared Vietnamese independence from France in 1945. American leaders, suspicious of his communism, backed France’s effort to retain Vietnam as a colony. Between 1950 and 1954 the United States contributed about $1.1 billion to France for the war, including some $746 million worth of Army matériel delivered to the French Expeditionary Corps in Indochina.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-army-aid-france]

The aid did not buy control. American advisers exercised little supervisory authority, and U.S. supplies were generally turned over to the French. The war ended at Dien Bien Phu, where the Viet Minh besieged and defeated the French garrison in 1954, ending French hopes of holding Indochina and opening the Geneva negotiations.[^ch41-army-aid-france][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 2.3 The Geneva settlement

The 1954 Geneva Accords partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with Viet Minh forces regrouping to the North and the State of Vietnam’s forces to the South, and scheduled elections in 1956 to reunify the country. The United States did not sign or endorse the accords; the Eisenhower administration regarded their provisions as potentially disastrous.[^ch41-army-aid-france]

American planners looked for a way around the elections. A CIA memorandum of December 20, 1954, put the problem plainly: “Perhaps the only course by which the Western signers of the Geneva agreement could concert to circumvent or avoid the elections would be through the accumulation of extensive, well-documented and persuasive evidence of Viet Minh violations of the agreement.” The memorandum proposed a campaign to document violations and build a case for postponing or canceling the vote.[^ch41-geneva-elections-frus]

The United States moved instead to strengthen a noncommunist state in the South, invoking the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization, created in 1954, as the legal frame for its support. The terms the signers signed and the interpretations the Americans placed on them were not the same.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

### 2.4 The canceled elections and the SEATO frame

Elections scheduled for July 1956 to reunite the country were never held. Prime Minister Ngô Đình Diệm of the South, with American support, canceled them, arguing that free elections were impossible in the North. The Office of the Historian’s account concludes that the communists seemed likely to win, chiefly through organization and rural appeal, and that the United States supported Diem’s refusal.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

By the late 1950s a communist guerrilla force in the South, the Viet Cong, was fighting to overthrow Diem’s government, with material support from the North. The Second Indochina War had begun.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

## 3. The client state (1954–1963)

### 3.1 The claim: aid built a state its own people did not trust

**The claim.** American aid and advice created a South Vietnamese state and army that depended on the United States, but Diem’s government never won the loyalty of a majority of southerners. His repression of Buddhists and political opponents and his failure on land reform strengthened the insurgency, and the 1963 coup that removed him left a succession of unstable governments.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-diem-coup-miller]

The section runs from Diem’s establishment of his government after 1954 through the Buddhist crisis of 1963, the American debate over a coup, and the coup and killing of November 1–2, 1963.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller]

### 3.2 Diem’s regime and American aid

Diem was a Catholic leading a predominantly Buddhist country. He governed with a military supplied and trained by the United States and with substantial American economic assistance. The Office of the Historian’s account concludes that he failed to capture the loyalties of southerners the way Hồ Chí Minh had in the North, and that his rural policies and ambivalence toward land reform bolstered the Viet Cong.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

The aid was substantial. After the Geneva accords the United States built up the South Vietnamese army under a military assistance program, bypassed the accords’ ceiling on advisers with temporary missions, and set a force goal that grew from 100,000 to 150,000 men by 1956. Diem’s government depended on that aid and on an army whose command structure he kept divided among rivals.[^ch41-army-aid-france]

Kennedy inherited and expanded the advisory mission. By the end of 1963 there were about 16,000 American military advisers in South Vietnam. The buildup was the work of two administrations, not one president.[^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 3.3 The Buddhist crisis, 1963

In the spring of 1963, government forces cracked down on Buddhist monks. On June 11 a monk, Thích Quảng Đức, burned himself to death in a Saigon street in protest. Diem imposed martial law, and special forces directed by his brother, Ngô Đình Nhu, raided Buddhist pagodas.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-quang-duc-cable]

Kennedy pressed Diem for reforms while trying to preserve the regime as the best available barrier to communism. Advisers divided over whether the United States should acquiesce in or support a coup. The embassy cable reported the self-immolation the same day; the Miller Center’s account records the debate that followed.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-quang-duc-cable]

### 3.4 The 1963 coup and the death of two presidents

On November 1, 1963, South Vietnamese army officers led by General Dương Văn Minh overthrew and killed Diem and Nhu. At a meeting on October 29, Kennedy’s advisers had been divided. Secretary of State Dean Rusk urged caution: “We should caution the generals that they must have the situation in hand before they launch a coup.” Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy expressed doubts about breaking with Diem. The killing occurred with the knowledge and tacit approval of the Kennedy administration.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

The coup produced not stability but a succession of governments. Three weeks later, President Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas, and the war continued under new leadership in both countries. The coup did not settle the war; the record shows neither side did in the months after it.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]

## 4. Tonkin, Americanization, the draft, and the supply war (1964–1967)

### 4.1 The claim: a narrow mandate becomes a land war

**The claim.** Between August 1964 and the end of 1967 the United States moved from advising and supporting a client to fighting its own ground war, on the strength of a congressional resolution that Johnson used as the functional equivalent of a declaration of war. The strategy of gradual pressure and attrition did not force Hanoi to negotiate, and the draft carried the war into American homes.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-escalation-miller][^ch41-draft-michigan]

The section runs from the resolution through Operation Rolling Thunder and the first Marine landings in 1965, the July 1965 decision, the attrition strategy and the supply war, and the draft. It reads Map 32 and closes with the In Focus on the resolution.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-map32]

### 4.2 The Tonkin Resolution and the mandate

Johnson used the resolution as a functional equivalent to a declaration of war, though critics argued he should have asked for a formal declaration. Congress supported it on the assumption that the president would return before further escalation.[^ch41-tonkin-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

The planning had begun before the incident. Johnson approved National Security Action Memorandum 273 on November 26, 1963, directing the government to help South Vietnam “win their contest against the externally directed and supported communist conspiracy.” NSAM 288, in March 1964, called for greater use of American force, including planning for air strikes on the North. Operation Plan 34-A, approved in January 1964, shifted stepped-up covert operations against the North from the CIA to the military.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

### 4.3 Rolling Thunder, the Marines, and the July 1965 decision

After Communist attacks on American installations, Johnson authorized a program of sustained bombing of North Vietnam, code-named Operation Rolling Thunder. The administration called its graduated approach “Option C”: a “slow squeeze” of gradually increasing military pressure intended to force Hanoi to negotiate without provoking China or the Soviet Union. Johnson authorized the program on February 13, 1965; sustained operations began on March 2. Both dates matter.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]

On March 8, 1965, two Marine battalions, about 3,500 troops, landed near Da Nang to protect the air base. They were the first American combat forces sent to mainland Asia since the Korean War. By mid-April the Marines had moved to offensive operations; in June, General William C. Westmoreland requested an additional 44 battalions. By November 1965 there were more than 175,000 American troops; by the end of Johnson’s presidency, 536,000.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]

On July 28, 1965, Johnson announced that draft inductions would rise from 17,000 to 35,000 per month, and the administration committed to a land war it had said it would avoid. Johnson had campaigned in 1964 on a peace platform.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

On April 7, 1965, at Johns Hopkins University, Johnson coupled the bombing with an offer of economic development for the Mekong region and a willingness to discuss a settlement: “We will not be defeated. We will not grow tired. We will not withdraw either openly or under the cloak of a meaningless agreement.” Hanoi rejected the terms.[^ch41-escalation-miller]

### 4.4 Attrition, the body count, and the supply war: Map 32

Westmoreland’s strategy of attrition measured progress in enemy “body counts,” a metric that rewarded reported kills and could not demonstrate that the insurgency was being defeated. In a 1967 memorandum, Secretary of Defense Robert S. McNamara summarized Westmoreland’s claim that “we are fighting a war of attrition”: the enemy was losing between 1,500 and 2,000 killed in action a week, while American and South Vietnamese forces were losing 175 and 250 respectively, and the command believed it had reached the “cross-over point” at which enemy losses exceeded replacements. The numbers described enemy dead; they did not show whether the government of South Vietnam was winning the loyalty of the living.[^ch41-mcnamara-dpm][^ch41-check-numbers]

The Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia carried men and matériel south despite the bombing, and the United States expanded operations in Laos and along the border to interdict it. McNamara’s memorandum noted that supplies continued to flow and that Cambodia was becoming “more and more important as a supply base.” Map 32, “Southeast Asia and supply routes, 1954–1968,” is the chapter’s registered visual. It will distinguish the borders of North Vietnam, South Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, label bases and the trail, and state its date rather than imply it.[^ch41-check-map32][^ch41-mcnamara-dpm]

The map cannot show the volume or the success of infiltration; a boundary line is not a measure of a route’s use. The bombing campaign was measured in sorties and tonnage, but the figures vary with the period and target set, and no single tonnage figure serves as a measure of success. The map’s caption and source line will state its date, sources, and limits.[^ch41-check-map32]

### 4.5 The draft and the home front

Between 1964 and 1973 the military drafted about 2.2 million men out of an eligible pool of about 27 million. Draftees made up about 25 percent of the force in combat zones, but the draft led many others to volunteer to choose their service, and deferments favored the college-educated and the connected.[^ch41-draft-michigan]

About 80 percent of enlisted men came from poor or working-class families, according to the studies the exhibit cites.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-appy]

The draft brought the war home: it intensified the antiwar movement, and the first campus teach-in was held at the University of Michigan on March 24–25, 1965. The draft was both a policy and a cause of protest.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-antiwar-mcny]

### 4.6 In Focus: the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution

**In Focus.** The document is one page of law: H.J. Res. 1145, Public Law 88-408, 78 Stat. 384, approved August 10, 1964. Section 1 approves the president’s determination “to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression.” Section 2 extends the authority to “any member or protocol state of the Southeast Asia Collective Defense Treaty requesting assistance in defense of its freedom,” and authorizes “all necessary steps, including the use of armed force.” Section 3 says the resolution expires when the president determines that peace and security are “reasonably assured,” unless Congress terminates it earlier by concurrent resolution.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]

The close reading is in the gap. The resolution Congress debated in August 1964 was a response to a naval incident; the text it passed authorized a land war. Section 1’s “all necessary measures” and “prevent further aggression” had no geographic or temporal limit. Section 2 made the president the judge of when a SEATO member needed help. Section 3 made the president the judge of when the emergency had ended, subject only to a congressional resolution that would require a majority willing to confront him. The resolution did not cause the escalation; it supplied the legal form that later decisions filled.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-hanyok]

## 5. Tet, My Lai, protest, and the 1968 decision (1968)

### 5.1 The claim: a battlefield decision becomes a political one

**The claim.** In 1968 the war’s political foundations cracked. The Tet offensive, though it failed in its immediate military aims, showed Americans that victory was not near; My Lai showed what the war had done to those fighting it; the antiwar movement grew while public approval fell; and on March 31 Johnson withdrew from the presidential race and limited the bombing. The year did not end the war, but it ended the strategy of escalation.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]

The section runs from Tet through the post-Tet troop request, My Lai and its cover-up, the growth of protest and the “credibility gap,” and the March 31 speech.[^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]

### 5.2 Tet, January–February 1968

In late January 1968, during the lunar new year holiday, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces launched coordinated attacks on towns, cities, and government sites across South Vietnam, including a breach of the outer wall of the United States Embassy in Saigon. A second phase struck smaller cities and towns in May and June, and a third began in August.[^ch41-tet-milestone]

American and South Vietnamese forces retook the territory and inflicted heavy losses on the insurgents, but the offensive showed that the enemy was not as weak as the Johnson administration had claimed and that victory was not imminent. Both sides claimed victory; the political effect in the United States was a sharp fall in confidence.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]

Tet was not purely a military victory and a political defeat to be applauded or deplored. It was both a costly failure for Hanoi and a decisive shift in American politics. The offensive cost the Viet Cong dearly and did not end the war; it changed what Americans believed about the war.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 The troop request and the review

After Tet, General Westmoreland requested about 206,000 additional troops beyond the authorized 525,000 level. The request set off a debate within the administration about whether to expand the war or to begin disengaging.[^ch41-westmoreland-request][^ch41-tet-milestone]

Johnson asked advisers, including the new Secretary of Defense, Clark Clifford, to reassess. The Clifford Task Force began on March 2, 1968. The review concluded that more troops would not achieve the political or military objectives, that the enemy could match any augmentation, that a reserve call-up of about 280,000 would be required, and that the cost would reach $12 billion by 1969. The review marked the turn toward de-escalation.[^ch41-clifford-task-force][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]

### 5.4 My Lai, March 16, 1968

On March 16, 1968, soldiers of Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, 11th Infantry Brigade, Americal Division, killed several hundred unarmed South Vietnamese civilians in the hamlets of My Lai (Son My) in Quảng Ngãi province. Estimates of the dead range from 347 to 507; a South Vietnamese report in 1968 alleged more than 400.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]

The first American investigation by Colonel Oran Henderson concluded that 20 civilians had been killed accidentally; a second, parallel investigation by Lieutenant Colonel Frank Barker, whose own task force was the subject, concurred. The killings became known through the efforts of the soldier Ronald Ridenhour, whose March 1969 letter to officials and Congress triggered an Army inquiry and the Peers inquiry; the episode became public in 1969.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]

The range of estimates, the initial account, and the later inquiries matter because they show how the Army first explained the killings and how it later documented them. The massacre involved multiple platoons and commanders, not a single soldier.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]

### 5.5 Protest and the “credibility gap”

Opposition grew from campus teach-ins in 1965 to mass demonstrations. On April 15, 1967, as many as 400,000 people marched in New York from Central Park to the United Nations, the largest antiwar demonstration in American history to that date. Martin Luther King Jr. delivered “Beyond Vietnam” at Riverside Church on April 4, 1967, denouncing “the giant triplets of racism, extreme materialism, and militarism.” Veterans and active-duty soldiers also protested.[^ch41-antiwar-mcny]

The Gallup figures reproduced by Digital History show approval of Johnson’s handling of the war falling from 39 percent before Tet to 26 percent after it, approval of his handling of the presidency from 48 to 36 percent, and the share calling the war a mistake rising from 45 to 49 percent; the share identifying as “hawks” fell from 60 to 41 percent. The figures describe answers to different questions, and the original Gallup releases should be consulted for wording and sample populations.[^ch41-pubopinion-digitalhistory][^ch41-check-polls]

Contemporary critics coined the phrase “credibility gap” for the distance between the administration’s optimistic statements and the war’s realities. The gap widened after Tet and shaped the politics of 1968. The phrase described a real gap, but it was also a political weapon.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-names]

### 5.6 March 31, 1968

On March 31, 1968, Johnson announced a unilateral reduction in the bombing of North Vietnam, renewed the offer of talks, and, at the end, withdrew from the presidential race: “I have concluded that I should not permit the Presidency to become involved in the partisan divisions that are developing in this political year.” He said he would not seek, and would not accept, his party’s nomination.[^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]

Six weeks after Tet, the first presidential primary in New Hampshire brought Senator Eugene McCarthy a strong showing against the president, and Senator Robert F. Kennedy entered the race days later. The decision came from the convergence of the military review, the Tet shock, and the primary challenge, not from any one event.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-check-debate-works]

The speech did not end the war. The war was still being fought, and the political system was in crisis; the election and the convention pass to Chapter 42.[^ch41-check-ch42][^ch41-check-bridge]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

The documentary record establishes that the United States escalated by a series of decisions, not one. It shows the shift from advice and support to combat, the bombing campaign, the Marine landings, and the troop buildup to 536,000 by 1968. No single decision or document contains the war; the sequence does.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]

The weight of the signals-intelligence record shows that the August 4, 1964, attack did not occur. The National Security Agency’s declassified study concluded that the August 2 attack took place, that the August 4 attack did not, and that reporting had been skewed toward the administration’s claim. The resolution nevertheless became the legal basis for the war and was repealed in January 1971. The text and the votes are documented.[^ch41-hanyok][^ch41-nsa-tonkin][^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara]

Tet changed American politics without deciding the war. The offensive was a costly failure for Hanoi and a decisive blow to American confidence; the war continued for years. My Lai happened and was investigated, with a range of victim estimates and a record of initial denial. The human cost is documented, though even the dead are counted by different rules: the National Archives file records 58,220 American deaths, including 16,899 in 1968 and 47,434 classed as hostile, while the Vietnam Veterans Memorial lists 58,318 names. Vietnamese and broader Indochinese losses are estimates that must be given as such.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-casualties-nara][^ch41-vvm]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the escalation avoidable? Logevall argues that the United States had viable alternatives in 1964–65 and chose war. Accounts centered on the Cold War consensus argue that the constraints were tighter than the choice model allows: a president who withdrew would have paid a heavy political price at home and abroad, and the client state might have collapsed. Both claims can be tested against the record of what the principals considered.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-herring]

Was the war winnable? The revisionist account associated with Mark Moyar argues that the United States could have prevailed, and dates the decisive turn to the 1963 coup against Diem and to American political failure rather than military failure. The orthodox account argues that the war was unwinnable at acceptable cost. The revisionist case must be tested against the documents, including the record of South Vietnamese political instability and the limits of American power.[^ch41-moyar][^ch41-herring]

How much of the war was American and how much Vietnamese? Lien-Hang T. Nguyen and Pierre Asselin, using Hanoi’s archives, argue that North Vietnamese leaders pursued their own strategy, often differing with their Chinese and Soviet patrons, and that American intervention ended on terms shaped as much in Hanoi as in Washington. This is a corrective to the American-centered account, not a denial of American power.[^ch41-nguyen-hanoi][^ch41-asselin]

Was the failure bureaucratic or structural? H. R. McMaster argues that the Joint Chiefs and civilian leaders failed to debate the war honestly, producing decisions by default. Other accounts locate the failure in the structural belief in containment and credibility that no honest debate could have dislodged. The two kinds of explanation are distinct: a failure of advice is not the same as a failure of premises.[^ch41-mcmaster][^ch41-logevall]

Did the media lose the war? A common charge holds that television and reporting turned the public against a war the military was winning after Tet. The record shows Tet’s political effect and the administration’s own loss of confidence; it does not show that reporting alone reversed a settled military outcome. The causal claim remains contested.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-check-debate-works]

What counts as the war’s measure? Body counts, pacification indexes, bombing tonnage, and election results each measure different things. A body count measures reported enemy dead, not control of territory or population. A pacification index measures official assessments, not necessarily popular allegiance. A tonnage figure measures bombs dropped, not damage done. Each measure can establish something different, and none can establish everything.[^ch41-check-numbers][^ch41-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the United States chose war within real constraints — a Cold War consensus that made abandonment politically costly, a client state that could not stand alone, and a bureaucracy that suppressed dissent — and in which Vietnamese actors, not only American ones, made the war what it was.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-nguyen-hanoi][^ch41-mcmaster]

The August 1964 resolution, the July 1965 troop decision, the November 1963 coup, and the post-Tet troop request were decisions people made and could have made otherwise. None was dictated by the outcome. The alternatives actually considered, and the reasons they were rejected, are part of the record.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-clifford-task-force][^ch41-diem-coup-miller]

The synthesis holds structural conditions — the Cold War, decolonization, the credibility doctrine, and the client state’s weakness — together with the contingent choices made within them. It does not choose one register of explanation over the other.[^ch41-check-scope][^ch41-herring]

Between 1953 and 1968 the United States extended containment through covert action and client states, chose to escalate in Vietnam rather than accept a negotiated settlement, and discovered that the same strategy could not produce the outcome it sought. The war’s costs fell on Vietnamese peasants and soldiers, on American working-class draftees, and on the American political system that had chosen it.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-appy][^ch41-tet-milestone]

## Legacies

The years from 1953 to 1968 left a pattern of American power that outlasted the war. The covert interventions, client states, and credibility reasoning of the early Cold War became the template that Vietnam tested to destruction. The war then reshaped how Americans judged later uses of force, a caution later called the “Vietnam syndrome,” and Congress passed the War Powers Resolution of 1973 over President Richard M. Nixon’s veto, seeking to require congressional notification and to limit sustained hostilities.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive][^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-war-powers]

The war also left a human aftermath on several continents. Vietnamese refugees, wounded veterans, and families of the dead carried the war into the following decades. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington became a national site of mourning, and the questions of Agent Orange, post-traumatic stress, and the missing continued long after the shooting stopped. Those developments belong to later chapters and are named here only as consequences.[^ch41-casualties-nara][^ch41-vvm][^ch41-check-ch42]

The years left a memory and a dispute. For its critics, the war became proof that containment had been a trap and that the government had not told the truth; for its defenders, proof that the United States had been denied the victory it could have won. Both verdicts are arguments to be tested against the record, not settled conclusions. The legal authority the resolution supplied was repealed in January 1971, but the fighting continued until the Paris agreement of January 1973 and the fall of Saigon in April 1975.[^ch41-hanyok][^ch41-moyar][^ch41-end-of-war]

## Summary

- On the night of August 4, 1964, the USS *Maddox* and the USS *C. Turner Joy* reported a second attack in the Gulf of Tonkin; the patrol commander doubted the report, McNamara withheld the doubts, and Johnson ordered retaliatory strikes and asked Congress for a resolution.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- Congress passed the Southeast Asia Resolution on August 7, 1964, by 98 to 2 in the Senate and unanimously in the House; Johnson signed it as Public Law 88-408, and it became the legal basis for the war until its repeal in January 1971.[^ch41-tonkin-resolution-nara][^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller]
- A 2005 NSA release, including Robert Hanyok’s study, concluded that the August 4 attack did not occur and that reporting had been skewed toward the administration’s claim.[^ch41-hanyok][^ch41-nsa-tonkin]
- The United States pursued containment through covert action and regional crises in Iran in 1953, Guatemala in 1954, Suez in 1956, Cuba in 1961–62, and the Congo after 1960.[^ch41-iran-nsarchive][^ch41-guatemala-frus][^ch41-suez-milestone][^ch41-jfk-foreign-miller][^ch41-congo-milestone]
- The 1954 Geneva Accords partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel and scheduled reunification elections in 1956; Prime Minister Ngô Đình Diệm, with American support, canceled the elections.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- American aid built a South Vietnamese state and army, but Diem’s repression of Buddhists and his failure on land reform strengthened the insurgency; he was overthrown and killed on November 1–2, 1963, and President Kennedy was assassinated three weeks later.[^ch41-diem-coup-miller][^ch41-tonkin-milestone]
- Johnson approved NSAM 273 and NSAM 288, authorized Operation Rolling Thunder, landed Marines at Da Nang on March 8, 1965, and by November 1965 had more than 175,000 troops in Vietnam; the number reached 536,000 by 1968.[^ch41-lbj-foreign-miller][^ch41-escalation-miller]
- The attrition strategy measured progress by enemy body counts, and the Ho Chi Minh Trail carried men and supplies south despite the bombing; Map 32 is the registered visual for the supply war.[^ch41-check-map32][^ch41-mcnamara-dpm]
- Between 1964 and 1973 the draft inducted about 2.2 million men out of about 27 million eligible; about 80 percent of enlisted men came from poor or working-class families.[^ch41-draft-michigan][^ch41-appy]
- The Tet offensive of late January 1968 failed in its immediate military aims but showed that victory was not near; approval of Johnson’s handling of the war fell from 39 to 26 percent, and Westmoreland requested about 206,000 more troops.[^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-pubopinion-digitalhistory][^ch41-westmoreland-request]
- On March 16, 1968, American soldiers killed several hundred civilians at My Lai; estimates range from 347 to 507, and the first investigations concluded that only 20 had died accidentally.[^ch41-mylai-pbs]
- On March 31, 1968, Johnson limited the bombing of North Vietnam, renewed the offer of talks, and withdrew from the presidential race; the war continued, and the political crisis passed to the election of 1968.[^ch41-lbj-march31-miller][^ch41-check-ch42]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1953 and 1968 the United States practiced containment through covert action, client states, and finally open war, and it discovered in Vietnam that the same instruments could not produce the outcome it sought. The war’s costs fell hardest on the Vietnamese countryside and on American working-class draftees, and its political costs fell on the presidents who chose it. The chapter’s question — why did a strategy of containment produce an escalating war it could not resolve? — is answered by the record of a choice made within real constraints, which no participant knew how to reverse.[^ch41-logevall][^ch41-mylai-pbs][^ch41-tet-milestone][^ch41-draft-michigan]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Chicago in August 1968, at the Democratic National Convention, where the party that had carried the war met the movement that opposed it in the streets. Chapter 42 takes up the election of 1968 and the political realignment, Vietnamization, the secret bombing of Cambodia, the Pentagon Papers, and the long unmooring of public trust. This chapter ends with the war still being fought and the president who escalated it out of the race; the next turns to the politics that the war remade.[^ch41-check-ch42][^ch41-check-bridge][^ch41-lbj-march31-miller]
