# Chapter 27. Populism, the Crisis of the 1890s, and Empire (1890–1902)

> “From the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes—tramps and millionaires.”[^ch27-omaha-platform]
>
> — The People’s Party, Omaha Platform, adopted at Omaha, Nebraska, July 4, 1892.[^ch27-omaha-platform]

> “We cannot fly from our world duties; it is ours to execute the purpose of a fate that has driven us to be greater than our small intentions.”[^ch27-beveridge]
>
> — Albert J. Beveridge, “The March of the Flag,” campaign speech, Indianapolis, September 16, 1898.[^ch27-beveridge]

> “It was in their more general picture of the world that they were most credulous and vulnerable.”[^ch27-hofstadter]
>
> — Richard Hofstadter, *The Age of Reform: From Bryan to F.D.R.*, 1955.[^ch27-hofstadter]

A party platform said that governmental injustice bred two classes at the extremes. An imperial orator said the nation could not refuse its world duties. A historian said the farmers’ movement was never more credulous than in its general picture of the world. The three disagree about whether the decade’s movements were democratic promise, providential duty, or dangerous credulity. This chapter asks how economic democracy and overseas expansion were connected, and where they diverged.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-beveridge][^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-check-epigraphs]

On July 9, 1896, the Democratic National Convention was meeting in the Chicago Coliseum, and its debate had come down to the platform’s money plank. William Jennings Bryan, a former congressman from Nebraska, rose to speak for silver. He told the convention that “the man who is employed for wages is as much a business man as his employer,” and that “the great cities rest upon our broad and fertile prairies.” Burn down your cities and leave our farms, he said, and your cities would spring up again; “but destroy our farms and the grass will grow in the streets of every city in the country.” He closed with the sentence that gave the speech its name: “You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.”[^ch27-cross-of-gold]

The platform the convention adopted demanded “the free and unlimited coinage of both silver and gold at the present legal ratio of 16 to 1,” condemned the act of 1873 that had demonetized silver, and called gold monometallism “a British policy.”[^ch27-dem-platform-1896] The next day, on the fifth ballot, the convention nominated Bryan for president.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign]

The record establishes a date, a place, a text, a platform, and a ballot. It does not establish the weather in Chicago, the composition of the galleries, or what Bryan thought as he spoke. In July 1896 the election was three months away, and nothing in the record shows anyone in the hall expecting that the fusion with the Populists would destroy the third party or that the republic would be at war with Spain within two years.[^ch27-check-scene]

How were economic democracy and overseas expansion connected, and where did they diverge?

This chapter covers the years from the farmers’ alliances of the late 1880s to the declared end of the Philippine-American War in 1902. Chapter 26 followed the making of the southern racial order; this chapter turns to the agrarian revolt, the depression, the election of 1896, and the turn to empire. It asks how a movement for economic democracy at home and a program of colonial rule abroad belonged to the same decade, and where they parted.[^ch27-check-scope]

It draws on party platforms and convention proceedings, the speeches and pamphlets of the period, the statutes and legal opinions of the silver and territorial questions, the treaties and the reports of the war departments, and the newspapers, memoirs, and organizational records that document the debate over empire. It treats “Populist,” “free silver,” “imperialism,” “the white man’s burden,” “benevolent assimilation,” and “insurrection” as contested period terms. “Populist” named a member or ally of the People’s Party and the alliance movement; “free silver” meant the coinage of silver at 16 to 1 without waiting for other nations; “imperialism” was a word of attack and of defense; “the white man’s burden” was Kipling’s phrase for the duty of imperial rule; “benevolent assimilation” was McKinley’s phrase for rule; “insurrection” was the American name for a war Filipinos fought for independence.[^ch27-check-names]

The archive over-represents the generals, officials, and editors and under-represents the Filipinos, Native Hawaiians, and Black farmers whose lives the decade changed. The imbalance is named here rather than filled with invented voices, and it bears on what the chapter can claim.[^ch27-check-race]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the farmers’ movements and the Omaha Platform. §2 follows the depression, Coxey’s Army, and Pullman. §3 examines silver and the election of 1896. §4 turns to the Spanish-American War and Hawaii. §5 asks how the Philippine-American War, anti-imperialism, and the Open Door remade the republic. §6 asks how historians have explained Populism and the turn to empire. The chapter begins in Chicago on July 9, 1896, when a former congressman told a party convention that mankind must not be crucified upon a cross of gold.[^ch27-check-scope]

## 1. Farmers’ movements and the Omaha Platform

The farmers’ movements began as an attempt to solve the problems of price and credit through cooperation. The political party came later, and it came because the cooperation failed. That order matters: the alliances were not a party that discovered economics, but a business movement that discovered politics.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-subtreasury]

### 1.1 The alliances and the cooperative movement

Southern farmers organized after the Civil War as the price of cotton fell. A gathering in Lampasas County, Texas, in September 1877 began what became the National Farmers’ Alliance and Industrial Union, commonly called the Southern Farmers’ Alliance. The organization eventually claimed almost three million members, which would have made it one of the largest protest organizations in American history. The figure is the organization’s own claim.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-check-numbers]

The Alliance tried to change the terms of trade directly. It negotiated with merchants, opened alliance-owned cooperative stores, mills, gins, and cotton exchanges, and finally organized a statewide exchange that bulked and sold cotton directly to manufacturers. The exchange made progress for a time, but the hostility of wholesalers, the cost of dividends, and the foreclosure of the Texas exchange in 1889 weakened it. The lesson the members drew was that isolated cooperatives could not survive in a market organized against them.[^ch27-farmers-alliance]

The subtreasury plan was the Alliance’s answer. Adopted in 1889 and associated with Charles W. Macune, it proposed federal warehouses, or subtreasuries, in counties that marketed at least $500,000 of crops a year. Farmers would deposit their crops and receive federal notes for up to 80 percent of their value, with a year to sell and interest at 1 percent a year. The plan would have moved rural credit from merchants and landlords to the national government, and that is why it was radical.[^ch27-subtreasury][^ch27-check-numbers]

Because the Southern Alliance barred Black members, Black farmers organized the Colored Farmers’ National Alliance and Cooperative Union in Houston County, Texas, on December 11, 1886. A White general superintendent, Richard Manning Humphrey, worked with a Black president and secretary. After a rival group merged into it in 1890, the Colored Alliance claimed 1,200,000 members in 1891, a figure that was probably inflated. Its members were largely landless cotton pickers, and in September 1891 the organization called for a cotton-pickers’ strike at a dollar per hundred pounds. It lacked the local leaders and the means of communication to hold a strike together. In one Arkansas community where pickers did strike, Whites crushed the action and killed 15 strikers.[^ch27-colored-farmers][^ch27-check-numbers]

### 1.2 The People’s Party and the Omaha Platform

When cooperative self-help and the major parties failed them, the alliances moved into independent politics. A third party became a reality in Texas in May 1891, and the national People’s Party was founded at Omaha in 1892; its platform was closely tied to the Alliance’s own program. In 1892 its presidential candidate, James B. Weaver, won 22 electoral votes and 1,029,357 popular votes, 8.5 percent of the total, carrying several western states.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-election-1892]

The Omaha Platform, adopted on July 4, 1892, is the movement’s best single statement of what it wanted. Its preamble charged that “corruption dominates the ballot-box, the Legislatures, the Congress, and touches even the ermine of the bench,” that “the newspapers are largely subsidized or muzzled,” and that “from the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes—tramps and millionaires.” The platform proper demanded a national currency issued by the government through the subtreasury plan; “free and unlimited coinage of silver and gold at the present legal ratio of 16 to 1”; circulating medium of not less than $50 per capita; a graduated income tax; postal savings banks; government ownership of the railroads, telegraph, and telephone; and the reclamation of land held by railroads and aliens.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-omaha-app]

The platform’s resolutions, offered as expressions of sentiment rather than as planks, demanded the secret ballot, the initiative and referendum, the direct election of senators, a one-term presidency, the eight-hour day on government work, the abolition of the Pinkerton system, and further restriction of immigration. The list matters because the retrospective image of Populism as a currency crusade distorts the movement. The platform proposed a reconstruction of the political economy and a widening of democratic procedure at the same time.[^ch27-omaha-platform]

The limit was racial. The Omaha Platform said nothing that would dismantle the southern racial order. The Populists’ southern wing depended on Black votes, and the party as a whole would not defend Black political rights; the Southern Alliance would not make Black suffrage its cause. That contradiction was the movement’s central weakness, and it belongs here even though the making of the new order belongs to Chapter 26.[^ch27-colored-farmers][^ch27-check-ch26]

### 1.3 Orators, factions, and the road to fusion

The movement produced a culture of mass meetings, newspapers, and orators. Mary Elizabeth Lease, a Kansas Populist lecturer, became its most famous woman speaker. The saying “raise less corn and more hell,” widely attributed to her, probably was not hers; the Kansas Historical Society records that she admitted it characterized her fight but did not originate it. The line is best treated as an attribution problem rather than as an undisputed quotation.[^ch27-lease][^ch27-check-lease]

Thomas E. Watson of Georgia was among the movement’s most committed southern voices. In 1896 he warned that “fusion means the Populist party will play Jonah, and they will play the whale.” His later turn to White supremacy belongs to Chapter 26’s account of the new order; here he stands for the southern wing’s dilemma, caught between a party that needed Black votes and a region that would not permit them.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-check-ch26]

By 1896 the movement had split between the “fusion” Populists, who wanted to join the Democrats, and the “mid-roaders,” who wanted to keep an independent party. The split was a strategic argument within a movement under pressure, not a contest of personalities. The movement’s decision to stake everything on free silver would set up the election of 1896, and the depression made the stake seem total.[^ch27-vassar-populists]

## 2. Depression, Coxey, and Pullman

The Panic of 1893 turned the farmers’ grievance into a general social emergency. It discredited the governing party, drained the currency from the interior, and made the money question the central issue of the decade. The political responses — a march of the unemployed, a strike broken by injunction, and a silver campaign — took their shape from its scale.[^ch27-panic-1893]

### 2.1 The Panic of 1893

Two instabilities drove the panic. The Treasury’s gold reserves fell from about $190 million in 1890 to about $100 million, raising fears that the United States might suspend the convertibility of its notes. At the same time, economic activity had slowed and loan defaults had weakened bank balance sheets, so depositors began to withdraw funds, and withdrawals accelerated into runs.[^ch27-panic-1893]

In June 1893 bank runs swept through midwestern and western cities such as Chicago and Los Angeles, and more than 100 banks suspended operations; from mid-July to mid-August another 340 suspended. As country banks drew down their deposits in New York, the money-center banks sold assets at falling prices, and the interior was left without currency. The crisis moved from the money center to the countryside through the same correspondent network that normally supplied credit.[^ch27-panic-1893]

Industrial production fell by 15.3 percent between 1892 and 1894, and unemployment rose to between 17 and 19 percent. The economy slumped again in late 1895 and did not fully recover until mid-1897. The figures are estimates, and their definitions belong with them, but the direction is not in doubt.[^ch27-panic-1893][^ch27-check-numbers]

### 2.2 Coxey’s Army and the politics of unemployment

The depression produced the first mass protest march on Washington. Jacob Coxey, a wealthy Ohio sand quarry operator whose own fortunes had suffered in the panic, believed the federal government should finance public works to create jobs. In the spring of 1894 he led several hundred unemployed followers from Massillon, Ohio, to Washington, and the march drew extensive press coverage.[^ch27-coxey]

The reception was mockery. *Harper’s Weekly* called the mission a quixotic “farce comedy,” and the marchers’ ragged appearance was derided. Coxey’s journey ended when he was arrested for trespassing on the Capitol lawn on May 1, 1894, and the press declared that Washington “looked upon the movement as a joke.” Congress eventually enacted legislation in the spirit of Coxey’s proposals, but only in later depressions.[^ch27-coxey]

The episode shows that the era’s political economy offered no recognized place for the unemployed as a class with claims on the federal government. The march was a petition for a policy that did not yet exist.[^ch27-coxey][^ch27-panic-1893]

### 2.3 Pullman and the injunction

The Pullman strike turned a wage dispute into a national test of whether the federal courts could break a strike by injunction. On May 11, 1894, thousands of workers at the Pullman Palace Car Company struck for wage increases and rent reductions in the company town. The American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs, launched a boycott in support, with members refusing to run trains carrying Pullman cars. The action began peacefully, then became violent, and it disrupted mail, food delivery, and passenger transportation.[^ch27-pullman]

The federal government sought and obtained an injunction against the union, and President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago on July 3, 1894, over the protest of Gov. John Peter Altgeld of Illinois, who had not requested them and had not been informed. Debs and other leaders were convicted of contempt and jailed.[^ch27-pullman]

In *In re Debs* (1895), the Supreme Court denied Debs’s petition for a writ of habeas corpus and upheld the government’s power to act against obstructions of interstate commerce and the mails. “The strong arm of the national government,” Justice David J. Brewer wrote, “may be put forth to brush away all obstructions to the freedom of interstate commerce or the transportation of the mails.” The Court also held that a defendant in a contempt proceeding is not entitled to a jury trial. The decision gave the labor injunction its constitutional warrant.[^ch27-in-re-debs]

The strike joined two grievances: wages the company had cut and rents it had not lowered. The labor history belongs to Chapter 24; what belongs here is the state’s answer.[^ch27-pullman][^ch27-check-ch24][^ch27-check-numbers]

Pullman shows the state acting on behalf of the railroads. Within four years the same federal power would be turned outward, in the Caribbean and the Pacific, and §4 takes up that turn.[^ch27-in-re-debs]

## 3. Silver and the 1896 election

The money question was a fight over the definition of money itself. The sequence of silver legislation — the act of 1873, the Bland–Allison Act, the Sherman Silver Purchase Act, and its repeal — is the record of that fight. Each statute chose who would bear the risk of falling prices.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896][^ch27-sherman-repeal]

### 3.1 The silver question

The Democratic platform of 1896 declared that “the act of 1873 demonetizing silver without the knowledge or approval of the American people” had appreciated gold, depressed prices, and enriched “the money-lending class at home and abroad.” Advocates called it the crime of ’73. The act was a technical revision of the coinage laws. What the law did and what its opponents believed it intended are two different questions.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896]

Congress required limited silver purchases under the Bland–Allison Act of 1878 and enlarged them under the Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890, but neither restored free coinage and neither stopped deflation. When the crisis of 1893 struck, Cleveland called a special session and won repeal of the Sherman Act’s silver-purchase provisions, overcoming an 88-day Senate filibuster.[^ch27-sherman-repeal][^ch27-panic-1893]

The platform’s money plank demanded “the free and unlimited coinage of both silver and gold at the present legal ratio of 16 to 1 without waiting for the aid or consent of any other nation.” It declared gold monometallism “a British policy,” called for an income tax, denounced government by injunction, and demanded protection against “foreign pauper labor.” The platform was a program, not a single-issue document, and its anti-injunction plank carried the memory of Pullman.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896]

### 3.2 Bryan and the cross of gold

Bryan’s speech and nomination converted the silver Democrats’ cause into a national campaign and forced the Populists to choose between independence and fusion.[^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-vassar-populists]

Speaking on July 9, 1896, Bryan defended the producing classes against the holders of capital. “The man who is employed for wages is as much a business man as his employer,” he said. “The great cities rest upon our broad and fertile prairies.” If the farms were destroyed, “the grass will grow in the streets of every city in the country.” He closed: “You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.”[^ch27-cross-of-gold]

Bryan won the nomination on the fifth ballot. His campaign staked the party on free silver and, in doing so, took the Populists’ central issue and left the third party with little distinctive ground.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists]

The quotations follow the text printed in Bryan’s *The First Battle* (1896); the transcript has been checked against the scan but not against the convention’s official proceedings.[^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-check-scene]

### 3.3 Fusion, the campaign, and the result

The election of 1896 was a contest between two conceptions of the economy and two campaign styles. Its result realigned national politics for a generation and destroyed the Populist party.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists]

The Populists met at St. Louis on July 24–26, 1896, and endorsed Bryan for president despite the warnings of the mid-roaders. The convention refused to accept Arthur Sewall, the Democrats’ vice-presidential choice, and nominated Tom Watson instead. When Bryan did not renounce Sewall, Watson refused to campaign. Bryan “virtually ignored the People’s Party, even though he was its nominee,” and the fusion “killed the party’s hopes for national power.”[^ch27-vassar-populists]

The Republicans nominated William McKinley at St. Louis on a platform endorsing protective tariffs and the gold standard, with a plank that left the door open to international bimetallism. McKinley’s manager, Marcus Hanna, raised a reported $4 million for a campaign that printed about 200 million pamphlets and employed about 1,400 speakers, while McKinley delivered some 350 speeches from his front porch in Canton, Ohio, to about 750,000 visiting delegates. The campaign’s organization belonged to the era’s political economy: it was a national industry for producing opinion.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign]

McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan’s 176, and he took 7,105,076 popular votes (51.1 percent) to Bryan’s 6,370,897 (45.8 percent), out of a total of 13,905,691. Other compilations give slightly different popular totals because they count fusion votes differently; the figures here follow the American Presidency Project’s series. McKinley’s victory launched a period of Republican national power that lasted until 1932, broken only by Woodrow Wilson’s victory in 1912.[^ch27-election-1896][^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-check-numbers]

The result ended the Populists’ national hopes and confirmed the Republicans as the majority party. It also confirmed the Solid South: because disfranchisement had already removed most Black voters, the southern electoral college was safe for the Democrats, and the fusion politics that had threatened the new order was defeated in the South along with Bryan nationally.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-check-ch26]

### 3.4 The end of the third party and the survival of its ideas

The Populist party vanished from the national map after 1896, but its program survived. Watson’s prediction was borne out: fusion on free silver “de-railed the rest of Populists’ agenda and killed the party’s hopes for national power,” and while Populists continued to hold power in a few western states, the party left the national stage.[^ch27-vassar-populists]

The survival was partial and delayed. Direct election of senators became law by constitutional amendment in 1913, and the graduated income tax came with the Sixteenth Amendment the same year. The demand for federal aid to farmers and for public works in depression returned in the New Deal. The legacy is a matter of dates, not vindication.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-seventeenth-amendment][^ch27-sixteenth-amendment][^ch27-check-ch29]

Free silver never became law. The issue that had convulsed the decade was settled by defeat at the polls, not by the coinage of silver at 16 to 1. The same election year that destroyed the Populists also brought the coming of empire closer, and §4 turns from the currency to the war.[^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]

## 4. The Spanish-American War and Hawaii

The war with Spain grew out of Cuba’s rebellion, the sinking of the USS *Maine*, and a press war at home. The decision for war was McKinley’s, and he made it after resisting it.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-de-lome][^ch27-check-decision]

### 4.1 Cuba, the Maine, and the decision for war

Cuban revolutionaries had been fighting Spain since 1895, and the conflict produced economic and political instability close to the United States. American sympathy for the rebels grew from outrage at Spanish tactics and from a long-standing interest in removing European colonial power from the hemisphere.[^ch27-spanish-war-state]

A private letter from the Spanish ambassador, Enrique Dupuy de Lôme, called McKinley “weak and a bidder for the admiration of the crowd.” Cuban revolutionaries intercepted it and released it to the Hearst press, which published it on February 9, 1898, in the *New York Journal*. The letter helped fuel readiness for war.[^ch27-de-lome]

On February 15, 1898, the battleship USS *Maine* exploded and sank in Havana harbor, killing 266 of her 354 crew members. The cause was never settled. A court of inquiry blamed a submarine mine; later investigations suggested an internal explosion. The dead numbered 266 of the 354 aboard, though *Harper’s Weekly* reported 246 killed and 57 wounded.[^ch27-maine][^ch27-check-numbers]

McKinley asked Congress on April 11, 1898, for authority to end the fighting and establish “a stable government.” Congress passed a joint resolution on April 20 that acknowledged Cuban independence and disclaimed any intention to annex Cuba, and Congress declared war on April 25. The Teller Amendment, proposed by Senator Henry M. Teller of Colorado, stated that the United States “hereby disclaims any disposition or intention to exercise sovereignty, jurisdiction, or control over said Island except for pacification thereof.”[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-teller]

### 4.2 The “splendid little war”

The war’s Caribbean and Pacific campaigns lasted only months, and the ease of the victories concealed how much the settlement would change. Secretary of State John Hay called it “a splendid little war.”[^ch27-spanish-war-state]

On May 1, 1898, Commodore George Dewey’s Asiatic Squadron destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay. Dewey brought the exiled Filipino leader Emilio Aguinaldo back to the islands aboard an American warship, and Aguinaldo’s forces kept up operations until American troops arrived at the end of July.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]

U.S. troops landed at Guantánamo Bay on June 10 and near Santiago later that month. On July 1, 1898, dismounted cavalry including the African American Ninth and Tenth cavalries and the Rough Riders under Lieutenant Colonel Theodore Roosevelt attacked the San Juan heights. The Spanish Caribbean squadron was destroyed on July 3, and Santiago surrendered in the middle of the month. The Black regiments belong in the account because the war’s racial order and the army’s segregation are part of its history.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-check-race]

A cease-fire was signed on August 12, 1898. The United States also invaded Puerto Rico in late July with little resistance and installed a military government in October. The war cost the United States about $250 million and about 3,000 lives, of whom roughly 90 percent died of infectious disease rather than combat. The disease share explains the war’s human cost better than the battlefield accounts alone.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-check-numbers]

### 4.3 The Treaty of Paris and the annexations

The Treaty of Paris gave the United States an overseas empire, and the Senate ratified it by the narrowest possible margin. Signed on December 10, 1898, the treaty required Spain to relinquish claims on Cuba and to cede Guam and Puerto Rico to the United States, and it provided that Spain would sell the Philippines to the United States for $20 million. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, by a vote of 57 to 27 — just over the two-thirds required.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-treaty-ratification]

The Senate debate turned on empire. Senator George Frisbie Hoar of Massachusetts warned that the treaty would “make us a vulgar, commonplace empire, controlling subject races and vassal states,” while Senator Knute Nelson of Minnesota held that Americans would go to the islands as “ministering angels, not despots,” and Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts argued that rejection would brand the nation as incapable of taking rank as one of the great world powers. These were arguments about what the republic was for.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-check-race]

Map 21 shows what the treaty and the war had made. It distinguishes annexation — Hawaii by joint resolution, and Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines under the treaty — from occupation, which was Cuba’s status under the Teller Amendment and later the Platt Amendment, and from the bases and later instruments of control that belong to Chapter 30. The map marks boundaries and claims that were contested rather than settled, and its date range stops at 1902.[^ch27-check-figure][^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-platt][^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-check-ch30]

With the treaty ratified, the republic held the Philippines, and the war that had been fought to free Cuba had produced a colonial state in Asia. §5 takes up that state and the war it required.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-philippine-war-state]

### 4.4 Hawaii and the annexation by joint resolution

Hawaii was annexed during the war by a joint resolution of Congress rather than by treaty, after a mass petition by Native Hawaiians failed to stop a government established by a coup.[^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-hawaii-petition]

In 1893, a group of non-native businessmen and politicians — the “Committee of Safety,” led by Sanford Dole and supported by the U.S. minister John Stevens and Marines from the USS *Boston* — overthrew Queen Lili‘uokalani in a bloodless coup and proclaimed a provisional government. A special investigator found that the queen had been overthrown illegally and ordered the American flag lowered, but she never regained power. The provisional government proclaimed the Republic of Hawaii in 1894 with Dole as president.[^ch27-hawaii-archives]

In June 1897 McKinley signed a treaty of annexation with representatives of the Republic of Hawaii and submitted it to the Senate. Native Hawaiian organizations mounted a mass petition drive that gathered 21,269 signatures against annexation — more than half of the roughly 39,000 Native Hawaiians and part-Hawaiians counted that year — and the treaty was defeated. [^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-hawaii-petition][^ch27-check-numbers]

After the *Maine*’s destruction made Hawaii look strategically valuable as a mid-Pacific fueling station and naval base, pro-annexation forces used a joint resolution, which required only a simple majority in both houses rather than a two-thirds treaty vote. House Joint Resolution 259, the Newlands Resolution, passed Congress and was signed by McKinley on July 7, 1898, formally annexing the islands. The transfer ceremony followed on August 12, and Dole became the first governor of the Territory of Hawaii.[^ch27-hawaii-archives][^ch27-spanish-war-state]

In 1993, the hundredth anniversary of the overthrow, Congress acknowledged that the Kingdom of Hawaii had been overthrown illegally, apologized for the overthrow and the deprivation of Native Hawaiians’ right to self-determination, and committed itself to reconciliation. That acknowledgment is a consequence of the history, not the view of 1898.[^ch27-hawaii-apology][^ch27-check-ch30]

## 5. The Philippine-American War, anti-imperialism, and the Open Door

Filipinos who had fought for independence from Spain fought the United States for independence in turn. The archipelago appears on Map 21 as the westernmost of the acquisitions of 1898. The war that followed was longer, deadlier, and more divisive than the war with Spain.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]

### 5.1 The war in the Philippines

Aguinaldo had returned to the islands with American forces and believed the United States would support independence. When the United States made clear that it intended to impose colonial control, the early clashes of 1899 became an all-out war. Filipinos proclaimed an independent republic, and Aguinaldo was its president.[^ch27-loc-1898][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-philippine-war-state]

Aguinaldo protested the American military government in his own words: “General Otis styles himself Military Governor of these Islands, and I protest one and a thousand times and with all the energy of my soul against such authority. … I hereby protest against this unexpected act of the United States claiming sovereignty over these Islands.” “Insurrection” was the American term for a war the Filipinos saw as a defense against invasion.[^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-check-names][^ch27-check-race]

McKinley’s executive order of December 21, 1898, instructed the military commander to tell the islanders that the United States came “not as invaders or conquerors, but as friends,” and that “the mission of the United States is one of benevolent assimilation, substituting the mild sway of justice and right for arbitrary rule.” The war began on February 4, 1899, two days before the Senate ratified the treaty.[^ch27-benevolent][^ch27-philippine-war-state]

The first phase of the war, from February to November 1899, was dominated by Aguinaldo’s conventional attempts against better-equipped American troops; the second was guerrilla war, beginning in November 1899 and lasting through Aguinaldo’s capture in 1901 and into 1902. President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed a general amnesty and declared the conflict over on July 4, 1902, although resistance continued in some areas for years, and Muslim Moro communities in the south resisted into the 1910s.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga][^ch27-check-dates]

The war was brutal on both sides. U.S. forces burned villages, implemented reconcentration, and tortured suspected guerrillas; Filipino fighters also tortured captured soldiers and terrorized civilians who cooperated with the Americans. Many civilians died from fighting, cholera and malaria, and famine. On September 28, 1901, Filipino fighters armed largely with bolos attacked an American company at Balangiga on Samar, killing 48 of the 74 men; the American response was a devastating campaign that crushed resistance on the island by mid-1902. The brutality ran both ways, and civilians paid most of its cost; “pacification” cannot stand as a description of the campaign.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga][^ch27-samar-michigan][^ch27-check-race]

The war killed over 4,200 American service members and over 20,000 Filipino combatants, and as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died from violence, famine, and disease. The figures are estimates with wide ranges, and the difference between combatant and civilian deaths matters.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-check-numbers]

Even as the fighting continued, the United States sent a commission headed by William Howard Taft in 1900 and pursued a “policy of attraction” that offered a degree of self-government and social reform while undermining the revolutionaries. Congress passed the Philippine Organic Act in 1902, providing for a civil government with an elected lower house; the first elected assembly convened in 1907.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-philippine-organic-act]

### 5.2 In Focus: “The White Man’s Burden”

**In Focus.** Rudyard Kipling’s “The White Man’s Burden” appeared in *McClure’s Magazine* in February 1899, in the weeks when the Senate ratified the treaty and the war in the Philippines began. It urges the United States to assume colonial rule: “Take up the White Man’s burden — / Send forth the best ye breed — / Go, bind your sons to exile / To serve your captives’ need.” Its first stanza describes the people it consigns to rule as “new-caught sullen peoples, / Half devil and half child,” and its later stanzas warn that empire brings “the blame of those ye better, / The hate of those ye guard.” The poem is an argument for empire that is also a warning about its costs. American imperialists took from it a justifying phrase, while anti-imperialists — and Filipinos — answered it. Senator Benjamin Tillman of South Carolina read three stanzas into the Senate on February 7, 1899, the day after ratification, to argue against the war, and his opposition rested on his own doctrine of racial difference. The same text served both sides. The poem must not be read as evidence about Filipinos; it is evidence about how empire was justified.[^ch27-kipling][^ch27-tillman-senate][^ch27-check-focus][^ch27-check-race]

Henry Labouchère’s “The Brown Man’s Burden,” published in February 1899, parodied Kipling’s form and diction to indict conquest: “Pile on the brown man’s burden / To gratify your greed,” and “in the name of freedom, / Don’t hesitate to shoot.” The parody reproduces degrading language deliberately, and the words remain degrading even in satire; the tension between its purpose and its diction is part of the text.[^ch27-brown-burden][^ch27-check-race]

### 5.3 Anti-imperialism at home

The annexation of the Philippines produced an organized anti-imperialist movement that joined old free-trade Democrats, reformers, and public figures, and it argued in the language of the Declaration of Independence.[^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-twain-loc]

The American Anti-Imperialist League was founded in 1899, after the United States occupied Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, by citizens including Andrew Carnegie and William James. Its platform held that “the policy known as imperialism is hostile to liberty and tends toward militarism,” that “the subjugation of any people is ‘criminal aggression,’” and that governments “derive their just powers from the consent of the governed.” It demanded the immediate cessation of the war and independence for the Filipinos.[^ch27-anti-imperialist]

Mark Twain joined the League and became one of its most quoted voices. He recalled that he had set out “a red-hot imperialist,” but after reading the Treaty of Paris had concluded that “we do not intend to free, but to subjugate the people of the Philippines,” and declared, “I am an anti-imperialist.” His essay “To the Person Sitting in Darkness” (1901) treated the mission of civilization as a fraud practiced on the world. Twain belongs here as a public voice, not as a neutral witness.[^ch27-twain-loc][^ch27-anti-imperialist]

The anti-imperialists did not speak with one mind. Some opposed empire because they thought it wrong to rule other peoples without consent; others feared that annexation would bring non-White subjects into the American political nation. Both motives belong in the account, and the League’s noble language should not hide the racism of part of its coalition.[^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-check-race]

Bryan opposed empire but urged ratification of the treaty so that the United States could then grant the Philippines independence. He made anti-imperialism a central issue in the 1900 rematch, with Roosevelt on the Republican ticket, and lost again.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]

### 5.4 The Open Door and the construction of colonial rule

While the Philippines were being conquered, the United States announced a China policy of equal commercial access and built a system of colonial government in the Caribbean and the Pacific whose legal categories were invented as it went.[^ch27-open-door][^ch27-platt][^ch27-downes]

Secretary of State John Hay sent the first Open Door Note on September 6, 1899, asking the powers active in China to keep the market open to all nations and to respect China’s territorial integrity; a second note on July 3, 1900, during the Boxer Rebellion, repeated the request. The notes were not binding and committed no one, but they became the official statement of American policy in East Asia. Hay announced an open door for trade in China while the United States was closing the door on Chinese immigration.[^ch27-open-door][^ch27-check-race]

The Foraker Act of 1900 ended military rule in Puerto Rico and established a civil government with an appointed governor and a limited elected house, designating the island an unorganized territory whose residents were not U.S. citizens. When the Supreme Court upheld the tariff the act imposed, Justice Henry Brown wrote in *Downes v. Bidwell* (1901) that Puerto Rico was “a territory appurtenant and belonging to the United States, but not a part of the United States within the revenue clauses of the Constitution.” The Insular Cases thus created the category of the unincorporated territory, in which the Constitution applied only in part.[^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-downes]

In 1901 Congress attached to the Army Appropriations Bill the Platt Amendment, which required Cuba to accept limitations on its sovereignty as a condition of the withdrawal of American troops. Article III gave the United States “the right to intervene for the preservation of Cuban independence,” and Article VII required Cuba to sell or lease lands for coaling or naval stations, the principal one at Guantánamo Bay. Cuba reluctantly wrote the amendment into its constitution, and it governed United States–Cuba relations until its repeal in 1934. The amendment qualified the Teller Amendment; the later instruments of Caribbean control belong to Chapter 30.[^ch27-platt][^ch27-teller][^ch27-check-ch30]

By 1902 the republic held an empire, governed it through invented legal categories, and had declared its war in the Philippines over. The later instruments of control belong to Chapter 30, and the culture of the new century belongs to Chapter 28.[^ch27-check-ch30][^ch27-check-ch28]

## 6. The Debate

The argument over how the decade should be explained began while it was being made. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open. The debate is not a contest between patriots and traitors; it is a disagreement about which mechanisms mattered most and how they fit together.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-check-table]

### 6.1 What is settled

The decade’s movements were connected. The farmers’ revolt, the depression, the silver campaign, the election of 1896, and the turn to empire followed one another, and the political economy tied them together: falling prices, credit scarcity, and the search for markets and outlets ran through the Populist program and the argument for expansion.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-panic-1893][^ch27-empire-debate]

The Populists were not merely a currency crusade. The Omaha Platform proposed a national currency, government railroads, a graduated income tax, the secret ballot, and a broad reform program, and the subtreasury plan sought to restructure rural credit. Any account of the movement must engage the program, not only the silver plank.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-subtreasury]

The war and the empire were contested as they were made. The Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris by a single vote over two-thirds, and the Anti-Imperialist League, Mark Twain, and Filipino leaders argued against annexation in the language of the Declaration. The empire was not a consensus.[^ch27-treaty-ratification][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]

The cost fell on the colonized. The Philippine-American War, however named, killed thousands of American and Filipino combatants and as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians, and U.S. forces used torture, reconcentration, and village burning. That cost belongs in any account of the decade.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga][^ch27-samar-michigan]

The decade realigned politics. McKinley’s victory began a period of Republican ascendancy, the Populist party dissolved, and the territorial acquisitions set the terms of American foreign policy for a generation.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-open-door]

### 6.2 What is open

Were the Populists democratic radicals or backward-looking losers? Hofstadter made them backward-looking losers in the process of modernization, driven by an “agrarian myth” and prone to conspiracy thinking; Goodwyn, Kazin, and Postel recast them as forward-looking radicals whose cooperative movement created a culture of democratic promise, and Postel argued they were commercially and intellectually engaged with the wider world. The question is interpretive and shapes how the whole decade is read.[^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-hofstadter-wiki]

Why did the United States take the Philippines? Economic accounts hold that the search for markets and outlets drove expansion; strategic accounts stress naval power, coaling stations, and the “large policy” of Lodge, Roosevelt, and Mahan; ideological accounts stress race, civilization, and manhood; and contingency accounts stress McKinley’s incremental decision and the absence of a plan. Each account should be stated in its strongest form and tested against the timetable of 1898.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-tnsr][^ch27-bederman]

Was empire a fulfillment or a departure? Samuel Flagg Bemis treated 1898 as an “aberration” in an otherwise anti-imperial history; William Appleman Williams and Walter LaFeber argued that the United States had been expanding economically long before 1898 and that empire was continuous with its history. The choice is partly a definitional one about what counts as empire.[^ch27-empire-debate]

What connected Populism to empire? Some accounts link the agrarian crisis to the search for foreign markets, and Hofstadter linked the 1890s’ domestic tensions to a “psychic crisis” that made war attractive. The evidence for each mechanism is partial; the connection is contested, not mechanical.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-tnsr][^ch27-hofstadter]

When did the Philippine-American War end? The conventional date is July 4, 1902, when Roosevelt declared the conflict over, but organized resistance continued, and Moro communities in the south resisted into the 1910s. The 1902 endpoint is a choice of this chapter, not a claim that resistance ended; anything after it appears here as consequence.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-check-dates][^ch27-check-decision]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the 1890s’ movements were at once democratic and exclusionary, modernizing and backward-looking, and in which the turn to empire was driven by economics, strategy, ideology, and contingency together. No single explanation accounts for it, and the positions overlap rather than form teams.[^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-check-sources]

Hofstadter made Populism a problem of political style and status; Goodwyn made it a problem of democratic movement culture; Postel and Kazin made it a modernizing, commercially engaged politics. Each captures part of the record and each reflects its author’s own political moment.[^ch27-hofstadter][^ch27-hofstadter-wiki]

Economic, strategic, ideological, and contingent accounts of 1898 each explain a different part of the decision, and the strongest accounts combine them rather than choosing one. The turn to empire was not a single decision made on a single day.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-tnsr][^ch27-bederman]

The republic took up both projects — a more democratic political economy at home and colonial rule abroad — in the same decade, and it pursued the second while failing at the first. The synthesis holds the two together without claiming that one was the other’s hidden purpose.[^ch27-empire-debate][^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-check-table]

## Legacies

The decade’s settlements outlasted the people who made them. The election of 1896 confirmed Republican national power until 1932, and it buried the Populist party. The direct election of senators and the income tax became constitutional law in 1913, and New Deal farm and public-works programs took up the demand. Free silver never became law; the currency question was settled against it, not by the coinage of silver at 16 to 1.[^ch27-mckinley-campaign][^ch27-vassar-populists][^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-seventeenth-amendment][^ch27-sixteenth-amendment]

The territorial settlements proved more durable. The Platt Amendment governed Cuba’s relations with the United States until its repeal in 1934, and the base at Guantánamo Bay outlasted the amendment. Puerto Rico’s status as an unincorporated territory, created in the Insular Cases, remained unresolved. Hawaii was annexed, and Congress apologized for the 1893 overthrow only in 1993.[^ch27-platt][^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-downes][^ch27-hawaii-apology]

The chapter’s central problem — how a republic that declared that governments derive their powers from the consent of the governed came to rule other peoples without their consent — did not end in 1902. The debate over the Philippines was the nation’s first sustained argument about the meaning of its own founding principles abroad, and the arguments then made for and against empire, in the language of liberty and of civilization, would recur. That contested memory is part of the history, and the evidence on each side belongs in the account.[^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest][^ch27-check-race]

## Summary

- The farmers’ alliances grew from cooperative buying and selling into a political movement; the People’s Party was founded at Omaha in 1892 and its platform demanded a national currency through the subtreasury plan, free silver at 16 to 1, a graduated income tax, and government ownership of railroads.[^ch27-farmers-alliance][^ch27-subtreasury][^ch27-omaha-platform]
- The Colored Farmers’ National Alliance organized Black farmers separately, claimed 1,200,000 members in 1891, and saw its 1891 cotton-pickers’ strike crushed; the Southern Alliance would not defend Black voting rights.[^ch27-colored-farmers]
- The Panic of 1893 brought the failure of more than 100 banks in June and 340 more by mid-August, a 15.3 percent fall in industrial production, and unemployment of 17 to 19 percent, setting off years of protest and political realignment.[^ch27-panic-1893]
- Jacob Coxey’s 1894 march was the first mass protest march on Washington and ended with his arrest for trespassing on the Capitol lawn; the Pullman strike of 1894 ended with a federal injunction, federal troops in Chicago, and the Supreme Court’s decision in *In re Debs* upholding the labor injunction.[^ch27-coxey][^ch27-pullman][^ch27-in-re-debs]
- The 1896 Democratic platform demanded free silver and called gold monometallism a British policy; Bryan won the nomination on the fifth ballot with the “cross of gold” speech, and the Populists fused with the Democrats and nominated Watson for vice president.[^ch27-dem-platform-1896][^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-vassar-populists]
- McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan’s 176 and 51.1 percent of the popular vote to Bryan’s 45.8 percent; the result began a period of Republican power and destroyed the Populist party.[^ch27-election-1896][^ch27-mckinley-campaign]
- The sinking of the USS *Maine* on February 15, 1898, killing 266 of 354 crew members, helped produce the war with Spain; the United States won the war in months and the Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris on February 6, 1899, by 57 to 27.[^ch27-maine][^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-treaty-ratification]
- Under the Treaty of Paris, Spain gave up Cuba, ceded Guam and Puerto Rico, and sold the Philippines to the United States for $20 million; Hawaii was annexed by the Newlands Resolution on July 7, 1898, after a petition of 21,269 Native Hawaiians failed to stop annexation.[^ch27-spanish-war-state][^ch27-hawaii-archives]
- The Philippine-American War began on February 4, 1899, and killed over 4,200 American service members and over 20,000 Filipino combatants, while as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died; Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902, though resistance continued.[^ch27-philippine-war-state][^ch27-balangiga]
- Kipling’s “The White Man’s Burden” (1899) justified empire as a civilizing duty, and the Anti-Imperialist League, Mark Twain, and Filipino leaders answered it in the language of the Declaration of Independence.[^ch27-kipling][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-twain-loc][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]
- The Platt Amendment gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuba and a naval base at Guantánamo Bay; the Foraker Act and the Insular Cases made Puerto Rico an unincorporated territory; and the Open Door Notes made equal commercial access to China an official policy.[^ch27-platt][^ch27-foraker-puerto-rico][^ch27-downes][^ch27-open-door]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* In the years between the Omaha Platform and the declared end of the Philippine-American War, the republic answered its farmers with a fusion that dissolved their party, and it answered its own declared principles with an empire that its citizens never fully endorsed. It promised economic democracy at home and preached civilization abroad, and it did the second while leaving the first unfinished. The chapter’s question — how economic democracy and overseas expansion were connected, and where they diverged — is answered by the record of a decade in which the producing classes were heard and then absorbed, and in which the republic that had declared that governments rest on the consent of the governed claimed rule over peoples who had not consented.[^ch27-omaha-platform][^ch27-cross-of-gold][^ch27-anti-imperialist][^ch27-aguinaldo-protest]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with a small box camera sold to the public in 1900, an object almost anyone could afford and almost anyone could point at the world. The empire and the mass publics of the 1890s had made an audience that the new century would organize in ways the Populists and the anti-imperialists could not have imagined. Chapter 28 takes up that audience — its press, its goods, its entertainments, and its universities — and asks whether a shared culture dissolved the boundaries of class, race, and region, or only reorganized them.[^ch27-check-ch28][^ch27-check-bridge]
