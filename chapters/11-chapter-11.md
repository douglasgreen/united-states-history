## Epigraphs

> “The disturbances in America give great trouble to all our Nations.”[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]

— Thayendanegea (Joseph Brant), March 14, 1776, as quoted by the Museum of the American Revolution.[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]

> “I do hereby farther declare all indented servants, Negroes, or others (appertaining to rebels) free, that are able and willing to bear arms, they joining his Majesty’s troops, as soon as may be…”[^ch11-gilder-dunmore]

— John Murray, Earl of Dunmore, “A Proclamation,” Williamsburg, November 7, 1775, as printed in the *Pennsylvania Journal*, December 6, 1775.[^ch11-gilder-dunmore]

> “We wish that our conduct, in maintaining inviolate our loyalty to our Sovereign, against the strong tide of oppression and tyranny, which has almost overwhelmed this Land…”[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration]

— “The Loyalist Declaration of Dependence,” subscribed by 547 inhabitants of the City and County of New York, November 1776.[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration]

> “This was quite simply a civil war — and routinely described as such by contemporaries on both sides of the Atlantic.”[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]

— Maya Jasanoff, *Liberty’s Exiles: American Loyalists in the Revolutionary World* (2011), as excerpted by the Museum of the American Revolution.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]

- **Why these four.** A Mohawk leader worried in 1776 about what independence would mean for Native homelands. A royal governor promised freedom to enslaved people who would fight for the king. Loyalists in occupied New York pledged their allegiance to the Crown and called the rebellion a tyranny. A modern historian calls the whole conflict a civil war. The four disagree about whose war this was and what it was for: a war for independence, a war to preserve an empire, a war among Native nations, and a war over slavery all at once.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Brant’s sentence is quoted as the museum reproduces it; locate it in a documentary edition of his papers before setting it in type. Dunmore’s proclamation is quoted from the Gilder Lehrman Institute’s transcription of the copy printed in the *Pennsylvania Journal*; collate it against that newspaper or the printed proclamation. The Loyalist Declaration is quoted from a *Journal of the American Revolution* article that reproduces a New-York Historical Society transcription; collate it against the manuscript. Jasanoff’s sentence is quoted from a museum excerpt of her book; locate it in the printed edition before use.[^ch11-check-epigraphs][^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers][^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that Native nations acted as one, that enslaved people trusted Dunmore’s promise, that all Loyalists signed the Declaration, or that the war was only a civil war. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The trap on Brooklyn Heights.** After the British evacuated Boston in March 1776, Washington moved his army to New York, guessing the next blow would fall there. By mid-April he had marched about 19,000 soldiers into Lower Manhattan and built forts on Brooklyn Heights across the East River. In early July a British fleet of some 400 ships carrying about 32,000 men under General William Howe arrived at Staten Island. Howe’s field columns worked through the unguarded Jamaica Pass on the night of August 26, and on August 27 the British and their Hessian auxiliaries broke the American line, capturing General John Sullivan and, after a stand by some 400 Maryland troops at the Old Stone House, General William Alexander, Lord Stirling.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn]
- **The decision to leave.** Howe halted the fighting on the afternoon of August 27 and began digging in rather than storming the American lines. Washington, who had crossed from Manhattan and watched the battle from a redoubt on Cobble Hill, chose to save the army rather than risk it. On the night of August 29–30, boat crews ferried the army from Brooklyn across the East River to Manhattan.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn]
- **The boats and the boatmen.** The crossing was worked by the seamen of Brigadier General John Glover’s Marblehead regiment, the 14th Continental Regiment, fishermen and sailors from a Massachusetts fishing port; the regiment was one of the army’s first integrated units, with roughly one-third of its ranks recorded as of “dark complexion.” British warships could not close the East River while an opposing wind held them back, and the same wind and darkness covered the crossing.[^ch11-armyhistory-glover][^ch11-usni-glover]
- **The fog.** Washington watched as about 9,000 Continentals were rowed back to Manhattan. According to eyewitnesses, a fog descended as the sun came up and the last men crossed, and Washington was the last man to leave Brooklyn.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn]
- **The cost.** The sources give different numbers. The Mount Vernon account lists American casualties of about 300 killed, 650 wounded, and 1,100 captured on Long Island, against British losses of 63 killed and 314 wounded, and describes Howe’s force that day as some 20,000 against about 10,000 Americans. The American Battlefield Trust counts the engagement at roughly 30,000 in all, with about 10,000 Americans and 20,000 British, and puts the evacuated force at 9,000. The scene must give the range and not settle it.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-check-numbers]
- **The remembered line.** Mount Vernon reproduces a report that Washington said, watching the fight, “Good God, what brave fellows I must lose,” but traces the sentence to a modern biography rather than to a contemporary source. The scene may quote it only with that provenance stated, or omit it.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A salvage, not a triumph.** The scene should begin in the trapped position on Brooklyn Heights and end with the last boat crossing the East River, making the escape a decision taken under necessity rather than a victory. The army was saved; New York was lost, and it stayed in British hands until the end of the war.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn]
- **The war’s texture.** The episode shows what the rest of the chapter will argue: that the rebellion survived less by winning battles than by keeping an army in being, and that the men who did the work were often small farmers, sailors, and free and enslaved people rather than a single commander.[^ch11-armyhistory-glover][^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: How did a weak rebellion survive and win a settlement?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that Washington outfought Howe. Howe’s caution and weather helped, but the deeper explanation lies in logistics, local allegiance, the wars against Native nations, self-emancipation, and eventually French intervention, none of which the scene can show by itself.
- **The widening.** The scene sits on the East River in August 1776. The framing paragraph follows and sends the reader back through the campaigns of the next seven years, from Trenton and Saratoga to the Carolinas and Yorktown.[^ch11-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- No invented dialogue, weather, or interior thoughts beyond what the sources describe. The fog and the boats are documented; the crossing’s fine detail is not.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-check-scene]
- No settled count of the two armies or the casualties; the accounts differ.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-check-numbers]
- No claim that Washington planned the evacuation long in advance or that the campaign was a hidden success. Nor a claim that the British blundered; Howe’s halt is debated.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-check-scene]
- No claim that the British later described the rebellion as having “no navy.” Continental forces had no fleet able to match the Royal Navy on the ocean, but American sailors and privateers, and later the French navy, contested the waters; the chapter must not repeat the absolute phrase.[^ch11-check-names]
- No claim that all of Glover’s men were free, English-speaking volunteers; the regiment’s mixed composition is part of the record.[^ch11-armyhistory-glover]

### Drafting target

Three hundred to four hundred words. Open on Brooklyn Heights with the army pinned against the East River, or on the boats coming across at dark, and establish Brooklyn and late August 1776 in the first sentences. Let Washington’s decision to evacuate be the hinge. Carry the reader across the river and let the fog and the last boat close the scene without commentary. Note that the numbers disagree and that the escape saved the army while losing the port. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. ## Framing paragraph and roadmap

The framing paragraph (150–200 words) should say: this chapter covers the years from the summer of 1776, when Britain carried the war to New York, to the Treaty of Paris in 1783. It draws on the correspondence and journals of the American, British, French, and German participants; the papers of Congress and the state governments; Loyalist petitions and confiscation records; the records of the British evacuation, including the register known as the Book of Negroes; the treaties; and the maps and returns of the campaigns. It treats “independence,” “allegiance,” “loyalty,” “savages,” “emancipation,” and “peace” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter must establish. It uses historical scholarship for what the records cannot show, names the silences left by archives that recorded enslaved and Indigenous people mainly through the complaints of others, and does not present the outcome as foreordained in 1776. Its dates must be kept precise and reconciled with Appendix D (Chronology).[^ch11-check-scope][^ch11-check-names][^ch11-check-dates]

## 1. Resources and armies

### 1.1 The two armies and the German auxiliaries

- **The claim.** Britain began the campaign of 1776 with a professional army, a fleet, and hired German soldiers, while the Americans fielded a small regular army, short-term militia, and a government with almost no power to tax or supply. The asymmetry shaped how the war was fought.
- **The British force.** After evacuating Boston, Britain assembled around New York a fleet and an army of roughly 32,000 men, the largest force it had sent to North America. The Royal Navy controlled the sea approaches to every American port, and the British held New York for the rest of the war.[^ch11-usni-glover][^ch11-abt-brooklyn]
- **The German auxiliaries.** Between 1776 and 1783 Britain hired an estimated 30,000 German soldiers under subsidy treaties with six territories within the Holy Roman Empire, chiefly Hessen-Kassel and Hessen-Hanau. Contemporaries lumped them together as “Hessians,” a label that flattens a diverse body of men who kept their own records and included accompanying civilians and some Black recruits; the chapter should explain the term rather than repeat it as a single category.[^ch11-amrev-hessians][^ch11-ari-hessians]
- **The American force.** Congress raised the Continental Army and called on the states for militia, but it lacked the power to tax, and its authority to supply and pay the army was weak. The British commander Andrew Jackson O’Shaughnessy’s point is useful here: Britain fought “an army of conquest not of occupation,” which let the rebellion lose battles and still keep an army in the field by retreating, regrouping, and recruiting again.[^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy]
- **The consequence.** Neither side could end the war quickly. The American army’s survival, not its battlefield wins, became the measure of the rebellion, which is the chapter’s central problem.[^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy]

### 1.2 Supply, pay, and the crisis of the army

- **The claim.** The Continental Army was chronically short of food, clothing, and pay, and its soldiers repeatedly had to choose between service and survival. Those shortages nearly broke the army more than any single defeat.
- **The supply problem.** The army drew provisions through state requisitions and a weak commissary; depreciation of paper money and the failure to pay soldiers in real value produced hardship and, at times, near-mutiny. Congress’s inability to supply the army is the wartime face of the same weakness that Chapter 12 will examine in the Confederation.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge][^ch11-check-ch12]
- **Valley Forge as evidence.** Washington’s army marched into winter quarters at Valley Forge on December 19, 1777: more than 12,000 soldiers with about 400 women and children, who built some 1,500 to 2,000 log huts against a winter of thaw, rain, and shortage. Army records show an average daily ration of about half a pound of beef per man in January 1778, a food crisis in February when the men went days without meat, and 2,898 men listed unfit for duty for lack of clothing in early March. The chapter should use these records against the popular image of a wholly ragged army.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]
- **Training and discipline.** Baron Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge in February 1778 and drilled the army into a more cohesive force. The park service treats the encampment as the place where the army “emerged under Washington’s leadership as a cohesive and disciplined fighting force,” which is a judgment about the army’s later performance rather than proof that the winter itself won battles.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]
- **The consequence.** By 1780 and 1781 the army’s patience was nearly exhausted. The chapter should present supply and pay as a political problem, pointing forward to the mutinies and the debt that Chapter 12 will treat.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge][^ch11-check-ch12]

### 1.3 Who fought and who followed

- **The claim.** The armies on both sides included people whose service had consequences larger than the war: Black soldiers and sailors, Native allies and scouts, and the women and children who followed and supported the camps.
- **Black soldiers.** Between about 5,000 and 8,000 people of African descent served with the Continental Army, and thousands more in state militias and at sea, while roughly 15,000 to 20,000 supported the British, the German corps, or the Loyalists. Washington at first barred their enlistment and tried to expel those already serving, then allowed free Black men already in service to remain; enslaved men were largely excluded, though some states let owners free substitutes at enlistment, and Rhode Island for a time in 1778 offered freedom to enslaved men who enlisted. The 1st Rhode Island Regiment was for a time made up entirely of soldiers of African and Native American ancestry under white officers.[^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers]
- **Self-emancipation begins the year before independence.** On November 7, 1775, Virginia’s royal governor, the Earl of Dunmore, proclaimed martial law and offered freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants who would bear arms for the king. Within a month about 300 Black men had joined his “Royal Ethiopian Regiment,” which grew to some 800; the proclamation inspired thousands of enslaved people to seek freedom behind British lines, though it applied only to Virginia and offered freedom only to those who could bear arms. Some estimates put the number who escaped to British lines across the war as high as 80,000 to 100,000, out of roughly 450,000 enslaved people in the thirteen colonies at the war’s start; the chapter must present these as estimates.[^ch11-gilder-dunmore]
- **Native allies and scouts.** More than 250,000 Native Americans lived east of the Mississippi in 1775, in more than 80 nations; both sides sought their support, and nations made different choices. The British had the advantage of trade goods, a fleet, and some limits on colonial settlement, while an independent United States seemed to many nations the greater threat.[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]
- **Camp followers.** Women and children followed and supplied the army; at Valley Forge hundreds of women and children lived in the encampment and took up labor the army needed. The chapter should name them as part of the army’s survival rather than as an afterthought.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

## 2. New York and survival

### 2.1 The fall of New York, 1776

- **The claim.** In the second half of 1776 the British took New York and nearly destroyed the Continental Army, driving it across New Jersey into Pennsylvania.
- **The campaign.** After the Brooklyn battle, Howe moved against Manhattan in mid-September. On November 16, 1776, a combined British and Hessian force of about 8,000 stormed Fort Washington, the last American stronghold on Manhattan, against about 3,000 defenders; the fort surrendered with about 2,800 men taken prisoner, ending Washington’s defense of New York.[^ch11-abt-fort-washington][^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn]
- **The pursuit.** Defeats at White Plains in late October and at Forts Washington and Lee in November forced Washington to retreat across New Jersey and into Pennsylvania. His force shrank to a few thousand, in threadbare clothing and many without shoes, and enlistments were set to expire.[^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-abt-fort-washington]
- **The political cost.** The British occupation of New York became a Loyalist stronghold and a base for the rest of the war; the British held the city until 1783. The loss showed that the Americans could not hold a major port against a professional army with sea power.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-gotham-black-loyalists]

### 2.2 Trenton, Princeton, and the fight to keep an army

- **The claim.** The Delaware counterstroke at the end of 1776 did not win the war, but it kept the rebellion’s army and its cause alive.
- **The crossing.** On Christmas night, December 25, 1776, Washington led a force of about 2,400 across the ice-choked Delaware River in a storm and marched some ten miles to surprise a Hessian garrison of about 1,500 at Trenton. A plan for three converging attacks failed when two columns could not cross; only the main body, with Washington, made it.[^ch11-abt-trenton]
- **The result.** The Americans captured the garrison and its stores with few casualties, then fought a delaying action and a second victory at Princeton a week later. The army took food and clothing it badly needed, and morale rose.[^ch11-abt-trenton]
- **The argument.** Washington’s decision to fight after retreating, and to keep asking his men to stay, is the moment the chapter can use to show that survival was a choice renewed under pressure, not a gift of geography or weather.[^ch11-abt-trenton]

### 2.3 The Philadelphia campaign and Valley Forge

- **The claim.** In 1777 the British took Philadelphia, the American capital, but failed to destroy the army, which wintered within a day’s march and emerged trained.
- **The campaign.** Howe moved on Philadelphia by sea and beat Washington in the field; the British occupied the capital in September 1777. Washington’s army fought and then withdrew to winter at Valley Forge, about a day’s march away, rather than risk destruction.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]
- **The winter.** The encampment tested the army’s supply and endurance, as §1.2 describes, and von Steuben’s drills rebuilt its discipline.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge]
- **The consequence.** The British held a capital and a port but could not end the war; the Americans kept an army in being and, at Saratoga in the same year, won the victory that brought France in. The two campaigns of 1777, the one near Philadelphia and the one in the north, belong to a single strategic story.[^ch11-abt-saratoga][^ch11-nps-valley-forge]

## 3. Saratoga and alliance

### 3.1 Burgoyne’s campaign and the northern army

- **The claim.** In 1777 Britain tried a three-part attack to occupy the Hudson River valley and isolate New England, and the northern prong under General John Burgoyne advanced too far to be rescued.
- **The plan and the advance.** British strategy called for three armies to converge near Albany; Burgoyne moved south from Canada with about 7,500 men and the Hudson valley as his route. By August he had taken Fort Ticonderoga and occupied Fort Edward, and after a detachment was beaten at Bennington he pushed on toward Saratoga.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]
- **The American army.** General Horatio Gates commanded the Northern Department with about 8,500 men, supported by Benedict Arnold and Daniel Morgan’s riflemen, behind fortifications on Bemis Heights overlooking the Hudson.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]
- **The fighting.** The armies fought at Freeman’s Farm on September 19; the British held the field at heavy cost. Burgoyne attacked again at Bemis Heights on October 7 and was repulsed, with Arnold prominent in the fighting. With no relief in sight, Burgoyne surrendered to Gates on October 17.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]

### 3.2 The Convention and its meaning

- **The claim.** The surrender of an entire British army was the war’s turning point because of its effect abroad, not because it ended the fighting in America.
- **The surrender.** Under the terms known as the Convention of Saratoga, Burgoyne’s army became prisoners rather than escaping; the American victory lifted patriot morale and made the case that the Americans could beat a British field army.[^ch11-abt-saratoga]
- **The meanings contested.** Gates became the hero of Saratoga and later lost the southern command; Arnold was hailed for his bravery and later defected. The chapter should present the victory as a political event whose consequences outran its military size, and should resist making any one general the author of the outcome.[^ch11-abt-saratoga][^ch11-abt-camden]

### 3.3 The French alliance and the widening war

- **The claim.** Saratoga brought France into the war as an ally, and the American conflict became a global war that Britain could not win on its own terms.
- **The treaties.** On February 6, 1778, Benjamin Franklin, Arthur Lee, and Silas Deane signed a Treaty of Alliance and a Treaty of Amity and Commerce with France. The alliance barred either country from making a separate peace and left the door open for other European powers to join; the commerce treaty recognized the United States as an independent nation.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-founders-alliance]
- **Why France moved.** The French foreign minister, the Comte de Vergennes, had wavered while the Americans lost in New York; word of Burgoyne’s surrender in December 1777, and reports of British peace overtures to Franklin, led him to ally openly and not to wait for Spain. France had begun secret loans earlier.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance]
- **The consequences.** Spain entered the war against Britain on June 21, 1779, and the Netherlands came in as well, while France provided money, troops, and the fleet that made Yorktown possible. The chapter should present the alliance as a shift in the war’s scale, not merely a subsidy.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-abt-yorktown]

## 4. Frontier and civil wars

### 4.1 The Haudenosaunee confederacy divides

- **The claim.** The war split the Six Nations of the Haudenosaunee, turning a confederacy built on unity into a civil war in which nations fought on opposite sides.
- **The division.** The Mohawk, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca generally sided with Britain, while the Oneida and Tuscarora allied with the United States; many individuals and whole communities tried to stay neutral and were pulled in as the war reached their homelands. The Oneida’s missionary and adviser, the Reverend Samuel Kirkland, was among the influences drawing them toward the Americans.[^ch11-nps-oneida][^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]
- **Oriskany.** On August 6, 1777, members of different nations fought one another at the Battle of Oriskany, near Fort Stanwix; the park service calls the battle the event that “upended the Great Tree of Peace,” the confederacy’s founding covenant. Native nations were not a single actor in the war but several, with different goals and different fates.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-nps-oneida]
- **Thayendanegea.** Joseph Brant, or Thayendanegea, a Mohawk leader, chose the British side early and became one of the war’s best-known Native commanders; his 1776 worry that “the disturbances in America give great trouble to all our Nations” states the dilemma the chapter must keep in view.[^ch11-amrev-native-soldiers]

### 4.2 The frontier war and the Sullivan–Clinton campaign

- **The claim.** The war on the New York and Pennsylvania frontier was a war of raids, reprisals, and the deliberate destruction of Native homelands, and its consequences outlasted the fighting.
- **The raids and the reprisals.** Destructive raids by British-allied Native warriors and Loyalists on the frontiers of New York and Pennsylvania in 1778 crippled the Continental Army’s access to food and manpower and depopulated settlements. In 1779 Washington committed a large part of the army to breaking the Six Nations’ ability to fight.[^ch11-nps-sullivan]
- **The campaign.** General John Sullivan and General James Clinton led about 4,469 Continentals, with riflemen, militia, and Oneida allies, into the heart of Haudenosaunee country to destroy the villages and food supplies of the Cayuga and Seneca. Washington’s orders of May 31, 1779, called for “the total destruction and devastation of their settlements and the capture of as many prisoners of every age and sex as possible,” and directed that the country “may not be merely overrun but destroyed.” Sullivan reported burning more than 40 settlements and great stores of crops and food.[^ch11-nps-sullivan][^ch11-amrev-memory-wars]
- **The refugees.** As many as 5,000 people fled westward to the Niagara region; contemporary counts reported thousands of refugees at Fort Niagara by September 1779. The campaign did not end the frontier war and did not produce a Native peace; it cleared Native land for postwar settlement and led in time to the seizure of millions of acres by the United States.[^ch11-amrev-memory-wars][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]
- **The consequence.** The destruction of Haudenosaunee towns and food was a war crime by modern standards and, in the chapter’s argument, an act of conquest disguised as retaliation. The account must let the Oneida and Tuscarora alliance and the British-allied nations appear as distinct decisions, not as one Indigenous side.[^ch11-nps-sullivan][^ch11-amrev-memory-wars]

### 4.3 Loyalists, confiscation, and the war at home

- **The claim.** The Revolution was also a war among Americans, in which Loyalists were organized, punished, dispossessed, and finally driven out.
- **The Loyalist declaration.** In late November 1776, 547 inhabitants of the City and County of New York signed a declaration to the Howe brothers, pledging their loyalty “against the strong tide of oppression and tyranny” and asking for the restoration of peace within the empire. Loyalism was not one ideology: motives ranged from principle to habit to the belief that the colonies were better off in the empire, and the signatures show organization as well as conviction.[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]
- **The internal war.** New York built one of the harshest confiscation regimes: provisional Committees of Sequestration in 1777, then an October 1779 forfeiture act that declared listed Loyalists to have forfeited their property and banished them from the state. Confiscation laws criminalized dissent and raised revenue by redistributing Loyalist property; they made the Revolution, in the words of the New York Public Library’s account, “a civil war” whose coercive measures muddled the later transition to government by consent.[^ch11-nypl-confiscation]
- **The exile.** After defeat, about 60,000 Loyalists left the United States: roughly 8,000 whites and 5,000 free Black people went to Britain, most of the rest to Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Quebec, with about 6,000 to Jamaica and the Bahamas carrying some 15,000 enslaved people, and nearly 1,200 Black Loyalists to Sierra Leone. Loyalists landed across the British Empire, and their departure reshaped Canada and the Caribbean.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]
- **The consequence.** The Loyalist experience shows that the war’s social result was not only expanded freedom but also exclusion and expulsion. The chapter should keep the Loyalist war and the Native war beside the better-known story of the thirteen colonies’ victory.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nypl-confiscation]

## 5. The southern war

### 5.1 The British southern strategy and the fall of Charleston

- **The claim.** Britain shifted the war to the South after 1778, believing that Loyalists there would rally to the Crown; the shift produced the war’s most destructive phase.
- **The strategy.** London expected the southern colonies’ cash crops and its supposed body of Loyalists to sustain a British campaign, and British forces moved south. Freeing the enslaved workers of southern planters, and intimidating or alienating the backcountry population, undercut the Loyalist support the strategy assumed.[^ch11-abt-cowpens]
- **Charleston and Camden.** British forces captured Charleston in May 1780 and then established a post at Camden. In July General Horatio Gates marched into South Carolina; on August 16, 1780, Cornwallis routed him at Camden, where the militia fled, the Maryland and Delaware Continentals under Johann de Kalb fought on, and de Kalb was mortally wounded. The defeat cleared South Carolina of organized American resistance and opened the way into North Carolina.[^ch11-abt-camden]
- **The consequence.** The southern strategy drew Britain deeper into a region where the population was divided, and it produced a civil war among neighbors as much as a war between armies.[^ch11-abt-cowpens][^ch11-abt-camden]

### 5.2 The partisan war in the backcountry

- **The claim.** Between the regular armies, the southern backcountry fought a brutal partisan war in which militia and irregulars on both sides raided, captured, and killed, and in which enslaved people seized their own chances at freedom.
- **The civil war.** The South Carolina backcountry was split between patriots and Loyalists, “neighbor pitted against neighbor,” with both sides organizing militia and mounting raids and reprisals. American historians have called it a “civil war within a civil war,” and the phrase should be used with the sources that warrant it.[^ch11-abt-cowpens]
- **The partisans.** Patriot partisan leaders and their bands harried British posts and supply lines, and Loyalist militia answered in kind; the fighting produced massacres and reprisals on both sides that the chapter should not soften or sensationalize.[^ch11-abt-camden][^ch11-abt-cowpens]
- **Enslaved people and the British.** British operations in the low country freed the labor force of southern planters, and enslaved people crossed to British lines seeking freedom under Dunmore’s and later proclamations. Their choices altered the war’s manpower and its meaning, and the chapter should present them as actors rather than as property in motion.[^ch11-abt-cowpens][^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-gotham-black-loyalists]

### 5.3 Greene, Cowpens, and Guilford Courthouse

- **The claim.** After Camden, Nathanael Greene took command and fought a war of attrition that wore the British down without requiring a decisive battle.
- **The command.** Greene replaced Gates in the Southern Department about two months after Camden and split his force, sending Daniel Morgan across the Catawba to cut British supply lines.[^ch11-abt-camden][^ch11-abt-cowpens]
- **Cowpens.** On January 17, 1781, Morgan’s mixed force of Continentals and militia defeated and nearly destroyed a British and Loyalist detachment under Banastre Tarleton at Cowpens, a victory built on Morgan’s use of his militia and his knowledge of his men.[^ch11-abt-cowpens]
- **Guilford Courthouse.** On March 15, 1781, Greene formed about 4,500 men in three lines to meet Cornwallis’s roughly 2,100 at Guilford Courthouse. Cornwallis won the field but lost about a quarter of his men and could not pursue; he withdrew to Wilmington and then shifted his operations to Virginia, a move that helped lead to Yorktown.[^ch11-abt-guilford]
- **The consequence.** Greene’s campaign shows the chapter’s larger point: the Americans did not need to win the big battles if they could keep an army and a cause alive, and the British could not replace what they lost.[^ch11-abt-guilford][^ch11-abt-cowpens]

## 6. Yorktown and peace

### 6.1 The war moves to Virginia

- **The claim.** After Guilford Courthouse, Cornwallis took his army into Virginia, where geography, a French fleet, and a combined American and French army closed a trap.
- **The move.** With his army unfit to chase Greene, Cornwallis moved to Virginia in the spring of 1781 and eventually occupied Yorktown, on a peninsula, to refit and await supplies and reinforcements from the Royal Navy.[^ch11-abt-guilford][^ch11-abt-yorktown]
- **The opportunity.** Washington and the French commander, the Comte de Rochambeau, chose to strike south rather than against New York because a French fleet under the Comte de Grasse would be available in the Chesapeake. Their combined armies marched hundreds of miles from north of New York City, the largest troop movement of the war.[^ch11-abt-yorktown]
- **The consequence.** Cornwallis’s position depended entirely on sea supply; once the French controlled the bay, his army was isolated.[^ch11-abt-yorktown]

### 6.2 The siege and the surrender

- **The claim.** The siege of Yorktown was a genuinely allied victory in which the French fleet and army were essential, and its result ended major fighting.
- **The sea fight.** On September 5, 1781, the French fleet kept the Royal Navy out of the Chesapeake at the Battle of the Capes, abandoning Cornwallis’s army. Allied forces reached Yorktown on September 28 and began siege works with French engineers; the bombardment began on October 9.[^ch11-abt-yorktown]
- **The surrender.** Cornwallis raised a flag of truce on October 17 and negotiated over two days; the British and Hessian garrison surrendered on October 19. Sources give Cornwallis’s force as about 9,000, with allied engaged totals near 28,900 counting American, French, and militia. The numbers should be given with their definitions.[^ch11-abt-yorktown][^ch11-check-numbers]
- **In Focus: the articles of capitulation.** **In Focus.** The terms of surrender, the “Articles of Capitulation settled at Yorktown,” were negotiated between Washington, Rochambeau, and de Grasse on one side and Cornwallis and the naval commander Thomas Symonds on the other. Read closely, the articles show what each side cared about. Article I divides the prisoners: the land troops remain prisoners to the United States, the navy to the French. Article II delivers the artillery, arms, and public stores unimpaired. Article III fixes the ritual: the garrison marches out at two o’clock “with shouldered arms, colors cased, and drums beating a British or German march,” then grounds its arms. Article IV lets officers keep their side-arms and both officers and soldiers keep their private property, with the qualification that property belonging to inhabitants of the states “shall be subject to be reclaimed.” A separate dispute over the treatment of Loyalists ran through the negotiation; Washington allowed Cornwallis latitude to let some Loyalists depart. The document is thus a compact about honor, property, and allegiance as much as a record of defeat, and it is the place to discuss Loyalist petitions and claims, which asked the British to protect the people who had sided with the Crown.[^ch11-mountvernon-capitulation][^ch11-gilder-yorktown][^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration]
- **The consequence.** Yorktown did not formally end the war, but it broke the British will to continue in America and set the terms for peace.[^ch11-abt-yorktown][^ch11-dos-treaty-paris]

### 6.3 The peace of Paris and the unfinished settlement

- **The claim.** The Treaty of Paris recognized American independence and a vast western boundary, but it left enslaved people, Loyalists, and Native nations outside the settlement it described.
- **The negotiation.** Formal negotiations began on September 27, 1782; Franklin, John Adams, and John Jay reached preliminary articles signed in Paris on November 30, 1782, which took effect once Britain and France concluded terms. The definitive Treaty of Paris was signed on September 3, 1783.[^ch11-dos-treaty-paris][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris]
- **The terms.** The treaty recognized United States independence and defined boundaries that opened western expansion; it provided for British evacuation of posts and access to the Mississippi, for payment of prewar debts, and for an end to retaliatory measures against Loyalists and their property. John Adams privately judged the definitive treaty “a Simple Repetition” of the provisional one, and understood that Britain, still the stronger power, would not grant more.[^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-masshist-treaty]
- **What it did not settle.** The treaty contained no terms for Britain’s Native allies, who had to negotiate their own peace with the United States; the first such treaty, at Fort Stanwix in 1784, imposed major land cessions on the four nations that had sided with Britain and left Oneida and Tuscarora lands insecure despite their alliance with the Americans. Enslaved people who had escaped to the British were not returned, and the British evacuated thousands of Black Loyalists from New York in 1783, recording them in the register called the Book of Negroes; the treaty’s article against carrying away “Negroes, or other Property of the American Inhabitants” was worked around by issuing certificates of freedom. Article V’s promise of restitution for Loyalist property was largely unenforced, and disputes over debts, posts, and Loyalists went unresolved until the Jay Treaty of 1794.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-masshist-treaty]
- **The consequence.** The peace ended the war without settling who belonged to the new republic or what would happen to the people the war had displaced. That unresolved settlement is the bridge to Chapter 12.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-check-ch12]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

- **The rebellion survived by keeping an army in being.** The Americans lost New York and Philadelphia but preserved a force that could retreat, regroup, and recruit, while Britain could not destroy it.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy]
- **The win was international.** American independence was secured as part of a world war, and the French fleet and army were essential at Yorktown.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-abt-yorktown]
- **The war divided Americans and nations.** Loyalists were organized, dispossessed, and exiled, and the Haudenosaunee confederacy split and was destroyed as a power in its homeland.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]
- **Enslaved people shaped the outcome.** Self-emancipation and Black service on both sides changed the war’s manpower and enlarged the question of who the Revolution was for.[^ch11-gilder-dunmore][^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers]
- **The peace was incomplete.** The treaty left Native nations, Loyalists, and enslaved people outside its settlement and generated disputes that outlasted 1783.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-masshist-treaty]

### 7.2 What is open

- **What won the war.** Was the outcome produced by American resilience, by British strategy’s contradictions, by French intervention, by Native and Loyalist choices, or by some combination? The sources weight these differently.[^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy][^ch11-gaf-mackesy]
- **How much the war was a revolution.** Did the war transform American society, or did it mainly secure independence for a society that changed more slowly? The answer depends on whether the measure is political structure, slavery, land, or ordinary people’s lives.[^ch11-kirkus-royster][^ch11-kirkus-taylor]
- **What the archives can show.** Enslaved and Indigenous people appear in these records mainly through the documents of officials, officers, and planters. The Book of Negroes is a rare register compiled by the British and is a record of inspection as much as of freedom, and the chapter should name those mediations.[^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-check-names]
- **What the numbers were.** The strengths, casualties, and refugee counts vary by source: British strength near New York is given as 20,000 or 32,000; Long Island casualties differ; the number of enslaved people who escaped to the British is an estimate.[^ch11-mountvernon-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-gilder-dunmore]
- **How to weigh the Loyalist and Native wars.** Whether to treat these as parts of one Revolution or as distinct civil and imperial wars is a live interpretive choice.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the war was at once a revolution, a civil war, and a world war, and in which the political settlement must be read alongside its consequences for enslaved people, Loyalists, and Native nations.
- **The war as revolution.** Charles Royster’s *A Revolutionary People at War* (1979) argues that Americans’ commitment to “virtue” and “freedom” collided with the demands of a standing army, and that only a minority served; the revolution’s ideals and its military practice pulled against each other. John Shy’s *A People Numerous and Armed* (1976) insists that war and society shaped each other and that “no other nation has had its official origin and constitutional preservation so clearly linked to warfare.”[^ch11-kirkus-royster][^ch11-shy-review]
- **The war as civil war.** Maya Jasanoff’s *Liberty’s Exiles* (2011) treats the conflict as a civil war and follows the 60,000 Loyalists who left, and the New York confiscation records show how revolutionary governments coerced allegiance and redistributed property. This position restores the losers and the internally divided to the center.[^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nypl-confiscation]
- **The war as world war.** Piers Mackesy’s *The War for America, 1775–1783* (1964) reconceives the conflict as a global imperial war seen from London; the National Archives likewise calls it “actually a world conflict” involving France, Spain, and the Netherlands. Andrew Jackson O’Shaughnessy’s *The Men Who Lost America* (2013) argues that Britain’s commanders were able rather than incompetent and that Britain fought “an army of conquest not of occupation,” which the rebellion could outlast.[^ch11-gaf-mackesy][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-notevenpast-oshaughnessy]
- **The war as dispossession.** Colin Calloway’s work on the Revolution in Indian country and Alan Taylor’s *American Revolutions* (2016) place Native nations, slavery, and westward expansion at the center, arguing that the Revolution’s liberty for some rested on the dispossession of others. Taylor’s reviewers note that his continental frame both widens the story and shifts attention away from the thirteen colonies’ politics.[^ch11-kirkus-taylor][^ch11-claremont-taylor][^ch11-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions say that the war’s outcome was not one thing: independence was won in a global war, at the cost of a civil war at home and the destruction of Native power in the North, and it left slavery’s contradiction unresolved. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch11-shy-review][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris]

## Legacies

The war’s settlement was as consequential as its battles. The United States gained independence and a western boundary that opened the continent to settlement, but the peace imposed no terms on Britain’s Native allies, and the 1784 Treaty of Fort Stanwix demanded vast land cessions from the Haudenosaunee nations that had fought for Britain and left the American-allied Oneida and Tuscarora insecure.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

It left a contradiction at the heart of the republic. Enslaved people had seized the war as a chance at freedom, some 5,000 to 8,000 people of African descent had served the American cause, and the northern states began to move against slavery, yet the institution survived in the South and the Constitution would protect it. The Black Loyalist exodus, recorded in the Book of Negroes, carried the war’s promise of freedom across the British Empire.[^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers][^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-gilder-dunmore]

And it left a debt and an unfinished settlement. The Continental Army’s supply crisis, the mutinies, the unpaid soldiers, and the unresolved disputes over Loyalist property and British debts fed the crisis of the Confederation and the movement for a stronger national government that Chapter 12 follows.[^ch11-nps-valley-forge][^ch11-masshist-treaty][^ch11-check-ch12]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The war became a global conflict among Britain, France, Spain, and the Netherlands, and the French alliance was the single most important diplomatic success of the rebellion. The chapter should place the American war inside the Atlantic imperial struggle, not outside it.[^ch11-dos-french-alliance][^ch11-nara-treaty-paris]
- **Archives and silences.** Enslaved and Indigenous people appear in these sources mainly through the documents of officers, officials, and planters. The Book of Negroes is a rare register of Black lives compiled by the British, and it is evidence of inspection as much as of freedom; the chapter should name the archive’s angle of vision.[^ch11-tna-book-of-negroes][^ch11-check-names]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 10 carries the road to independence; Chapter 12 carries the constitutional settlement; later chapters return to the dispossession of Native nations (Chs. 14 and 17) and to slavery (Chs. 16 and 20). The questions this chapter opens about who belonged to the republic run through the book.[^ch11-check-ch10][^ch11-check-ch12][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]

## Summary

- Britain carried the war to New York in 1776 with a fleet, a professional army, and about 30,000 hired German soldiers, against a Continental Army that Congress could barely supply.[^ch11-usni-glover][^ch11-amrev-hessians]
- The Americans lost New York and Philadelphia but kept an army in being through Trenton, Princeton, and Valley Forge.[^ch11-abt-brooklyn][^ch11-abt-trenton][^ch11-nps-valley-forge]
- Black soldiers served on both sides, and Dunmore’s 1775 proclamation and later British offers drew thousands of enslaved people toward British lines.[^ch11-amrev-black-soldiers][^ch11-gilder-dunmore]
- Burgoyne’s surrender at Saratoga on October 17, 1777, brought France into the war through the treaties of February 6, 1778, and the conflict became global.[^ch11-abt-saratoga][^ch11-dos-french-alliance]
- The Haudenosaunee confederacy split, and Washington’s 1779 Sullivan–Clinton campaign destroyed more than 40 villages and displaced as many as 5,000 people.[^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-amrev-memory-wars]
- Loyalists were organized, dispossessed under confiscation laws, and exiled; about 60,000 left the United States after the war.[^ch11-ati-loyalist-declaration][^ch11-nypl-confiscation][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff]
- After Camden, Nathanael Greene fought a war of attrition and Morgan’s victory at Cowpens helped wear down Cornwallis, who was beaten in the field at Guilford Courthouse and then moved to Virginia.[^ch11-abt-camden][^ch11-abt-cowpens][^ch11-abt-guilford]
- The French fleet won the Battle of the Capes, and the allies besieged Yorktown until Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781, on articles that divided prisoners, delivered the stores, and preserved private property.[^ch11-abt-yorktown][^ch11-mountvernon-capitulation]
- The Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783, recognized independence and a western boundary but left Native nations, Loyalists, and enslaved people outside its settlement.[^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784][^ch11-masshist-treaty]

> *A closing note.* The War for Independence was won, but not in the way the word “victory” usually means. The Americans lost their largest port and their capital and rarely beat a British army in the open field. They survived because they kept an army in being, because Britain could not occupy a continent, and because France, then Spain, turned a colonial rebellion into a world war. The cost was borne unequally: Loyalists were dispossessed and driven out, the Haudenosaunee confederacy was broken and its homelands destroyed, and enslaved people who seized the war’s promise of freedom had to leave the country to keep it. The peace of 1783 recognized a new nation and drew its boundaries, but it left the republic’s deepest questions, about slavery, about Native nations, and about who belonged, still open. That is why the chapter calls the American victory a settlement as much as a triumph.[^ch11-nara-treaty-paris][^ch11-amrev-jasanoff][^ch11-nps-stanwix-1784]
