## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the dates.** Levittown was built on Long Island, in the town of Hempstead, by Levitt & Sons between 1947 and 1951, on the former potato fields of a place first called Island Trees. It became the paradigmatic mass-produced American suburb, and William Levitt appeared on the cover of *Time* on July 13, 1950.[^ch38-levitt-hales]
- **The scale.** The development contained 17,447 houses. The Levitt organization advertised low down payments and long mortgages for veterans, and it built fast and cheaply with standardized parts and crews.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-levitt-hales]
- **The lease.** At first Levitt wrote racial exclusion into the leases. The clause read: “The tenant agrees not to permit the premises to be used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race.”[^ch38-levitt-lease]
- **The legal change.** On May 3, 1948, the Supreme Court held in *Shelley v. Kraemer* that although racially restrictive covenants were not themselves unconstitutional, state courts could not enforce them, because enforcement would be state action barred by the Equal Protection Clause.[^ch38-shelley-oyez]
- **The response.** After the decision Levitt dropped the clause. He said in 1949 that the change “changed absolutely nothing,” and he continued to refuse to sell or rent to Black families; the practice outlasted the clause by more than a decade.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]
- **The exclusionary result.** *A Consumers’ Republic* records that in 1953, when Levittown’s population reached about 70,000, it was the largest community in the United States with no Black population. By 1960 the census counted 65,276 residents, 57 of them Black; Kenneth T. Jackson reports Levittown at 82,000 residents and none of them Black. The two figures must be reconciled.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-check-levittown]
- **The challenge.** A local Committee to End Discrimination in Levittown organized, and in 1952 its supporters gathered at the home of a threatened white tenant to resist an eviction; Levitt backed down. A Black family that gained occupancy in 1952 was evicted a year later, and a white homeowner later sold a Levittown house to a Black buyer.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the leases, the decision, the developer’s statements, the census counts, and the organizing. It cannot show what most residents privately thought, how many wanted integration, or how much discrimination was Levitt’s alone rather than an industry and federal practice. The chapter should attribute those claims to their evidence.[^ch38-check-scene][^ch38-check-silences]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In 1950 no one knew that *Brown* would come in 1954, that the sit-ins would begin in 1960, or that the Fair Housing Act of 1968 lay ahead. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch38-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A lease opens a question rather than settling it.** The scene’s claim is not that one clause caused segregated suburbia but that it made exclusion a matter of routine contract, and that ending the clause while keeping the exclusion showed how much discrimination rested on private choice, custom, and public subsidy rather than on one enforceable rule.[^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-fha-redlining]
- **The stakes.** The federal government had helped create the demand and the financing for suburbs like Levittown through the GI Bill and the Federal Housing Administration. If the same state that underwrote the suburb also declined to protect access to it, then the abundance the chapter describes was distributed by design.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: for whom was postwar abundance secure?[^ch38-check-scope]
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from a Long Island lease to the GI Bill and the consumer boom, to the highways and the census, to television and Elvis, to the suburban housewife and the woman in the typing pool, to the terminated reservation and the relocated family, and to *Brown*, Emmett Till, Montgomery, and Little Rock.[^ch38-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented couple, dialogue, or interior thought.** The date, the lease, the decision, and the developer’s statement are documented. The scene may not stage a fabricated family’s application, a salesman’s speech beyond the recorded exchange, or a resident’s private feelings.[^ch38-check-scene][^ch38-check-levittown]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of *Brown*, the sit-ins, the 1964 and 1965 statutes, or the Fair Housing Act.[^ch38-check-scene]
- **No single-villain framing.** The scene must not reduce segregation to one developer. Federal underwriting guidance, local zoning, real-estate practice, and white homeowner demand all did work, and the chapter should present them together.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-check-housing-terms]

## 1. Politics, prosperity, and anticommunism

### 1.1 The claim: growth and its limits

- **The claim.** Between 1945 and 1960 the American economy grew on a scale that lifted millions into a new middle class, and national politics narrowed around that growth and around anticommunism. The chapter should present prosperity and the politics of loyalty as connected, because both rested on choices about who belonged.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-mccarthy-censure]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should show the boom as produced, not natural: wartime savings, federal spending, the GI Bill, and consumer credit built it, and its benefits were distributed unevenly by race, gender, and region.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining]
- **What the record can show.** It can show programs, statutes, and aggregate growth. It cannot show that everyone shared in it, and the chapter should present “the affluent society” as a claim to test rather than a description.[^ch38-galbraith][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 1.2 The GI Bill and the prosperity it helped build

- **The claim.** The Servicemen’s Readjustment Act, signed on June 22, 1944, offered World War II veterans federally financed education, training, unemployment insurance, and home loans, and it helped convert a feared postwar depression into a generation of growth.[^ch38-gi-bill]
- **The terms.** Within seven years about eight million veterans received educational benefits: approximately 2,300,000 attended colleges and universities, 3,500,000 received school training, and 3,400,000 received on-the-job training. The number of degrees granted by American colleges more than doubled between 1940 and 1950. By 1955, 4,300,000 home loans had been granted with a face value of about $33 billion, and veterans bought about 20 percent of all new homes built after the war.[^ch38-gi-bill]
- **What the record can show.** It can show enrollment, loan, and spending figures. It cannot show that the benefits were equal, because Black veterans were often unable to obtain mortgages in Black neighborhoods and were excluded from “white” suburban developments, and the GI Bill’s administration is part of the chapter’s argument about unequal opportunity.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-check-levittown]

### 1.3 Galbraith and the affluent-society critique

- **The claim.** In 1958 the Harvard economist John Kenneth Galbraith published *The Affluent Society*, arguing that an economy devoted to producing and consuming private goods had neglected public needs and manufactured the wants it then satisfied.[^ch38-galbraith]
- **The argument.** Galbraith warned that an economy in which “wants are increasingly created by the process by which they are satisfied” was unsound and, in his view, immoral. The phrase “private opulence and public squalor” became the shorthand for his charge that the private sector flourished while public provision lagged, and it must be collated against the 1958 edition.[^ch38-galbraith][^ch38-check-epigraphs]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the book, its argument, and its reception. It cannot show that Galbraith’s forecasts were correct, and the chapter should present his critique as an argument of the period rather than a settled verdict.[^ch38-affluent-works][^ch38-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 Anticommunism after McCarthy

- **The claim.** The second Red Scare reached its most visible phase in the early 1950s and then broke, and the break came through television and the Senate rather than through the courts alone.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure]
- **The episode.** On April 22, 1954, the Senate’s Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, chaired by Senator Joseph R. McCarthy of Wisconsin, opened hearings into security in the United States Army. The Army–McCarthy hearings ran to June 17, 1954, and were carried on national television; McCarthy “badgered witnesses while ignoring parliamentary procedures and the rules of common courtesy,” and by the time they ended he had damaged his standing with the public. On July 30, 1954, Senator Ralph Flanders of Vermont introduced a resolution to censure him; the Senate voted 67 to 22 on December 2, 1954, to censure McCarthy for abuse of a Senate committee.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure]
- **Ownership.** Chapter 37 owns McCarthy’s 1950 Wheeling speech, the Smith Act prosecutions, and the Rosenberg execution. This chapter takes up the televised hearings, the censure, and the political aftermath.[^ch38-check-ch37][^ch38-mccarthy-censure]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the hearings, the vote, and the aftermath. It cannot show that the censure alone ended the broader politics of accusation, which continued in loyalty programs, blacklists, and local campaigns.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure][^ch38-check-silences]

### 1.5 Who was left out of the boom

- **The claim.** The comfortable picture of the decade rested on exclusions the next sections examine: segregated suburbs, segregated schools, women pushed out of or confined within the paid labor force, and Native nations pressed to dissolve their political status.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-termination][^ch38-womens-labor]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the “consensus” as a surface and the exclusions as the structure beneath it, without treating either as total.[^ch38-check-scope][^ch38-exclusion-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show policies and their distribution. It cannot show a single national experience, and the chapter should avoid speaking for the excluded.[^ch38-check-silences]

## 2. Housing, highways, and migration

### 2.1 The claim: a federally built, racially sorted metropolis

- **The claim.** Federal housing and highway policy after the war subsidized a largely White suburban middle class, sorted the metropolis by race and income, and reshaped where Americans lived.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-act]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should show the mechanism — insurance, underwriting guidance, covenants, zoning, and expressway routing — because the outcome was produced by policy, not by an invisible market.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-segregation]
- **What the record can show.** It can show program rules and patterns of construction and displacement. It cannot prove individual motive, and the chapter should distinguish a racially neutral rule with a racial effect from a rule designed to exclude.[^ch38-check-housing-terms]

### 2.2 Levittown and the In Focus

- **The claim.** Levittown made the mass-produced suburb the model of postwar success and, at the same time, made racial exclusion a condition of access to it.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-levitt-lease]
- **The evidence.** The development’s 17,447 houses and its first leases carried the Caucasian-occupancy clause; after *Shelley v. Kraemer* the clause was dropped while the exclusion continued; and by 1960 the community had almost no Black residents. Local organizers challenged the policy, and Levitt defended it as a private business judgment.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-shelley-oyez]

**In Focus.** The In Focus reads a single sentence from a Levittown lease as a document of postwar exclusion: “The tenant agrees not to permit the premises to be used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race.” The sentence repays a close reading because it places race in the grammar of routine contract. It is a private covenant between landlord and tenant, not a statute, and it borrows the authority of property law to make race a term of occupancy. The box should then set the sentence beside the legal change that followed it. In *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) the Supreme Court held that such covenants were not themselves unconstitutional but that state courts could not enforce them, because judicial enforcement would be state action violating the Equal Protection Clause. The decision therefore made the clause unenforceable without making the exclusion illegal. Levitt dropped the clause and kept the practice, saying in 1949 that the change “changed absolutely nothing,” and the community remained almost entirely White a decade later. The box should read the clause, the decision, and the developer’s response together, because the sequence is the chapter’s argument in miniature: the law withdrew its hand from enforcement while private power and federal subsidy carried the exclusion forward. The box must distinguish a restrictive covenant from a lease clause, an unenforceable covenant from a lawful refusal, and a private act from the federal underwriting that made the suburb possible; the exact clause should be collated against an original lease before publication.[^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-check-focus][^ch38-check-housing-terms]

- **The registered status.** No image is assigned to this chapter, so the section is planned as a textual close reading of the lease clause. Reproducing a lease page would require a new register entry with its own caption, credit, rights, and alt text.[^ch38-check-focus][^ch38-check-image]
- **The exact text.** The clause is quoted at second hand from Martha Biondi’s *To Stand and Fight* (2003), p. 230, as reproduced in a secondary compilation, and must be collated against an original Levittown lease or an archival facsimile.[^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-check-levittown]

### 2.3 GI Bill loans, FHA redlining, and Shelley v. Kraemer

- **The claim.** The GI Bill’s mortgage guarantees and the Federal Housing Administration’s insurance made suburban homeownership possible for millions, and both operated within a system that steered Black borrowers away from new suburbs and older urban neighborhoods.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining]
- **The evidence.** The Federal Reserve’s history records that the FHA “began redlining at the very beginning of its operations in 1934,” and that its 1938 *Underwriting Manual* emphasized the negative effect of the “infiltration of inharmonious racial groups” on credit risk and recommended restrictive covenants prohibiting “the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are intended.” Federal policy reflected the segregation of American society, and public and private redlining were intertwined.[^ch38-fha-redlining]
- **The legal turn and its limits.** *Shelley v. Kraemer* barred judicial enforcement of racial covenants in 1948. It did not bar private discrimination, and it left the FHA’s underwriting practice untouched, so the chapter should present the decision as a real limit on state power that did not by itself open the suburbs.[^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-check-housing-terms]

### 2.4 The highway act and the built environment

- **The claim.** The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, popularly the National Interstate and Defense Highways Act, authorized the largest public-works program in American history and helped remake both the suburbs and the cities.[^ch38-highway-act]
- **The terms.** The act expanded the interstate system to 41,000 miles and authorized $25 billion for fiscal years 1957 through 1969, with the federal government paying 90 percent of the cost. The National Archives records that “in urban areas, massive superhighways cut through neighborhoods and destroyed housing, much of it in poorer sections of the city.”[^ch38-highway-act]
- **The distribution.** One journalistic account drawing on Department of Transportation estimates reports that more than 475,000 households and more than a million people were displaced nationwide by federal roadway construction, disproportionately in Black and other communities of color. The chapter should treat that figure as an estimate with a method and a cutoff, and should not present expressway routing as an accident.[^ch38-highway-segregation][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 2.5 Migration and metropolitan growth, with Map 29

- **The claim.** The postwar decades saw the largest internal migration in American history: Black migration out of the South into northern and western cities, white migration to the suburbs, and regional shifts in population and industry that the chapter’s map shows.[^ch38-check-map29]
- **Figure and map.** The registered visual `ch38-map-01` (Map 29, “Migration and metropolitan growth, 1940–1960”) belongs to §2. Its caption and source line must state the observation years and distinguish interstate migration, metropolitan growth, and the Great Migration, and it must not imply that all movement ran in one direction. Its evidence targets are Census regional and county series and Appendix B, Table 1, and its production record is `repo-map29`. The map must distinguish total population change from Black population change and must not infer individual motives from aggregate movement.[^ch38-check-map29][^ch38-gi-bill]
- **What the record can show.** It can show direction and scale. It cannot show why any individual moved, and the chapter should keep causation in the prose.[^ch38-check-map29]

## 3. Television, music, and national audiences

### 3.1 The claim: one nation, many audiences

- **The claim.** The 1950s invented a genuinely national mass audience, and that audience was also a battlefield over race, gender, region, and youth.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-elvis]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present television, popular music, and the Beats together, because each both standardized taste and provoked conflict about the terms of belonging.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-howl]
- **What the record can show.** It can show distribution, ratings, and censorship. It cannot measure what a viewer or listener took from a program, and the chapter should mark that limit.[^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 3.2 Television: diffusion and the freeze

- **The claim.** Television spread faster than any earlier medium. The Library of Congress notes that in 1950 only 9 percent of American households had a television set, and that by 1960 the figure had reached 90 percent.[^ch38-television-loc]
- **The regulatory pause.** On September 30, 1948, the Federal Communications Commission announced a freeze on new television licenses that lasted until 1952, a pause that helped consolidate the dominance of the national networks. The chapter should state the freeze and its dates without claiming that it alone caused the network system.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-check-tv-stat]
- **What the record can show.** It can show ownership and licensing. It cannot settle how many households watched a given program, and the various diffusion figures differ by measure and year and must be reconciled.[^ch38-check-tv-stat]

### 3.3 Rock and roll, Elvis, and youth culture

- **The claim.** Rock and roll made youth a national market and a national anxiety. When Elvis Presley first appeared on *The Ed Sullivan Show* on September 9, 1956, about 60 million viewers watched, and the program reported about 82 percent of the television audience — a share, not a rating.[^ch38-elvis]
- **The conflict.** Presley’s blend of rhythm and blues, country, and gospel drew on Black musical traditions while the industry marketed him as a white star; his performances were attacked in the press, and by his final appearance on January 6, 1957, CBS censors required that he be filmed from the waist up. The chapter should treat the “gyrations” controversy as a debate about race, respectability, and the young.[^ch38-elvis][^ch38-check-silences]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the appearances, the audience figures, and the censorship. It cannot show that Presley alone caused a cultural change, and the chapter should set him within the broader traffic between Black and white music.[^ch38-elvis][^ch38-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 The Beats and the Howl trial

- **The claim.** The Beat writers offered the decade’s sharpest literary dissent from conformity, and their reputation was made in part by a censorship trial. Allen Ginsberg wrote *Howl* in the summer of 1955, read it publicly at the Six Gallery in San Francisco in October 1955, and published it in 1956 with City Lights.[^ch38-howl]
- **The trial.** When copies printed in London arrived through United States Customs, City Lights’s Lawrence Ferlinghetti and Shigeyoshi Murao were arrested on obscenity charges in San Francisco. At the trial poets, critics, and academics testified to the poem’s redeeming social value, and the court ruled *Howl* not obscene, a decision that widened the boundary of what American literature could print.[^ch38-howl]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the reading, the publication, and the trial. It cannot establish that the verdict alone liberalized American culture, and the chapter should present the Beats as a small avant-garde whose trial became a national test case.[^ch38-howl][^ch38-check-debate-works]

## 4. Gender, family, employment, and Native termination/relocation

### 4.1 The claim: an ideal that excluded

- **The claim.** The decade’s settled picture of the male breadwinner and the suburban housewife rested on a labor market, a welfare state, and a federal Indian policy that pushed many people outside the picture.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-check-names]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should hold the ideal and its exclusions together, because the same prosperity that made the housewife possible depended on underpaid domestic and service work and on the dissolution of Native political status.[^ch38-womens-labor][^ch38-termination]
- **What the record can show.** It can show marriage, birth, and employment patterns and federal statutes. It cannot show that the ideal was universally believed, and the chapter should present it as a widely promoted norm, not a consensus of conduct.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-check-silences]

### 4.2 Domesticity and the baby boom

- **The claim.** The years after the war produced a marriage and baby boom that made the family the center of national life and the suburb its setting.[^ch38-coontz]
- **The evidence.** Stephanie Coontz records that the birth rate rose from a low of 18.4 per 1,000 women during the Depression to a high of 25.3 per 1,000 in 1957, that “the birth rate for third children doubled between 1940 and 1960, and that for fourth children tripled,” and that the divorce and illegitimacy rates were about half their later levels.[^ch38-coontz]
- **What the record can show.** It can show rates and the cultural promotion of domesticity. It cannot show that the 1950s family was the traditional norm rather than an aberration, and the chapter should present the nostalgic picture as an argument Coontz and others contest.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-exclusion-works]

### 4.3 Women, work, and the labor market

- **The claim.** The domestic ideal coexisted with rising numbers of women in the paid labor force, most of them in sex-segregated and low-paid work.[^ch38-womens-labor]
- **The evidence.** About a third of women worked outside the home in 1950, and married women’s participation rose steadily through the decade; the chapter should give the series, its definition, and its source, and should not confuse the total participation rate with the rate for married women or for women with young children. The bracero program and the 1954 deportation drive known as Operation Wetback supplied and expelled low-wage labor on which parts of the economy depended.[^ch38-womens-labor][^ch38-wetback][^ch38-check-womens-work][^ch38-check-wetback]
- **What the record can show.** It can show participation and earnings. It cannot show why any woman worked or left work, and the chapter should avoid treating the housewife and the working woman as opposites.[^ch38-womens-labor][^ch38-check-silences]

### 4.4 Native termination and relocation

- **The claim.** Federal policy in the 1950s tried to end the special legal relationship between the United States and Native nations and to move Native people into cities, with damaging results.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-relocation]
- **The statutes.** On August 1, 1953, Congress adopted House Concurrent Resolution 108, calling for termination of federal supervision “at the earliest possible time” of named tribes and those in California, Florida, New York, and Texas; on August 15, 1953, Public Law 280 transferred criminal and civil jurisdiction on many reservations to state authorities. From 1953 to 1970 Congress began 60 termination proceedings, and more than three million acres of tribal land were relinquished.[^ch38-termination]
- **The relocation program.** The Bureau of Indian Affairs ran a voluntary urban relocation program from the early 1950s, offering help with housing and employment in Chicago, Denver, Los Angeles, Cleveland, and Seattle. Many who moved faced unemployment, low-wage work, discrimination, and the loss of cultural ties, and the 1956 Indian Relocation Act expanded the effort. The National Archives records that when relocation began nearly 8 percent of Native Americans lived in cities, and that the 2000 Census found about 64 percent did.[^ch38-relocation]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the resolutions, the statutes, and the program’s design. It cannot show that relocation was uniformly forced or uniformly chosen, and the chapter should present individual Native decisions as decisions made under heavy pressure.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-check-silences]

## 5. The freedom struggle through the 1960 sit-ins’ background

### 5.1 The claim: legal victory and mass movement

- **The claim.** The 1950s combined a legal campaign against segregation with mass protest, and the two built on each other; the decade ended with a partly dismantled legal order, a national movement, and strong resistance.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the decade’s movement as foreshadowing the 1960 sit-ins without absorbing them, because Chapter 39 owns the sit-ins and what followed.[^ch38-check-ch39]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the cases, the campaigns, and the violence. It cannot show that *Brown* caused the movement, and the chapter should distinguish the decision’s effect from its symbolic role.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 Brown and “all deliberate speed”

- **The claim.** On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court unanimously held in *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka* that segregating children in public schools solely on the basis of race deprived them of equal protection, and that “separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.” It overruled the “separate but equal” doctrine of *Plessy v. Ferguson*.[^ch38-brown]
- **The remedy.** A year later, on May 31, 1955, the Court returned in *Brown II* and instructed the states to proceed “with all deliberate speed,” a formula that left the pace to lower courts and local officials and produced years of delay.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-little-rock]
- **Ownership.** Chapter 38 owns *Brown* and its immediate aftermath; Chapter 39 owns the sit-ins and later movement history.[^ch38-check-ch39][^ch38-brown]

### 5.3 Emmett Till and the galvanizing of a movement

- **The claim.** The murder of Emmett Till in 1955 and his mother’s decision to open his casket turned a private grief into a national image of Jim Crow violence.[^ch38-till]
- **The episode.** On August 28, 1955, Till, a 14-year-old from Chicago visiting family in Money, Mississippi, was abducted and killed after an encounter with a white store clerk; his body was recovered from the Tallahatchie River. Two white men, Roy Bryant and J. W. Milam, were tried and acquitted by an all-white, all-male jury; they later sold their account of the killing to *Look* magazine.[^ch38-till]
- **The decision and its effect.** Mamie Till-Mobley insisted on an open-casket funeral in Chicago, attended by more than 50,000 people and pictured in *Jet*, and the photographs carried the violence to a national audience. One hundred days after the murder, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery city bus. The chapter should present the connection as the movement’s own testimony, not as a claim that one death caused a movement.[^ch38-till][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-check-till-record]

### 5.4 Montgomery and Browder v. Gayle

- **The claim.** The Montgomery bus boycott transformed local grievance into a 13-month mass protest and made a new leader, the Reverend Martin Luther King Jr., nationally visible.[^ch38-montgomery]
- **The campaign.** The boycott began on December 5, 1955, after the arrest of Rosa Parks on December 1; on the first day, about 90 percent of Montgomery’s Black residents stayed off the buses. The Montgomery Improvement Association coordinated a carpool of some 300 cars, the city indicted more than 80 boycott leaders, and King’s home and E. D. Nixon’s home were bombed. The federal district court ruled in *Browder v. Gayle* on June 5, 1956, that bus segregation was unconstitutional, and the Supreme Court affirmed in November 1956; the boycott ended on December 20, 1956.[^ch38-montgomery]
- **The organizers.** The Women’s Political Council, the MIA’s women volunteers, and earlier challengers including Claudette Colvin and Mary Louise Smith were central, and the chapter should not reduce the campaign to one leader or one arrest.[^ch38-montgomery]

### 5.5 Little Rock and federal power

- **The claim.** The Little Rock crisis of 1957 tested whether the federal government would enforce *Brown*, and it showed both the reach and the limits of federal action.[^ch38-little-rock]
- **The episode.** Nine Black students enrolled at Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, in September 1957. Governor Orval Faubus deployed the Arkansas National Guard to bar them; on September 4 a mob threatened Elizabeth Eckford as she tried to enter alone. After Faubus withdrew the Guard, a full-scale riot followed, and on September 23, 1957, President Dwight D. Eisenhower issued Proclamation 3204 and Executive Order 10730, federalizing the Arkansas National Guard and sending about 1,000 Army paratroopers of the 101st Airborne Division to restore order.[^ch38-little-rock][^ch38-eo10730]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the orders, the troops, and the students’ entry. It cannot show that federal force desegregated the schools, since Little Rock’s schools closed rather than integrate and reopened under a different plan.[^ch38-little-rock][^ch38-check-silences]

### 5.6 The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 and the eve of the sit-ins

- **The claim.** Congress passed the first civil rights legislation since Reconstruction, and it was weak; the gap between the statutes and enforcement set the stage for the direct-action phase that began in 1960.[^ch38-cra1957]
- **The statutes.** The Civil Rights Act of 1957, signed on September 9, 1957, created the Civil Rights Section of the Justice Department and a federal Civil Rights Commission and empowered federal prosecutors to seek injunctions against interference with voting, but it was weakened in Congress. A second act in 1960 added modest tools to challenge discriminatory registration practices.[^ch38-cra1957]
- **The hand-off.** On February 1, 1960, four Black students sat down at a segregated lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina. The chapter should name that date and stop there, leaving the sit-ins and what followed to Chapter 39.[^ch38-check-ch39][^ch38-check-bridge]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The economy grew and consumption reshaped daily life.** Federal programs, the GI Bill, consumer credit, and rising incomes produced broad, uneven growth.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-galbraith]
- **The suburb was federally made and racially sorted.** The GI Bill’s mortgage guarantees, the FHA’s underwriting practice, and restrictive covenants and leases shaped who could buy new housing.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-levitt-lease]
- **The interstate highway program was authorized in 1956 and displaced large numbers of people.** The act’s scope and the urban effects are documented.[^ch38-highway-act][^ch38-highway-segregation]
- **Television became a mass medium and a site of conflict.** Diffusion, the FCC freeze, and the Elvis and *Howl* controversies are documented.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-elvis][^ch38-howl]
- **Federal Indian policy pursued termination and relocation.** HCR 108 and Public Law 280 are documented, as is the urban relocation program.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-relocation]
- **The decade’s movement dismantled parts of the legal order.** *Brown*, *Browder v. Gayle*, Little Rock, and the 1957 and 1960 acts are documented.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-cra1957]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the decade a period of prosperity and consensus or of exclusion and incipient revolt?** The affluent-society and conformity frameworks (Galbraith; Whyte, Riesman, and Mills) emphasize growth, affluence, and social pressure; histories of exclusion and resistance (Coontz, Cohen, Sugrue, Hall) emphasize who was left out and who fought back. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch38-affluent-works][^ch38-exclusion-works][^ch38-check-debate-works]
- **Did the Cold War advance civil rights or confine them?** Mary L. Dudziak argues that international criticism of American racism pressured the federal government to reform but limited reform to formal equality and away from “a broader critique of the American economic and political system.” Others center Black activism and southern politics. The chapter should weigh both.[^ch38-dudziak][^ch38-exclusion-works]
- **Was segregation a matter of law, custom, or federal subsidy?** The evidence supports a combined explanation: covenants and clauses, unenforceable after *Shelley* but effective through custom, rested on federal insurance and underwriting. The chapter should not choose one cause.[^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-levitt-lease]
- **What caused the urban crisis?** One position locates it in 1960s riots and white flight; another, exemplified by Thomas J. Sugrue, locates it in deindustrialization, discrimination, and housing policy in the 1940s and 1950s. The chapter should present the timing as a question.[^ch38-exclusion-works]
- **Was the 1950s family traditional or exceptional?** Coontz and others argue that the era was an aberration rather than a norm; the chapter should present the demographic evidence and the contested framing.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-exclusion-works]
- **How much did policy displace, and how many were terminated?** Displacement and termination figures rest on different methods and cutoffs, and the chapter should give ranges and name the methods.[^ch38-check-numbers][^ch38-highway-segregation][^ch38-termination]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which broad growth and durable exclusion were produced together, and in which the excluded repeatedly acted. Neither a story of affluence and consensus nor a story of exclusion alone explains the record.[^ch38-affluent-works][^ch38-exclusion-works]
- **Contingency.** The GI Bill’s administration, the FHA’s underwriting rules, the route of an expressway, the wording of a lease, and the choice to terminate a tribe were decisions people made and could have made otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-segregation]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter’s synthesis should hold structural conditions — federal subsidy, labor demand, regional politics, and the Cold War — together with the contingent choices made within them.[^ch38-exclusion-works][^ch38-check-debate-works]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that the United States after 1945 built a mass middle class on federal foundations and drew its boundaries with law, contract, and violence, and that the people drawn outside those boundaries began the movement that would dominate the next decade.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery]

## Legacies

The years from 1945 to 1960 left the United States a suburban nation and a metropolitan crisis. The mortgage guarantees, underwriting rules, and expressway contracts of those years fixed a pattern of race and residence that outlasted the decade and shaped the geography of opportunity for generations.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-segregation]

The decade left a movement and a legal framework. *Brown*, the Montgomery boycott, and Little Rock made segregation a national question and produced the first civil rights statutes since Reconstruction, while leaving enforcement and economic justice unresolved; the sit-ins of 1960 built directly on that base.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-cra1957]

The decade also left a memory. The television family, the suburban house, and the “silent generation” became a widely recycled picture of American normality, and historians from Coontz to Sugrue have argued that the picture obscured the exclusions that made it possible.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-exclusion-works]

- **The transnational frame.** The chapter should show how the Cold War’s competition for the allegiance of decolonizing nations, and the migration of Mexican labor, set part of the American domestic agenda, so that the world appears as an actor in the story rather than as scenery.[^ch38-dudziak][^ch38-wetback]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents homeowners, network executives, and federal officials and under-represents the excluded, the displaced, the terminated, the relocated, and the poor. The chapter should name these asymmetries rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]

The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch38-check-ch37][^ch38-check-ch39][^ch38-check-dates-appD]

## Summary

- The postwar economy grew broadly on federal foundations: the GI Bill of 1944 provided education, training, and home loans to about eight million veterans, and by 1955 it had backed 4,300,000 home loans worth about $33 billion.[^ch38-gi-bill]
- In 1958 John Kenneth Galbraith’s *The Affluent Society* argued that an economy which manufactured its own wants neglected public needs.[^ch38-galbraith]
- The Army–McCarthy hearings of April–June 1954 were televised, and on December 2, 1954, the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure Senator Joseph R. McCarthy.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure]
- Levittown’s leases barred occupancy by any but members of “the Caucasian race”; after *Shelley v. Kraemer* made such covenants unenforceable in 1948, Levitt dropped the clause but said the change “changed absolutely nothing,” and the community remained almost entirely White.[^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]
- The FHA’s 1938 *Underwriting Manual* warned of the “infiltration of inharmonious racial groups” and recommended restrictive covenants, embedding discrimination in federal housing policy.[^ch38-fha-redlining]
- The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 authorized 41,000 miles and $25 billion, and urban expressways displaced more than 475,000 households by one federal estimate.[^ch38-highway-act][^ch38-highway-segregation]
- Television ownership rose from 9 percent of households in 1950 to 90 percent in 1960, and Elvis Presley’s first *Ed Sullivan Show* appearance on September 9, 1956, drew about 60 million viewers.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-elvis]
- Allen Ginsberg read *Howl* at the Six Gallery in October 1955; City Lights published it in 1956, and the 1957 obscenity trial ended with the poem ruled not obscene.[^ch38-howl]
- The birth rate rose to 25.3 per 1,000 in 1957, and third and fourth children became far more common, even as about a third of women worked outside the home.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-womens-labor]
- House Concurrent Resolution 108 of August 1, 1953, sought termination of federal supervision, Public Law 280 shifted jurisdiction to the states, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs relocated Native people to cities.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-relocation]
- *Brown v. Board of Education* of May 17, 1954, held school segregation unconstitutional; *Brown II* set “all deliberate speed”; Emmett Till was murdered in 1955; the Montgomery boycott ran from December 1955 to December 1956 and ended with *Browder v. Gayle*; and the Little Rock crisis of 1957 brought federal troops.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-till][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-little-rock]
- The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 were the first such statutes since Reconstruction and were weak, leaving the direct-action phase to begin at Greensboro on February 1, 1960.[^ch38-cra1957][^ch38-check-ch39]

> *A closing note.* Between 1945 and 1960 the United States built the most prosperous mass society in its history, and it drew the boundaries of that society with leases, underwriting rules, expressway routes, and statutes. The decade gave millions of families a house, a car, and a television, and it gave the people it excluded a movement. The chapter’s question — for whom was postwar abundance secure? — is answered by that double record of provision and exclusion, and by a freedom struggle that was already, by 1960, about to become a mass phase.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on February 1, 1960, when four Black students sat down at a segregated lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and refused to leave. Chapter 39 takes up the civil rights revolution from the sit-ins through the freedom rides, Birmingham, the March on Washington, Mississippi, Selma, and Black Power. The legal victories and the unmet demands that Chapter 38 describes did not settle the movement; they defined the ground on which the next phase would be fought.[^ch38-check-bridge][^ch38-check-ch39]
