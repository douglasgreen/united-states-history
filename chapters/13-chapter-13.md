The four quotations disagree about what the new government was for. Hamilton began with credit, because he believed a nation that could not borrow could not survive. Washington warned against the spirit of party in the act of leaving office. Jefferson, taking that office from Washington’s party, insisted that the parties were brethren of the same principle. Wood answers that, apart from the Civil War, these were the most contentious years in the nation’s history. The chapter keeps all four in view, because each names something the others leave out: fiscal necessity, partisan danger, conciliation, and the depth of the conflict.[^ch13-cfr-wood]

New York, April 30, 1789. The city filled before noon. Crowds moved toward Wall and Nassau Streets, where Federal Hall, the old City Hall made over with a new balcony, waited under a clear, cool sky.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-inaugural-senate] At half past noon, George Washington rode alone in the state coach from Franklin Square. Troops of the city, members of Congress, and the heads of the executive departments of the old Confederation went before him. Foreign ministers and ordinary citizens followed.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]

He entered the Senate Chamber, where Vice President John Adams and both houses waited. Then he stepped onto the outer gallery above Broad Street, and the senators and representatives came after him. Chancellor Robert Livingston of New York administered the oath: “I do solemnly swear that I will faithfully execute the office of President of the United States, and will, to the best of my ability, preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution of the United States.” Washington repeated the words. He kissed the Bible. Livingston turned to the crowd and shouted, “Long live George Washington, President of the United States.” Thirteen cannon fired.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-mountvernon-timeline]

The Bible had been borrowed at the last moment from St. John’s Lodge No. 1, Ancient York Masons, after someone discovered that none had been provided. It was a King James Version printed in London in 1767, and it lay open at Genesis 49:13.[^ch13-nps-inaugural-bible][^ch13-inaugural-senate] The most familiar version of the scene adds four words to the oath: “So help me God.” No contemporary account includes them. The French minister, the Comte de Moustier, who attended the ceremony, repeated the constitutional oath verbatim and stopped there. The story first appeared in print more than six decades later, attributed to a childhood memory of Washington Irving. It may be true. The record does not show it.[^ch13-henriques-oath]

After the oath, Washington read his inaugural address in the Senate Chamber. He spoke in a surprisingly quiet voice, and he declined any salary beyond his actual expenses.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-mountvernon-inaugural] Then he and Congress walked to St. Paul’s Church for divine service. Fireworks ended the evening.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural]

The Constitution had described a president, a Congress, and a judiciary in a few pages. It had not described a cabinet, a party, a fiscal program, or a capital city. Those would have to be invented. The question was not whether the new government would act. It had already begun. The question was how it would act, for whom, and at whose expense. How did a written frame become a working and partisan government?

This chapter follows the federal government from its commencement in 1789 to the transfer of power in March 1801, with its center on the 1790s. It draws on the journals and acts of the First Congress; Hamilton’s treasury reports and the debates they provoked; the statutes and proclamations of the new government; the papers of Washington, Adams, Jefferson, Hamilton, and Madison; the records of the war in the Ohio Country and the texts of the treaties; the diplomatic correspondence of the neutrality crisis and the Quasi-War; the Sedition Act prosecutions; and the electoral and House records of 1800. It treats “credit,” “consolidation,” “party,” “neutrality,” “sedition,” and “the people” as contested terms whose meanings the chapter establishes in use. It names the people those terms excluded: enslaved people, Native nations, women, and the propertyless. It uses scholarship for what the records cannot show, and it does not present the party conflict as foreordained in 1789. Its dates are kept precise and reconciled with Appendix D (Chronology).[^ch13-check-scope]

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 examines the First Congress, the executive departments, and the reach of the new government. §2 turns to Hamilton’s fiscal program and the opposition it produced. §3 follows the war for the Ohio Country, the Treaty of Greenville, and the Whiskey Rebellion. §4 sets the American government inside the Atlantic revolutionary crisis, from neutrality to Saint-Domingue. §5 examines the Jay and Pinckney treaties and the election of 1796. §6 reaches the Quasi-War, the Sedition Act, and the election and transfer of 1800. §7 asks how historians explain the invention of party and the consolidation of national power. The chapter begins on the balcony of Federal Hall, where a written frame became a government.

## 1. Government begins

### 1.1 The First Congress and the machinery of government

The First Congress did not merely legislate. It built the departments and courts through which the government would act, inventing machinery that the Constitution had described only in outline. The House reached a quorum on April 1, 1789, and the Senate on April 6. The Senate then counted the electoral votes and certified Washington’s election before his inauguration.[^ch13-house-first-congress][^ch13-archives-senate-journal]

Congress created the Department of Foreign Affairs, soon renamed the Department of State, along with the Treasury and War departments. The Judiciary Act of 1789 created the office of attorney general, and Washington named Edmund Randolph to fill it. The cabinet that later met around the president’s table was not mentioned in the Constitution. It was an invention of practice, assembled from the heads of departments Congress had created.[^ch13-state-founding][^ch13-gwu-executive][^ch13-fjc-judiciary]

The Judiciary Act was one of the First Congress’s most consequential constructions of constitutional language. Passed on September 24, 1789, it created a Supreme Court with a chief justice and five associate justices, a district court in each state, and circuit courts that served as the principal federal trial courts. It allowed state courts to keep concurrent jurisdiction over many federal questions, required federal juries to follow state procedures, and guaranteed trial in the district where the defendant lived. It also sent the justices out on circuit, traveling to hear cases across the country. The federal judiciary was not a finished design. It was a working compromise between the Constitution’s outline and the states’ fear of a distant court system.[^ch13-fjc-judiciary]

The same Congress took up Madison’s amendments and sent 12 to the states. Ten were ratified by December 15, 1791, and became the Bill of Rights. Chapter 12 owns that story, including the bargain that produced it. This chapter notes only that the amendments were part of the First Congress’s work, not a separate settlement after it.[^ch13-archives-bor][^ch13-check-ch12]

By the end of its first session, the government had departments, courts, revenue, and a promised bill of rights. It had no settled answer to how powerful it would be. The fiscal program would test that question first.[^ch13-fjc-judiciary][^ch13-hamilton-report]

### 1.2 Washington’s cabinet and the invention of the presidency

The presidency had no precedents, and Washington’s choices about advice, ceremony, and retirement shaped the office as much as the Constitution did. He took the oath prescribed by Article II and then delivered an address that the law did not require, setting a precedent for the inaugural address. In it he declined any salary beyond actual expenditures and asked Congress to consider amendments under Article V. Those were choices about what the office should look like, made in public and without a model.[^ch13-archives-washington-inaugural][^ch13-mountvernon-inaugural]

Washington gathered the heads of departments as advisers, and the cabinet became the arena where Hamilton and Jefferson fought over policy. The Constitution did not describe the cabinet. It gave the president the power to require written opinions from the principal officer in each executive department, but it did not create a council or say how advice should be organized. Washington’s practice made the cabinet a working institution, and his successors inherited it. The cabinet was, in Lindsay Chervinsky’s phrase, an “entirely extra-legal institution,” created after the fact to meet the realities of governing.[^ch13-gwu-executive][^ch13-mountvernon-cabinet][^ch13-state-genet]

The first president also had to decide how much ceremony the republic could bear. He held weekly levees, received male guests with a formal bow, and was criticized for importing the manners of a court. Washington said he sought “a just medium between too much state and too great familiarity.” The point is not that he got the balance right. The point is that the balance was contested, and that the presidency became a model through choices rather than through a single constitutional rule.[^ch13-mountvernon-levees]

Because the office’s powers were ambiguous, the disputes of the 1790s were partly disputes about the presidency itself: who could advise it, how it could act, and how it should pass power on. That question returns in §6, when a president of one party had to hand power to a president of another.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

### 1.3 Revenue, the census, and the reach of the federal government

The federal government’s first business was money. Congress passed a tariff act on July 4, 1789, imposing duties on imports, and a separate tonnage act on July 20. Those duties became the primary source of federal revenue, and Hamilton’s later reports built on that foundation. The government could not fund a debt, charter a bank, or pay an army without a reliable stream of income.[^ch13-tariff-1789][^ch13-gwu-funding]

The first census made the government’s reach concrete. Congress passed the enumeration act on March 1, 1790, and the count began on August 2. Federal marshals, the first federal law enforcement officers, supervised assistants in each judicial district. The schedule asked for the name of each head of family and counted free White males above and below 16, free White females, all other free persons, and enslaved people. The Constitution’s three-fifths rule then turned the enslaved population into a fraction for representation and taxation. The census made enslaved people visible to the federal government as a fraction of power while denying them any voice in it.[^ch13-mountvernon-census][^ch13-archives-census]

Customs collection, the marshals, and the census created a federal administration that touched ports, households, and the frontier. The government was not only a set of ideas about liberty. It was a working apparatus with offices, salaries, forms, and coercive power.[^ch13-mountvernon-census][^ch13-gwu-funding]

Revenue made the fiscal program possible, and it set up the excise crisis of §3. A government that taxed whiskey would have to enforce the tax. That link between revenue and coercion is the chapter’s spine.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-gwu-funding]

## 2. Hamilton’s system

### 2.1 The Report on Public Credit and funding at par

Hamilton’s first great report proposed to fund the national debt at face value and to assume the states’ debts, converting the war’s IOUs into a permanent national obligation. The Confederation had amassed a large war debt but lacked the power to tax. The Constitution gave Congress the power to levy duties and taxes, and the first tariff supplied revenue. Hamilton argued that sound public credit was a condition of borrowing, prosperity, and national survival.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-gwu-funding]

He delivered the report on January 9, 1790. It ran to roughly 40,000 words. It called for “redemption,” full payment at face value to the current holders of government securities, and for federal assumption of state debts. Hamilton counted $54,124,464.56 in federal debt, of which about $42.4 million was domestic and $11.7 million foreign. He estimated the state debts at about $25 million, which put the combined obligation near $79 million. The exact totals depend on how interest and arrears are counted, but the scale is not in doubt.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-debt-totals][^ch13-wiki-public-credit]

Hamilton held that any compromise on the sanctity of contracts would destroy confidence, and that concentrating capital would encourage investment and economic growth. He rejected “repudiation” and Madison’s “discrimination,” which would have paid original holders and current purchasers separately. Discrimination sounded fair, because many soldiers and farmers had sold their certificates cheaply. Hamilton answered that the buyer had taken the risk, and that the public had promised to pay the bearer. The argument was constitutional as well as financial: Article VI made the debts contracted before the Constitution valid against the new government.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-debt-totals]

Funding at par rewarded speculators who had bought depreciated certificates and angered those who had sold them cheaply. That grievance became the first organized opposition to the government and the first theme of party conflict. It also made the debt a permanent presence in national politics. The government would not pay it off quickly. It would manage it, and the management would shape the government.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-gwu-funding]

### 2.2 Assumption and the Compromise of 1790

Assumption stalled on sectional interest and passed only through the bargain that moved the permanent capital to the Potomac. Southern states, especially Virginia, had largely paid down their war debts and resisted assuming the debts of others. Madison led the opposition and demanded a settlement of accounts first. By June 1790 the question had brought Congress to a standstill.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790]

Jefferson, newly returned as secretary of state, brokered a private agreement. Madison withdrew his opposition to assumption, and Hamilton supported locating the permanent capital on the Potomac. The Residence Bill passed the House on July 9, 1790, and the Assumption Bill on July 26. The bargain was not a public debate about the nature of the union. It was a deal among three men who each wanted something else.[^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790][^ch13-wiki-public-credit]

Assumption consolidated the states’ debts into a national debt and tied creditors to the new government’s success. It also gave the federal government a direct stake in the states’ fiscal affairs, which opponents called “consolidation.” The word described a real transfer of authority. The states had borrowed, and the nation would pay.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790]

The compromise linked the capital’s location to the fiscal program and made the sectional character of the new politics visible. It also deepened the division between Hamilton’s allies and the Virginia opposition. The capital would sit between North and South, and the debt would bind the sections together. Both facts would matter for the rest of the decade.[^ch13-battlefields-compromise1790][^ch13-state-jay]

### 2.3 The bank and the constitutional argument

The bill for a national bank forced the first great constitutional argument over implied powers, and Washington’s decision to sign it set a lasting precedent. Hamilton proposed a Bank of the United States, modeled partly on the Bank of England, with branches, a uniform currency, and a place to deposit and borrow federal money. Congress passed the bill. The House vote was 39 to 20, and Washington signed it on February 25, 1791.[^ch13-house-first-bank]

Jefferson objected that the incorporation of a bank was not among the enumerated powers. The “necessary and proper” clause, he argued, allowed only means without which an enumerated power would be nugatory, not means that were merely convenient. To take a single step beyond the boundaries drawn around Congress’s powers, he wrote, would be to claim “a boundless field of power, no longer susceptible of any definition.”[^ch13-jefferson-bank-opinion]

Hamilton answered that every power vested in a government is sovereign and includes the right to employ all means requisite and fairly applicable to its ends, unless the Constitution precludes them. The bank was a means to carry out the powers to tax, borrow, regulate commerce, and support an army. Washington accepted this reading and signed the bill. The decision did not settle the meaning of the clause, but it gave the federal government a working financial institution and a precedent for later exercises of implied power.[^ch13-hamilton-bank-opinion][^ch13-house-first-bank]

The bank controversy gave the opposition a constitutional vocabulary. It also gave the government a bank. Both mattered. The argument over implied powers would return in disputes over internal improvements, the second Bank of the United States, and the New Deal, and it began here, in a cabinet quarrel over a charter.[^ch13-hamilton-bank-opinion][^ch13-jefferson-bank-opinion]

### 2.4 Manufactures, the excise, and the emerging opposition

Hamilton’s program for manufactures and his excise taxes completed a design for a commercial, fiscal-military state, and the opposition to that design became a party. In December 1791 he submitted his *Report on the Subject of Manufactures*, urging protective tariffs, bounties, and internal improvements to break Britain’s manufacturing hold. Jefferson and his allies feared that manufacturing would create dependent wage workers and undermine the agrarian independence they saw as the basis of republican citizenship.[^ch13-gilder-manufactures]

Hamilton also proposed excise taxes, including the tax on distilled spirits that Congress enacted in 1791, to service the assumed debt. The whiskey excise fell hardest on western farmers who distilled their grain for market. They had little cash and few roads, and whiskey was easier to carry than grain. The tax reached them as a demand for money they did not have.[^ch13-wiki-whiskey][^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey]

Figure 2 plots the funded debt against customs revenue from 1790 to 1801, in nominal dollars, distinguishing the debt stock from annual receipts. Customs duties supplied most federal revenue while the funded debt remained large. As context, one estimate has the debt falling from about 30 percent of gross domestic product in 1790 to about 6 percent by 1811. The figure’s last fiscal observation is 1801; its caption gives the units and the fiscal-year convention, and it distinguishes a stock from a flow.[^ch13-check-figure][^ch13-treasury-debt][^ch13-hsus-revenue][^ch13-cato-debt]

By 1791 an organized opposition had formed around the memory of the Revolution, suspicion of “consolidation,” and the conviction that the fiscal program enriched a monied interest at the expense of farmers and veterans. That opposition was not a preexisting party. It was the product of the program, and it is the subject of §4.[^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-gwu-funding]

## 3. The Northwest war and Whiskey Rebellion

### 3.1 The Ohio Country and the war of the confederacy

The new government inherited a war in the Ohio Country, where a confederation of Native nations fought to hold a boundary the Treaty of Paris had ignored. The 1783 treaty ended the war with Britain, but it allowed the British to remain in the Northwest posts until the United States resolved a land issue with Native nations who had been British allies. The United States treated the treaty as transferring sovereignty. The nations did not.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]

The Chippewa, Ottawa, Potawatomi, Shawnee, Delaware, Miami, and Wyandot formed a federation to halt American incursions, and they rejected unilateral land cessions. Their position rested on the Ohio River as a boundary, not on the purchase of particular tracts. American negotiators wanted treaties with individual nations and specific cessions. The confederacy insisted on a common boundary and common consent.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-myaamia-greenville]

In 1790 a federal expedition under General Josiah Harmar marched into the Miami villages and was roughly handled. The confederacy under Mihšihkinaahkwa, or Little Turtle, and Blue Jacket demonstrated that it could fight the new army to a standstill. The war made the federal government’s military weakness visible and gave the confederacy leverage. The next campaign would be larger and would fail worse.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-myaamia-stclair]

### 3.2 St. Clair’s defeat and Wayne’s Legion

In November 1791 the confederacy destroyed the largest federal army sent west, the worst defeat the United States Army would suffer at Native hands. General Arthur St. Clair, governor of the Northwest Territory, moved from Fort Washington near Cincinnati with about 1,400 men to build Fort Jefferson and then advance against the confederacy.[^ch13-myaamia-stclair]

On the morning of November 4, 1791, a coalition under Little Turtle of the Miami, Blue Jacket of the Shawnee, and Buckongahelas of the Delaware attacked near the Wabash River and drove the Americans from the field. St. Clair’s force suffered 647 killed and 271 wounded, with camp followers among the dead. Other accounts give 632 killed and 264 wounded, or about 630 killed and 260 wounded. The range is wide; the figures here follow the Myaamia Project’s count.[^ch13-myaamia-stclair][^ch13-check-casualties][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]

Washington placed General Anthony Wayne in command of the Legion of the United States and rebuilt the army through disciplined training and a chain of forts. Wayne advanced slowly, avoiding the ambush that had destroyed St. Clair. The defeat forced the government to take the western war seriously and made military capacity a federal project. It also gave the confederacy its greatest victory and its greatest dilemma.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]

### 3.3 Fallen Timbers and the Treaty of Greenville

Wayne’s victory at Fallen Timbers in 1794 broke the confederacy’s military position and produced the Treaty of Greenville, which ceded most of Ohio and opened the Northwest to American settlement. On August 20, 1794, Wayne led the Legion from Fort Roche de Bout. After a five-mile march the Americans met about 1,100 warriors in dense, tornado-felled timber. Wayne’s infantry broke the Native line and drove it from the field. The warriors retreated toward the British Fort Miamis, whose commander refused them entry.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]

The Treaty of Greenville, signed August 3, 1795, ended hostilities and established a boundary beginning at the mouth of the Cuyahoga River and running west and south. In exchange for peace, goods, and annuities, the nations ceded their claims to the lands east and south of the line, including strategic posts and river crossings. The treaty transferred most of present-day Ohio and parts of what became Indiana, Illinois, and Michigan to the United States. It set the pattern of treaty-and-encroachment that would continue across the continent.[^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers]

The Myaamia describe the treaty as an independent agreement made after the military defeat, when their villages faced another winter without shelter or food. The peace was real, but the boundary was soon violated by settlement. The chapter presents the treaty from both sides, because the document and the memory are both evidence of what happened.[^ch13-myaamia-greenville]

Greenville is the chapter’s clearest link between fiscal-military consolidation and dispossession. The same government that funded a debt and chartered a bank also raised an army, marched it into the Ohio Country, and took land from nations that had defeated it twice. The two projects were not separate. They were the same government acting in different theaters.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-avalon-greenville]

### 3.4 In Focus: the Treaty of Greenville

**In Focus.** The treaty opens by naming the parties: “the United States of America, and the tribes of Indians called the Wyandots, Delawares, Shawanees, Ottawas, Chippewas, Pattawatimas, Miamis, Eel Rivers, Weas, Kickapoos, Piankeshaws, and Kaskaskias.” Article 1 declares that “all hostilities shall cease; peace is hereby established, and shall be perpetual.” Article 2 restores prisoners on both sides. Article 3 draws the boundary. In consideration of “the peace now established; of the goods formerly received from the United States; of those now to be delivered; and of the yearly delivery of goods now stipulated to be made hereafter,” the nations “cede and relinquish forever, all their claims to the lands lying eastwardly and southwardly of the general boundary line now described.”[^ch13-avalon-greenville]

Read closely, the document is a peace treaty and a land cession at once. It treats the nations as contracting parties with the power to cede and to receive annuities, even as the United States understood the cession as extinguishing Native title to most of Ohio. The treaty also allowed American free passage “through their country, along the chain of posts hereinbefore mentioned,” converting military posts into a permanent infrastructure of movement. The Myaamia remember the same document as the end of a war they could no longer sustain and the beginning of a peace the United States did not keep. The box lets both readings stand. The treaty text says nothing about a survey, and its enforcement clauses assumed a government able and willing to restrain its own settlers.[^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-myaamia-greenville]

### 3.5 The Whiskey Rebellion and federal authority

When western distillers resisted the excise, Washington called out the militia and marched west, and the government proved it could enforce federal law without firing a shot. The whiskey excise of 1791 provoked violence and intimidation in western Pennsylvania. In July 1794 a federal marshal serving writs was driven off, and more than 500 armed men attacked the fortified home of the tax inspector John Neville.[^ch13-wiki-whiskey][^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey]

On September 25, 1794, Washington issued a proclamation declaring that “the moment is now come” to reduce the refractory “to a due subordination to the law.” He called out the militia of New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia. He rode west at the head of about 13,000 militiamen. The rebel leaders fled, and there was no battle.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]

About 150 men were arrested, only 20 were held for trial, and only two were convicted. Both were eventually pardoned. The episode demonstrated federal authority and divided opinion about whether it had been exercised justly. Republicans read the militia march as an overreach. Federalists read it as a necessary display of law.[^ch13-wiki-whiskey][^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey]

The rebellion connected the fiscal program to the frontier. A tax to service the debt had to be enforced by an army. It also linked the western country to the wider party conflict, because the same government that taxed whiskey also negotiated with Britain and France. The whiskey tax was not a local grievance. It was a test of whether the new government could reach the places it claimed to govern.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]

## 4. France and party

### 4.1 The French Revolution and the Neutrality Proclamation

The war between revolutionary France and Britain forced the United States to define neutrality, and that definition became a partisan issue. After the overthrow of the French monarchy in 1792, revolutionary France went to war with Spain and Britain. The United States had to decide whether its 1778 treaty with France obliged it to fight.[^ch13-state-genet]

On April 22, 1793, Washington issued a proclamation declaring that the duty and interest of the United States required “a conduct friendly and impartial” toward the belligerent powers. He warned citizens that acts against either side would forfeit the protection of the United States. The proclamation did not use the word “neutrality,” but it described a policy that would become the center of American foreign policy.[^ch13-avalon-neutrality]

Jefferson and the Democratic-Republicans sympathized with the French Revolution. Hamilton and the Federalists preferred ties with Britain. Washington steered a neutral course, fearing that war would expose the weak republic to invasion and economic ruin. Neutrality was not a middle position that avoided conflict. It was a policy that created conflict, because each side read it as favoring the other. It became the first foreign-policy issue to organize domestic politics.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-state-jay]

### 4.2 Genêt’s mission and the democratic societies

The French minister Edmond Charles Genêt tested American neutrality and helped the opposition organize, until his own government recalled him. Genêt arrived in Charleston on April 8, 1793, calling himself “Citizen Genêt.” He began issuing privateering commissions against British shipping and recruiting Americans, with the consent of South Carolina’s governor. In Philadelphia, Jefferson told him that outfitting French privateers in American ports violated neutrality.[^ch13-state-genet]

Genêt ignored the warnings and allowed the arming of another privateer, the *Little Democrat*. The cabinet demanded his recall, and he was replaced when the French government changed course. He chose to remain in the United States. The affair ended with Genêt living in the United States, not with a diplomatic rupture.[^ch13-state-genet]

Popular Democratic-Republican societies formed in several cities, corresponding with each other and pressing the French cause. More than 40 societies appeared between 1793 and 1796. Their members included mechanics, artisans, and yeoman farmers, along with lawyers and merchants. Federalists condemned them as factional and foreign-inspired. The societies are the era’s clearest example of organized extra-governmental politics.[^ch13-mountvernon-societies]

The Genêt affair and the societies widened the split between the cabinet factions into a public contest. They gave the emerging opposition a name and a network. They also gave the Federalists a language for attacking organized politics as foreign meddling. Both parties learned from the same episode.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-mountvernon-societies]

### 4.3 The revolution in Saint-Domingue and the limits of American liberty

The revolution in the French colony of Saint-Domingue exposed the contradiction between American claims of liberty and the defense of slavery, and it shaped American policy toward the Atlantic revolutions. Saint-Domingue was the most profitable colony in the Americas, worked by an enslaved majority. A massive rebellion began on August 22, 1791. Toussaint Louverture emerged as its leading figure, and the colony became a multi-sided civil war in which Spanish and British forces also intervened.[^ch13-state-haiti]

American leaders, many of them slaveholders, provided limited aid to suppress the revolt and later supported Toussaint’s forces against Britain. The United States did not recognize Haitian independence until 1862. Jefferson favored limited aid to the planters and feared the example of slave revolt. His sympathy for the French Revolution stopped at the point where it threatened slave property.[^ch13-state-haiti]

In 1793 the fighting and a fire destroyed much of Cap-Français, and refugees fled to Norfolk, Baltimore, Philadelphia, and New York, including enslaved people brought by their owners. The revolution arrived in American ports as a refugee crisis. It also arrived as a political warning. The same revolutionary language that American patriots had used against Britain could be used against American slaveholders.[^ch13-state-haiti]

Saint-Domingue made the era’s revolutionary language dangerous for slaveholders and tied American politics to Caribbean slavery. The revolution’s freedom was won by enslaved people, and American policy sought to protect the slaveholders who had owned them. Their revolution was not a sideshow to the American 1790s. It was one of the reasons the decade’s politics became so intense.[^ch13-state-haiti][^ch13-cfr-wood]

### 4.4 The cabinet quarrel and the invention of party

The disputes over credit, neutrality, and the bank turned two factions inside the government into a party system, even though both sides condemned parties. Hamilton’s program and Washington’s neutrality drew support from commercial and Federalist interests. Opposition came from agrarian and Republican ones. Jefferson resigned as secretary of state at the end of 1793, and the contest moved into the press and the public.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-loc-jefferson-resign]

Partisan newspapers, foreign policy, and the societies organized opinion outside Congress and made national politics a public contest rather than a cabinet argument. Party was an invention of the decade, not a fixed structure. The Constitution did not mention parties. The men who built them did not set out to do so. They organized to defend a program, a foreign policy, and a version of the Revolution.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-mountvernon-societies]

The parties argued about the meaning of “the people” while excluding enslaved people, Native nations, and women from the electorate. Both sides worked to preserve existing hierarchies even as the franchise expanded for White men. The exclusion was not incidental. It was part of what made the new politics possible.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-names]

By 1794 the contest had organized itself around two competing accounts of the Revolution, one Federalist and one Republican, and around the question of how far the federal government should consolidate power. That question dominates §§5–7.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-state-jay]

## 5. Treaties and the 1796 election

### 5.1 Jay’s Treaty and the price of peace

Jay’s Treaty with Britain avoided war but conceded most disputed points and provoked the era’s greatest popular protest. Britain still occupied the northwestern posts promised in 1783, restricted American trade, and seized American ships and impressed sailors. Washington sent Chief Justice John Jay to negotiate, against Republican objection. Jay was the chief justice and a Federalist, and his mission was a gamble.[^ch13-state-jay]

The treaty was signed on November 19, 1794. It gained the surrender of the posts and “most favored nation” commercial status, but it restricted access to the British West Indies and left the boundary, debts, and seizures to arbitration. Jay even conceded that Britain could seize American goods bound for France if it paid for them. The terms were less than the United States had demanded and more than the Republicans could accept.[^ch13-state-jay]

The treaty was immensely unpopular. The Senate approved it 20 to 10 on June 24, 1795, and Washington implemented it as the price of peace and time to consolidate. The treaty divided the country, made Washington’s neutrality policy concrete, and deepened the party conflict. Its unpopularity is the background to the 1796 election.[^ch13-state-jay]

### 5.2 Pinckney’s Treaty and the Mississippi

A second treaty, with Spain, won navigation of the Mississippi and the port of New Orleans, and it showed how European war could produce American advantage. Spain’s defeats in war and its fear of an Anglo-American alignment led it to negotiate. Thomas Pinckney, the American minister to Britain, signed the Treaty of San Lorenzo on October 27, 1795.[^ch13-state-pinckney]

The treaty set the southern boundary at the 31st parallel, granted American ships free navigation of the Mississippi and duty-free transport through New Orleans, and resolved territorial disputes in the present-day Southeast. It also voided Spanish guarantees of military support to Native nations in the disputed regions, which weakened their ability to resist American encroachment. The treaty was popular in the West and South, and it gave the Federalists a rare success outside New England.[^ch13-state-pinckney]

The treaty secured the western country’s access to market and made the union more attractive to western settlers, which Washington cited in his Farewell Address. Together with Jay’s Treaty, it defined the decade’s diplomacy. One treaty bought peace with Britain at a high price. The other opened the Mississippi at a low one.[^ch13-state-pinckney][^ch13-avalon-farewell]

### 5.3 Washington’s Farewell Address

Washington’s Farewell Address announced his retirement and warned against party and foreign entanglement, but its warnings described conflicts already underway. Published on September 19, 1796, the address declined a third term and warned “in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally.” It argued that party opens the door to foreign influence and corruption.[^ch13-avalon-farewell]

Washington urged the nation to extend commercial relations while having “as little political connection as possible” with other nations. He recommended temporary alliances only in emergencies. The advice was not isolationism. It was a preference for commerce over conquest and for time over entanglement.[^ch13-avalon-farewell]

The address condemned parties in the very act of framing the election as a choice between Federalists and Republicans. Its warnings are evidence of the conflict, not proof that the conflict was avoidable. Washington’s retirement removed the one figure both sides claimed and left the succession open. That succession is the subject of §5.4.[^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-cfr-wood]

### 5.4 The election of 1796 and the sectional ticket

The election of 1796 was the first contested presidential succession, and its outcome placed the leaders of the two factions in the same administration. The Federalists backed Vice President John Adams and Thomas Pinckney. The Republicans backed Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr. The campaign was fought through newspapers and correspondence rather than public meetings.[^ch13-loc-1796][^ch13-wiki-1800]

Adams won the presidency with 71 electoral votes to Jefferson’s 68. Thomas Pinckney received 59 votes and Burr 30. Jefferson, as the runner-up, became vice president. The totals mattered less than the arrangement. The outcome was a Federalist president with a Republican vice president, each with a different mandate and a different foreign policy.[^ch13-loc-1796][^ch13-wiki-1800]

The Constitution’s rule of one vote per elector, with no separate designation of president and vice president, produced a divided administration and set up the crisis of 1800. The Twelfth Amendment would later separate the offices. In 1796 the parties were already acting like parties, but the Constitution still assumed that electors would choose individuals.[^ch13-constitution-12th][^ch13-wiki-1800]

The election confirmed that the parties would contest national power and that the constitutional machinery had not anticipated organized opposition. Adams inherited Washington’s cabinet and the French crisis. He also inherited a party that had already learned how to organize against him.[^ch13-state-xyz][^ch13-wiki-1800]

## 6. Quasi-War, repression, and the 1800 election

### 6.1 The XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War

A French demand for bribes turned the diplomatic quarrel with France into an undeclared naval war and a domestic crisis. France resented the Jay Treaty and in 1796 authorized the seizure of American merchant ships. President Adams sent Elbridge Gerry, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, and John Marshall to restore harmony.[^ch13-state-xyz]

The envoys could not meet Foreign Minister Talleyrand directly. Intermediaries demanded a low-interest loan, payment of American claims, and a substantial bribe. The American envoys refused, and the publication of the dispatches, with the intermediaries’ names replaced by the letters W, X, Y, and Z, produced outrage. The letters gave the affair its name and the Federalists their issue.[^ch13-state-xyz]

The United States and France fought an undeclared naval war from 1798 to 1800, and the United States built up its navy. Peace was restored by the Convention of 1800, also called the Treaty of Mortefontaine. The crisis strengthened the Federalists’ argument for military preparedness and for suppressing domestic dissent, which produced the Alien and Sedition Acts.[^ch13-state-xyz][^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

### 6.2 The Alien and Sedition Acts and the assault on dissent

The Federalist Congress used the war scare to restrict immigration and to criminalize criticism of the government, the era’s clearest test of the First Amendment. In 1798 Congress passed four laws. The Naturalization Act extended the residency requirement from five years to 14. The Alien Friends Act allowed the president to deport non-citizens deemed dangerous. The Alien Enemies Act allowed the detention or deportation of male citizens of a hostile nation in wartime. The Sedition Act criminalized “false, scandalous, and malicious writing” against the government and its officers.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

The Sedition Act was used against Republican editors and politicians, and it expired at the end of Adams’s term in 1801. Matthew Lyon, a Republican congressman from Vermont, was the first person tried under the act. He was convicted for writings critical of the government. The prosecutions made martyrs and rallied the opposition.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

The acts connected the foreign crisis to the domestic contest and gave the Republicans their strongest argument: that the Federalists would use national security to destroy political opposition. The argument was not abstract. It was made by editors and congressmen who had been fined, jailed, or threatened.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

### 6.3 The resolutions, Fries’s Rebellion, and the peace of 1800

The Republicans answered with states’-rights resolutions, and a second tax revolt tested the government again before peace with France. Jefferson and Madison drafted the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions of 1798–99, arguing that the Alien and Sedition Acts were unconstitutional and asserting the states’ role in judging federal overreach. The resolutions advanced a theory of state authority that would be used later for different ends.[^ch13-battlefields-alien-sedition]

In 1799 Pennsylvania German farmers resisted a direct federal property tax. John Fries led the resistance, and federal troops suppressed it. Adams later pardoned Fries and the others, choosing conciliation over severity. The pardon was a political decision as much as a legal one. It denied the Federalists a spectacle of punishment and kept the peace in southeastern Pennsylvania.[^ch13-fjc-fries]

Adams reopened negotiations with France and accepted the Convention of 1800, ending the Quasi-War. The decision split his own party, since Hamilton and the High Federalists wanted war. Adams’s peace removed the war as a campaign issue but did not heal the party split. It also spared the republic a war it was unprepared to fight.[^ch13-state-xyz][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

### 6.4 The election of 1800 and the House crisis

The election of 1800 produced a tie between Jefferson and Burr and threw the presidency into the House, where the Federalists nearly blocked the transfer of power. The Republicans ran Jefferson and Burr. The Federalists ran Adams and Charles Cotesworth Pinckney. Jefferson and Burr each received 73 electoral votes; Adams received 65 and Pinckney 64. But every Republican elector voted for both Jefferson and Burr, leaving the two tied.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-wiki-1800]

Under the Constitution, the tied election went to the House, where each state delegation cast one vote. Federalists opposed to Jefferson maneuvered to elect Burr or to prevent a choice. The contest went to 36 ballots before Jefferson won on February 17, 1801, with 10 states to Burr’s four. The crisis brought threats of violence and fears of dissolution.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-loc-peaceful]

The election exposed the Constitution’s failure to anticipate party tickets. It also showed that the parties themselves could supply the mechanism of settlement. The Federalists who abstained did not do so out of affection for Jefferson. They chose a president they disliked over a constitutional breakdown they feared.[^ch13-loc-peaceful][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

The election was decided, but the rules that produced the tie were not yet fixed. The Twelfth Amendment would separate the presidential and vice-presidential votes. It was ratified in 1804, in time for the next presidential election.[^ch13-constitution-12th][^ch13-wiki-1800]

### 6.5 The transfer of power

Jefferson’s inauguration in March 1801 was the first transfer of national power between rival parties, and it set a precedent that was not inevitable. Jefferson took office on March 4, 1801, and the Federalists surrendered power without a coup. His inaugural address sought to calm the country: “We are all republicans. We are all federalists.”[^ch13-loc-peaceful][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

Jefferson preserved the Bank of the United States, honored but paid down the national debt, and shrank the army and navy rather than abolishing them. He conciliated moderate Federalists while pushing his own program. The moderation was strategic as well as temperamental. It kept the government running and kept the opposition divided.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

The transfer rested on a politics that was opening to more White men but not to women, free Black people, or the enslaved. The calm at the top was purchased in part at the expense of those at the bottom. That limit belongs in the story, because the peaceful transfer is often celebrated without it.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-names]

The machinery built in the 1790s—a funded debt, a bank, a revenue system, a frontier army, a party press—passed to the opposition. Chapter 14 owns what Jefferson did with it. This chapter ends with the transfer, because the transfer is the decade’s last great invention.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-check-ch14]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

The fiscal program built a working national state. Funding, assumption, the bank, and the excise gave the government revenue, credit, and a financial institution, and they created the first organized opposition.[^ch13-hamilton-report][^ch13-wiki-public-credit][^ch13-house-first-bank]

Consolidation rested on dispossession. The war for the Ohio Country and the Treaty of Greenville transferred most of Ohio to the United States and set the pattern for later treaty-and-encroachment.[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-myaamia-greenville]

Federal authority was enforced. The militia march against the Whiskey Rebellion showed that the government could and would enforce federal law, and it divided opinion about how far it should.[^ch13-mountvernon-whiskey][^ch13-wiki-whiskey]

The Atlantic revolutions shaped American politics. Neutrality, the Genêt affair, and the revolution in Saint-Domingue turned foreign policy into domestic conflict and exposed the contradiction between liberty and slavery.[^ch13-state-genet][^ch13-state-haiti]

A party system was invented. Despite near-universal condemnation of parties, organized contest emerged around credit, neutrality, and the meaning of the Revolution.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-avalon-farewell]

The transfer of 1801 was contested. The election of 1800 produced a tie and a House crisis before Jefferson took office.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-wiki-1800]

### 7.2 What is open

How much economic interest drove the parties is open. Were the Federalists and Republicans chiefly the agents of competing economic interests, as Beard argued, or of ideas about republicanism, corruption, and the Revolution, as the republican synthesis held?[^ch13-beard-origins][^ch13-s-usih-appleby][^ch13-check-sources]

Whether the parties were inevitable or contingent is open. Did the Constitution’s structure produce parties, or did particular choices—assumption, the bank, neutrality, the Sedition Act—create them? The chapter holds the contingency open.[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-sources]

How to weigh the Federalist achievement is open. Did the Federalists build a durable state that their opponents inherited, or did they overreach and destroy themselves, as Elkins and McKitrick argue?[^ch13-goodreads-elkins][^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty][^ch13-check-sources]

Whether consolidation was inseparable from dispossession is open. Could the fiscal-military state have been built without the western war and the land cessions, or were they the same project?[^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-myaamia-greenville][^ch13-check-sources]

What the archives can and cannot show is open. Enslaved people, Native nations, and women appear mainly through the documents of those who governed, owned, or excluded them. The treasury reports and party press are the records of the winners.[^ch13-state-haiti][^ch13-check-names]

How to read the transfer of 1801 is open. Was it a genuine democratic advance, or the consolidation of a White male democracy purchased by excluding others?[^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the 1790s created a more capable national government and, at the same time, an organized politics that excluded most of the people it governed. Neither the fiscal achievement nor the exclusions cancels the other.[^ch13-check-sources]

Charles A. Beard’s *Economic Origins of Jeffersonian Democracy* (1915) read the Federalist–Republican struggle as a conflict between agrarianism and fluid capital and extended the class-conflict thesis of his work on the Constitution. It is the origin of the chapter’s question about whose interests the fiscal program served.[^ch13-beard-origins][^ch13-britannica-beard]

Stanley Elkins and Eric McKitrick’s *The Age of Federalism* (1993) treats the 1790s as a contest over the meaning of the Revolution, driven by political ideas and by the Federalists’ own political ineptitude as much as by interest. It is the standard modern synthesis of the decade.[^ch13-goodreads-elkins][^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty]

Lance Banning and others argued that the Republicans opposed a corrupt “consolidated” government in the name of a classical republican tradition. Joyce Appleby answered that the Republicans were the party of a new liberal, commercial society rather than of classical virtue. The debate over what the parties believed is the chapter’s central historiographical problem.[^ch13-s-usih-appleby][^ch13-check-sources]

Gordon Wood narrates the era as the rise of egalitarian democracy for ordinary White men. His critics, including Nancy Isenberg and John L. Brooke, argue that this consensus narrative homogenizes Americans and marginalizes women, slavery, and dispossession. The chapter presents both.[^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty][^ch13-cfr-wood]

Read together, these positions say that the 1790s built a fiscal-military state, invented a party system, and extended political voice to White men while defending slavery and taking Native land. The chapter presents the debate as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch13-wiki-empire-liberty][^ch13-s-usih-appleby][^ch13-check-sources]

## Legacies

The 1790s created a national government that could tax, borrow, charter a bank, raise an army, and enforce its laws, and it did so while taking Native land and defending slavery. The funded debt, the bank, the revenue system, and the frontier army passed to Jefferson in 1801 and became the instruments of later expansion.[^ch13-house-first-bank][^ch13-nps-fallen-timbers][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]

The decade also invented the party system. Washington warned against the spirit of party, yet the disputes over credit, neutrality, and the Sedition Act produced organized national parties and a partisan press that outlasted the founders. The election of 1800 proved that power could change hands without violence, but only after a constitutional crisis that the Twelfth Amendment tried to prevent.[^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-constitution-12th]

And it left the chapter’s central contradiction. The government that claimed to secure the blessings of liberty rested on the enslavement of people it counted for representation, on the dispossession of Native nations, and on the exclusion of women and the propertyless from the political nation. The United States did not recognize Haitian independence until 1862, in part because the example of a successful slave revolution was too dangerous for a slaveholding republic.[^ch13-state-haiti][^ch13-cfr-wood][^ch13-check-slavery]

## Study Questions

1. Read the National Archives account of Washington’s inauguration and Peter Henriques’s essay on the oath. What does the record establish about the oath, and what does the dispute over “So help me God” show about how historical detail is added after the fact?
2. Using Hamilton’s *Report on Public Credit*, identify his argument for funding at par. Why did Madison object, and what did the “discrimination” alternative propose?
3. Read Jefferson’s and Hamilton’s opinions on the bank. Where exactly do they disagree about the “necessary and proper” clause, and which reading has shaped later American governance?
4. The Compromise of 1790 linked assumption to the capital’s location. What does that bargain reveal about how the new government made decisions, and who paid the price?
5. Read the Treaty of Greenville. What does it cede, what does it promise in return, and what does the document not say about enforcement?
6. Compare the Myaamia account of Greenville with the treaty text. What does each source establish, and what does each leave out?
7. Washington’s proclamation on the Whiskey Rebellion invoked his duty “to take care that the laws be faithfully executed.” How did the government’s response to the excise compare with its response to the Alien and Sedition Acts?
8. The Neutrality Proclamation declared “a conduct friendly and impartial.” Why did a policy of neutrality provoke so much conflict?
9. Read the Office of the Historian’s account of the Haitian Revolution and the United States. How did American leaders reconcile their revolutionary language with their defense of slavery?
10. The election of 1800 tied Jefferson and Burr. Given the constitutional rules in place, what options did the House have, and how did the crisis produce the Twelfth Amendment?
11. Use Table 1. Which interpretation best explains the emergence of party in the 1790s, and what does each position leave unexplained?
12. The contingency question: if Adams had not made peace with France, or if the House had chosen Burr, how might the 1790s have ended? What evidence would you need to answer?

> *A closing note.* The decade that began on a balcony in New York ended with a peaceful transfer of power in Washington, and the distance between those two moments measures what the new government had built: a funded debt, a bank, a revenue system, a frontier army, a party press, and a habit of organized contest. It also measures what it had not settled. The government that could enforce an excise tax on western farmers had made peace with the confederacy by taking its land, had built its fiscal power on a debt that counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person, and had answered criticism with a sedition law. The men who warned against the spirit of party were themselves its founders. What they created was not a finished republic but a working one, capable of both the transfer of 1801 and the exclusions that transfer preserved.[^ch13-millercenter-peaceful][^ch13-avalon-farewell][^ch13-avalon-greenville][^ch13-check-slavery]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on March 4, 1801, when Thomas Jefferson walked to the Capitol and took the oath as the third president, the first to reach the office by defeating an incumbent party. This chapter has followed the machinery from Washington’s balcony through the fiscal program, the western war, the Atlantic crisis, and the election of 1800. Chapter 14 takes up the government once Jefferson held it—the program of limited government, the Louisiana Purchase, the embargo, and the War of 1812—and asks whether a republic of restraint could expand without enlarging the coercive power it criticized.[^ch13-check-ch14][^ch13-millercenter-peaceful]
