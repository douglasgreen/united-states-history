# Appendix B. Tables and Data

*Stage 3 researched outline — November 22, 2026. Page allowance: 14 pages. Appendices. Source: [README](../README.md), Appendix B. This file specifies eight datasets, their sources, definitions, and production checks. It is not the data and not a draft of the appendix.*

## 1. How Appendix B Works

### 1.1 Purpose, numbering, and limits

- Appendix B supplies eight numbered reference tables. Each table may have lettered panels, but a panel is not a new table number. The stable display numbers are App. B, Table 1 through App. B, Table 8.
- The tables support comparisons that chapters make in passing: population scale, election returns, territorial cessions, war mobilization, immigration, slavery, economic benchmarks, and the New Deal landscape. They do not replace the direct sources cited in the chapters.
- Appendix B does not publish a ninth “Tables in the Chapters” crosswalk. That crosswalk remains a README production record, as the plan requires. The crosswalk is summarized in §10.1 for the editor, not for the reader.
- A table is finished only when it has a caption, a source line, definitions for every column, notes on breaks and missing data, and a textual reference from the appendix prose. A list of sources is not a table.
- The appendix is planned at 14 text pages. That allowance accommodates the eight tables at a compact reference setting. The row caps below are chosen to fit that setting; if a table cannot be set legibly within the cap, request a further reallocation rather than cutting definitions or source lines.

### 1.2 Common conventions

- **Time.** Label every date as a census date, calendar year, fiscal year, or observation year. Colonial figures are annual estimates, not censuses. Election returns are election-year returns. Immigration admissions are federal fiscal years. War costs are for the spending period stated in the source.
- **Geography.** Use present-day U.S. boundaries unless a row says otherwise. For historical rows, give the boundaries and jurisdictions in force at the time. Do not compare a colonial “population of the colonies” with the 2020 resident population without saying that the geographic base changed.
- **Population.** Use resident population. Census counts are not adjusted for underenumeration unless the row says “adjusted.” Do not mix de jure and de facto populations, or state and national totals from different vintages.
- **Money.** Use nominal dollars of the stated year. Add constant-dollar figures only with the named index and base year. Do not combine war appropriations, outlays, veterans’ costs, and interest in one “cost” column.
- **Rates.** Give the numerator and denominator. Percentages are rounded to one decimal unless the source reports more precision. A change in percentage points is not a percent change.
- **Race and status.** Keep the historical categories and explain them. “White,” “Black,” “Negro,” “colored,” “free,” and “enslaved” do not mean the same thing in every source or decade. Do not use “Black” and “enslaved” interchangeably.
- **Missing values.** Use “n.a.” for not available, “—” for not applicable, and “0” for a true zero. Do not leave a blank cell; a blank cell invites a reader to infer zero.
- **Rounding and arithmetic.** Follow the source’s rounding. If a total is calculated, say so in the source line or a note. Do not force a column to add when the source’s components are independently rounded.
- **Source lines.** Give the responsible institution or author, the exact title, the series or table number, the observation years, the URL or DOI, and the access date. “Compiled for this volume” is acceptable only when the transformation is documented in a production note.
- **Rights.** Official statistical tables and treaty texts are generally public domain in the United States. Quoted captions, modern maps, and third-party photographs are not automatically public domain. Appendix B uses no images; if a table reproduces a third-party chart or facsimile, the register and rights review must be updated first.

### 1.3 Source hierarchy and verification status

- Official statistical series come first: the Census Bureau, the Office of Management and Budget, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of Defense, the Bureau of Land Management, and the National Archives.
- The core historical compendium for several tables is the Census Bureau’s 1975 Bicentennial edition of *Historical Statistics of the United States, Colonial Times to 1970*. It supplies the colonial population series, the long-run election returns, the slavery and finance series, and several communications series. Its chapter definitions are part of the evidence, not background.[^app-b-hsus-1975]
- Scholarly compilations and peer-reviewed articles come second. They are used for definitions, corrections, and contested estimates, not as substitutes for the official series when an official series exists.
- Secondary summaries and reference entries are leads. They may establish a date or a name, but they do not support a numerical claim in the finished table.
- “Consulted” means the source was opened and read for this outline. “Discovered” means the item was located but not read. This outline marks unread items and does not present their contents as verified.
- Citations in this outline are source targets as well as evidence records. A note may identify a table that was consulted for its definitions and series while leaving row-level verification for drafting. The limitations field distinguishes the two; no table value should be treated as checked merely because it appears in this outline.
- The values printed in this outline are benchmarks for drafting. They are not the final table values and must be rechecked against the cited locator before the table is called drafted.

## 2. Table 1. Population and Urbanization, 1630–2020

### 2.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 1 shows the scale of population growth, the changing share of the population classified as urban, the regional redistribution of the population, and the growth of the largest cities.
- The table supports a claim the chapters make repeatedly: the United States did not grow by adding equal increments to a fixed society. It grew by incorporating new regions, reclassifying settlement, and moving people across a continent. The urban share crossed 50 percent between 1910 and 1920, and the South and West gained share after 1950.
- The table must not imply that colonial estimates are censuses, that “urban” means the same thing in 1790 and 2020, or that city boundaries are stable.

### 2.2 Table design

- **Panel A. National population and urban share.** Benchmark years: 1630, 1700, 1750, 1780, 1790, 1820, 1860, 1900, 1920, 1950, 1970, 2000, 2010, and 2020. Columns: year; total population; White population; Black population; enslaved population where the source separates it; urban population; urban share.
- **Panel B. Regions.** Years: 1790, 1860, 1900, 1950, 2000, and 2020. Columns: Northeast, Midwest, South, and West, in counts and shares of the national total. Use the modern Census regions consistently and say that they are applied retrospectively.
- **Panel C. Largest cities.** Years: 1790, 1860, 1920, and 2020. Columns: rank, city, population, and city share of the national population. Use city-proper boundaries, not metropolitan areas, and note that annexation affects the series.
- **Row cap.** About 14 national rows, six regional rows, and 20 city rows, for roughly 40 data rows. Target: 1.5 to 2 pages.
- **Captions and notes.** The caption must state that colonial figures are estimates and that urban definitions changed. A note must identify the 1950 and 2020 definition breaks.

### 2.3 Source series and exact locators

- **Colonial estimates.** *Historical Statistics of the United States, Colonial Times to 1970*, series Z 1–19, “Estimated Population of American Colonies, 1610–1780,” p. 1168. The table gives 1630 as 4,646; 1700 as 250,888; 1750 as 1,170,760; and 1780 as 2,780,369. The 1780 enslaved total is 675,420; the 1760 total is 459,822; the 1750 total is 325,806.[^app-b-hsus-z1-19]
- **Decennial totals, 1790–1970.** *Historical Statistics*, series A 1–5, p. 8. The series gives 1790 as 3,929,214; 1860 as 31,443,321; 1900 as 75,994,575; 1920 as 106,021,537; 1950 as 151,325,798; and 1970 as 203,302,031.[^app-b-hsus-a1-5]
- **Race and enslaved population, 1790–1860.** *Historical Statistics*, series A 91–104, pp. 13–14 and footnote 1. The footnote reports the total slave population for each census; the table prints the race totals by sex.[^app-b-hsus-a91-104]
- **Urban and rural population, 1790–1970.** *Historical Statistics*, series A 57–72, pp. 11–12. The series gives urban and rural population by size of place and carries the definition notes for the 1940 and 1950 urban concepts.[^app-b-hsus-a57-72]
- **Regions, 1790–1970.** *Historical Statistics*, series A 172–194, pp. 22–23. The table gives population by region, race, residence, age, and nativity.[^app-b-hsus-a172-194]
- **2020 totals and regions.** U.S. Census Bureau, “2020 Census Apportionment Results,” Table E. The 2020 resident population is 331,449,281. The regional totals are Northeast 57,609,148; Midwest 68,985,454; South 126,266,107; West 78,588,572.[^app-b-census-2020-apportionment]
- **2020 urban share.** Census Bureau, “2020 Census Urban Areas Facts.” The urban population is 265,149,027, or 80.0 percent; the rural population is 66,300,254, or 20.0 percent; the census delineated 2,611 urban areas.[^app-b-census-2020-urban]
- **2020 cities.** Census Bureau, “Decennial Census Historical Facts,” 2020 section. The five largest cities are New York City 8,804,190; Los Angeles 3,898,747; Chicago 2,746,388; Houston 2,304,580; and Phoenix 1,608,139.[^app-b-census-2020-fast-facts]
- **Historical cities, 1790–1990.** Campbell Gibson, “Population of the 100 Largest Cities and Other Urban Places in the United States: 1790 to 1990,” Working Paper POP-WP027, Tables 2–26. The report gives the largest urban places at each census and explains annexation and boundary effects.[^app-b-gibson-1998]

### 2.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Urban share.** Divide the urban population by the total resident population and multiply by 100. Use the urban population published for that census, not a reclassification of earlier censuses. For 2020, use the 2020 urban-area criteria. For 1950–2010, use the published urban population under the definition in force; note that the 1950 definition introduced urbanized areas.
- **Regional shares.** Divide each regional total by the national total. The regional groups are the four modern Census regions. A state that moved from one region to another in the census’s classification must be noted; do not silently redraw the regions.
- **City share.** Divide city population by the national resident population. City boundaries are those used at the census date. Do not substitute metropolitan-area population for a city row without labeling the change.
- **Race columns.** For the colonial period, the source’s “White and Negro” total is not the same as the later “White” and “Black” categories. For 1790–1860, “Black” includes free and enslaved Black people; the enslaved column is a subset. Do not add the enslaved column to the Black column.
- **Rounding.** The colonial figures are rounded to the nearest person in the source but rest on estimates. Do not print them to a precision the source does not support. For the 2020 figures, print the census counts as published.
- **Arithmetic check.** Every panel total must reconcile to the published national total, or the note must explain why it does not. The 1970 census, for example, left 23,372 persons unallocated by race in the published table; the race columns therefore do not sum to the total.[^app-b-hsus-a1-5]

### 2.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Colonial estimates are reconstructions.** The Z 1–19 series was compiled from colonial officials’ reports, tax lists, militia rolls, and estimates. The 17th-century enslaved figures are especially uncertain; the compilers themselves called them “doubtlessly too low.”[^app-b-hsus-z1-19]
- **Census undercounts are not uniform.** The 1870 census is notoriously incomplete in the South, and undercounts vary by race, region, and age. A growth rate calculated across 1860–1870 without a note will overstate or understate change depending on the group.
- **“Urban” is an administrative category.** The threshold was 8,000 in 1874, 4,000 in 1880, and 2,500 in 1910; the 1950 census added urbanized areas; the 2020 census uses housing-unit density and a 5,000-person or 2,000-housing-unit threshold. A series that treats “urban” as a natural category will mislead.[^app-b-census-2020-urban]
- **Regional totals hide state and local variation.** The South’s share can rise while particular states lose population. The West’s share can rise while rural counties decline. The table is a national and regional summary, not a settlement map.
- **City populations hide metropolitan growth.** New York City’s 2020 population is smaller than its metropolitan area; Houston’s city boundaries include vast annexed territory. City-proper comparisons are useful only if the boundary rule is stated.
- **The 2010 urban share is reported in two ways.** The 2010 census reported 80.7 percent urban. Later 2020 reclassification of 2010 territory can produce a different figure in a backcast. Choose one vintage and label it; do not print both without explanation.

### 2.6 Open checks before drafting

- Editorial check — decide whether Table 1 includes Puerto Rico and the island areas. The 2020 total of 331,449,281 is the 50 states and the District of Columbia; Puerto Rico is separate. If the table includes the territories, the total and the regional panel must say so.
- Editorial check — resolve the 2010 urban-share discrepancy (80.7 percent in the 2010 census, 80.3 percent in some later tables) and the treatment of 2010 urban areas under 2020 criteria.
- Editorial check — confirm the 1790 national total. The Census Bureau’s headline is 3,929,214; some compilations print 3,929,326 after corrections. Choose the census publication and note the variant.
- Editorial check — choose the city-boundary basis for 2020. Do not compare a 2020 city-proper figure with a 1920 metropolitan figure.
- Editorial check — verify the 1970 race-column footnote and decide whether to print “not allocated” as a separate row or in a table note.

## 3. Table 2. Presidential Elections, 1789–2024

### 3.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 2 gives the electoral vote, the reported popular vote where a comparable national record exists, and a turnout estimate for each presidential election.
- The table supports three claims: the Electoral College and state election rules shaped outcomes; the popular vote is not a complete national series before the 1820s; and turnout is a constructed rate, not a raw count.
- The table must not invent a national popular vote for elections in which many states chose electors by legislature or did not report a popular vote.

### 3.2 Table design

- **Rows.** One row for each presidential election from 1789 through 2024, 59 elections.
- **Columns.** Election year; winner; party; winner’s electoral votes; runner-up; runner-up’s electoral votes; other electoral votes; winner’s reported popular vote and share; runner-up’s reported popular vote and share; turnout rate and denominator series; notes.
- **Panel A. 1789–1824.** Electoral votes and state selection methods. Popular vote is “n.a.” except for the states that held popular elections in 1824; label that figure “partial.”
- **Panel B. 1828–1900.** Reported popular vote with coverage notes. Some states still had incomplete returns or legislature-chosen electors.
- **Panel C. 1904–2024.** Modern official returns. Use the House Clerk’s series from 1920 and the FEC’s official 2024 results.
- **Row cap.** 59 rows. Target: 2.5 to 3 pages in a compact two-column setting.
- **Notes.** Flag the 1876, 1888, 2000, and 2016 electoral-vote/popular-vote splits; flag faithless electors; flag the 1824 partial popular vote.

### 3.3 Source series and exact locators

- **1789–1968.** *Historical Statistics*, series Y 79–83, pp. 1073–81. The table gives electoral and popular vote by party. Its general note states that “the record of popular votes is inadequate to explain the elections until after 1824.”[^app-b-hsus-y79-83]
- **1920–2024.** U.S. House of Representatives, Office of the Clerk, *Election Statistics, 1920 to Present*. The Clerk collects official vote counts from the states and publishes the presidential and congressional returns.[^app-b-house-election-statistics]
- **2024 official results.** Federal Election Commission, *Official 2024 Presidential General Election Results*. The FEC records 312 electoral votes for Donald J. Trump and 226 for Kamala D. Harris; the popular vote is 77,302,580 (49.80 percent) for Trump and 75,017,613 (48.32 percent) for Harris.[^app-b-fec-2024]
- **2024 Electoral College.** National Archives and Records Administration, “2024 Electoral College Results.” The certificate count is 312–226, with 538 total and 270 needed to win; Maine and Nebraska split their votes.[^app-b-nara-2024]
- **Turnout.** University of Florida Election Lab, “United States Voter Turnout” and “National Turnout Rates Graph.” The Lab defines turnout as ballots counted divided by the voting-eligible population. Pre-1948 rates are Walter Dean Burnham’s; 1948 to the present are Michael McDonald’s.[^app-b-uf-election-lab]
- **2024 turnout.** Election Lab, “2024 General Election Turnout,” estimates dated March 2, 2026. The numerator is total ballots counted, not votes for president; the denominator is the voting-eligible population.[^app-b-uf-2024]
- **Pre-1824 selection.** *Historical Statistics*, series Y 1–26, “Methods of Electing Presidential Electors, 1788–1836,” pp. 1071–72, for the state-by-state switch from legislative selection to popular election. The series is a necessary check on any claim that a national popular vote existed in 1789.[^app-b-hsus-y1-26]

### 3.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Popular vote.** Sum the state returns only where the source reports them. Do not estimate missing states from neighboring states or from the Electoral College. If a state chose electors by legislature, its popular vote is not zero; it is “n.a.”
- **Popular-vote share.** Divide the candidate’s reported votes by the total reported presidential votes in the states with returns. If the total is incomplete, label the share “partial” and do not compare it with a complete national share.
- **Electoral vote.** Use the official count of votes cast, not the number of electors appointed. Note faithless electors and any vote that Congress rejected. The 1872 count, for example, rejected both sets of Louisiana electors and the Arkansas vote.[^app-b-hsus-y79-83]
- **Turnout.** Divide ballots counted by the voting-eligible population and multiply by 100. Do not use the voting-age population, registered voters, or the adult citizen population as if they were the same denominator. The Election Lab’s VEP subtracts noncitizens and disenfranchised felons and adds eligible overseas voters at the national level.[^app-b-uf-election-lab]
- **Turnout series break.** Pre-1948 turnout is Burnham’s reconstruction; 1948 to the present is McDonald’s. The two series are not a single homogeneous series. Label the break in the table note.
- **Third parties.** Include third-party candidates only when the source reports them. Do not lump all “other” votes into a major-party total. A note should give the largest third-party vote where it matters.
- **2024.** Use the FEC’s official state returns for the popular vote and the NARA certificate count for the electoral vote. Do not use media calls or network projections.

### 3.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **The electorate was restricted.** Before the Fifteenth Amendment, Black men were enslaved or disenfranchised in much of the country; before the Nineteenth, women could not vote; property, tax, and citizenship qualifications excluded many White men. A turnout rate is a rate among the eligible, not among the adult population.
- **Popular-vote coverage is incomplete.** In 1824, six states had no popular vote for president. In 1789, New York did not cast an electoral vote because the legislature failed to agree. A national popular-vote total before 1828 is a construct, not a contemporaneous count.[^app-b-hsus-y79-83]
- **The 1876 election was contested.** The Compromise of 1877 resolved the dispute, but the table should flag the competing slates and the congressional commission rather than print a smooth result.
- **The 2020 election was contested.** Official certifications and court decisions, not media projections, are the record. The table should use the certified results and note the contest.
- **Turnout denominators are estimates.** The VEP depends on estimates of noncitizens, felons, and overseas voters. A one-point difference between sources may be a denominator difference, not a mobilization difference.
- **The 2024 turnout figure is provisional.** The Election Lab’s estimates may be revised as states report final ballots. Do not freeze a number that the source labels an estimate without saying so.

### 3.6 Open checks before drafting

- Editorial check — reconcile the 1824 popular-vote totals in *Historical Statistics*, the American Presidency Project, and the state returns. Decide whether to print a number, a range, or “partial.”
- Editorial check — confirm the 2024 turnout rate from the Election Lab data file. Do not compute it from FEC total votes without matching the denominator and the ballot-count definition.
- Editorial check — verify the 1876 and 2020 electoral-vote counts against the *Congressional Record* and NARA, not against a news summary.
- Editorial check — decide whether the 1789–1824 elections belong in the same table as the modern elections or in a separate panel with a shorter column set.
- Editorial check — confirm the number of faithless electors in each election and whether to include a separate column or a table note.

## 4. Table 3. Territory, Treaties, and Jurisdiction, 1763–1917

### 4.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 3 lists the instruments by which the United States acquired, claimed, annexed, leased, or occupied territory between 1763 and 1917.
- The table supports a claim about legal form: continental expansion included cessions, purchases, annexations, and boundary settlements, not only purchases. Overseas expansion included a treaty cession, an annexation by joint resolution, a lease-like canal zone, and a purchase.
- The table must distinguish sovereignty from control, and it must not treat Indigenous lands as empty or unowned before a European or American instrument.

### 4.2 Table design

- **Rows.** 1763 Treaty of Paris (imperial baseline); 1783 Treaty of Paris; 1803 Louisiana Purchase; 1819 Florida cession; 1845 Texas annexation; 1846 Oregon Treaty; 1848 Mexican Cession; 1853 Gadsden Purchase; 1867 Alaska Purchase; 1898 Hawaii annexation; 1898 Spanish treaty; 1903 Panama Canal Zone; 1917 Danish West Indies purchase.
- **Columns.** Instrument and date; counterparty; legal form; area (land, water, and total in square miles); nominal cost and what it covered; sovereignty or control status; termination or current status; notes.
- **Panel A. Continental acquisitions, 1763–1867.** Include the 1763 baseline in a shaded row labeled “not a U.S. acquisition.”
- **Panel B. Overseas and insular acquisitions, 1898–1917.** Include Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines, the Canal Zone, and the Danish West Indies.
- **Row cap.** 13 rows. Target: 1 to 1.5 pages.
- **Notes.** Identify which figures are land area, water area, or total area. Identify which costs are cash, assumed claims, or debt assumption.

### 4.3 Source series and exact locators

- **Continental area and cost.** Bureau of Land Management, *Public Land Statistics 2020*, Table 1-1, “Acquisition of the Public Domain, 1781–1867,” p. 3. The table gives acres of land, water, and total area, the percentage of total U.S. area, and cost. Key rows: Louisiana Purchase 529,911,680 total acres and $23,213,568; Florida cession 46,144,640 acres and $6,674,057; Oregon Compromise 183,386,240 acres and no cost; Mexican Cession 338,680,960 acres and $16,295,149; purchase from Texas 78,926,720 acres and $15,496,448; Gadsden Purchase 18,988,800 acres and $10,000,000; Alaska Purchase 378,242,560 acres and $7,200,000.[^app-b-blm-pls-2020]
- **1783 boundaries.** Treaty of Paris, September 3, 1783, Avalon Project transcription. The treaty recognizes the United States and defines its boundaries; it is the legal baseline for the public-domain cessions that follow.[^app-b-treaty-paris-1783]
- **1819 Florida.** Adams-Onís Treaty, February 22, 1819, Avalon Project transcription. Spain cedes East and West Florida; the United States assumes up to $5 million in claims.[^app-b-treaty-florida-1819]
- **1846 Oregon.** Oregon Treaty, June 15, 1846, Avalon Project transcription. The boundary is the 49th parallel; the treaty ends joint occupancy but does not involve a purchase price.[^app-b-treaty-oregon-1846]
- **1848 Mexican Cession.** Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, February 2, 1848, National Archives milestone page. The United States pays $15 million and assumes up to $3.25 million in claims; the cession is more than 525,000 square miles.[^app-b-treaty-guadalupe-1848]
- **1853 Gadsden.** Gadsden Purchase Treaty, December 30, 1853, Avalon Project transcription. The United States pays $10 million for about 30,000 square miles; the Senate amended the treaty.[^app-b-treaty-gadsden-1853]
- **1867 Alaska.** National Archives, “Check for the Purchase of Alaska.” The United States agrees to pay Russia $7.2 million for 586,412 square miles; the check is dated 1868.[^app-b-alaska-1867]
- **1845 Texas.** Joint Resolution for Annexing Texas, March 1, 1845, Texas State Library transcription; U.S. Senate Journal, 1845–46. The joint resolution annexes Texas and provides that the United States assumes Texas’s public debt up to $10 million. This is not a cash purchase; the 1850 settlement in the BLM table is a separate transaction.[^app-b-texas-1845]
- **1898 Hawaii.** Joint Resolution to Provide for Annexing the Hawaiian Islands, July 7, 1898, National Archives milestone page. The resolution annexes Hawaii; there is no purchase price. Native Hawaiian sovereignty is contested, and the resolution is not a treaty.[^app-b-hawaii-1898]
- **1898 Spanish treaty.** Treaty of Peace between the United States and Spain, December 10, 1898, Avalon Project transcription. Spain cedes Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines; the United States pays $20 million; Cuba is not ceded.[^app-b-treaty-spain-1898]
- **1903 Panama.** Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty, November 18, 1903, Avalon Project transcription. The United States pays $10 million and an annuity of $250,000 for a 10-mile-wide canal zone; the treaty grants control “in perpetuity,” not sovereignty over Panama.[^app-b-panama-1903]
- **1917 Danish West Indies.** Convention for the Cession of the Danish West Indies, August 4, 1916, 39 Stat. 1706; State Department *Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917*, document 881. The United States pays $25 million in gold; the islands are transferred on March 31, 1917.[^app-b-danish-1917]
- **Island areas.** Census Bureau, “State Area Measurements and Internal Point Coordinates,” for Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Virgin Islands. The Philippines and the Canal Zone require treaty or State Department area figures because they are not current states or territories.[^app-b-census-area]

### 4.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Area.** The BLM table gives acres. Divide by 640 to convert to square miles. Keep land, water, and total area separate. Alaska is the clearest case: the common 586,412-square-mile figure and the BLM total of 378,242,560 acres, or 591,004 square miles, differ because they use different area definitions and boundary surveys.[^app-b-blm-pls-2020]
- **Cost.** Use nominal dollars of the instrument date. Do not inflation-adjust in this table. If a constant-dollar column is added, name the index and base year.
- **What the cost covers.** The Louisiana figure of $23,213,568 includes $15 million principal plus assumed claims; do not print $15 million as the total. The Florida figure of $6,674,057 is assumed claims, not cash paid to Spain. The Texas figure of $15,496,448 in the 1850 settlement is not the 1845 annexation price.[^app-b-blm-pls-2020]
- **Legal form.** Define “cession,” “purchase,” “annexation,” “lease,” and “occupation.” The 1845 Texas joint resolution is an annexation, not a treaty. The 1898 Hawaii joint resolution is an annexation by domestic law, not a treaty. The Canal Zone is a grant of control, not a transfer of sovereignty.
- **Sovereignty and control.** A row may record control without sovereignty: the Canal Zone, Guantánamo, and the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands are examples. Do not put them in a simple “U.S. territory” column without qualification.
- **Termination.** The Philippines became independent in 1946; the Canal Zone reverted to Panama under the 1977 treaties and the 1979 transfer; Hawaii and Alaska became states in 1959. The table should give the current status, not only the acquisition date.
- **Arithmetic check.** The sum of the BLM rows is 1,840,709,120 total acres, or 75.6 percent of the 2,429,914,880-acre total U.S. area. The table should not add overseas areas to that continental total without a separate note.

### 4.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Area figures are not neutral.** They depend on water/land definitions, boundary surveys, and the treatment of disputed claims. The same acquisition can appear as 828,000 or 529,911,680 acres depending on the unit and the boundary.
- **Cost figures are not prices for land.** They include claims, debt assumption, and diplomatic settlements. They do not measure the value of the land, resources, or labor taken.
- **Indigenous title is prior.** The treaties and statutes record the claims of European and American states. They do not establish that Indigenous nations had no title. The table must not present a cession as a transfer from an empty landscape.
- **Some acquisitions were not purchases.** Oregon was a boundary settlement; Texas was an annexation with debt assumption; Hawaii was a joint resolution; the Canal Zone was a control arrangement. A “purchase price” column will be mostly “—” for these rows.
- **The 1763 baseline is not U.S. territory.** The Treaty of Paris of 1763 ended the Seven Years’ War and transferred Canada and Florida to Britain. It is included only to show the imperial boundary before the United States existed.
- **The table cannot measure the consequences.** It does not show how much land remained in Indigenous hands, how much was sold or granted to settlers, or how federal land policy worked. Those are chapter subjects, not appendix data.

### 4.6 Open checks before drafting

- Editorial check — confirm the Texas annexation area and decide whether to list the 1845 joint resolution, the 1850 settlement, or both. Do not double-count the same land.
- Editorial check — confirm Hawaii’s area and the legal status of the 1898 joint resolution. Consult a Native Hawaiian source before describing annexation as settled.
- Editorial check — confirm the Philippines’ area and the 1946 independence date; decide whether to include the Philippines as an acquisition row with a termination note.
- Editorial check — confirm the Canal Zone area and the 1977 treaties and 1979 transfer. Do not describe the zone as sovereign U.S. territory.
- Editorial check — decide whether to include the 1763 baseline. If included, label it “imperial baseline, not a U.S. acquisition.”
- Editorial check — confirm the Danish West Indies area (133 square miles) and the $25 million payment date.

## 5. Table 4. Wars, Mobilization, and Cost

### 5.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 4 gives service totals, death and wound totals, and cost estimates for selected U.S. wars from the Revolution through 2021.
- The table supports a claim about scale and definition: mobilization and mortality varied enormously, and cost estimates are not comparable unless the definition is held constant. The Civil War death toll is a range, not a single number.
- The table must not present a single “war cost” series across 1775–2021 without breaks. It must distinguish military operations from veterans’ costs, interest, and allied contributions.

### 5.2 Table design

- **Rows.** Revolutionary War; War of 1812; Mexican War; Civil War (Union); Civil War (Confederate); Spanish-American War; World War I; World War II; Korean War; Vietnam War; Persian Gulf War; Afghanistan; Iraq.
- **Columns.** Conflict and years; U.S. service total; battle deaths; other deaths; total deaths; wounded; cost in current dollars; cost in constant dollars with base year; cost definition; notes.

### 5.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Service total.** Use the number who served, not peak strength, where both are available. The Civil War Union figure of 2,213,363 is a service total; the Confederate figure is unknown, so a combined “Civil War” service total is not defensible.
- **Deaths.** Total deaths = battle deaths + other deaths. Do not add wounded to deaths. Where the source gives only total deaths, do not invent a battle/other split.
- **Civil War.** Choose one headline estimate for the table and show the range in a note. Do not add Hacker’s or Barceló’s total to the Union/Confederate service deaths; those estimates are war-related excess deaths, not a roll of soldiers by side.
- **Wounds.** Wounds are not unique persons in every source. The CRS table notes that Marine Corps data for some wars count individuals, while other data count the incidence of wounds. Do not compute a “casualty rate” by adding deaths and wounds without a note.
- **Cost.** Current dollars are the dollars of the war period. Constant dollars use the CRS base year, FY2011. Do not convert to another base without recalculating. Do not compare CRS military-only costs with Costs of War totals that include veterans, homeland security, and interest.
- **Post-9/11.** The DoD casualty figures are military and DoD civilian deaths; they exclude contractors and civilians in the war zones. The Costs of War figures include broader budgetary categories. The table must keep the two definitions separate.
- **Arithmetic check.** Every total must equal the sum of its components, or the note must explain the difference. The Civil War and post-9/11 rows will not reconcile to a single total because the definitions differ.

### 5.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Early war records are incomplete.** The Revolutionary War and War of 1812 totals are estimates compiled from pension records and other sources, not a complete roster. The CRS table itself warns that data before World War I are based on incomplete records.[^app-b-crs-rl32492]
- **Civil War mortality is contested.** The conventional 618,222, Hacker’s ~750,000, and Barceló’s 698,000 use different methods and populations. A table that prints one number without the range will overstate certainty.
- **Cost definitions are not comparable.** CRS military operations, OMB outlays, and Costs of War budgetary costs answer different questions. The table can show them side by side only with a definition column and a note.
- **Allied contributions matter.** The Persian Gulf War’s net cost to U.S. taxpayers was much lower than the gross cost because allies reimbursed much of it. The CRS table notes a net cost of $4.7 billion in current dollars; a gross-cost column without that note will mislead.[^app-b-crs-rs22926]
- **Civilian deaths are excluded.** The table’s mortality columns are U.S. military deaths unless a note says otherwise. It cannot support a claim about total war deaths, enemy deaths, or civilian casualties.
- **The Afghanistan cutoff matters.** The August 30, 2021, evacuation-end date excludes later deaths and includes the 13 service members killed at Kabul airport. A different cutoff will produce a different total.

### 6.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Admissions.** “Persons obtaining lawful permanent resident status” is a legal admission category. It is not net migration. Before 1906, the series is compiled from immigrant arrival records; after 1906, it is an administrative count of LPR grants. Label the early series “arrivals/admissions” and do not call it a count of all entrants.
- **Net migration.** Net migration = immigration − emigration, with adjustments for temporary and unauthorized flows. Table 5 does not compute net migration. Do not subtract admissions from foreign-born stock and call the result net migration.
- **Foreign-born stock.** The foreign-born population includes naturalized citizens, LPRs, temporary migrants, refugees, asylees, and unauthorized residents. It is a stock, not a flow. A stock can rise even if admissions fall, because of births, deaths, emigration, and status changes.
- **Unauthorized population.** The DHS estimate is a residual: total foreign-born minus legally resident foreign-born. It includes people with temporary protected status, DACA, and pending removal proceedings. It is a model, not a census. Do not present it as a count of all unauthorized entries or as a count of all foreign-born noncitizens.
- **Quota totals.** The 164,667 and 153,714 figures are sums of the proclaimed national and area quotas, not statutory totals. State “calculated from the schedule.” The 1921 act sets a percentage formula; do not print a total unless a source gives one.
- **Regions.** DHS “America” includes North and South America; “North America” includes Mexico, Canada, the Caribbean, and Central America. The categories change between tables and years. Do not compare a “region of last residence” row with a “region of birth” row without a note.
- **Decades.** The 1820s begin in 1820, the first year of the series. The 2020, 2021, and 2022 columns in DHS Table 2 are separate years, not a full decade; the 2023 table gives only annual data. Label the partial period.
- **Rounding.** Table 1 of the 2023 Yearbook rounds cells to the nearest ten to protect privacy. Do not sum rounded cells and expect the published total to match exactly.

### 6.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Admissions are not migration.** A person can be admitted as a temporary worker, student, refugee, or asylee without becoming an LPR. A person can be an LPR and later emigrate. The table measures one legal flow.
- **Pre-1906 data are arrivals.** The early series counts immigrant arrivals, not LPR grants. The legal category did not exist in the same form. A table that labels 1820–1905 “green cards” is wrong.
- **Quotas were discriminatory.** The 1921 and 1924 acts used census ancestry to favor Northern and Western Europe and to exclude most Asians. The 1929 national-origins schedule embedded those preferences. The table should show the formula and the exclusions, not only the totals.
- **The Western Hemisphere was treated differently.** The 1924 act did not impose the same national-origins quotas on Western Hemisphere countries; later statutes changed the system. A quota table that covers only the Eastern Hemisphere must say so.
- **Foreign-born stock includes the unauthorized.** The Census Bureau’s foreign-born total and the DHS unauthorized estimate overlap. Do not add them.
- **Unauthorized estimates differ.** DHS, Pew, MPI, and CIS use different methods and produce different totals. Use one series, name it, and do not average them.
- **Recent data are affected by policy and pandemic.** 2020 admissions fell sharply; 2021–2023 rebounded. A decade total for the 2020s will be incomplete and should not be compared with a full decade.

### 7.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Enslaved population.** Use the census counts in Panel B. If the table also shows Hacker’s adjusted series, put it in a separate column or a note; do not mix adjusted and unadjusted values in the same series. The 1790 count is the largest single discrepancy; choose one and explain.
- **Enslaved share.** Divide the enslaved population by the total population and by the Black population. The second share is not 100 percent before 1865 because free Black people existed in every census. Do not label the enslaved share “Black share.”
- **Natural increase.** Natural increase = births − deaths. It is not the same as the change in the enslaved population, which also includes imports, sales, and escapes. Hacker’s estimates of births and imports are models; use them only with the method note.
- **Transatlantic trade.** Embarked and disembarked are different counts. The difference is mortality during the Middle Passage. Do not use “12.5 million arrived” or “10.7 million shipped.”
- **Domestic trade.** The 835,000 and 1,110,000 figures are net-migration estimates, not counts of sales. - **Caribbean benchmarks.** The Barbados 1643 and 1679 figures are two points. The 1679 census is a property-and-enslaver census, not a full demographic census. Do not interpolate a smooth series.
- **Prices.** The price of a “prime male field hand” is a standardized category used by historians, not a market price for every enslaved person. Prices varied by age, sex, health, skill, family ties, and guarantees. Use the sample’s definition and state the transformation. If the table deflates to real dollars, name the deflator and base year.

### 7.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **SlaveVoyages is a voyage database.** It records ships and captives where records survive. It does not record every voyage, every overland coffle, or every sale. Its own methodology essay warns that the voyage model cannot capture most overland domestic trafficking.[^app-b-slavevoyages-intra]
- **The transatlantic total is an estimate.** The 12.5 million embarked and 10.7 million disembarked figures are model-based estimates, not a census. Regional totals have margins of error.
- **Domestic-trade estimates disagree.** The 835,000 and 1,110,000 figures use different assumptions about children and census survival. The gap is not a rounding error; it is a substantive methodological disagreement.
- **Caribbean data are sparse.** The 1643 Barbados figure comes from a colonial report; the 1679 census is unusually detailed. Neither is representative of all British, French, Dutch, or Danish islands.
- **Prices are urban and selected.** New Orleans was a major market, but its prices may not represent Upper South or rural sales. The sample overrepresents sales with notarized bills.
- **Census slave schedules are not a full count of individuals before 1850.** The 1790–1840 census aggregates enslaved people by household; the 1850 and 1860 slave schedules list individuals but are still subject to undercount and transcription error.
- **The 1790 count is unstable.** The census’s printed total, Hacker’s adjusted count, and some modern compilations differ by a few dozen people. The table must choose one and explain; it must not average them.

### 8.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **Public finance.** Use fiscal years, not calendar years. The OMB Table 1.1 series begins in 1789; Table 7.1 begins in 1940. For 1789–1939, use the *Historical Statistics* series and note the administrative-budget basis. Do not splice the series without a note.
- **Deficits and debt.** The deficit is receipts minus outlays. The gross federal debt is not the same as the deficit; it includes debt held by the public and by government accounts. Do not add the deficit to the debt.
- **Railroad mileage.** Miles operated is a stock at a point in time. Miles built is a flow. Do not sum annual mileage to get a total.
- **Steel.** Steel ingots and castings are a primary product, not finished steel. Do not compare with a finished-steel series without a note.
- **Unemployment.** The unemployment rate is unemployed persons divided by the labor force. The labor force includes employed and unemployed persons actively seeking work; it excludes people outside the labor force. The Lebergott and Darby series for the 1930s differ; choose one and label it.
- **CPI.** The CPI is a price index for a market basket, not a cost-of-living index for every household. Use it to deflate nominal values only with a note. Do not use the CPI-U before 1913; use the historical index.
- **GDP per capita.** Real GDP per capita is output divided by population. The Maddison and BEA series have different base years and coverage. Do not compare their levels; compare growth rates only with a note.
- **Top shares and Gini.** The top 1 percent share is a tax-based share of pre-tax national income among adults. The Gini index is a survey-based measure of household money income inequality. They measure different things; do not use one as a substitute for the other.
- **Union density.** Union density is union members divided by wage and salary workers, not collective-bargaining coverage. The historical series and the BLS series may use different denominators; label the break.
- **Newspapers.** Daily circulation is not readership; print circulation is not total audience. The 2020 Pew figure may include digital subscriptions; state the definition.
- **Telephones.** Telephones in service, telephone lines, and households with telephone are different measures. Do not combine them in one column.
- **Regional employment.** Manufacturing employment is not total employment. The Census of Manufactures counts production workers and wage earners; the BEA counts total employment. Label the series and the region definitions.

### 8.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **Early public finance is not modern accounting.** The 1789–1915 figures are based on warrants issued; trust funds and the unified budget change the totals after 1934. A long series that ignores these breaks will mislead.[^app-b-hsus-y335-342]
- **Railroad mileage peaks and declines.** The series is useful for the 19th-century build-out, not as a measure of transport output. Traffic, ton-miles, and revenue are different series.
- **Steel is cyclical.** A single benchmark year can misstate capacity or decline. Use several years or label the cycle.
- **Unemployment before 1940 is reconstructed.** The 1930s rate is one of the most contested numbers in U.S. economic history. The table must not present one estimate as the estimate.
- **CPI is not a universal deflator.** Deflating war costs, slave prices, or GDP with the CPI is approximate. Use the deflator that matches the series where possible.
- **GDP per capita before 1929 is a scholarly reconstruction.** The Maddison series is not an official U.S. series. It is useful for long-run comparison, not for precise annual claims.
- **Inequality measures are not interchangeable.** Top income shares, the Gini index, wealth shares, and consumption inequality can move in different directions. The table must say which measure it uses.
- **Union density is not union power.** Density measures membership, not bargaining coverage, strikes, or political influence.
- **Newspaper and telephone data have different denominators.** Circulation is copies; telephone is lines or households. Do not plot them on a common index without saying so.
- **Regional employment shares depend on industrial classification.** The shift from manufacturing to services, and the reclassification of industries, can change the regional shares without any real geographic move.

## 9. Table 8. Land Use, Public Works, and Settlement, 1930–1940

### 9.1 Analytical job and claims

- Table 8 gives the New Deal’s spatial footprint: TVA dams, CCC camps, agricultural land use, and Dust Bowl population change.
- The table supports a claim about transformation and limits: the New Deal built institutions and infrastructure, but its reach varied, and the Dust Bowl migration was real but not the uniform “Okie” exodus of popular memory.
- The table must not claim that TVA or the CCC ended the Depression, that all Dust Bowl migrants went to California, or that a camp count measures a program’s effects.

### 9.2 Table design

- **Panel A. TVA dams.** Norris, Wheeler, Pickwick, Guntersville, Chickamauga, Watts Bar, and Kentucky. Columns: dam; river; state; construction start; completion; purpose; capacity.
- **Panel B. CCC camps.** National totals for 1933, 1935, 1936, 1940, and 1942; state counts for August 1933 (all 48 states or a selected set for Map 26). Columns: date; camps; enrollees; source definition.
- **Panel C. Land use.** 1930, 1935, and 1940: farms; land in farms; cropland harvested; selected Great Plains states. Columns: year; farms; land in farms; cropland harvested; unit; source.
- **Panel D. Dust Bowl population.** 1930 and 1940: most affected counties 120,859 to 97,606; Southern Plains 11,561,000 in 1930; California 5,677,251 to 6,907,387; migrants 1935–1940. Columns: area; 1930; 1940; change; note.
- **Row cap.** Seven dam rows, six CCC rows, six land-use rows, and five population rows, for roughly 24 rows. Target: 1.5 to 2 pages.
- **Notes.** State that CCC camp counts vary by date and definition. State that “land in farms” is not “cropland harvested.”

### 9.3 Source series and exact locators

- **TVA plan and dams.** Tennessee Valley Authority, “Building a Better Life for the Tennessee Valley.” The 1933 act created TVA; the 1936 unified plan proposed a system of dams including Wheeler, Pickwick, Guntersville, Chickamauga, Hiwassee, Watts Bar, Fort Loudoun, Fontana, and Kentucky. The page lists the dams and the wartime expansion.[^app-b-tva-plan]
- **Dam dates.** TVA dam pages: Norris, construction 1933 and completion 1936; Wheeler, 1933–1936; Pickwick, 1934–1938; Guntersville, 1935–1939; Chickamauga, 1936–1940; Watts Bar, 1939–January 1942; Kentucky, 1938–1944.[^app-b-tva-dams]
- **CCC national counts.** National Park Service, “Guide to the NPS Civilian Conservation Corps Collection.” The guide states that by June 29, 1933, 270,000 young men were working in 1,330 camps; average strength was 300,000 men in 1,500 camps; at the height in 1936 there were over 500,000 enrollees; almost three million men enrolled in total.[^app-b-nps-ccc]
- **CCC camps by 1935.** National Archives at Atlanta, *Civilian Conservation Corps* finding aid, pp. 2–3. The finding aid states that by 1935 over 2,650 camps had been established in all states, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands, and more than 600,000 people were working for the CCC; Congress abolished the CCC on June 30, 1942.[^app-b-nara-ccc]
- **CCC state counts, August 1933.** PBS *American Experience*, “CCC Camps in the U.S. in 1933.” The page reproduces a 1933 map and lists camps and men by state, including California 168 camps and 33,600 men; Pennsylvania 97 camps and 19,400 men; New York 33 camps and 6,600 men; Texas 34 camps and 6,800 men.[^app-b-pbs-ccc-1933]

- **Land use.** U.S. Census of Agriculture, 1940, *Farms and Farm Property*, Table 4, “All Land in Farms and Specified Uses of Land, in the United States: Censuses of 1850 to 1940,” and Table 10, state and division data for 1940, 1935, and 1930. The NASS PDFs blocked automated access on November 22, 2026; the tables are identified but not yet read.[^app-b-census-ag-1940]
- **Dust Bowl population.** Jason Long and Henry E. Siu, “Refugees from Dust and Shrinking Land: Tracking the Dust Bowl Migrants,” NBER Working Paper 22108 (2016), pp. 1–5 and 14–20. The most affected counties fell from 120,859 in 1930 to 97,606 in 1940, a 19.2 percent decline; the Southern Plains states grew from 4,496,000 in 1890 to 11,561,000 in 1930. Long and Siu find that out-migration from the Dust Bowl was only slightly higher than in the 1920s, that farmers were the least likely to move, and that Dust Bowl migrants were no more likely to move to California than other migrants.[^app-b-long-siu-2016]
- **1940 population.** Census Bureau, *1940 Census of Population*, vol. 1, *Number of Inhabitants*. The 1940 total is 132,164,569; the 1930 total is 123,202,624; California is 6,907,387 in 1940 and 5,677,251 in 1930. The published volume is identified but not yet read for this outline.[^app-b-census-1940-pop]

### 9.4 Transformations, definitions, and arithmetic

- **TVA dates.** Construction start and completion are not the same as first power generation. Use the dates on the dam pages and label them. Capacity is summer net dependable capacity, not installed nameplate capacity.
- **CCC camp counts.** The 1,330 figure is June 1933; the 2,650 figure is 1935; the 6,036 figure is all recorded camps across the program. These are not the same measure. Choose one date for the national series and label the state map separately.
- **CCC enrollees.** “Enrollees” is a cumulative count, not a simultaneous strength. The 500,000 figure is a peak strength; the three million figure is cumulative enrollment. Do not compare them as if they were the same.
- **Land in farms.** Land in farms includes cropland, pasture, woodland, and wasteland. Cropland harvested is a subset. Do not use “land in farms” as a measure of cropland or of total land use.
- **Dust Bowl counties.** The 120,859 to 97,606 figure is for a defined set of most-affected counties, not all Great Plains counties. The decline is a population change, not a net migration count; births and deaths are part of the change.
- **Migration.** The 1935–1940 census asks where people lived five years earlier. It measures gross moves, not net migration. A state can gain migrants and still lose population if deaths and out-migration exceed births and in-migration.
- **California.** The state’s 1930–1940 growth includes migration from the South, the Midwest, and the East, not only the Dust Bowl. Do not attribute all of California’s growth to the Dust Bowl.
- **Arithmetic check.** The CCC state counts should sum to the national camp count for the same date, or the note must explain the difference. The TVA dam list should match the agency’s count; the “nine more” list must be counted carefully.

### 9.5 Limitations, counterevidence, and what the sources cannot show

- **TVA is an agency history.** The TVA pages are useful for dates and capacity, but they are institutional accounts. They do not measure the distribution of benefits, the displacement of valley residents, or the environmental costs.
- **CCC camp counts are inconsistent.** The NPS, NARA, PBS, and CCC Legacy figures use different dates and definitions. A table that prints one number without the date will mislead.
- **CCC camps were segregated.** The program included Black and Native American enrollees, but most camps were segregated, and the Indian Division operated differently. A camp count does not show who was served or under what conditions.[^app-b-nps-ccc]
- **Census of Agriculture data are farm-based.** They cover land in farms, not all land. They miss non-farm land, and they classify fallow and pasture differently across censuses.
- **Dust Bowl migration is contested.** Long and Siu find that the popular “Okie” exodus is overstated: out-migration was only slightly higher than in the 1920s, farmers were less likely to move, and California was not a disproportionate destination. The table should present the measured record, not the myth.[^app-b-long-siu-2016]
- **The Dust Bowl was not only drought.** Mechanization, wheat prices, credit, and federal policy all mattered. A table of population and land use cannot establish causation.
- **TVA and CCC counts do not measure outcomes.** They measure projects and enrollees, not income, soil conservation, electrification, or long-run economic change.
