## Epigraphs

> “There were never Englishmen left in a forreigne Countrey in such miserie as wee were in this new discovered Virginia.”
>
> — George Percy, eyewitness narrative of the 1609–1610 winter, quoted in Encyclopedia Virginia, “The Starving Time”[^ch04-starving-time]

> “For wee must consider that wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us.”
>
> — John Winthrop, “A Modell of Christian Charity” (1630), transcript at Hanover College[^ch04-winthrop-model]

> “The puritans who settled New England in 1630 were not coming to America to promote religious freedom for all, but to achieve for themselves a freedom from the church and civil officials in England who had prevented them from pursuing their faith as they believed God wanted them to.”
>
> — Francis J. Bremer, “The Puritans and Dissent: The Cases of Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson,” *History Now*, no. 29 (Fall 2011)[^ch04-dissent]

- **Why these three.** Percy supplies the cost: a company colony that nearly devoured itself. Winthrop supplies the purpose: a godly community whose example would be watched. Bremer supplies the deflation: the freedom the puritans sought was their own, not a general liberty of conscience. The epigraphs disagree about what “freedom” meant in English America — survival, a covenant with God, or a privilege held at others’ expense — and that disagreement is the chapter’s problem.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Percy’s words survive through a narrative written by a participant and published long after; they are a mediated record, not a transcript. Winthrop’s words come from the single surviving manuscript copy, a text discussed in the In Focus box and subject to a live debate about whether it was ever preached.[^ch04-winthrop-manuscript] Bremer’s line is a historian’s summary, not a period voice.
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the puritans rejected all religious liberty, that Winthrop spoke aboard the *Arbella*, or that Virginia’s misery was caused by idleness. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The object and the day.** On November 11, 1620, the adult men aboard the *Mayflower*, at anchor off Cape Cod rather than at their intended destination near the Hudson River, signed an agreement sometimes translated as a covenant to “combine ourselves together in a Civill body politick.” The text survives in a form that calls itself “In the name of God, Amen,” recalls the “loyall subjects of our dread soveraigne Lord King James,” and binds the signers to “enact, constitute, and frame such just and equall Lawes … for the generall good of the Colonie.”[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **Why the patent mattered.** The passengers held a patent to settle farther south, in the northern parts of Virginia. Weather and the leaky consort ship *Speedwell* left them in New England, outside their legal grant, with no civil authority over them. The Compact supplied a temporary, self-made civil order while they waited for a new patent.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **Who signed.** The Compact was signed by adult men; the exact list is reconstructed. Plimoth Patuxet notes that the surviving signer list may include 41 of the men — all but one of the freemen, some hired men, and two of the servants — a list that later copies add.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **The missing original.** The original document does not survive. It was first printed in *Mourt’s Relation* (1622), a pamphlet on the colony’s first year, and reprinted by Plymouth’s town historian Nathaniel Morton in *New England’s Memorial* (1669), which supplied a possible signer list absent from the earlier text.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **The mediation.** The Compact reaches the present only through printed and copied texts, made after the event, for readers with their own purposes. The scene may read what the text says and what the record can and cannot confirm; it may not stage a cabin, invent speeches, or supply weather and gestures the sources do not describe.[^ch04-mayflower-compact][^ch04-check-scene]
- **The silence.** The Wampanoag, whose homeland the colonists had entered, are not parties to the Compact and leave no comparable written record of the moment. The document is a record of the colonists’ self-government, not of the world they had landed in.[^ch04-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A document, not a tableau.** The scene should open on the agreement as a text: who wrote it, why a self-made covenant was needed, and how it survived. It should not stage a reenactment of signing.
- **The freedom claim is limited.** The Compact created a “Civill body politick” for signers whose religion and standing varied. It did not extend political voice to women, children, servants generally, or the Native people whose land they occupied. The chapter should say this plainly, because the Compact is often cited as an origin of American self-government.
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: What kinds of freedom did English colonists claim, and for whom?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that the Compact invented democracy or that it was a fraud. It was one workable instrument among several — the Virginia Company’s charter, the General Assembly, and the towns of Massachusetts Bay were others — and each defined liberty by defining who was bound.
- **The move to 1607.** The scene sits in 1620, thirteen years after Jamestown. The framing paragraph follows immediately and sends the reader back to the company colony that came first.

### What must not be invented

- No invented cabin, table, signing order, weather, clothing, dialogue, or crowd. The record cannot establish those details.[^ch04-check-scene]
- No claim that the Compact was called the “Mayflower Compact” in 1620; Plimoth Patuxet dates that name to 1793.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- No claim that every adult man signed; the sources give a reconstructed list, and the margin of uncertainty should be stated.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- No claim that the Compact created religious freedom or universal suffrage. Its own words promise “due submission and obedience” to the laws the signers would make.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- No claim that the Compact governed women, the enslaved, Native people, or non-signers, or that its authority extended beyond the temporary need it addressed.[^ch04-check-scene]

### Drafting target

Three hundred to four hundred words. Open on the text and the missing original. State why a self-made agreement was needed — the colonists were outside their patent — and what it actually committed them to. Distinguish the signers from those left out. Note that the surviving forms are later printings, not the lost manuscript. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows and supplies the years, scope, and limits.

## 1. Jamestown and the Virginia Company

### 1.1 A company, not a kingdom

- **The claim.** Virginia began as a profit-seeking joint-stock venture, not a royal colony. The Virginia Company of London raised private capital, spread risk, and pursued commercial, religious, and strategic goals at once, and its governance changed repeatedly as the enterprise faltered.
- **The charter.** On April 10, 1606, James I chartered the Virginia Company, which consisted of the Virginia Company of London and the Virginia Company of Plymouth. The London investors were authorized to settle between 34 and 40 degrees latitude; the Plymouth group, to the north. The Plymouth colony at Sagadahoc, in present-day Maine, was planted in August 1607 and abandoned the next spring.[^ch04-virginia-company]
- **The design.** A joint-stock company pooled resources and shared profits, allowing the Crown to reap colonization’s benefits without bearing its costs. The company’s aims mixed commerce, national rivalry with Spain, and Richard Hakluyt’s case for converting Native people, exploiting resources, and resettling England’s poor.[^ch04-virginia-company]
- **The reorganization.** A second charter on May 23, 1609 transferred power toward the investors, created a treasurer’s office, and enlarged the council. Governance in Virginia remained centralized and unstable, and the colony’s early leaders quarreled and replaced one another.[^ch04-virginia-company]
- **The consequence.** The company’s commercial frame explains much of Jamestown’s behavior — the search for gold and a passage to Asia, the communal store, the drive for a marketable staple — without explaining away its religious language.

### 1.2 Founding and the Starving Time

- **The claim.** Jamestown survived its first decade mainly by luck, violence, and Native tolerance, and it nearly failed in the winter of 1609–1610. The colony’s near-collapse was produced by poor siting, a regional drought, disease, dependence on others for food, and a leadership crisis — not by simple idleness.
- **The founding.** The ships reached the Chesapeake on April 26, 1607. The colonists planted a cross at Cape Henry and, on May 13, situated their fort on a marshy peninsula up the James River. The location satisfied instructions for deepwater access and defense but had poor fresh water and few local resources.[^ch04-early-jamestown]
- **The first mortality.** Sickness began within months; by the end of September 1607, about half the colonists had died. George Percy attributed deaths to the “bloudie Flixe,” swellings, wounds, and famine. The colony survived on food bargained for or taken from the people of Tsenacomoco.[^ch04-early-jamestown]
- **The drought.** Tree-ring evidence shows the colonists arrived at the start of a seven-year drought (1606–1612), the driest period in 770 years, which cut both English and Native food supplies and made English demands destabilizing.[^ch04-starving-time]
- **The Starving Time.** Powhatan’s people laid siege to Jamestown beginning in November 1609, preventing hunting, fishing, and trade. By spring, only 60 of about 240 colonists in the fort remained alive, roughly 75 percent dead. Reports of cannibalism circulated; archaeologists have found butchered horse and other remains, and in 2013 announced evidence of cannibalism on a skull they called “Jane.” Historians, including Rachel B. Herrmann, caution that the stories had authors with motives and that the evidence remains debated.[^ch04-starving-time]
- **The rescue and reversal.** In May 1610, survivors of the *Sea Venture*, wrecked in Bermuda the previous year, reached Jamestown and found the colony ruined. Governor Sir Thomas Gates resolved to abandon it; the colony was saved only when it met the new governor, Thomas West, baron De La Warr, arriving with supplies and a year’s provisions.[^ch04-starving-time][^ch04-early-jamestown]
- **The lesson the leaders drew.** Gates and his secretary William Strachey blamed idleness and misgovernment and answered with discipline. Karen Ordahl Kupperman and William Kelso argue the colonists were weakened by malnutrition and disease, whose symptoms — listlessness and despair — were mistaken for laziness.[^ch04-starving-time]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the Starving Time as a leadership and environmental catastrophe, not a morality tale, and should avoid converting contested cannibalism stories into settled fact.[^ch04-check-dating]

### 1.3 Dale, martial law, and the turn to tobacco

- **The claim.** The colony was stabilized by coercion, external authority, and a marketable crop. Sir Thomas Dale’s martial law and the arrival of a profitable tobacco strain converted a failing outpost into an expanding plantation society.
- **The law.** Arriving in May 1611, Dale enforced the *Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall*, the first English-language body of laws in the western hemisphere, with harsh punishments for theft, idleness, and religious offenses. The code was criticized in Virginia and England as too severe.[^ch04-early-jamestown]
- **Expansion.** Dale founded the City of Henrico and, after defeating the Appamattucks, Bermuda Hundred, moving settlement beyond Jamestown. A 1614 peace and John Rolfe’s marriage to Amonute/Matoaka (Pocahontas) ended the First Anglo-Powhatan War (1609–1614).[^ch04-early-jamestown]
- **The staple.** Rolfe introduced a West Indian variety of tobacco, *Nicotiana tabacum*, around 1611–1612. English smokers preferred it to the native *Nicotiana rustica*. Virginia shipped about 20,000 pounds of tobacco to England in 1617, double that in 1618, and about 60,000 pounds in 1622, despite the Indian uprising that spring.[^ch04-tobacco]
- **The consequence.** Tobacco created the colony’s economy and its hunger for land and labor. It also made the colony dependent on a single volatile crop and on continuous immigration to replace the dead.

### 1.4 Powhatan’s country and the English

- **The claim.** The English entered a working Native political order and survived at its sufferance. Tsenacomoco’s paramount chief, Wahunsenacawh (Powhatan), managed the newcomers through food, diplomacy, adoption, and limited violence; his successors fought the English when the balance shifted.
- **The polity.** Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom of roughly 28–32 Algonquian-speaking groups. The English Crown’s 1608 attempt to make Powhatan a subject by presenting him a crown failed to establish the authority it claimed. This chapter should treat that order as already established and cross-refer to Chapter 3.[^ch04-check-ch3]
- **The war after 1622.** On March 22, 1622, Opechancanough’s warriors attacked settlements along the James, killing perhaps a quarter of the colonists, beginning the Second Anglo-Powhatan War (1622–1632). A third war followed in 1644, after which Virginia’s growing population and the weakening of the Native nations ended their ability to threaten the colony’s existence.[^ch04-early-jamestown]
- **The rejection of dependency.** After 1622 the company and colony increasingly rejected coexistence and pursued dispossession; the historian Edmund S. Morgan argues that this hostility reshaped the colony’s labor and freedom questions as well.[^ch04-morgan]
- **The consequence.** Virginia’s expansion was conquest as well as settlement. The chapter should not let the tobacco story obscure the Indigenous history that made it possible.

## 2. Tobacco, 1619, labor, and government

### 2.1 The transforming crop

- **The claim.** Tobacco made Virginia profitable and made it dependent on coerced labor and ever more land. Its cultivation shaped law, migration, and the colony’s social order.
- **The market.** Tobacco exports rose from about 20,000 pounds in 1617 to about 60,000 pounds in 1622. By the 1630s, soil exhaustion around Jamestown pushed planters to seek virgin land; a 1632 law capped each settler’s planting at 1,500 plants. Expansion moved east along the James and, by 1635, to the York and later the Rappahannock and Potomac valleys.[^ch04-tobacco]
- **The labor demand.** Tobacco required heavy seasonal labor. Planters relied on indentured servants for most of the seventeenth century; only later, by 1705, did Virginia become a society in which enslaved labor predominated.[^ch04-indentured-servants]
- **The consequence.** The crop linked the Chesapeake to a transatlantic market and to a recurring shortage of workers — the problem the rest of the section addresses.

### 2.2 The 1619 General Assembly

- **The claim.** The 1619 General Assembly made Virginia the site of the first elected legislative body in English North America, but it was a company instrument that limited as much as it granted. Its creation is a landmark of self-government and an example of corporate governance at the same time.
- **The Great Charter.** The so-called Great Charter of 1618 replaced martial law with a Crown-appointed governor and advisory council and authorized the governor to summon a General Assembly. The company treasurer Sir Edwin Sandys saw the assembly as a way to build settlers’ stake in the colony and answer criticism at home.[^ch04-house-of-burgesses]
- **The meeting.** In summer 1619, Governor Sir George Yeardley called for two burgesses from each of the colony’s eleven settlements. Twenty-two burgesses met from July 30 to August 4 in the Jamestown church with the governor and his six-man council. John Pory, the only member with parliamentary experience, was chosen speaker.[^ch04-house-of-burgesses]
- **The work.** The assembly asserted its own authority over burgesses’ qualifications, confirmed land grants made under earlier governors, named the Church of England as the established church, and passed laws on tobacco, Indian trade and relations, and personal morality.[^ch04-house-of-burgesses]
- **The company’s role.** The first charter of 1606 had promised colonists and their children “all liberties, franchises and immunities” of English subjects; the assembly gave that promise a local institution. But the governor and council retained veto power and the burgesses legislated by the company’s leave.[^ch04-first-legislative-assembly]
- **The afterlife.** The Crown revoked the company’s charter in 1624, and Virginia became a royal colony in 1625. The assembly continued to meet without explicit royal authorization; in 1643 Governor Sir William Berkeley divided it into two houses.[^ch04-house-of-burgesses]
- **The consequence.** The assembly is a genuine precedent and a bounded one. It excluded women, servants, the enslaved, and Native people from the polity it created.

### 2.3 Labor: indentured servants

- **The claim.** Virginia’s labor system rested on indentured servitude, a contractual bondage that was harsher in Virginia than in England and that made free labor temporary and precarious.
- **The origin.** Servitude descended from English labor law and practice. An indenture was a contract, or “covenant merely personal,” typically for a term of years; English people became servants “in hope thereby to amend theyr estates,” and children were sometimes bound by parents.[^ch04-indentured-servants]
- **The Virginia version.** With tobacco’s rise, the company sold servants directly to planters at a price based on passage, and planters set the terms of service to recoup their costs. Servants could be bought and sold as commodities. Edmund S. Morgan observed that servitude in Virginia’s tobacco fields “approached closer to slavery than anything known at the time in England.”[^ch04-indentured-servants]
- **The headright.** The Great Charter of 1618 created the headright system, which awarded 50 acres of land for each person whose passage a planter paid. The system concentrated land in the hands of those who could import laborers and tied expansion to coerced migration.[^ch04-early-jamestown][^ch04-tobacco]
- **The risk.** Disease and hard labor meant most servants died before completing their terms. Those who survived faced high land prices and lengthening terms as the century advanced. Morgan’s account stresses that freed servants had reason to resent the planters who held both land and power.[^ch04-morgan]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present servitude and slavery as connected labor regimes whose boundary hardened over time, not as separate stories.

### 2.4 The first Africans, 1619, and the limits of the claim

- **The claim.** In late August 1619, about twenty Africans were sold at Point Comfort to leaders of the Virginia colony. Their arrival began African life in English Virginia, but it did not begin a fully codified hereditary slave system: that system was made by later law.
- **The arrival.** The Portuguese slave ship *São João Bautista* carried roughly 350 enslaved Africans from West Central Africa toward New Spain. Two English privateers, the *White Lion* and the *Treasurer*, intercepted the ship in the Gulf of Mexico and took 50 to 60 captives.[^ch04-first-africans]
- **The sale.** The *White Lion* arrived at Point Comfort in late August. John Rolfe reported that it “brought not any thing but 20. and odd Negroes,” whom Governor Sir George Yeardley and the cape merchant Abraham Peirsey “bought for victualle [food] … at the best and easyest rate they could.” The *Treasurer* arrived a few days later with more Africans and sold two or three before sailing to Bermuda.[^ch04-first-africans]
- **The origins.** The Africans were probably Kimbundu-speaking Ndongo people from the region of present-day Angola, captured in Portuguese campaigns and baptized under Portuguese law. The historian John K. Thornton’s work underlies the identification.[^ch04-first-africans]
- **The status.** Virginia had no law codifying slavery in 1619. The Africans were bought and sold and listed in the March 1620 muster as “Others not Christians in the Service of the English”; records call them servants. The buying and selling suggests many historians that they were treated as enslaved rather than as ordinary indentured servants, but their legal status was not yet fixed.[^ch04-first-africans][^ch04-africans-jamestowne]
- **Angela.** One woman bought by Captain William Peirce appears in the 1624 and 1625 musters as “Angelo a Negro Woman in the *Treasurer*.” Her probable birthplace was Angola, and she survived the Middle Passage, the 1622 attack, and the colony’s early mortality to live in the Peirce household in Jamestown.[^ch04-angela]
- **The later trajectory.** Virginia’s African population fell from 32 in 1620 to 21 in 1624, then rose again after the 1628 arrival of about 100 Angolans taken by the *Fortune*. Some Africans gained freedom and property, including Anthony and Mary Johnson, who owned 250 acres on the Eastern Shore by the 1650s. The legal definitions of hereditary slavery came later; Chapter 5 owns Virginia’s 1662 maternal-status statute and the wider consolidation.[^ch04-first-africans][^ch04-check-africans]
- **The distinction the chapter must keep.** The chapter should say clearly that 1619 marks the arrival of Africans in the English Chesapeake, not the codification of racial slavery, and that the labor system of 1619 was a continuum of servitude that later law hardened. Do not equate the two.[^ch04-check-africans]
- **The transition.** While Virginia built its assembly and its labor regime, a second English colony to the north organized itself around a covenant and a Native alliance. §3 turns to Plymouth.

## 3. Plymouth and the Wampanoag alliance

### 3.1 The voyage, the Compact, and the first winter

- **The claim.** Plymouth was founded by religious Separatists and others, financed and legalized uncertainly, and saved by the Compact, by a Native alliance, and by an economy the settlers built after the first winter. It was not a model of religious toleration.
- **The voyage.** *Mayflower* left England in July 1620, turned back twice because the *Speedwell* leaked, and finally sailed on September 6, 1620. It carried 102 passengers. A storm damaged the ship; a passenger, John Howland, was swept overboard and rescued. The ship reached Cape Cod on November 11, 1620.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **The settlers.** The core group were Separatists who had left the Church of England; others remained in the Church of England. Not all shared the same religion, which is why a civil covenant, not a church covenant, was needed.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **The first winter.** About half the passengers died during the first winter from a “general sickness” of colds, coughs, and fevers. The colonists lived aboard ship until enough houses existed; *Mayflower* left for England on April 5, 1621.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **The consequence.** Plymouth’s survival depended less on the Compact alone than on the Wampanoag alliance that followed, and on a working economy of fur, fish, and maize.

**In Focus.** The Mayflower Compact survives as a text without an original. Its manuscript is lost; the agreement first appeared in print in *Mourt’s Relation* (1622), a promotional pamphlet about Plymouth’s first year, and was reprinted in 1669 by Nathaniel Morton in *New England’s Memorial*, which added a possible list of signers that earlier versions lacked. Read closely, the Compact is a conditional and temporary instrument: the signers call themselves loyal subjects of King James, explain that they have “undertaken for the Glory of God and advancement of the Christian Faith and Honour of our King and Country” a voyage “to plant the First Colony in the Northern Parts of Virginia,” and agree to “Covenant and Combine ourselves together into a Civill body politick” to make “just and equall Lawes” for “the general good of the Colony,” to which they promise “all due submission and obedience.” The phrase “into a Civill body politick” is the closest its surviving text comes to a theory of self-government, and its logic is practical: outside their patent, the settlers needed a body with authority to make law. The comparison of the surviving versions is the point. *Mourt’s Relation* gives the agreement without a signer list; Morton supplies names that later scholarship has had to test against other records. The chapter should teach the Compact as a document transmitted through print and manuscript, and should not treat Morton’s list as a signature page or the lost original as recoverable.[^ch04-mayflower-compact][^ch04-check-compact]

### 3.2 The 1621 alliance

- **The claim.** The 1621 treaty between Plymouth and Ousamequin (Massasoit) was a strategic alliance between peoples, not a friendship. Chapter 3 owns the Wampanoag side and the epidemic that motivated it; this chapter reads it as the colonists’ foreign policy.
- **The terms and interpretation.** The treaty bound each side to keep peace and to defend the other against attacks. The Wampanoag sought protection against the Narragansett after the 1616–19 epidemic; the English sought survival. The alliance is discussed in Chapter 3, §2 and should not be re-narrated here.[^ch04-check-ch3]
- **The duration.** The treaty held, with tensions, until after Ousamequin’s death in 1661; the war that followed belongs to Chapter 6. This chapter should end its account at the alliance and the peace it bought.[^ch04-check-ch3]
- **The consequence.** The alliance was real, contingent, and unequal in its long effects, opening the way for English expansion even as it protected the Wampanoag in the short term.

### 3.3 Economy and society

- **The claim.** Plymouth’s economy diversified and its society became a set of self-governing towns. Neither the Compact nor the alliance made the colony self-sufficient; trade did.
- **The economy.** Plymouth’s economy combined agriculture, fishing, and the fur trade. Massachusetts’s early economy rested on fishing, shipbuilding, and trade, and Plymouth’s settlers also traded with Native people and with other English colonies.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The government.** Plymouth functioned as a collection of towns with a general court and elected officers, developing colonial self-government in practice rather than from a single charter. Its legal standing remained uncertain until it was incorporated into Massachusetts in 1691.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The limits.** Free male church members held the most voice; women, servants, and Native people did not. Plymouth tolerated religious difference within limits and excluded those outside them, foreshadowing Massachusetts Bay’s sharper conflicts.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The consequence.** Plymouth’s importance is as much exemplary as material: it supplied the Compact, the alliance, and the Thanksgiving story, and it demonstrated both the reach and the boundaries of colonial self-rule.

### 3.4 What Plymouth wanted for itself

- **The claim.** Plymouth’s founders sought freedom to worship as they believed, not a general freedom of conscience. The same pattern appears in Massachusetts Bay and is the chapter’s recurring theme.
- **The Separatists.** The Plymouth Separatists had separated from the Church of England; the colony’s religious establishment was their own. Roger Williams later found Plymouth more congenial than Massachusetts but still too restrictive.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The forward link.** The pattern — a colony of the godly that defines and punishes dissent — becomes explicit in §4 and §5. Plymouth supplies the first English version of it.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The transition.** In 1630 a larger, chartered, and better-financed Puritan colony began at Massachusetts Bay. §4 turns to its design.

## 4. Massachusetts Bay

### 4.1 The 1629 charter and the transfer

- **The claim.** Massachusetts Bay was a chartered company that became a self-governing commonwealth because its charter did not require the company to remain in England. Law, land, and religion were combined in a single corporate frame.
- **The charter.** On March 4, 1629, Charles I granted a corporate charter to the Massachusetts Bay Company to settle between the Charles and Merrimack rivers and to trade. It named a governor, deputy governor, eighteen assistants, and a General Court of the freemen to make laws.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The crucial absence.** Unlike most charters, the 1629 grant did not require the company to hold its meetings in England. The leaders moved the government and the charter itself to New England, which let the colony govern itself as an English corporation.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The leadership.** John Winthrop was elected governor in England in 1629, replacing the company’s first named governor, Matthew Cradock. The Winthrop Fleet of sixteen ships sailed in 1630; eleven were company-funded and carried roughly 700 to 1,000 emigrants. Winthrop traveled on the flagship *Arbella*.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The consequence.** Massachusetts’s self-rule was a legal accident as much as a design, and the colony used it to build a religious commonwealth that excluded more people than it admitted.

### 4.2 The Great Migration and the puritan purpose

- **The claim.** Between 1630 and 1640, about 20,000 puritans migrated to New England, driven by religious conflict in England and by the prospect of a godly commonwealth. Migration was a family and community movement, not only a religious one.
- **The scale.** Estimates place at least 20,000 puritans arriving in Massachusetts during the Great Migration. Charles I dissolved Parliament in March 1629 and ruled without it for eleven years, which deepened puritan alarm and emigration.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The purpose.** The puritans of Massachusetts Bay sought freedom to practice their faith, not religious liberty for all. Francis J. Bremer writes that they “were not coming to America to promote religious freedom for all, but to achieve for themselves a freedom” from English church and civil officials.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The economic side.** Migration also had an economic dimension. Emigration drew on a mobile English population and on the attraction of land; the Massachusetts economy depended on fishing, shipbuilding, and trade from the start.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The consequence.** The Great Migration created a society of towns whose members had to be admitted, whose ministers had to be supported, and whose boundaries of belief had to be enforced.

### 4.3 Winthrop’s “citty upon a hill” and its manuscript

- **The claim.** Winthrop’s lay sermon is the best-known statement of the colony’s purpose and the most overread. Its single surviving manuscript is not in Winthrop’s hand, its delivery is disputed, and the text as printed may be incomplete.
- **The text.** The sermon calls on the colonists to be “knitt together, in this worke, as one man,” warns that “if wee shall deale falsely with our God in this worke wee haue undertaken … wee shall be made a story and a by-word through the world,” and concludes that “wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us.” The famous line is a warning, not a boast.[^ch04-winthrop-model]
- **The manuscript.** Only one contemporary copy survives, at the New-York Historical Society. It is not in Winthrop’s hand; a pasted-on cover sheet, probably from the nineteenth century, claimed the sermon was written aboard the *Arbella*. Scholars now doubt that attribution.[^ch04-winthrop-manuscript]
- **The debates.** Abram Van Engen argues that the surviving copy is “corrupt” and may be missing its opening text, which he identifies as Galatians 5:13–14. Daniel Rodgers and Richard Gamble argue the text was a treatise, not a sermon. Jerome McGann argues a layman did not preach it. Francis J. Bremer argues that it was preached, by Winthrop, probably at Southampton before departure, on the same occasion as John Cotton’s departure sermon.[^ch04-winthrop-manuscript]
- **The rule for the chapter.** The chapter should quote the sermon as a text, explain that its delivery circumstances are contested, and not date the “citty upon a hill” line to a shipboard moment the evidence does not support.[^ch04-check-dating]
- **The contract.** The sermon’s argument is that the colony is bound to God by covenant, with blessings for faithfulness and ruin for betrayal. Its social teaching is corporate and unequal: mercy to the poor, submission of the mean, and unity under God’s providence.[^ch04-winthrop-model]
- **The consequence.** The “citty upon a hill” phrase has become an American civic text while its original meaning — a warning to a covenant people — has narrowed. The chapter should restore the context without dismissing the phrase’s later career.

### 4.4 The godly commonwealth

- **The claim.** Massachusetts Bay was a self-governing colony in which political and religious membership were linked. Its towns, its General Court, and its Congregational churches distributed authority — and narrowed it.
- **The government.** The charter’s General Court, made up of freemen, became the colony’s legislature. The governorship and the assistants concentrated early power; towns governed local affairs and distributed land. Boston became the capital in 1632.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The churches.** The colony’s churches were Congregational and independent of the Church of England. Church membership was tested and limited, and the colony’s leaders treated religious unity as necessary to its civil covenant.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The exclusions.** Women, servants, the enslaved, Native people, and non-members stood outside political voice. Dissenters were expected to conform or leave.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The “city” in practice.** The colony valued literacy and education for religious reasons: the Boston Latin Grammar School opened in 1635 and Harvard College in 1636. Public education and a printing press followed within two decades.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The consequence.** Massachusetts was a real experiment in self-government and a real experiment in exclusion. Both features are part of the same design, and §5 shows what happened when the boundary was tested.

## 5. Dissent and the Pequot War

### 5.1 Roger Williams

- **The claim.** Roger Williams challenged the colony’s authority over religion, the legitimacy of its charter, and the established church, and was driven out. His expulsion helped create Rhode Island.
- **The person.** Williams arrived in New England in 1631 and was welcomed by Winthrop as “a godly minister.” He argued that godly magistrates should not compel ungodly people, questioned the red cross on the English flag, and denied that the king could grant Massachusetts land he did not own.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The charges.** In April 1635, the General Court ordered Williams to stop promulgating his views; he refused. He was accused of sedition and heresy and ordered banished. He fled in winter 1635–36, took refuge with the Narragansett, and purchased land for Providence, the settlement that grew into Rhode Island.[^ch04-dissent][^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The principle.** Williams’s position was not modern secularism; he believed error should be refuted, not tolerated for its own sake. But he insisted that civil power should not enforce religious orthodoxy, and he grounded Native land title in Native possession rather than royal grant.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The consequence.** The colony’s treatment of Williams established that its religious settlement would be enforced. It also produced the colony that later sheltered more dissenters.

### 5.2 Anne Hutchinson and the Antinomian Controversy

- **The claim.** Anne Hutchinson challenged the authority of the colony’s ministers and was tried, banished, and excommunicated. Her case shows how the godly commonwealth handled a religious challenge from a woman who claimed direct spiritual authority.
- **The person.** Hutchinson arrived in Boston in 1633, worked as a midwife and healer, and held weekly meetings to discuss religion. Her following grew to include the new governor, Sir Henry Vane, and alarmed the ministers, who labeled the group “Antinomians” — heretics who believed they were not bound by law and custom because of direct communion with God.[^ch04-hutchinson-mhs]
- **The trial.** In 1637 Hutchinson was tried for failing to honor the colony’s ministers and for teaching without authority. John Winthrop called her the “American Jezebel.” She was banished from Massachusetts Bay and, in a separate proceeding, excommunicated. She moved to Rhode Island and later to Long Island, where she was killed in a Native attack in 1643.[^ch04-hutchinson-mhs]
- **The argument.** The controversy concerned the relationship between grace, works, and the authority of the ministry. Hutchinson’s claim to inner illumination seemed to the ministers to dissolve the need for their teaching and for the law.[^ch04-dissent]
- **The gendered dimension.** The colony’s leaders treated a woman’s public teaching as itself a disorder. The trial and the language used against Hutchinson show how religious authority and gender authority were entangled.[^ch04-hutchinson-mhs]
- **The consequence.** The Antinomian Controversy clarified the colony’s boundaries: grace was orthodox; independent spiritual authority was not. The controversy belongs to the chapter’s larger question of whose freedom counted.

### 5.3 The Pequot War

- **The claim.** The Pequot War (1636–37) destroyed the Pequot as a regional power and was driven by competition for trade, land, and the punishment of English deaths. Its climax at Mystic was a massacre of a fortified village, and its settlement enslaved survivors.
- **The Pequot.** In the early seventeenth century, about 8,000 Pequot lived in southeastern Connecticut. An epidemic in the early 1630s, probably smallpox, reduced them to about 4,000 by 1635. Through warfare, diplomacy, and marriage, the Pequot had built a regional confederacy controlling the fur and wampum trade and exercising influence from Long Island Sound into the Connecticut Valley.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **The trade rivalry.** Dutch traders dominated the fur and wampum trade between 1611 and 1633. English traders entered the Connecticut Valley in 1633, and the struggle to break the Dutch-Pequot monopoly set the war’s conditions.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **The precipitating deaths.** In 1634, the Pequot killed the Virginia trader John Stone and his crew on the Connecticut River. In 1636, they killed the English captain John Oldham off Block Island. Massachusetts demanded the culprits; the Pequot did not surrender them.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **The escalation.** In August 1636, Massachusetts sent 90 men under John Endicott to Block Island and then to the Thames River, where they attacked and burned a Pequot village. The Pequot laid siege to the English settlement at Saybrook Point for seven months and, on April 23, 1637, attacked Wethersfield, killing nine men and two women.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **The declaration and the force.** On May 1, 1637, Connecticut’s General Court ordered an “Offensive War against the Pequot” and levied 90 soldiers under Captain John Mason. Mason’s force, joined by Mohegan, Wangunk, and other allies and by 250 Narragansett, totaled 77 English and more than 300 Native fighters.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **Mystic.** Before dawn on May 26, 1637, the force attacked the fortified village at Mistick. Within two hours, more than 400 Pequot men, women, and children were dead, about 200 of them burned; between 150 and 200 warriors were killed. The English and their allies reported killing more Pequot during their retreat.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **The settlement.** The war ended with the 1638 Treaty of Hartford, which outlawed the Pequot name and language, seized their lands, and dispersed or enslaved the survivors. Many were sold into slavery, including into the West Indies; Massachusetts’s involvement in the slave trade is often dated from this episode.[^ch04-pequot-war][^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The consequence.** The war was not a border skirmish but the destruction of a Native polity and the seizure of its land. Its survivors and descendants persist as the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation and the Eastern Pequot, and the war belongs to the chapter as the military face of English expansion.[^ch04-pequot-war]

### 5.4 The cost of the English order

- **The claim.** By 1640 the two colonial centers had defined the boundaries of their freedom: Virginia through labor and land, New England through church and town. Both had expanded by war and dispossession.
- **The pattern.** Virginia fought three wars with the Powhatan peoples between 1609 and 1646. New England’s Pequot War was followed by a settlement that made the Connecticut River Valley English. The two regions were different and shared one method.[^ch04-early-jamestown][^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **The transition.** By 1640 the English had established self-governing colonies with elected assemblies, importable labor, and a working relation to Native dispossession. §6 asks what explains them.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The colonies were different and connected.** Virginia and Massachusetts Bay were both company foundations that became self-governing, but they organized religion, labor, and land differently and traded within one Atlantic economy.[^ch04-virginia-company][^ch04-mass-bay]
- **Both claimed freedom for a bounded group.** Virginia’s assembly and New England’s towns created self-government for propertied and church members, not for all.[^ch04-house-of-burgesses][^ch04-dissent]
- **Tobacco and trade drove expansion.** The Chesapeake’s economy was built on tobacco and coerced labor; New England’s on fishing, shipbuilding, and trade.[^ch04-tobacco][^ch04-mass-bay]
- **The first Africans arrived in 1619, and the law of hereditary slavery came later.** The records show buying and selling and the word “servant,” not a codified racial slave system, in the 1610s and 1620s.[^ch04-first-africans][^ch04-africans-jamestowne]
- **The Pequot War was catastrophic and consequential.** The Mystic attack killed more than 400 people, and the 1638 settlement dispossessed and enslaved the survivors.[^ch04-pequot-mystic][^ch04-pequot-war]
- **Winthrop’s sermon is a single, later manuscript copy, and its delivery is disputed.** No eyewitness account of the sermon’s delivery survives.[^ch04-winthrop-manuscript]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Why Virginia nearly failed.** Idleness, poor siting, drought, disease, and leadership each have advocates; the weight among them is not settled. Kupperman’s malnutrition argument competes with the colonists’ own idleness explanation.[^ch04-starving-time]
- **Whether cannibalism occurred at Jamestown.** Archaeologists report evidence on one skull; historians, including Herrmann, caution that the textual accounts had interested authors and that the question remains open.[^ch04-starving-time]
- **The Mayflower Compact’s signers.** The lost original and the varying printed lists leave the exact signatories uncertain.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **Whether Winthrop delivered the sermon, and where.** Bremer argues for Southampton before departure; Rodgers and Gamble call the text a treatise; McGann doubts lay preaching; Van Engen reads the surviving copy as corrupt. No position is settled.[^ch04-winthrop-manuscript]
- **The precise causes of the Pequot War.** The trade monopoly, the punishment of English deaths, Pequot diplomacy, and English expansion all figure; scholars weigh them differently, and the English and Native accounts describe different wars.[^ch04-pequot-mystic]
- **How fast, and how deliberately, racial slavery was made.** Morgan locates the hardening in the later seventeenth century; this chapter deliberately stops before the legal statutes, which Chapter 5 owns.[^ch04-check-africans][^ch04-morgan]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The choice is not between religion and economics, nor between freedom and slavery as separate stories. The evidence supports a history in which English colonists pursued faith, profit, and self-government together, and in which their freedom for some was built on the labor and dispossession of others.
- **Perry Miller’s contribution and limit.** Miller’s *The New England Mind* treated puritan ideas as a coherent intellectual system and took religion seriously as a cause. Critics note that his “New England Mind” spoke mainly for church members and ministers and slighted the majority who were not full members, as well as women, Native people, and the enslaved.[^ch04-miller]
- **The commercial corrective.** The Virginia Company’s charter, the headright system, the tobacco trade, and New England’s fishing and trade show that these were economic ventures as well as religious ones. Commercial explanations restore the market, but they can reduce belief to interest.[^ch04-virginia-company][^ch04-indentured-servants]
- **Morgan’s paradox.** Edmund S. Morgan, a student of Miller’s, argued that Virginia’s elite developed a commitment to liberty for White men alongside a system of racial slavery, calling the combination “the central paradox of American history.” His emphasis on labor, race, and class connects the Chesapeake’s freedom to its unfreedom.[^ch04-morgan]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, the positions say that the colonies were experiments in both belief and interest, and that “freedom” was defined locally by who was excluded. The chapter should give each position its strongest form and show what its evidence can and cannot bear.[^ch04-miller][^ch04-morgan]
- **The lesson.** The best history refuses the single-cause story. Virginia was not merely a business, New England was not merely a church, and neither was a simple promise of liberty.[^ch04-check-sources]

## Legacies

The English colonies left two competing origin stories. One begins in 1619, with the first Africans and the first elected assembly in the same year; the other begins in 1620, with the Mayflower Compact and the “citty upon a hill.” The chapter shows that the two stories are inseparable: the Chesapeake’s freedom came with the buying and selling of human beings, and New England’s self-government came with the expulsion of dissenters and the destruction of the Pequot.[^ch04-first-africans][^ch04-mayflower-compact][^ch04-pequot-mystic]

The Mayflower Compact’s later career is a study in memory. Invoked as an origin of American self-government, it was in fact a temporary agreement among a few men outside their patent, transmitted through later printings after the loss of its original. Its elevation reflects a national preference for founding documents over the unfinished struggles around them.[^ch04-mayflower-compact][^ch04-check-compact]

The Pequot War’s legacies are material and living. The 1638 Treaty of Hartford tried to abolish the Pequot name and language and gave their lands to Connecticut; survivors and their descendants persist in Connecticut today. The war shows that the English colonies were built through conquest as well as covenant, and that the survivors of conquest remained.[^ch04-pequot-war]

The legacies are double, as in earlier chapters. The same decades produced the first elected assembly in English North America and the sharpening of servitude, the first African communities in English Virginia and the legal silence that left their status open, the aspiration to a godly commonwealth and the machinery that excluded the ungodly. The chapter should end with both possibilities intact and unresolved, not with a verdict about which was “really” America.

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The colonies were nodes in an Atlantic economy of tobacco, fish, fur, and enslaved people, and they sat within English rivalry with Spain and the Netherlands. The Pequot War began in a contest among Dutch, English, and Native traders; Virginia’s first Africans arrived through the capture of a Portuguese slaver on its way to New Spain.[^ch04-pequot-mystic][^ch04-first-africans]
- **Archives and silences.** The first Africans are known largely through the records of the people who bought them; the Wampanoag side of the alliance is filtered through English chronicles; the Mayflower Compact’s original is lost; the Pequot appear in English accounts of their own destruction. The chapter should name these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch04-first-africans][^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 3 supplies Native political worlds and the 1621 alliance; Chapter 5 owns the Middle Colonies and the legal consolidation of racial slavery; Chapter 6 owns King Philip’s War and the imperial turn; Chapters 9–12 return to themes of representation and covenant rooted here. This chapter’s job is to establish the English colonies as contested orders of freedom and unfreedom.[^ch04-check-ch3][^ch04-check-ch5]

## Summary

- Virginia began as a joint-stock company and nearly failed; the winter of 1609–1610 killed about three-quarters of the Jamestown colonists, and recovery came through martial law and tobacco.[^ch04-virginia-company][^ch04-starving-time]
- Tobacco made Virginia profitable and created a lasting demand for coerced labor; exports rose from about 20,000 pounds in 1617 to about 60,000 in 1622.[^ch04-tobacco]
- The 1619 General Assembly, meeting July 30 to August 4, was the first elected legislature in English North America, created by a company charter and limited by the governor and council.[^ch04-house-of-burgesses][^ch04-first-legislative-assembly]
- The Great Charter of 1618 created the headright system and, with it, a land-and-labor regime built on importing servants.[^ch04-early-jamestown]
- Indentured servitude in Virginia was harsher than English servitude; servants could be bought and sold, and most died before completing their terms.[^ch04-indentured-servants]
- About twenty Africans were sold at Point Comfort in late August 1619, but Virginia had no law codifying hereditary racial slavery; that law came later.[^ch04-first-africans][^ch04-africans-jamestowne]
- The Mayflower Compact (1620) was a civil agreement among adult men outside their patent; its original is lost, and it survives through later printings.[^ch04-mayflower-compact]
- Plymouth survived through the 1621 Wampanoag alliance and a diversified economy; it sought freedom for its own faith, not for all.[^ch04-check-ch3][^ch04-mass-bay]
- Massachusetts Bay’s 1629 charter, lacking a residency requirement, let the company become a self-governing commonwealth; about 20,000 puritans migrated in the 1630s.[^ch04-mass-bay]
- Winthrop’s “citty upon a hill” came from a single, later manuscript copy of a sermon whose delivery is disputed.[^ch04-winthrop-model][^ch04-winthrop-manuscript]
- Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson were expelled for challenging the colony’s religious authority; their cases defined the godly commonwealth’s boundaries.[^ch04-dissent][^ch04-hutchinson-mhs]
- The Pequot War ended in the massacre at Mystic, where more than 400 died, and the 1638 Treaty of Hartford dispossessed and enslaved the survivors.[^ch04-pequot-mystic][^ch04-pequot-war]

## Study Questions

1. What did the Virginia Company set out to accomplish, and how did its goals change between 1606 and 1624?
2. Why did Jamestown nearly fail? Weigh drought, disease, leadership, and English dependence on Native food, and explain which explanation the evidence best supports.
3. Who could vote or hold office under the 1619 General Assembly, and who could not? Cite the assembly’s own record and the charter behind it.
4. Compare indentured servitude in England and in Virginia. What made the Virginia version harsher, and who profited from the difference?
5. The chapter distinguishes the 1619 arrival of Africans from the codification of hereditary slavery. Why does that distinction matter, and what evidence supports it?
6. Read the Mayflower Compact’s text. What did the signers actually agree to, and what does the document leave out? What can the loss of the original tell you about how historians work?
7. Was the 1621 Wampanoag alliance a friendship, a treaty, or a temporary bargain? Use at least two sources.
8. How did Massachusetts Bay’s 1629 charter allow the colony to govern itself, and what limits did the charter place on that self-rule?
9. Winthrop’s sermon survives in one copy, not in his hand. How should that affect the way a historian uses the “citty upon a hill”?
10. Compare the banishment of Roger Williams with the banishment of Anne Hutchinson. What did each challenge, and what did the colony’s response reveal about its definition of freedom?
11. How did the Pequot War begin, and what did the 1638 settlement do? Whose accounts of the war survive, and whose do not?
12. Choose one source cited in this chapter. Identify its author, audience, purpose, and silences. What can it establish, and what can it not?

Miller, Bailyn, Morgan’s book, and Brown were consulted through summaries or reference works, not read in full; their entries say so.

### Company records and period voices

- **Encyclopedia Virginia, “Virginia Company of London,” “Early Jamestown Settlement,” and “The Starving Time.”** Short, sourced syntheses of the company, the founding, and the 1609–10 catastrophe. They are encyclopedia entries, not monographs, and the Starving Time entry carefully presents the cannibalism debate.
- **Encyclopedia Virginia, “Indentured Servants in Colonial Virginia,” “Tobacco in Colonial Virginia,” and “House of Burgesses.”** Clear accounts of the colony’s labor, staple, and assembly. They quote Morgan on servitude and give the burgesses’ dates and powers.
- **John Winthrop, “A Modell of Christian Charity” (1630),** transcript at Hanover College, and **Francis J. Bremer, “Sermon? Treatise? or not by Winthrop at all?”** The text of the sermon and a scholar’s guide to the manuscript and delivery debates. Read them together.

### Surveys and syntheses

- **Plimoth Patuxet Museums, “Mayflower and Mayflower Compact.”** The Compact’s text, its transmission through *Mourt’s Relation* and Morton, and the voyage and first winter. A museum account aimed at students, reliable on basics.
- **American History Central, “Massachusetts Bay Colony.”** A broad reference account of the 1629 charter, the Great Migration, and the colony’s government. It is a popular reference and contains at least one apparent date error, so verify specifics against the charter.
- **Connecticut History (CTHumanities), “Pequot War.”** A concise institutional account of the war and the 1638 Treaty of Hartford, useful for the settlement’s terms.

### Specialized studies

- **Kevin McBride and Laurie Pasteryak Lamarre, “Exploring and Uncovering the Pequot War,” *Connecticut Explored* (Fall 2013).** A detailed account of Pequot power, the trade rivalry, the Mystic attack, and its casualties, by the archaeologists of the Battlefields of the Pequot War project.
- **Martha McCartney, “Virginia’s First Africans” and “Angela (fl. 1619–1625),” Encyclopedia Virginia.** The most detailed open account of the 1619 arrivals, their African origins, and the documentary traces of individual lives. It relies on John K. Thornton and Engel Sluiter for the origins.
- **Historic Jamestowne, “The First Africans.”** A concise account of the *São João Bautista*’s capture and the arrival at Point Comfort, stressing that Virginia had no clear-cut slavery law in 1619.

### The debate

- **Perry Miller, *The New England Mind: The Seventeenth Century* (1939).** The founding statement of puritan intellectual history and the target of later revision. Consulted here through a reference summary; read the book before quoting its argument.
- **Bernard Bailyn, *The New England Merchants in the Seventeenth Century* (1955).** The classic commercial interpretation of New England. Named here as the commercial position; discovered but not yet consulted.
- **Edmund S. Morgan, *American Slavery, American Freedom: The Ordeal of Colonial Virginia* (1975).** The book that named the freedom-and-slavery paradox. It is summarized in the sources cited below and should be read directly before its argument is stated as fact.

> *A closing note.* The chapter is framed by two documents that look like origins and are not. The Mayflower Compact survives in later printings, not in the original the colonists signed, and it bound a few men in a temporary civil body outside their patent. In 1619, Virginia’s first elected assembly met in the same year and season that the first Africans were sold at Point Comfort, and the colony did not yet have a law of hereditary slavery. Both episodes are smaller, stranger, and more contested than their later fame suggests, and together they show that English America’s freedom and unfreedom were made in the same decades. The chapter ends with two colonial orders established, each claiming liberty for its own, each built on labor and land taken from others.[^ch04-mayflower-compact][^ch04-first-africans]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with the Dutch West India Company’s records of the first African laborers in New Netherland and asks how a labor regime became hereditary racial slavery. This chapter ends with Virginia’s servants and first Africans and with the legal question left open; Chapter 5 takes up the Middle Colonies, the Caribbean, and the laws of the 1660s that answered it.[^ch04-check-ch5][^ch04-first-africans]
