# Chapter 5. The Middle Colonies, the Caribbean, and the Origins of Racial Slavery (1620s–1680s)

> “We, Willem Kieft, director general, and the council of New Netherland, having considered the petition of the Negroes named Paulo Angola, Big Manuel, Little Manuel, Manuel de Gerrit de Reus, Simon Congo, Antony Portuguese, Gracia, Piter Santomee, Jan Francisco, Little Antony and Jan Fort Orange, who have served the Company for 18 or 19 years, that they may be released from their servitude and be made free.”
>
> — Resolution of the Council of New Netherland, February 25, 1644, English translation in the New Netherland Institute’s digital exhibition *Slavery in New Netherland*[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]

> “WHEREAS some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree, Be it therefore enacted and declared by this present grand assembly, that all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother.”
>
> — Virginia General Assembly, “Negro womens children to serve according to the condition of the mother,” December 1662, in William Waller Hening, ed., *The Statutes at Large*, vol. 2, p. 170[^ch05-ev-1662]

> “Barbados, the beautiful Caribbean island known today for its social amiability and political civility, was Britain’s colonial site of the first ‘black slave society’ — the most systemically violent, brutal and racially inhumane society of modernity.”
>
> — Sir Hilary McD. Beckles, *The First Black Slave Society: Britain’s “Barbarity Time” in Barbados* (Kingston: University of the West Indies Press, 2016), preface[^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

The three quotations disagree about what slavery was. The 1644 resolution treats enslavement as a service that might end. The 1662 statute makes bondage pass through the mother, so that it can be inherited. Beckles locates the model of a hereditary, racialized slave society in Barbados, not in the Chesapeake. Together they frame the chapter’s problem: how did a labor regime become hereditary racial slavery?

The record of February 25, 1644, is a council resolution, not a description of a ceremony. It names 11 men and says that they and their wives had petitioned the council of New Netherland. They had served the Dutch West India Company for 18 or 19 years. They claimed the company had promised them freedom and asked to be released so they could provide for their families. The company granted a conditional freedom that historians call half-freedom.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]

The men would pay yearly dues of “30 skepels of maize, wheat, peas or beans, and one fat hog.” They would assist the company whenever it needed them. Their children, including those not yet born, would remain enslaved. If they failed to pay, they would be re-enslaved. The company gave them plots of land north of New Amsterdam so that they could support their families and meet the dues. The council declared that if they kept these terms they would be “free and at liberty on the same footing as other free people here in New Netherland.”[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]

Half-freedom was therefore neither freedom nor ordinary slavery. It kept the men bound to company service and their children in bondage. The company enforced it unevenly: it later claimed that only three children remained enslaved, and some parents petitioned successfully for children in their care.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]

The men are known through records kept by their owners: court minutes, land deeds, church registers, and official correspondence. They left no written records of their own. The earliest enslaved Africans reached New Netherland in the mid-1620s. The New Netherland Institute dates the first laborers to 1625 or 1626; the Museum of the City of New York says 1624. No single landing can be dated securely, and the chapter does not stage one.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant][^ch05-check-scene]

What the record does show is a labor regime that was real but not yet hereditary. Enslaved people in New Netherland worked as caulkers, blacksmiths, bricklayers, and masons. They lived, worked, and worshipped beside free White settlers. Some earned wages, owned property, married and baptized children in the Dutch Reformed Church, obtained conditional freedom, and received farmland in Manhattan.[^ch05-nni-slavery]

That is the baseline the rest of the chapter measures against. New Netherland had bondage without a hereditary, racialized code. Virginia and Maryland would build the code. Barbados would build it first and most completely. How did a labor regime become hereditary racial slavery?

This chapter follows the Middle Colonies, the Caribbean sugar islands, and the Chesapeake from the 1620s to the 1680s. It draws on Dutch company records and court cases, Swedish and Dutch colonial papers, Caribbean and mainland session laws, the printed statutes of Virginia, the legal records of Maryland, the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, and Quaker meeting records. It treats “servant,” “slave,” “Christian,” and “Negro” as legal and social categories that changed over the period. It distinguishes the arrival of Africans from the legal creation of hereditary racial slavery and names the silences kept by the archives of slavery.[^ch05-check-names][^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 sets out New Netherland, its enslaved laborers, and New Sweden on the Delaware. §2 turns to Barbados, Jamaica, and the Atlantic trade that made sugar and slavery one enterprise. §3 examines the Chesapeake’s turn from servants to enslaved Africans and Bacon’s Rebellion. §4 compares Maryland, Carolina, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware. §5 reads the statutes that made bondage heritable and permanent, from Virginia’s 1662 act to the code of 1705. §6 explains the debate over whether race, class, gender, or law best explains the origins of racial slavery.

## 1. New Netherland and New Sweden

### 1.1 A company colony and its enslaved laborers

New Netherland was a trading colony, not a plantation colony, but slavery was present from its first years, and enslaved people built its infrastructure. The Dutch West India Company organized the colony, founded New Amsterdam on Manhattan, and made it the seat of government.[^ch05-nni-slavery] The colony had no cash crop, but its enslaved laborers were valuable to its growth. They worked as caulkers making boats watertight, as blacksmiths, bricklayers, and masons, and on company and private farms that fed the colony and grew food for export.[^ch05-nni-slavery]

Most of the colony’s enslaved people came through the intercolonial or transatlantic trade, often in small groups on company ships or privateers that had taken captives from Portuguese or Spanish ships. A large majority were of Central African origin, especially from the region of Angola, where the Dutch held Luanda from 1641 to 1648.[^ch05-nni-slavery] The trade was small by Caribbean standards, but it was not incidental. In 1655 a company ship, *Witte Paert*, anchored in the East River with almost 300 enslaved Africans for local buyers. By the mid-1660s some 700 enslaved people lived in New Netherland, about 300 of them in New Amsterdam, where they made up nearly 20 percent of the town’s population. About 75 free Black people lived in the colony at the same time.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]

The law had not caught up with the trade. New Netherland had no comprehensive slave code; its early development, as the New Netherland Institute puts it, “was not carefully directed, and the institution of slavery itself was not clearly defined.” Enslaved people could own property, receive wages, and petition for their freedom. In 1639, five men asked the company to pay them 8 guilders per month they had earned; four years later, Manuel claimed 15 guilders in wages, and Pedro Negretto demanded maize for tending hogs.[^ch05-nni-slavery]

These possibilities had limits. The wages were often unpaid, the property was small, and the freedom was conditional. The 1644 grant made that clear: it gave 11 men and their wives land and a measure of legal standing, but it kept their children in slavery and made their freedom contingent on yearly payments and service on demand.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom]

Peter Stuyvesant, the colony’s longest-serving and last Dutch director, governed from 1647 until the English seized the colony in 1664. He expanded its role in the Atlantic trade in enslaved Africans and made New Amsterdam more orderly, though he had to compromise with leading residents and Indigenous inhabitants.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant] The rise of slavery in New Netherland was not inevitable. It was the product of decisions by administrators and colonists who wanted labor and profit, and of a legal order that had not yet fixed the status of the people they bought.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]

### 1.2 The Swedes, the Dutch, and the Delaware valley

Sweden planted a short-lived colony on the Delaware in 1638, and the Dutch absorbed it in 1655. The New Sweden Company was formed in 1637 by Dutch, German, and Swedish investors. Peter Minuit, the former Dutch director, led its first expedition. Minuit and his settlers sailed from Gothenburg in December 1637 and landed on the Delaware in the spring of 1638. They bought from five Lenape chiefs 67 miles of riverfront centering on the Christina River, and they built Fort Christina.[^ch05-psu-newsweden]

New Sweden was a small trading and farming colony of Swedish and Finnish settlers.[^ch05-psu-newsweden] Slavery was present from the start. When Minuit sailed for Gothenburg in June 1639, he left at Fort Christina 23 soldiers, a commissioner, and Antonius, an enslaved Black man.[^ch05-psu-newsweden] The Delaware valley’s European colonies did not begin as free-labor societies and then acquire slavery later. Unfree labor arrived with the first settlements.

The Dutch under Stuyvesant conquered New Sweden in 1655 and absorbed it into New Netherland. In 1664 the English took New Netherland, and the Delaware valley passed to English proprietors. The region’s European governments changed; its dependence on unfree labor did not.[^ch05-psu-newsweden][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant] The Middle Colonies were a patchwork of charters and conquests, and slavery existed in all of them; that variety, not an absence of slavery, sets up the comparison in §4.

### 1.3 The English conquest and the persistence of slavery

In 1664 an English fleet under Richard Nicolls arrived at New Amsterdam. Stuyvesant tore up the letter demanding surrender, but a crowd of townspeople forced him to put it back together and read it aloud. On August 27, representatives met at his farm and negotiated the surrender. Fort Amsterdam became Fort James, and New Amsterdam became New York.[^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]

The English did not free the Dutch colony’s enslaved people. They inherited an enslaved population and a port already involved in the Atlantic slave trade.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant] The scale of that trade was visible on the eve of conquest. On August 15, 1664, the slave ship *Gideon* arrived with 290 enslaved people, 153 men and 137 women. Of about 421 captives boarded at Loango, 348 survived to Curaçao, and 10 more died on the final passage.[^ch05-nni-slavery]

Some half-free men, fearing re-enslavement under English rule, petitioned for full freedom as the English ships lay in the harbor. They received it and kept their status as freedmen in English New York.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom] But the institution passed from Dutch to English rule, and the legal hardening to come would reach the Middle Colonies too. The Middle Colonies belonged to the same Atlantic story as the Chesapeake and the islands, though they lacked a plantation staple.[^ch05-check-names]

## 2. Barbados, Jamaica, and the Atlantic trade

### 2.1 The sugar revolution in Barbados

Barbados converted from tobacco and White indentured labor to sugar and enslaved African labor in the middle decades of the seventeenth century. English settlement began in 1627 with private capital and the Crown’s blessing. Early tobacco profits helped finance the shift to sugar after large-scale Virginian tobacco production glutted the European market and prices fell.[^ch05-bbc-barbados] Planters began growing sugarcane in the 1640s and 1650s; Dutch traders and Sephardic refugees from Brazil brought the technology, and by the 1650s sugar had transformed the island.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

As sugar production intensified, planters replaced White indentured servants with enslaved Africans, and small landholdings gave way to large plantations. Between 1627 and 1807, some 387,000 Africans were shipped to Barbados, and the high mortality rate on the sugar plantations required a constant input of fresh captives.[^ch05-bbc-barbados] In the last two decades of the seventeenth century, Blacks outnumbered Whites by two to one.[^ch05-bbc-barbados]

Beckles calls Barbados Britain’s colonial site of the first “black slave society.” He dates the legal turn earlier than the 1661 code: in 1636, a political directive provided that all Africans brought to the island were to be received as lifelong chattels.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles] The 1661 code consolidated a practice already in motion; it did not invent racial slavery from nothing, but it gave the practice a comprehensive legal form.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles][^ch05-rugemer]

Sugar grown by enslaved Africans produced enormous wealth, and the planters it enriched carried its model to Jamaica and the Carolinas.[^ch05-bbc-barbados]

### 2.2 The Barbados slave code of 1661

Barbados passed a comprehensive slave code on September 24, 1661, only 34 years after settlement. It was the first comprehensive slave law in the English colonies.[^ch05-barbados-code][^ch05-rugemer] The code defined Africans as “an heathenish, brutish, and an uncertaine dangerous kind of people,” and it treated enslaved people as property outside the protections English law gave to free people.[^ch05-barbados-code][^ch05-un-slavecode] Its preamble admitted that English law offered “noe tract to guide us where to walke, nor any rule sett us, how to governe such Slaves.” It claimed to protect them “as we doe many other goods and Chattels, and alsoe somewhat further as being created Men, though without the knowledge of God.”[^ch05-barbados-code]

The code regulated movement, assembly, and punishment. It allowed a master to kill an enslaved person during correction without legal account, and it fined the willful killing of another person’s enslaved property. Chattel and person at once, the enslaved were caught in a legal order that wanted the profit of property and the power of punishment.[^ch05-barbados-code]

The surviving version dates to 1667 and had already been amended twice. The 1661 original does not survive. That textual problem does not erase the code’s importance, but it should caution against treating every clause as the exact language of 1661.[^ch05-check-barbados] What the surviving text establishes is the code’s design: a comprehensive, hereditary, racialized system of mastery, enforced by public authority and justified by the supposed character of the people it enslaved.

The code became a model. Jamaica copied a Barbadian code in 1664, revised it in 1684, and South Carolina copied the revision in 1691.[^ch05-rugemer] The transmission ran through Jamaica, not directly from Barbados to Carolina. One island’s code alone did not create mainland slavery, but Barbados supplies the clearest early model and the strongest evidence that the law was made deliberately.[^ch05-aaihs-beckles][^ch05-rugemer]

By 1661 the English Atlantic already had a comprehensive racial slave code. The Virginia and Maryland statutes of the 1660s are part of that legal movement, not isolated inventions.[^ch05-un-slavecode][^ch05-rugemer]

### 2.3 Jamaica, the Royal African Company, and the Atlantic circuit

In 1655, under Cromwell’s Western Design, England seized Jamaica from Spain. Spanish colonists fled, and some of the enslaved escaped into the mountains, where their communities became the Jamaican Maroons.[^ch05-lancaster-jamaica] Maroon communities took root in the Blue Mountains and Cockpit Country, and the British made a treaty with them in 1739.[^ch05-lancaster-jamaica] Jamaica became a sugar colony and a base for English privateering.[^ch05-lancaster-jamaica]

Charles II chartered the Royal African Company in 1672 and gave it a monopoly on English trade in West Africa, including the slave trade.[^ch05-smithsonian-rac][^ch05-nps-rac] Its predecessor, the Company of Royal Adventurers, had already realized that the money lay in supplying enslaved people to the West Indies and Virginia. By 1668 more than a quarter of the new company’s profits came from the slave trade.[^ch05-nps-rac] Between 1673 and 1688 it transported an estimated 90,000 to 100,000 enslaved Africans on about 23 voyages a year, or roughly 5,000 people a year in the 1680s.[^ch05-smithsonian-rac] Virginia and the Chesapeake received Africans through the company and through intercolonial traders. The company had agents in Virginia who received a 7 percent commission on sales.[^ch05-nps-rac]

The English Atlantic ran on enslaved labor. African captives were carried to the Caribbean and the mainland; sugar, tobacco, and provisions moved among the islands, the mainland, and England; and mainland colonies traded food and timber to the islands in return for sugar and cash.[^ch05-bbc-barbados] New Netherland’s enslaved people were sometimes sent to Virginia or Maryland, and Barbados re-exported enslaved people to North America and other islands.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-bbc-barbados] Map 5 shows this circuit.[^ch05-check-map] The Caribbean and the mainland formed one connected economy and one connected legal project, however differently their societies developed.

## 3. Chesapeake labor and Bacon’s Rebellion

### 3.1 The turn from servants to enslaved Africans

In the later seventeenth century the Chesapeake shifted from White indentured servants to enslaved Africans. Indentured servitude had supplied most Chesapeake labor through the middle of the century. As the cost of White labor rose and emigration fell, planters turned to the more expensive but permanent alternative taken from Africa.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-tcu-morgan] Tobacco’s demand for labor, the declining supply of servants, the rising availability of Africans, and planters’ pursuit of lifetime, inheritable workers moved together.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]

The race line in law sharpened. Virginia’s statutes separated “Negroes” from normal legal protections, declared in 1667 that baptism did not free the enslaved, and in 1682 declared imported non-Christian servants enslaved.[^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws][^ch05-ev-1667][^ch05-ev-1682] These acts are the Chesapeake’s version of the legal hardening under way in the islands.[^ch05-rugemer]

The timing is contested. The Virginia encyclopedia places the planters’ decision several decades before Bacon’s Rebellion, but the supply of enslaved Africans expanded only later, roughly 20 years after it.[^ch05-ev-bacon] That chronology complicates the idea that the rebellion caused the turn; the turn was already under way. The next subsection turns to the crisis that historians connect to that change.

### 3.2 Bacon’s Rebellion, 1676–1677

The rebellion began as a dispute over Indian policy and became a civil war in Virginia. It traces to 1675, when a trading dispute between the Doeg and the planter Thomas Mathew escalated into violence. Virginia militia pursued the Doeg into Maryland and attacked Susquehannock hunters, igniting the Susquehannock War.[^ch05-ev-bacon] Virginians divided sharply over how to fight it. In March 1676, Governor Sir William Berkeley and the assembly planned frontier forts and patrols; many objected that the forts were expensive and useless and suspected all Indians, even allies, of being enemies.[^ch05-ev-bacon]

Nathaniel Bacon, an ambitious recent arrival appointed to Berkeley’s Council, led unauthorized militia attacks against Native nations, including allies of the colony’s leadership.[^ch05-ev-bacon] He pursued the Susquehannocks to the Roanoke River, persuaded the Occaneechi to attack them, then turned on the Occaneechi, killing men, women, and children and looting their town.[^ch05-ev-bacon] In May, Berkeley declared Bacon in rebellion and called for a new House of Burgesses. Bacon was elected from Henrico County. On June 23 he returned to Jamestown with 500 men, and the burgesses, under pressure, named him commander in chief.[^ch05-ev-bacon]

The war of words became a civil war. Berkeley fled to the Eastern Shore. In September, Berkeley retook Jamestown without a shot. Six days later, Bacon laid siege. On the evening of September 18, Berkeley abandoned the town. The rebels entered the next morning and burned it, resolving to “laye itt level with the Ground.”[^ch05-ev-bacon] Bacon died on October 26, 1676, of typhus and dysentery, the “Bloody Flux”; his body was buried secretly so it could not be exposed on a gibbet.[^ch05-ev-bacon] Fighting continued into 1677. Berkeley’s revenge hangings and the royal commissioners’ investigation divided the colony further.[^ch05-ev-bacon]

Bacon’s followers included tobacco planters, poor Whites, and enslaved Africans. Women helped spread word of his plans and urged their husbands to join.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory] Contemporary accounts disagreed about him, seeing him either as the colonists’ guardian against a self-interested governor or as an egotistical leader who took advantage of lesser men’s fears. Later historians made the rebellion a turning point in the history of race.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory] The rebellion is both an event and a layered memory, and the two must be kept apart.

### 3.3 The aftermath and the labor question

Edmund S. Morgan argued that after Bacon’s Rebellion Virginia’s elite turned decisively toward racial slavery to divide poor Whites from Africans. Morgan connected the Chesapeake’s freedom to its unfreedom: the same society that prized liberty for White planters was organized against Native people and dependent on bound labor. He called the simultaneous development of slavery and freedom “the central paradox of American history.”[^ch05-tcu-morgan] The rebellion, he wrote, showed that “resentment of an alien race might be more powerful than resentment of an upper class.”[^ch05-tcu-morgan] The danger was an alliance. “If freemen with disappointed hopes should make common cause with slaves of desperate hope,” Morgan wrote, “the results might be worse than anything Bacon had done.”[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]

The qualifications are serious. The turn to enslaved Africans also had economic causes: the declining supply of servants and the rising availability of African captives.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-ev-bacon] The Virginia encyclopedia places the planters’ decision before the rebellion and the supply of enslaved Africans about 20 years after it, which does not fit a simple cause-and-effect story.[^ch05-ev-bacon] Morgan’s thesis is a powerful argument, not a settled fact, and the demographic and economic evidence must be weighed beside it.[^ch05-check-dating]

Virginia restricted the vote to property holders and freeholders in 1670–71 and sharpened the racial line among servants and the enslaved. These measures narrowed the political community as the labor regime hardened.[^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws][^ch05-tcu-morgan] The Chesapeake is the mainland case where the legal question left open in Chapter 4 was answered. §5 reads the statutes that did it.

## 4. Maryland, Carolina, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware

### 4.1 Maryland’s first slave law

Maryland practiced slavery from the 1640s but gave it legislative sanction only in 1664. In 1640 Maryland had about 20 Black people among roughly 600 colonists. By 1660 there were about 760 among 8,500, a shift from one in 30 to one in 10.[^ch05-msa-maryland] The colony’s tobacco economy demanded cheap labor, and the Company of Royal Adventurers, chartered in the early 1660s, seemed to guarantee a supply of enslaved Africans.[^ch05-msa-maryland] The specific issue was baptized Blacks who claimed freedom. The Lower House asked the Upper House to draw up an act “obliging negroes to serve durante vita,” to prevent masters’ loss from “such Slaves pretending to be Christened And soe pleade the lawe of England.”[^ch05-msa-maryland]

The 1664 law declared that “negroes and other slaves” already in the province, and those later imported, should serve for life. It made children born of any Black or other enslaved person “Slaves as their Fathers.” It required a free woman who married an enslaved man to serve her husband’s master during her husband’s lifetime, and it made the children of such marriages slaves. Children born before the act were to serve until 31.[^ch05-msa-maryland]

Maryland followed the father; Virginia, two years earlier, had followed the mother. Each colony solved the problem its own mix of free and enslaved parents created. Both rules made bondage heritable, but they did not use the same mechanism.[^ch05-msa-maryland][^ch05-ev-1662]

The 1664 law was inadequate to its purpose. In 1671 the assembly passed a new act declaring that baptism could not affect a Black slave’s status, to reassure masters and merchants who feared losing their human property.[^ch05-msa-maryland] Maryland’s path ran parallel to Virginia’s and used similar legal devices; neither was the single origin of American slavery.

### 4.2 Carolina and the Barbadian model

Carolina was designed from the start around the West Indian model of enslaved African labor. Charles II granted Carolina to eight Lords Proprietors in 1663, chartered again in 1665. Before the first successful settlement at Charles Town in 1670, the proprietors studied Barbados’s sugar plantation system and recruited White settlers from the island to launch the colony.[^ch05-ldhi-barbados] Barbadian families such as the Middletons, Draytons, and Gibbes became prominent Carolina planters.[^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]

The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, commissioned in 1669, provided that “every freeman of Carolina shall have absolute power and authority over his negro slaves, of what opinion or religion soever.”[^ch05-avalon-carolina] Another section allowed enslaved people to join any church but kept them under their masters’ civil dominion, while the document promised religious liberty to “Jews, heathens, and other dissenters.”[^ch05-avalon-carolina] That combination is the point: a framework that imagined religious tolerance and absolute mastery in the same text.[^ch05-avalon-carolina][^ch05-slp-carolina]

The Constitutions were a plan, not a functioning government. They went through several versions between 1669 and 1698 and were never fully implemented. John Locke, secretary to the Earl of Shaftesbury, was one of several contributors, and his precise responsibility for the slavery clauses is debated.[^ch05-slp-carolina] His authorship and the document’s effect should not be overstated.[^ch05-slp-carolina][^ch05-avalon-carolina]

The proprietors offered 20 acres for each enslaved Black male and 10 for each enslaved Black female, and Carolina’s leaders modeled the colony’s slave code on Barbados’s laws.[^ch05-ldhi-barbadians] The transmission ran through Jamaica: Jamaica copied Barbados’s code in 1664, revised it in 1684, and South Carolina copied the revision in 1691.[^ch05-rugemer] The Carolina code was therefore Barbadian in origin but Jamaican in its immediate form.[^ch05-rugemer]

Carolina’s planters failed at olives, grapes, mulberry, and English grains, then traded furs and produced tar and pitch. By 1690 they had applied the West Indian model to rice.[^ch05-ldhi-barbados] Carolina shows the Caribbean and the mainland as a single migration of people, capital, and law.

### 4.3 Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware

Pennsylvania, founded as a haven of religious freedom, also practiced slavery. Charles II granted William Penn a 45,000-square-mile tract in 1681, and Penn guaranteed religious freedom to Quakers and other persecuted Europeans.[^ch05-pah-penn] He and other Quakers enslaved Africans. Penn owned enslaved people at his Pennsbury estate; the names Sam, Sue, Yaff, Jack, and Peter survive, along with others whose names do not.[^ch05-pah-penn][^ch05-pennsbury] The first enslaved people came to the region in the 1640s, and by 1682 slavery was well established in small households. Penn was not an exception but a prominent participant.[^ch05-pennsbury]

On April 18, 1688, four German Quakers in Germantown petitioned their monthly meeting to protest Quaker slaveholding. Garret Hendericks, Francis Daniel Pastorius, and the brothers Derick and Abraham op de Graeff, cousins to Penn, asked Quakers to practice what they preached.[^ch05-nps-germantown] It was the first formal protest of its kind in British North America. The petition argued that “we shall doe to all men licke as we will be done ourselves; macking no difference of what generation, descent or Colour they are,” called the slave trade “this traffic of men body,” and asked whether enslaved people “have not as much right to fight for their freedom, as you have to keep them slaves?”[^ch05-nps-germantown] The Yearly Meeting at Burlington, New Jersey, voted not to take a stand, and antislavery would not become Quaker policy for decades.[^ch05-nps-germantown]

New Jersey and Delaware were small proprietary and former Dutch and Swedish jurisdictions where slavery took root alongside the larger colonies. New Jersey held enslaved Africans from its founding; a 1664 law counted them for allocating land. The 1676 charter of West Jersey promised inhabitants would be “free from oppression and slavery,” even as Quakers there owned plantations worked by enslaved people.[^ch05-princeton-nj] East Jersey regulated slavery repeatedly: a 1682 law required food and clothing; 1694 laws restricted guns, property, and movement; a 1695 law created a separate court for enslaved defendants; and a 1704 code declared that baptism would not free them.[^ch05-princeton-nj] By 1776 enslaved people were about 12 percent of New Jersey’s population.[^ch05-princeton-nj]

Delaware passed through Swedish, Dutch, and English hands. In 1664 the Duke of York took the region; in 1683 he deeded the three lower counties to Penn. By then about 400 nonnative inhabitants and some 100 enslaved Africans lived between Cape Henlopen and New Castle.[^ch05-phila-delaware] English settlers moving into the region often brought enslaved Africans with them.[^ch05-phila-delaware]

Pennsylvania exposes the gap between a promise of religious freedom and the practice of slavery, and it produces the era’s clearest antislavery argument. Both facts belong together.[^ch05-check-middle]

## 5. Hereditary status in law

### 5.1 The statutes that made status heritable

A cluster of statutes passed between 1661 and 1705 converted a labor system into a hereditary racial order; the code of 1705 completed the work the 1660s began. The most important provisions made bondage follow the mother, made baptism irrelevant to freedom, and made servitude permanent for Africans.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-msa-maryland]

Barbados led. Its 1661 code fixed Africans’ legal status as hereditary chattel and excluded them from the protections of English law.[^ch05-barbados-code][^ch05-rugemer] Virginia followed in 1662. Its act “Negro womens children to serve according to the condition of the mother” reversed the common-law presumption that a child’s status followed the father. It declared that “all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother,” and it doubled the fine for a Christian who committed fornication with a “negro man or woman.”[^ch05-ev-1662] The act enabled enslavers to reproduce their own labor force.[^ch05-ev-1662]

The Elizabeth Key case shows what the 1662 act was meant to settle. Key was the daughter of an enslaved African woman and Thomas Key, a free English planter. In 1656 she sued for freedom. The Northumberland County jury found her free, the General Court reversed, and the General Assembly’s committee concluded that “by the Comon Law the Child of a Woman slave begott by a freeman ought to bee free” and that her baptism mattered.[^ch05-lva-key][^ch05-ev-key] The county court freed her on July 21, 1656.[^ch05-lva-key] Her suit was probably one reason the assembly reversed the rule six years later.[^ch05-lva-key]

Maryland made “negroes and other slaves” serve for life in 1664 and made children follow the father.[^ch05-msa-maryland] Virginia’s 1667 act closed the baptismal door: “the conferring of baptisme doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome.” The preamble explained the purpose: to free masters from doubt so that they “may more carefully endeavor the propagation of christianity by permitting children, though slaves … to be admitted to that sacrament.”[^ch05-ev-1667] The assembly chose to protect slavery and encourage conversion rather than let conversion free anyone.

Virginia’s 1668 act taxed free Black women. It declared that “negro women, though permitted to enjoy their ffreedome yet ought not in all respects to be admitted to a full fruition of the exemptions and impunities of the English, and are still lyable to payment of taxes.”[^ch05-ev-1668] In 1669 it provided that if an enslaved person died while resisting a master’s correction, the death was not a felony; in 1670 it barred “Negroes nor Indians” from buying Christian servants; in 1680 it restricted weapons and movement; in 1682 it declared imported non-Christian servants enslaved; and in 1691 it ordered White men and women who married Black, mulatto, or Indian people banished, and punished White women who bore mixed-race children with a fine or servitude.[^ch05-gilder-laws][^ch05-ev-1682]

Each act closed a door: to birth-status freedom, to baptismal freedom, and to the blurred line between servant and slave. By the 1680s the legal category existed before the word “slave” was fully settled.[^ch05-check-1662]

### 5.2 In Focus: Virginia’s 1662 maternal-status statute

**In Focus.** Virginia’s December 1662 act is short and decisive. It begins from doubt: “WHEREAS some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree.” Under English common law, a child’s status usually followed the father, so the child of an enslaved woman and a free father could claim freedom. Elizabeth Key had done exactly that six years earlier, and she had won. The assembly cut through the doubt by reversing the rule: “all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother.” The same act doubled the penalty for a Christian who committed fornication with a “negro man or woman,” enforcing the boundary the heredity rule drew.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-gl-1662]

Read closely, the act is a labor measure as much as a moral one. By making bondage pass through the mother, it guaranteed enslavers a self-reproducing workforce and ended the debate over mixed-status children. A child born to an enslaved mother was now a slave, no matter who the father was or what the child’s baptism might later claim. The act did not use the phrase “hereditary racial slavery,” and it did not settle every question, but it built one of the system’s essential mechanisms. Barbados had adopted a comprehensive code the year before, while New Netherland’s enslaved children remained in a different, contested condition.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-un-slavecode][^ch05-nni-half-freedom][^ch05-check-focus]

### 5.3 The consequences of the legal regime

By 1705 Virginia had gathered its scattered acts into a comprehensive code. “An act concerning Servants and Slaves” consolidated the earlier statutes and regulated the treatment, punishment, and legal status of enslaved people. It declared that servants imported who were not Christians in their native country “shall be accounted and be slaves,” notwithstanding later conversion. It protected Christian White servants from naked whipping but not enslaved people, and barred “negros, mulattos, or Indians” from purchasing Christian servants.[^ch05-ev-1705] The code made race the legal shorthand for a set of disabilities, even as it kept Christian origin in its formula.[^ch05-ev-1705][^ch05-ev-1667]

The contrast with New Netherland is the proof that the outcome was chosen. Half-freedom, wage earning, and property holding had existed in the Dutch colony. The English and mainland codes narrowed those possibilities.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom] The comparison shows that the outcome was not inevitable, and that the Dutch regime was not benign: it was a slave regime with more room to bargain, and the room closed as the English Atlantic hardened.[^ch05-nni-slavery]

The central claim — that a labor regime became hereditary racial slavery through law and political choice — is established by the statutes themselves. §6 weighs the competing explanations for why that happened.[^ch05-check-1662]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Racial slavery was made in law, not born fully formed. The statutes of 1661–1705 created hereditary, racialized bondage; it did not exist as a complete system in 1619 or 1626.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-msa-maryland]

The Caribbean led. Barbados passed a comprehensive slave code in 1661 and became the model that Carolina drew on through Jamaica.[^ch05-barbados-code][^ch05-rugemer]

Sugar and the slave trade transformed the English Atlantic. The sugar revolution, Jamaica’s capture, and the Royal African Company made enslaved African labor central to England’s empire.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-smithsonian-rac]

The Chesapeake shifted from servants to enslaved Africans. Declining servant supply, rising African imports, and tobacco’s labor demand moved together.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]

Bacon’s Rebellion was real and consequential. It became a civil war in Virginia, killed its leader in October 1676, and unsettled the colony’s elite.[^ch05-ev-bacon][^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]

Pennsylvania practiced slavery and produced the first antislavery protest. The 1688 Germantown petition is the earliest formal protest of its kind in British North America.[^ch05-nps-germantown][^ch05-pennsbury]

### 6.2 What is open

Why the turn to racial slavery happened is open. Morgan emphasizes elite political choice after Bacon’s Rebellion. Jordan emphasizes deep cultural attitudes toward Africans. The Handlins emphasize the gradual decay of the servant system and the legislative pursuit of labor. The relative weight is not settled.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-jordan][^ch05-handlin]

When race prejudice began is open. Jordan argued that English perceptions of Africans were prior to and helped cause slavery; the Handlins argued that racism emerged to justify a labor system created for other reasons. The debate is about sequence and causation, not a choice between “race” and “class” as separate substances.[^ch05-jordan][^ch05-handlin]

The importance of Bacon’s Rebellion is open. Many historians treat it as a turning point; others question whether the chronology matches Morgan’s account. The Virginia encyclopedia places the planters’ decision before the rebellion and the supply of enslaved Africans about 20 years after it, which rules out the simplest version of the argument.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory][^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-ev-bacon]

The place of gender is open. Kathleen M. Brown argues that gender was constitutive of racial slavery: the labor and reproductive roles of women, and the laws governing them, made race and slavery together. The 1662 statute’s focus on mothers supports her emphasis; others treat gender as one factor among several.[^ch05-brown][^ch05-ev-1662]

The role of the Caribbean in mainland law is open. Barbados supplied a model, but the transmission ran through Jamaica and local adaptation; whether mainland statutes were primarily borrowed or independently developed is debated.[^ch05-rugemer][^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]

What the archives cannot show is part of the problem. The enslaved left few records of their own; their experience reaches us through the people who owned them, and the historian must not mistake the enslavers’ categories for the whole of the past.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-check-names]

Who the early Africans were is open. Ira Berlin argues that the Africans of the seventeenth-century “charter generations” were often Atlantic Creoles: culturally and linguistically plural people with prior experience of European colonialism. Their world differed from that of later generations of enslaved plantation workers. Whether the earliest mainland Africans are best understood this way, and how far the model applies to the Caribbean, is open.[^ch05-berlin] The Anthony Johnson case shows the same complexity: Johnson, a free Black planter in Northampton County, claimed his servant John Casor for life, and the county court ruled for him in 1655. Freedom, servitude, and mastery could coexist within Black life even as the law narrowed Black freedom.[^ch05-ev-johnson]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The choice is not between race and class, or between culture and economics. The evidence supports a history in which commercial demand supplied the workers, cultural attitudes made them available for racialized bondage, and law and political choice fixed the status and made it inheritable.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-jordan][^ch05-handlin]

Morgan connected the Chesapeake’s freedom to its unfreedom and gave the turn to racial slavery a political motive, but his chronology and his Virginia-centered emphasis have been questioned.[^ch05-tcu-morgan]

Jordan took English attitudes toward Africans seriously as a cause, not only a justification, but later scholars charged that his framework understated law, labor, and the deliberate making of status.[^ch05-jordan]

Brown put gender and patriarchy at the center of the making of racial slavery and read the maternal-status acts as instruments of a gendered order as well as a racial one.[^ch05-brown]

The Handlins insisted that racial slavery emerged gradually from the decay of the servant system; their chronology has been largely rejected, but their attention to the blurry boundary between servant and slave remains useful.[^ch05-handlin][^ch05-billings]

Read together, the positions say that racial slavery was made, not discovered, and that it was made in several places at once — Barbados, Virginia, Maryland, Carolina — through choices traceable in statutes and court records. New Netherland’s half-freedom shows that the outcome was contingent.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom][^ch05-check-sources]

The best history refuses both the single-cause story and the single-origin story, and shows the choices, the alternatives, and their cost.

## Legacies

The statutes of the 1660s left a regime that outlived the English Atlantic empire. By making bondage heritable through the mother and independent of religion or birthplace, Virginia, Maryland, Carolina, and Barbados created a racial order that the eighteenth-century plantation economy would deepen and the new republic would inherit. Slaveholders had not invented enslavement, but they invented a law that reproduced it across generations by design.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-msa-maryland]

The Caribbean’s legacy reached the mainland two ways. Barbadian planters, capital, and law moved to Carolina, and the sugar colonies supplied the model of a society organized around the ownership of human beings as chattel. What mainland colonists called a “peculiar” institution was, in its origins, a Caribbean export as much as a Chesapeake development.[^ch05-ldhi-barbadians][^ch05-aaihs-beckles]

Bacon’s Rebellion became a theme in American memory. Invoked as evidence that racial slavery was chosen to divide the poor, it has been read and reread for three centuries, its meaning shifting with each generation’s politics.[^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]

Pennsylvania’s Germantown Protest left a quieter legacy. The Quakers who wrote in 1688 had no power to end slavery, and the Yearly Meeting set their petition aside. But their argument — do to others as you would have them do to you — gave antislavery a text later generations would recover.[^ch05-nps-germantown]

The legacies are double: the same decades that made hereditary slavery into law produced the first formal protest against it, and the same Atlantic economy that enriched planters impoverished the people it enslaved.

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** New Netherland, New Sweden, Barbados, Jamaica, and the mainland sat inside one English and Dutch Atlantic economy of sugar, tobacco, provisions, and enslaved people. The Royal African Company supplied Virginia and the islands; Dutch traders brought sugar technology to Barbados; Barbadian planters carried their law and laborers to Carolina.[^ch05-smithsonian-rac][^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-ldhi-barbadians]
- **Archives and silences.** The enslaved appear in the records of the people who owned them: inventories, deeds, court minutes, church registers. The 1644 petitioners are known by the names their enslavers recorded; the children who remained enslaved are mostly numbers.[^ch05-nni-half-freedom][^ch05-nni-slavery]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 4 supplies the 1619 arrival and Virginia’s labor question; Chapter 6 owns King Philip’s War, the Pueblo Revolt, and the imperial turn; Chapter 7 owns Stono and colonial society. Later chapters carry the regime built here into the revolution, the republic, and the Civil War.[^ch05-check-ch4][^ch05-check-ch6]

## Summary

- New Netherland, a Dutch company colony, held enslaved Africans from the mid-1620s; 11 men and their wives won half-freedom in 1644, but their children remained enslaved.[^ch05-nni-slavery][^ch05-nni-half-freedom]
- New Sweden, planted on the Delaware in 1638, held a Black slave named Antonius from its first year; the Dutch absorbed it in 1655, and the English took the region in 1664 with slavery intact.[^ch05-psu-newsweden][^ch05-mcny-stuyvesant]
- Barbados shifted from tobacco and White servants to sugar and enslaved Africans in the middle decades of the century and became the model of a racialized slave society.[^ch05-bbc-barbados][^ch05-ldhi-barbados]
- The Barbados slave code of 1661 was the first comprehensive slave code in the English colonies and a template for Jamaica and Carolina.[^ch05-barbados-code][^ch05-rugemer]
- England captured Jamaica in 1655; the Royal African Company, chartered in 1672, transported an estimated 90,000 to 100,000 enslaved Africans during its monopoly.[^ch05-lancaster-jamaica][^ch05-smithsonian-rac]
- The Chesapeake shifted from indentured servants to enslaved Africans, and Virginia’s statutes increasingly distinguished servants by race.[^ch05-tcu-morgan][^ch05-pbs-coloniallaws]
- Bacon’s Rebellion (1676–1677) became a civil war in Virginia; Bacon burned Jamestown in September 1676 and died that October.[^ch05-ev-bacon][^ch05-ev-bacon-memory]
- Maryland gave slavery legislative sanction in 1664, making “negroes and other slaves” serve for life and making children follow the father.[^ch05-msa-maryland]
- The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina (1669) granted freemen “absolute power and authority over his negro slaves”; Carolina’s code drew on Barbados through Jamaica.[^ch05-avalon-carolina][^ch05-rugemer]
- Pennsylvania, founded for religious freedom, also practiced slavery; its 1688 Germantown Protest was the first formal antislavery protest in British North America.[^ch05-pennsbury][^ch05-nps-germantown]
- Virginia’s 1662 act made bondage follow the mother; its 1667 act made baptism irrelevant to freedom; the 1705 code consolidated a racial slave regime.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-ev-1667][^ch05-ev-1705]
- Historians disagree about whether class, culture, gender, or gradual change best explains racial slavery, but they agree that law made the status heritable.[^ch05-check-table][^ch05-check-sources]

> *A closing note.* The chapter’s subject is a set of short statutes that look technical and were not. In 1662 Virginia’s assembly answered a doubt about the children of enslaved mothers by making bondage pass down the maternal line; in 1667 it declared that baptism changed nothing; in 1664 Maryland made “negroes and other slaves” serve for life; in 1661 Barbados had already written the model. None of these acts invented human bondage, and none used the modern phrase “racial slavery.” Together they built a machine that reproduced itself: a status that would be inherited, justified by descent, and defended as natural. The chapter begins in New Netherland, where an enslaved man could win “half-freedom” and where the law had not yet closed around his children, and it ends with the statutes that closed it.[^ch05-ev-1662][^ch05-ev-1667][^ch05-msa-maryland]
