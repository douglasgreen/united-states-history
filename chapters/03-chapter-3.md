## Epigraphs

> “If your king have sent me presents, I also am a king, and this my land, 8 days I will stay [at Werowocomoco] to receive them. Your father [Newport] is to come to me, not I to him, nor yet to your fort, neither will I bite at such a bait [fearing capture if he came to Jamestown].”
>
> — Wahunsenacawh (Powhatan), as reported by John Smith, in *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New-England, and the Summer Isles* (1624), quoted in Encyclopedia Virginia, “Powhatan (d. 1618)”[^ch03-powhatan]

> “Neither of us will try to steer each other’s vessel as we travel side by side down the river of life.”
>
> — the Haudenosaunee account of the Two Row Wampum, as told by the Onondaga Nation, “Two Row Wampum – Gaswéñdah”[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

> “The middle ground is thus a process of mutual and creative misunderstanding.”
>
> — Richard White, “Creative Misunderstandings and New Understandings,” *William and Mary Quarterly* 63, no. 1 (2006), p. 10[^ch03-white-creative]

- **Why these three.** Powhatan supplies the language of sovereignty: a king in his own land who would not go to the English fort. The Haudenosaunee teaching supplies the language of treaty: two peoples traveling side by side, equal, neither steering the other. White supplies the language of intercultural process: peoples who could not fully understand each other building a workable world out of partial and creative misreading. The epigraphs disagree about what contact was — a meeting of sovereigns, a compact of equals, or a fabric of misunderstandings — and that disagreement is the chapter’s problem.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Powhatan’s words survive only through an English colonist’s later book, so they are a mediated record of Native speech, not a transcript. The Two Row statement is a contemporary account of a living tradition, not a document from 1613. White’s line is an analytical definition, not a verdict on any particular encounter.
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that Powhatan’s claim was recognized by the English, that the Two Row agreement was written in 1613, or that the middle ground operated everywhere. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The object.** The Two Row Wampum, called Gaswéñdah or Kaswentha, is a wampum belt of white shell beads with two parallel purple rows running lengthwise. In the Haudenosaunee account, one purple row is a canoe carrying Haudenosaunee ways — language, songs, dances, government, and ceremonies — and the other is a ship carrying the newcomers’ ways. The rows are equal in weight, and the two purple rows run off the end of the belt to show that the agreement is to last as long as the water flows downhill, the grasses grow green, and the sun rises in the east and sets in the west.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]
- **The three principles.** The Haudenosaunee and the Dutch agreed on friendship, peace, and a duration of “forever.” The Dutch, in the account, said they would record the agreement “with a pen and paper,” while the Haudenosaunee would make a wampum belt for future generations. The belt is the memory.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]
- **The equality claim.** When the newcomers proposed that the Haudenosaunee call them “Father” and themselves “Son,” the Haudenosaunee refused, because a father may command a son. They agreed to address each other as “Brothers,” which in this account means equals.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]
- **The living tradition.** The Onondaga Nation describes the Two Row Wampum as “a living treaty,” a way for two peoples to live in peace while each respects the other’s traditions, laws, and customs. The Haudenosaunee cite it in the present, including against later impositions such as the 1924 Citizenship Act, which they say one government cannot place on another.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-onondaga-timeline]
- **The date claimed.** The Onondaga Nation places the treaty with the Dutch in 1613. The tradition holds that the newcomers arrived on the “River that Flows both Ways” (the Hudson) unannounced, that the Mohawks first encountered them, and that the agreement followed long deliberation.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-onondaga-timeline]
- **The documentary problem.** The principal written “treaty” said to record the 1613 agreement, the so-called Tawagonshi document, surfaced in 1968 under undocumented circumstances. In 1987, Charles Gehring, William Starna, and William Fenton judged it a fabrication; later linguists found more than 40 anachronisms and concluded that the text was written in the twentieth century. Its place names, clan totems, and spellings belong to English treaty practice, not Dutch or 1613. The document named the Mohawk, yet the people living on the lower Hudson when the Dutch arrived were the Mahican.[^ch03-tawagonshi]
- **The durable relationship.** Whatever the fate of that particular paper, the Haudenosaunee and the Dutch, and later the English, built a long relationship remembered as the Silver Covenant Chain. The first known written non-Native record of the Covenant Chain dates from negotiations at Albany in 1677, more than sixty years after the claimed Two Row agreement.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline]
- **The silence.** No 1613 written instrument in Dutch or Mohawk survives to confirm the traditional date. The evidence for the tradition is the belt, centuries of Haudenosaunee oral teaching, and the later documentary record of the Covenant Chain. The scene must hold the tradition’s reality and the document’s unreliability together.[^ch03-tawagonshi][^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]

### The scene’s argument

- **A tradition, not a reenactment.** The scene should read the belt and its teaching, not stage a council in 1613 that no record describes. It may describe the belt’s pattern, the canoe-and-ship meaning, and the three principles, because the Haudenosaunee account supplies them.
- **The date is claimed, not proven.** The Haudenosaunee place the treaty in 1613. The one document offered to prove it is judged a forgery. The scene should say both things plainly: the tradition is real and long-lived; the paper is not what it claims to be.
- **Sovereignty is the point.** The belt encodes a claim that two peoples could live side by side without one governing the other. That claim is the chapter’s central subject: how Native nations interpreted newcomers and insisted on their own sovereignty.
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: How did Indigenous nations interpret and manage newcomers?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that contact was either a meeting of equals or a simple conquest. It lies in diplomacy, kinship, trade, disease, and the different political systems that Native nations brought to the encounter. The chapter moves from one belt to a continent of political worlds.

### What must not be invented

- No invented council, speaker, gesture, setting, weather, clothing, or dialogue for 1613. The scene may quote the Haudenosaunee account of the agreement, but it must not dramatize a meeting the record does not document.
- No claim that the Tawagonshi document is authentic, and no claim that the tradition is therefore false. The document’s forgery and the tradition’s persistence are separate facts.
- No claim that the belt’s physical object dates exactly to 1613. The Haudenosaunee hold the agreement and the belt in a long tradition; the surviving object’s material date is a separate question.
- No claim that the Mohawk were the only people on the lower Hudson. The Mahican lived there; Mohawk territory lay to the west and north.[^ch03-tawagonshi][^ch03-fort-orange]
- No claim that the Dutch and Haudenosaunee entered a modern-law treaty with all its present meanings. The tradition’s language of “treaty” is the Haudenosaunee’s and must be attributed to them.
- No claim that the Two Row agreement governed every later encounter. It is one tradition among many, and it did not prevent dispossession or war.[^ch03-check-scene]

## 1. Tsenacomoco

### 1.1 A paramount chiefdom, not a tribe

- **The claim.** Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom, a political alliance of Algonquian-speaking peoples in Tidewater Virginia, not a single tribe and not a centralized state. Its ruler, the mamanatowick, held real authority that was checked by councils and by the consent of the constituent chiefdoms.
- **The origins.** The historian James D. Rice argues that before about 1300 the region’s peoples were largely hunters and gatherers with little central authority; by 1607 they had organized into chiefdoms and a paramount chiefdom. Population growth, agriculture, the Little Ice Age, competition for good town sites, and increased warfare and trade were interdependent forces.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The structure.** Powhatan inherited the core six groups — the Powhatans, Youghtanunds, Mattaponis, Pamunkeys, Arrohatecks, and Appamattucks — and expanded by diplomacy and force until roughly 28–32 lesser chiefdoms and tribes paid him tribute by 1607. Each chiefdom had its own weroance, or chief, and the chiefs sat on a council, or cockarouse, that advised the paramount chief.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The boundaries.** Tsenacomoco ran from the south bank of the James River north to the south bank of the Potomac, bounded west by the fall line and east by the Chesapeake Bay. The boundaries were not fixed; they reflected the reach of the paramount chief’s power at any moment. April Hatfield notes that the English largely co-opted them as the early, unofficial borders of Virginia.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The economy and land.** The people lived by farming, fishing, and hunting, using fire to clear space and harvest wood. Women tended corn and beans and gathered; men hunted and fished. The English saw unfenced, part-cleared land as “unimproved” and therefore open to seizure — a fatal misreading of a managed landscape.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The name.** William Strachey reported that Powhatan’s people called their land “Tsenacommacah.” Scholars have translated it variously as “densely inhabited place,” “a nearby dwelling-place,” or “our place,” which itself shows how much depends on whose language is doing the translating.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]

### 1.2 Powhatan, the mamanatowick

- **The claim.** Wahunsenacawh, whom the English called Powhatan, ruled as mamanatowick and took the lead in diplomacy with outsiders, but his power was not absolute and he had no standing army.
- **The person.** Born probably around 1550, he came to power in the town of Powhatan and by 1607 had built a polity of 28–32 groups. Succession among the Algonquian-speaking peoples was matrilineal: he became weroance as the son of a sister of a Powhatan chief.[^ch03-powhatan]
- **The title and role.** The title mamanatowick, meaning “powerful spirits” or “powerful shaman,” suggests a priestly as well as political role. Archaeological work at Werowocomoco found a large house inside a double ditch, unique among Tidewater towns, consistent with a dwelling of a chief.[^ch03-powhatan]
- **The limits of power.** Powhatan moved populations, installed sympathetic chiefs, and married the sisters of subchiefs, making his sons potential heirs to their chiefdoms. But the ties binding tribes to his authority were stronger at the center and weaker at the edges, and his standing rose and fell with his people’s welfare.[^ch03-powhatan][^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The tribute.** A good deal of his wealth came from tribute — by one account as much as 80 percent of what his people produced, though he did not accept all of it — held in storehouses for feasts, trade, ritual, and relief in hard times. In good times his legitimacy was unquestioned; in famine or defeat it declined.[^ch03-powhatan][^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The consequence.** Tsenacomoco was a government, not a backdrop. The chapter should describe its institutions before describing the English, because the English entered a working political order.

### 1.3 Managing the English

- **The claim.** Powhatan treated the Jamestown colonists as a potential subject people and useful source of iron, copper, and weapons, and tried to control them through diplomacy, food, adoption, and limited violence. He misjudged their intransigence and their numbers.
- **The arrival.** On April 26, 1607, three ships entered the Chesapeake with 144 men and boys. A landing party at Point Comfort was attacked, the first hostile contact between the English and the people of Tsenacomoco. By June 1607, 104 men and boys lived at Jamestown; by the end of the winter, all but 38 were dead.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The drought.** Tsenacomoco was in the second year of a drought that lasted until 1612, which cut garden yields and deer browse and made the settlers’ demands for food destabilizing. Powhatan forbade English expansion up the James, probably to prevent an alliance with his Monacan enemies.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The ceremony.** When Opechancanough captured John Smith in December 1607 and brought him to Powhatan, the chief put him through what was probably an “adoption” ceremony to make him a subject weroance. Smith later wrote that Pocahontas saved his life; most historians conclude the rescue probably did not happen as Smith described, and that the episode was a ritual of mock execution and adoption.[^ch03-powhatan]
- **The crowning.** In 1608 the English tried to bind Powhatan to the crown. He refused to come to Jamestown — “I also am a king, and this my land” — so the English came to him and, after he refused to kneel, leaned on his shoulders to bend his knees and fitted a crown on his head. To the English he was a subject who would not behave as one; to Powhatan, Smith was a weroance acting like a paramount chief.[^ch03-powhatan]
- **The warfare.** Relations collapsed into the First Anglo-Powhatan War (1609–14). In April 1613 Samuel Argall kidnapped Pocahontas; her conversion and marriage to John Rolfe in 1614 ended the war. Powhatan died in April 1618, and his successors inherited a weakening polity as the English made their own alliances inside and outside it.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-pocahontas]

### 1.4 What Tsenacomoco was not

- **The claim.** Tsenacomoco was neither a tribe nor an empire. Calling it a “confederacy,” an “empire,” or a “nation” each imports a different misleading model.
- **Not a tribe.** It contained the Chickahominies, who lived in its heart on the Chickahominy River yet were independent of Powhatan’s rule, and the Patawomecks, who were not reliably under his control. A single name covered many peoples.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **Not an empire.** Powhatan controlled no standing army and possessed nothing like an emperor’s absolute power, though his violence and population moves led some anthropologists to use the word “empire.”[^ch03-powhatan]
- **The population question.** Scholars disagree about Tsenacomoco’s population. Thomas Jefferson estimated “about 8000 inhabitants”; Helen Rountree suggested about 15,000 in roughly 6,000 square miles; other estimates run from 13,000 to just over 22,000, and Rice puts the whole Chesapeake region at more than 30,000 in 1607.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should use the name Tsenacomoco while explaining that its boundaries and numbers were contested and shifting, and should not let a 1784 estimate stand as a fact.[^ch03-check-population]
- **The transition.** If the Chesapeake was one political order, New England was another. §2 turns to the Wampanoag, whose world was remade by epidemic and who chose an alliance with the English.

## 2. Wampanoag country, epidemics, and alliance

### 2.1 The Wampanoag world

- **The claim.** The Wampanoag were a confederacy of communities and sachems in present-day southeastern Massachusetts and eastern Rhode Island. Their world was organized by kinship, territory, and the authority of sachems, and it was already entangled with European fishermen, traders, and kidnappers before 1620.
- **The country.** The Wampanoag lived around Narragansett and Massachusetts bays and the islands, in villages such as Patuxet, later the site of Plymouth, and in the interior. They were one of several Algonquian-speaking confederacies, alongside the Narragansett, Massachusett, and others.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-great-dying]
- **The kidnapping.** In 1614 Captain Thomas Hunt kidnapped a group of Wampanoags from Patuxet to sell into slavery, including Tisquantum, later called Squanto. The kidnapping turned Native wariness of Europeans into something harder and changed the terms of later encounter.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-pbs-plague]
- **The evidence limit.** The Wampanoag left few written records, so most surviving detail comes from English chroniclers — William Bradford, Edward Winslow, and *Mourt’s Relation* (1622) — which means the record of Wampanoag motives is filtered through English purposes.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should describe the Wampanoag as political actors with their own goals, not as helpers in an English story.

### 2.2 The Great Dying, 1616–19

- **The claim.** An epidemic swept the New England coast from about 1616 to 1619, killing a large share of the Wampanoag and other coastal peoples. Its cause is not securely identified, and it must not be called a smallpox winter without qualification.
- **The toll.** The Wampanoag are said to have lost at least 75 percent and as much as 90 percent of their population. The village of Patuxet, home to about 2,000 people, lost nearly everyone; only one survivor is known by name, Tisquantum.[^ch03-great-dying]
- **The cause in dispute.** Candidates have included smallpox, measles, a combination of the two, yellow fever, cerebrospinal meningitis, bubonic plague (widely discounted), and, in a 2010 study, leptospirosis. The historian David J. Silverman argues that malignant confluent smallpox is most likely, but no diagnosis is settled.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The regional pattern.** The disease ran down the Maine coast into Massachusetts and struck some communities harder than others. The Narragansett, west of Narragansett Bay, largely escaped, which shifted the regional balance of power.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The survival.** Despite the catastrophe, the region’s Native communities were not destroyed. They survived, reorganized, and continued. The chapter should resist the word “extinction.”[^ch03-great-dying]
- **The silence.** Because the Wampanoag left few written records, the epidemic’s toll is known mainly through English accounts and later estimates. The numbers are ranges, not counts.[^ch03-check-epidemic]

### 2.3 Ousamequin’s choice

- **The claim.** Ousamequin, called Massasoit by the English, allied with Plymouth in 1621 as a strategic choice under pressure, not from simple goodwill or simple desperation. The epidemic had weakened the Wampanoag while the Narragansett pressed them for tribute.
- **The pressure.** With the Wampanoag in crisis, Narragansett leaders pressured Ousamequin to pay them tribute, which might have bought protection but also risked subordination. Ousamequin concluded that an alliance with the new English arrivals could help protect his people and preserve his own authority.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The encounter.** In March 1621, about four months after the Mayflower’s arrival, the English and Wampanoag opened contact. Samoset, an Abenaki who knew some English, came first; Ousamequin arrived about a week later. Negotiations used Tisquantum as interpreter.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The terms.** As Bradford and Winslow wrote in *Mourt’s Relation* (1622), Ousamequin “has a potent adversary in the Narragansetts, that are at war with him, against whom he thinks we may be of some strength to him, for our pieces [guns] are terrible to them.” The treaty bound each side to keep peace and to defend the other against attacks by other Indigenous groups.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The internal dissent.** Even some Wampanoags disagreed bitterly with Ousamequin’s decision to align with the English, which shows that the alliance was a contested political choice, not a consensus.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The consequence.** The Wampanoag gained a temporary ally and the English gained survival skills, but the alliance also opened the way for English expansion that would eventually overwhelm the Wampanoag.

### 2.4 The alliance and its afterlives

- **The claim.** The 1621 treaty held until after Ousamequin’s death in 1661, making it the only treaty between Native Americans and English colonists honored throughout the lives of all who signed it. Its collapse belonged to a later generation.
- **The duration.** Peace survived periodic tensions until after Ousamequin’s death. His son Wamsutta died in 1662 during land negotiations; his son Metacom, called King Philip by the English, claimed Wamsutta had been poisoned, and the escalating conflict became King Philip’s War (1675–76).[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The forward connection.** King Philip’s War belongs to Chapter 6. This chapter should end its Wampanoag story at the alliance and the peace, naming the later war as the outcome the treaty postponed rather than prevented.[^ch03-check-ch4]
- **The reinterpretation.** Later generations reimagined the alliance as a Thanksgiving of harmony. The chapter should present the feast of 1621 as a brief, contingent peace built on a hard political calculation, not as the origin of a permanent friendship.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **The transition.** South and west of the Wampanoag, in present-day New York, another confederacy had built a more formal and longer-lasting union. §3 turns to the Haudenosaunee and the Great Law of Peace.

## 3. The Great Law

### 3.1 The Great Law of Peace

- **The claim.** The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was founded on the Great Law of Peace, a body of law and ceremony attributed to the Peacemaker and his helper Hiawatha (Aionwatha), with the Mother of Nations, Jigonhsasee. It organized five nations into one house with a common council and a common law.
- **The founding story.** The Peacemaker is said to have uprooted the tallest white pine, the Tree of Peace, and buried the weapons of war beneath it. He converted 50 “evil thinking men” into good leaders, the Hoyane or chiefs, and gave clan mothers the power to install and remove them.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt]
- **The structure of the house.** The five nations are pictured as one longhouse: the Mohawk and Seneca as the eastern and western door-keepers, the Onondaga as the Firekeepers who tend the council fire at the center, and the Oneida and Cayuga between them. Each nation keeps its own council; the Grand Council decides matters affecting all.[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-hiawatha-belt]
- **The chiefs and the mothers.** The 50 chiefs are distributed among the nations — nine Mohawk, nine Oneida, 14 Onondaga, ten Cayuga, and eight Seneca — and the clan mothers hold the authority to replace and remove them. This distribution of power, and the balance of Elder and Younger Brothers, is the confederacy’s constitutional form.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]
- **The civic code.** The Smithsonian’s educator guide describes the Peacemaker story as “the civic and social code of ethics” that governs how Haudenosaunee people treat one another and outsiders and how they run their traditional government. It is a living constitution, not only an origin myth.[^ch03-nmai-guide]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the confederacy as a working government with institutions, not as a colorful legend or a mere precursor to American democracy.

### 3.2 The confederacy at work

- **The claim.** The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was a participatory government that balanced national autonomy with collective decision-making, and it acted in diplomacy, war, and trade as a set of allied nations rather than a single centralized state.
- **The Grand Council.** Each nation maintains its own council with chiefs chosen by the clan mothers and handles its own internal affairs, while the Grand Council deals with matters affecting the nations within the confederacy. The main meeting place was, and remains, on Onondaga territory.[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]
- **The roles.** The Mohawk, Seneca, and Onondaga acted as Elder Brothers; the Cayuga and Oneida as Younger Brothers. The Onondaga kept the fire — the council that “never dies.”[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-hiawatha-belt]
- **The later influence.** Haudenosaunee leaders later urged the English colonies to unite on the confederacy’s example; Canasatego, an Onondaga chief, did so at Lancaster in 1744, and Benjamin Franklin invoked the confederacy in 1751 and in the 1754 Albany Plan of Union. Those later borrowings belong to Chapters 8 and 12, but the constitution that made them possible belongs here.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline]
- **The sovereignty claim.** The Haudenosaunee insist the Confederacy existed “from time immemorial” and predated European arrival, making it, in their words, “one of the first and longest lasting participatory democracies in the world.”[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should let the confederacy’s own account of its government stand beside the scholars’ dates, then turn to the dating problem in §3.3.

### 3.3 Dating the founding

- **The claim.** The founding date of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy is contested. Haudenosaunee tradition places it in time immemorial or well before European contact; scholarly estimates cluster around either 1142 or roughly 1450–1550, and the archaeology of tribal consolidation and the oral tradition do not agree. The chapter must present a range and a debate, not a single year.
- **The tradition.** The Haudenosaunee say the exact date of the joining of the nations is unknown and “said to be time immemorial”; the Hiawatha Belt was made “at the beginning of our confederacy of peace,” long before the French, Dutch, and English came.[^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-hiawatha-belt]
- **The 1142 claim.** The Onondaga Nation’s timeline states that 1142 is the earliest date scholars place on the confederacy’s formation, while the Haudenosaunee believe it was formed much earlier. The Library of Congress repeats the 1142 date, which appears to derive from an eclipse-based argument.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline][^ch03-loc-1142]
- **The archaeological view.** Iroquoian archaeology has generally indicated that the confederacy consolidated between about 1450 and 1550, based on settlement patterns and the archaeology of the five nations. This later range and the 1142 date cannot both be a single settled fact.[^ch03-check-dating]
- **The stakes.** A 1142 founding gives the Haudenosaunee a deep pre-contact constitution; a fifteenth- or sixteenth-century founding ties the confederacy to the crisis of the contact era. The dating question is not idle, and the chapter should say so.
- **The rule for the outline.** Treat the founding as contested, report each position with its proponents and its evidence, and do not print a single year as fact. The later scholarly sources (Dean Snow, Barbara Mann and Jerry Fields) are named in the Further Reading and in an editorial check, to be read in full before drafting.[^ch03-check-dating][^ch03-check-sources]

### 3.4 The Two Row Wampum and the Covenant Chain

- **The claim.** The Two Row Wampum encoded the Haudenosaunee principle that two peoples should travel side by side without one governing the other. Its 1613 date is traditional; the written document offered to prove it is a forgery; and the durable Haudenosaunee-Dutch, then Haudenosaunee-English, relationship is documented later as the Covenant Chain.
- **The principle.** One purple row is the Haudenosaunee canoe, the other the European ship, equal in weight, with friendship, peace, and “forever” as its three principles and the two rows running off the belt’s end to mark that the agreement lasts as long as the world.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]
- **The forgery.** The Tawagonshi document, “discovered” in 1968, was judged a fabrication by Gehring, Starna, and Fenton in 1987 and shown by later linguists to contain more than 40 anachronisms that place its writing in the twentieth century. It named the Mohawk, not the Mahican who lived on the lower Hudson.[^ch03-tawagonshi]
- **The later record.** The first known written non-Native record of the Silver Covenant Chain dates from Albany negotiations in 1677. The Dutch named the relationship with three silver chains for peace, friendship, and forever.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline][^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]
- **The living claim.** The Haudenosaunee still cite the Two Row Wampum as binding: in 1924 they refused the U.S. Citizenship Act on the ground that one government cannot impose laws on the other government and its people.[^ch03-onondaga-timeline]
- **The consequence.** The Two Row tradition is the chapter’s opening and its emblem: Native nations insisted on sovereignty and on a relationship of equals even when the documentary record was thin and the newcomers’ power grew. The chapter must keep the tradition, the surviving belt, the traditional 1613 date, and the forged document distinct rather than collapsing them into one claim.[^ch03-check-twowrow]

**In Focus.** The Hiawatha Belt is the national belt of the Haudenosaunee and the visual constitution of their confederacy. Five symbols joined together record the moment when five warring nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca — buried their weapons and agreed to live under one law: a single longhouse in which each nation keeps its own council fire but all govern by “one common law, one heart, and one mind.” Read from east to west, the belt names the Mohawk council fire first, then the Oneida, then the Onondaga as the Tree of Peace at the center, then the Cayuga, then the Seneca as the western door-keeper. The distribution of the 50 chiefs, the power of clan mothers to raise and remove them, and the Onondaga duty to tend the council fire are all encoded in the belt’s form. The Haudenosaunee say the belt is “extremely old,” created at the beginning of the confederacy and held long before Europeans came; the object’s material date is not established, and the belt’s own tradition places its meaning beyond a single calendar year. It is not a treaty with outsiders but a record of an internal peace — the founding of a government. The chapter reads the belt for its political argument, not as an illustration, and any reproduction must be authorized and credited in consultation with the Haudenosaunee stewards of the object.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-check-image]

## 4. Wendat, Caddo, Pueblo, and Plains political worlds

### 4.1 Wendake: the Wendat confederacy

- **The claim.** The Wendat (called Huron by the French) were a confederacy of four, later four or five, Iroquoian-speaking nations in Wendake, the country between Georgian Bay and Lake Simcoe. Their government was not centralized; most politics happened at the local, clan level, with collective decision-making among the nations.
- **The nations and the union.** The confederacy began when the Cord nation allied with the Bear nation around 1400; the Deer joined in 1570 and the Rock in 1610, and a fifth group, the People of the Marsh, may have joined later without full political membership. The Wendat were a matrilineal society in which women controlled fields and domestic life and had substantial influence over who became headmen.[^ch03-wendat]
- **The economy and towns.** Wendat society was built on maize agriculture and palisaded villages of longhouses, which families occupied for up to about 20 years before the soil was exhausted and the clan moved. At its peak the confederacy held about 30,000 people.[^ch03-wendat]
- **The French alliance.** The French needed allies, and the Wendat needed a partner against the Haudenosaunee to their south and access to European goods. Champlain’s arrival in 1603 began a partnership that made the Wendat central to New France.[^ch03-wendat]
- **The epidemics and the shift of power.** A series of epidemics in the early 1600s killed somewhere between half and two-thirds of the Wendat, devastating clan leadership. As the pool of potential headmen shrank, women lost influence, and war leaders took on more civil authority; mourning wars intensified. By 1650 about 12,000 Wendat remained.[^ch03-wendat]
- **The coming dispersal.** In 1648–49 the Haudenosaunee attacked the Wendat, and in 1650 the confederacy chose to disperse. The dispersal belongs to Chapter 6; here the chapter should show the Wendat as one of the great powers of the Great Lakes before the hammer fell.[^ch03-wendat][^ch03-check-ch4]

### 4.2 The Caddo confederacies

- **The claim.** South and west, the Caddo were not one tribe but a set of at least 25 closely affiliated groups around the Red River, in present-day Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana, and Oklahoma, described by Europeans as the Hasinai, Kadohadacho, and Natchitoches confederacies — though these were better understood as kin-based affiliated groups than as states.
- **The economy.** By about 900 CE the Caddo were a ranked, mound-building, maize-farming society with well-planned civic-ceremonial centers, elaborate mortuary ritual, and far-reaching trade in bison hides, salt, bois d’arc bows, copper, turquoise, and marine shell.[^ch03-caddo]
- **The political order.** Descent ran through the maternal line, and authority rested in a hierarchy: the xinesi, who mediated with the supreme god Caddi Ayo; the caddi, the principal headman who made political decisions and led councils; and the canahas, subordinate headmen and elders. Leaders sought consensus and led by generosity rather than coercion, creating loyalty by redistributing wealth and food.[^ch03-caddo-structure][^ch03-caddo]
- **The first European record.** The first European description of the Caddo came in 1542 from diarists with the De Soto expedition, then under Luis de Moscoso Alvarado, who described dense populations and abundant corn.[^ch03-caddo]
- **The later pressure.** European missions, ranches, and trading posts grew through the eighteenth century, and epidemics between 1691 and 1816 sharply reduced Caddo populations before removal. That later history lies beyond the chapter’s 1620 endpoint but should be named as the direction of change.[^ch03-caddo]
- **The consequence.** The Caddo show a southern Plains political order organized around religion, kinship, and trade rather than around a single paramount chief — a different answer to the same question of how to hold a people together.

### 4.3 The Pueblo world

- **The claim.** The Pueblo peoples of the Southwest lived in independent, ancient towns and held authority through hereditary, theocratic leadership rather than a single supreme chief. The towns collectively became known as “Pueblos” only through the Spanish encounter.
- **The towns.** Acoma, Zuni, and Hopi, among others, are described by anthropologists as hereditary oligarchies in which a core group of related individuals dominated leadership by controlling access to office. Their government was town-based and religious.[^ch03-pueblo]
- **The Spanish intrusion.** Juan de Oñate invaded in 1598 and established Spanish rule by terror; when Acoma resisted, Oñate ordered one leg cut from every man over 15 and the rest of the population enslaved, setting a pattern that lasted 80 years.[^ch03-pueblo]
- **The religious dimension.** Pueblo people endured what the historian Ramón Gutiérrez called an imposed theocratic utopia, backed by armed force and the whip, which suppressed Pueblo religion and made the missions a target of later revolt.[^ch03-pueblo]
- **The forward connection.** The Pueblo Revolt of 1680, the greatest and most successful Native rebellion in North American history, and the Spanish reconquest belong to Chapter 6. This chapter should establish the Pueblo towns and their subjection, not narrate the revolt.[^ch03-pueblo][^ch03-check-ch4]
- **The consequence.** The Pueblo world shows that “political organization” could mean independent towns and theocratic councils, not a paramount chief or a confederacy.

### 4.4 Plains societies

- **The claim.** The peoples of the Great Plains were as culturally varied as any population in North America. There was no single Plains way of life, and the horse — later the stereotype’s defining feature — was a late and transformative addition, not the original condition.
- **The range.** More than 30 separate tribes, each with its own language, religion, and customs, lived between the Mississippi and the Great Basin. Some were mobile bison hunters; some were settled agriculturalists in river valleys who also hunted bison; many raided for horses and goods.[^ch03-plains-nps]
- **The deep economy.** People lived on the Plains for about 13,000 years as hunter-gatherers following bison until about 700–900 CE, when maize cultivation spread from Mesoamerica and communities became semi-nomadic, combining permanent agricultural settlements with hunting.[^ch03-plains]
- **The pre-horse world.** Before the horse, few peoples settled or traveled far outside the major river valleys, because of distance and the difficulty of hunting bison on foot. Mobility and the mounted warrior came with Spanish horses in the sixteenth century and spread after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 released thousands of animals.[^ch03-plains-nps][^ch03-plains]
- **The forward connection.** The horse revolution and the rise of mounted powers such as the Comanche and Lakota belong to later chapters; this chapter should mark the Plains as a set of distinct societies on the eve of that transformation, not as the horse nomads of later images.[^ch03-plains]
- **The consequence.** The Plains complete the chapter’s demonstration that Native North America held many political orders, each adapted to its country.

## 5. European representations and Spanish, French, and Dutch footholds

### 5.1 Reading European representations

- **The claim.** The richest European descriptions of Native North America in this period were produced by people with something to gain. They are indispensable evidence and unreliable guides. The chapter should read them for what they reveal about European purposes as well as about Native societies.
- **The source problem.** The Wampanoag left few written records; most of what survives about the 1621 treaty comes from Bradford and Winslow. Tsenacomoco is known largely through John Smith, William Strachey, and other colonists. The Caddo are known first through Spanish expedition diarists.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-pocahontas][^ch03-caddo]
- **Smith and Pocahontas.** In his 1624 *Generall Historie*, Smith wrote that Pocahontas saved his life; in a letter written months after the event, he said only that he was feasted and interviewed. Smith wrote the rescue story during the Second Anglo-Powhatan War and gave his book treacherous Natives, a heroic Smith, and one “good” Indigenous figure. The Pocahontas legend grew not from her lifetime but from the 1820s, when southerners sought a colonial heroine.[^ch03-pocahontas]
- **The mediated voice.** Powhatan’s words reach us only through Smith’s later book, and they are edited and translated. The chapter should quote them as a mediated record, not as a transcript.[^ch03-powhatan]
- **The consequence.** European representations must be handled as sources with authors, audiences, and purposes, not as windows onto Native life.

### 5.2 Spanish Florida: St. Augustine, 1565

- **The claim.** St. Augustine, founded on September 8, 1565, by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, became the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in what became the United States — but it began as a small enclave beside an existing Native town and depended on the Timucua and other Native peoples.
- **The founding.** Menéndez sighted land on August 28, 1565, named the site San Augustin after Augustine of Hippo, sailed into Matanzas Bay, and disembarked near the Timucua town of Seloy on September 7. A mass was held by Father Francisco López as the Spanish came ashore.[^ch03-staugustine]
- **The Native context.** The Spanish settled beside not on empty land. The Timucua and their neighbors, and later the Guale and Apalachee, were the peoples of Spanish La Florida, and the mission system that followed tied Native communities to Spanish rule.[^ch03-staugustine]
- **The comparison.** St. Augustine predates Jamestown by 42 years and Quebec by 43. The chapter should use it to show that the Spanish were first in the territory that became the United States, a fact the English-colonial origin story tends to bury.[^ch03-staugustine]
- **The consequence.** St. Augustine is not a Spanish chapter’s stray detail. It is the earliest durable European foothold in the future United States and a case of a settlement built within a Native world.

### 5.3 French footholds: Quebec, 1608

- **The claim.** Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec in 1608 as the first permanent French settlement in America, but the colony survived only through alliances with the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat. The French foothold was a set of Native alliances as much as a town.
- **The founder.** Champlain was a soldier, cartographer, navigator, author, and colonial administrator, and the founder of Quebec in 1608. He devoted his life to justifying the colony at court and securing its support.[^ch03-champlain]
- **The alliances.** Champlain forged alliances with the Innu (Montagnais), Algonquin, and Wendat peoples, and joined them in campaigns against the Haudenosaunee. Those relationships proved essential to the survival of the colony and shaped the fur trade.[^ch03-champlain][^ch03-wendat]
- **The dependency.** The French needed partners and trade as much as the Wendat needed a counterweight to the Haudenosaunee. The relationship was a bargain between powers, not patronage.[^ch03-wendat]
- **The consequence.** New France at this date was a narrow foothold held by Native consent, its power real but dependent, and its maps built on Indigenous knowledge of the land.[^ch03-champlain]

### 5.4 Dutch footholds: the Hudson valley

- **The claim.** In 1609 Henry Hudson, sailing for the Dutch East India Company, entered New York Harbor and traveled up the river that would bear his name, meeting Mahican communities along its banks. The Dutch then built a series of fur-trading posts beginning with Fort Nassau in 1614 and Fort Orange, the first permanent Dutch settlement in New Netherland.
- **The voyage.** Hudson had been sent to find a northern route to Asia. The river led nowhere, but he recorded the region’s resources, its protected harbor, and its beavers, whose fur was valuable in Europe. His report inspired the colony of New Netherland.[^ch03-dutch-dpla]
- **The Native peoples.** The Mohican (Mahican), an Algonquian-speaking people, lived in small villages along the river, moving inland in the fall and winter; perhaps 2,000–3,000 lived in the area by the early 1600s. The Mohawk, an Iroquoian-speaking Haudenosaunee nation, lived to the west and traded at the same forts.[^ch03-fort-orange]
- **The posts.** Fort Nassau was built in 1614 as a trading house near present-day Albany; Fort Orange became the first permanent Dutch settlement in the colony. The trade in beaver fur shaped the Dutch presence and their relations with Native peoples.[^ch03-fort-orange][^ch03-dutch-dpla]
- **The Two Row connection.** The Dutch-Haudenosaunee relationship is the setting of the Two Row Wampum tradition. The Dutch, like the French and Spanish, held their foothold by trade and Native tolerance, not by settlement alone.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-dutch-dpla]
- **The consequence.** The Dutch foothold, like the others, was an enclave inside an Indigenous world; the newcomers’ maps and posts were thin lines on a Native landscape.

### 5.5 The balance by 1620

- **The claim.** By 1620 European power in North America consisted of small, dependent footholds — St. Augustine, Quebec, Fort Orange — while Native nations still held the land, the trade, and the military advantage in most places. The balance favored Native peoples at the moment the English began to settle in numbers.
- **The footholds.** The Spanish held Florida; the French held a post on the St. Lawrence; the Dutch held fur-trading forts on the Hudson; the English had Roanoke and Jamestown and would soon add Plymouth. None dominated more than a sliver of the continent.[^ch03-champlain][^ch03-fort-orange][^ch03-staugustine]
- **The Native advantage.** In the Chesapeake, Powhatan’s people vastly outnumbered the English for at least a decade, had better woodland warfare, and could have destroyed Jamestown. In New England, the Wampanoag and other nations retained their political organization even after the epidemic.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-great-dying]
- **The fragility.** The footholds were fragile and Native-dependent, but they were also the beachheads of later empires, and their growth, not their weakness, is the story Chapter 4 tells.[^ch03-check-ch4]
- **The transition.** By 1620 the newcomers were few, but they had learned the routes, the trade, and the politics. §6 turns to the historical debate over how to explain what came next.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Native nations were political actors.** Tsenacomoco, the Wampanoag, the Haudenosaunee, the Wendat, the Caddo, the Pueblos, and the Plains peoples all had governments, diplomacy, and strategies of their own.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who][^ch03-wendat]
- **Contact was plural.** There was no single encounter, no single date, and no single kind of relationship; each region produced its own pattern of trade, alliance, and conflict.[^ch03-check-names]
- **Diplomacy, not only disease or force, shaped contact.** Ousamequin’s alliance, Powhatan’s management of the English, and the Two Row principle all show Native nations managing newcomers.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-powhatan][^ch03-twowrow-onondaga]
- **The confederacy predated the Europeans.** Whatever its exact date, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy and the Hiawatha Belt existed before the French, Dutch, and English arrived.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt]
- **The 1616–19 epidemic was devastating.** It killed a large majority of the Wampanoag and reshaped the region’s balance of power, whatever its cause.[^ch03-great-dying]
- **The Tawagonshi document is a forgery.** The 1987 finding by Gehring, Starna, and Fenton and the later linguistic evidence are not seriously disputed.[^ch03-tawagonshi]

### 6.2 What is open

- **The confederacy’s founding date.** 1142 and the later archaeological range of about 1450–1550 cannot both be settled fact; the oral tradition and the archaeology have not been reconciled.[^ch03-check-dating]
- **The Two Row tradition’s documentary basis.** The tradition is real and long-lived, but no 1613 instrument survives, and the one offered document is a fabrication. How far the traditional date can be asserted remains open.[^ch03-tawagonshi]
- **The epidemic’s cause.** Smallpox, measles, yellow fever, meningitis, leptospirosis, and other diseases have all been proposed; no diagnosis is settled, and Silverman’s smallpox argument is a leading but not a unanimous view.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- **John Smith and Pocahontas.** Whether Smith’s rescue story happened, and what Pocahontas’s role was, remain disputed; the 1624 version contradicts Smith’s earlier letter.[^ch03-pocahontas]
- **Population estimates.** Tsenacomoco’s population ranges from about 8,000 to more than 30,000 depending on the estimate and the area counted; the differences reflect assumptions as much as evidence.[^ch03-tsenacomoco]
- **The reach of the middle ground.** Whether the middle ground as a process is nearly universal or the middle ground as a historical space was confined to the Great Lakes region is a live disagreement, stated by Richard White himself.[^ch03-white-creative]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The choice is not between an Indigenous-centered story and a European one, nor between Native agency and Native catastrophe. The evidence supports a history in which Native nations acted, adapted, allied, and governed, while also suffering disease and dispossession.
- **“Contact” is a poor frame.** One word cannot carry the Norse, the Spanish, the French, the Dutch, the English, the fishermen, and the traders, or the Wampanoag, Powhatan, Haudenosaunee, Wendat, Caddo, Pueblo, and Plains peoples. The chapter should use it while explaining why it obscures as much as it reveals.[^ch03-check-names]
- **The middle ground and its limits.** Richard White defined the middle ground as “a process of mutual and creative misunderstanding,” built on a rough balance of power, mutual need, and an inability of either side to compel the other. He also warned that the process is common but the durable space it produced was not; he explicitly rejected treating Native peoples as “a people of tradition,” outside modernity, whose true culture survives only in descendants.[^ch03-white-creative]
- **Applied to this chapter.** The middle ground fits the Great Lakes and the French alliance well, and fits Tsenacomoco and the Wampanoag less well. The chapter should use it where it explains and name its limits where it does not, rather than making it a universal model.[^ch03-white-creative]
- **Oral tradition and dating.** The Haudenosaunee tradition and the archaeologists’ dates are different kinds of evidence. A sound history reports both, distinguishes what each can establish, and resists converting either into the other. The tradition can establish what the Haudenosaunee hold and teach; the archaeology can establish the material chronology; neither alone settles the founding.[^ch03-check-dating]
- **The lesson.** The best history gives the strongest version of the rival account and shows what the evidence can bear. It should say that Native nations managed newcomers with diplomacy, kinship, trade, and force; that they were not passive; and that their political worlds survived the first century of contact even as they were transformed.[^ch03-white-creative][^ch03-check-sources]

## Legacies

The Age of Contact did not end Native government. Tsenacomoco fell apart under English expansion, but its descendant communities live on the Mattaponi and Pamunkey reservations and in other Virginia tribes. The Wampanoag survived epidemic and war and remain in their homeland. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy still governs through its Grand Council, and the Hiawatha Belt is still carried to council. The Caddo Nation lives in Oklahoma; the Pueblo towns are among the oldest continuously inhabited communities in North America; the Wendat live on as the Wyandot and Huron-Wendat.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-wendat][^ch03-caddo][^ch03-pueblo]

The Two Row Wampum and the Covenant Chain matter beyond the seventeenth century. The Haudenosaunee have continued to cite the Two Row principle against later impositions, and the belt’s teaching — two peoples, equal, neither steering the other — became a language of sovereignty that Native nations still use. The treaties of the contact era were not relics; they are the ground of present claims.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-onondaga-timeline]

The legacies are double, as in Chapter 2. The same century that produced diplomacy, confederacy, and alliance also produced epidemic, dispossession, and the footholds of empire. The chapter should end with the political worlds intact and under pressure, not with a verdict about who deserved what, and it should connect that pressure to the present without turning the past into a modern score.

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The Spanish, French, and Dutch footholds connected Native North America to imperial systems in Europe, the Caribbean, and Asia; the Wendat and Haudenosaunee were players in a trade that reached the Great Lakes and the Atlantic. The book’s fifty-state frame is a political geography, not a cultural one, and the chapter should say so once before returning to its Native center.[^ch03-champlain][^ch03-dutch-dpla]
- **Archives and silences.** The Wampanoag left few written records; Powhatan speaks through John Smith; the Caddo are known first through Spanish diarists; the Haudenosaunee tradition is oral and living. The chapter should name these mediations without treating them as reasons to ignore the evidence.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty][^ch03-caddo]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 2 establishes the Atlantic and epidemic frame; Chapter 4 takes up English settlement and its alliances; Chapter 6 owns King Philip’s War, the Pueblo Revolt, and the Wendat dispersal; Chapters 11–12 return to the Haudenosaunee example in the founding of the United States. This chapter’s job is to establish Native political institutions as the ground those later chapters presuppose.[^ch03-check-ch4]

## Summary

- Tsenacomoco was a paramount chiefdom of roughly 28–32 tributary groups, ruled by the mamanatowick Powhatan, whose power was real but checked by councils and by consent.[^ch03-tsenacomoco][^ch03-powhatan]
- Powhatan managed the Jamestown colonists as a potential subject people and useful source of goods, and misjudged their intransigence and numbers.[^ch03-powhatan]
- An epidemic from about 1616 to 1619 killed at least three-quarters and perhaps nine-tenths of the Wampanoag; its cause is not settled.[^ch03-great-dying][^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- Ousamequin allied with Plymouth in 1621 to protect the Wampanoag against the Narragansett and to preserve his own authority; the treaty held until after his death in 1661.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
- The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was founded on the Great Law of Peace and organized five nations under a Grand Council, with clan mothers holding the power to install and remove chiefs.[^ch03-hiawatha-belt][^ch03-haudenosaunee-who]
- The confederacy’s founding date is contested between a deep 1142 tradition and a later archaeological range of about 1450–1550.[^ch03-check-dating]
- The Two Row Wampum encodes a principle of equal, side-by-side sovereignty; its 1613 date is traditional, and the written Tawagonshi document is a twentieth-century fabrication.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-tawagonshi]
- The Wendat, Caddo, Pueblo, and Plains peoples organized power through confederacy, kinship, theocratic towns, and varied regional economies, not a single model.[^ch03-wendat][^ch03-caddo][^ch03-pueblo][^ch03-plains]
- European representations of Native people were shaped by their authors’ purposes; Smith’s Pocahontas story grew into legend long after her death.[^ch03-pocahontas]
- By 1620 the Spanish, French, and Dutch held narrow, Native-dependent footholds, and Native nations still controlled most of the land, trade, and military advantage.[^ch03-staugustine][^ch03-champlain][^ch03-fort-orange]

## Study Questions

1. What was Tsenacomoco, and why is “tribe,” “empire,” and “confederacy” each an imperfect label for it?
2. How did Powhatan try to manage the English, and where did his strategy fail? Use at least two pieces of evidence.
3. Why does the chapter call the 1616–19 epidemic “the Great Dying” while refusing to name a single disease? What would be needed to settle its cause?
4. Compare Ousamequin’s decision in 1621 with Powhatan’s diplomacy toward Jamestown. What did each leader want, and what did each risk?
5. What were the main institutions of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, and how did the Hiawatha Belt encode them?
6. Why is the founding date of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy contested? Compare the oral tradition’s evidence with the archaeologists’ evidence.
7. The Tawagonshi document is a forgery, yet the Two Row Wampum is a living treaty. How can both be true, and what does that tell you about how historians weigh different kinds of evidence?
8. Compare the Wendat, Caddo, and Pueblo political orders. What did they share, and where did they differ?
9. How did the Plains societies of about 1600 differ from the horse-nomad image of later centuries, and why does the difference matter?
10. Read John Smith’s two accounts of his meeting with Powhatan. Why do they disagree, and what can a historian responsibly conclude from them?
11. Richard White says the middle ground produced a durable “space” only where there was a rough balance of power and mutual need. Does that model fit Tsenacomoco, the Wampanoag, or the Haudenosaunee? Where does it strain?
12. Choose one source cited in this chapter. Identify its author, audience, purpose, and silences. What can it establish, and what can it not?

## Further Reading

This is a selective guide, not a citation ledger. The groups move from nation sources and primary voices to syntheses and specialized studies, then to the historiographical debate. Unless the annotation says otherwise, the works below were consulted for this outline and are cited in the combined notes. Richter, Merrell, Hämäläinen, White’s 1991 book, Snow, and Mann and Fields were consulted through samples, reviews, or related articles; their entries say so.

### Nation sources and primary voices

- **Onondaga Nation, “Two Row Wampum – Gaswéñdah” and “Hiawatha Belt.”** The Haudenosaunee account of the Two Row principle and the national belt. Read them as statements of a living tradition and of sovereignty, not as neutral chronicles.
- **Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, *Haudenosaunee Guide for Educators*.** A concise, community-informed account of the Peacemaker story and Haudenosaunee government. It is an educator’s guide, so it does not carry scholarly footnotes.
- **The Wyandot Nation of Kansas, “Wendat Confederacy.”** A nation source on Wendat origins, government, and dispersal. It supplies the community’s own framing of Wendat history.
- **John Smith, *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New-England, and the Summer Isles* (1624).** The source of the Pocahontas rescue story and of Powhatan’s words. Read it against Smith’s earlier letter and with attention to its wartime purpose.
- **Florida Historical Society, “St. Augustine founded on this date.”** A short institutional account of the September 8, 1565, founding and its Native context.

### Surveys and syntheses

- **Encyclopedia Virginia, “Tsenacomoco (Powhatan Paramount Chiefdom)” and “Powhatan (d. 1618).”** The clearest short syntheses of the Chesapeake polity, its institutions, and its paramount chief. They are encyclopedia entries, not full monographs.
- **Timothy K. Perttula, “Caddo Indians,” *Handbook of Texas Online*.** A detailed account of Caddo archaeology, society, and trade. It is a state historical association entry with a bibliography.
- **Richard White, “Creative Misunderstandings and New Understandings,” *William and Mary Quarterly* 63 (2006).** White’s own retrospective on the middle ground, its scope, and its limits. Indispensable for stating the position fairly.
- **Pekka Hämäläinen, *Lakota America* (2019) and *The Comanche Empire* (2008).** Accounts of Indigenous power and empire in the West. Their cases are later and regional, so they should not be generalized to the eastern woodlands.

### Specialized studies

- **Daniel K. Richter, *Facing East from Indian Country* (2001).** The central statement of Indigenous-centered early American history. It was consulted through reviews and publisher materials for this outline; page locators must be checked before quotation.
- **James H. Merrell, *The Indians’ New World* (1989).** A classic study of Native adaptation in the colonial Southeast. It was not read in full for this outline.
- **Bruce G. Trigger, *The Children of Aataentsic* (1976).** The standard history of the Huron to 1660. It is named as a research target, not yet consulted.
- **Dean R. Snow, *The Iroquois* (1994).** A leading archaeological account, including the confederacy’s chronology. Read alongside the Haudenosaunee tradition before fixing any date.

### The debate

- **Richard White, *The Middle Ground* (1991).** The book that named the concept. The 2006 article above is the best short guide to what White meant and did not mean.
- **Daniel K. Richter, *Facing East from Indian Country* (2001).** The best single introduction to seeing the period from Native ground.
- **Pekka Hämäläinen, *Lakota America* (2019).** A recent argument for Native power and expansion. Use it to test the limits of the middle ground in a different region and period.

> *A closing note.* The two records that anchor the chapter are a belt and a book. The Two Row Wampum survives as a living teaching about two peoples traveling side by side, yet the written “treaty” offered to date it is a twentieth-century forgery, and no 1613 instrument survives. Smith’s *Generall Historie* gives us Powhatan’s voice and Pocahontas’s rescue, but Smith wrote the rescue decades later, in wartime, and contradicts his own earlier letter. One record is a tradition without a paper; the other is a paper without a reliable memory. Together they show that the Age of Contact is documented unevenly and from the newcomers’ side, and that Native nations governed, negotiated, and remembered even where the archive is thin. The chapter ends with the political worlds intact and under pressure, not with a verdict.[^ch03-twowrow-onondaga][^ch03-pocahontas]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins with the Mayflower Compact, the surviving record of the English settlers’ own agreement aboard ship in November 1620, and asks what kinds of freedom the colonists claimed. This chapter ends with the Wampanoag alliance of 1621 and the newcomers’ narrow footholds; Chapter 4 takes up the English colonies and their alliances as the political worlds of Tsenacomoco and Wampanoag country came under settler pressure.[^ch03-wampanoag-treaty]
