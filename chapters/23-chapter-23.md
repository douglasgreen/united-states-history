## Epigraphs

> “Slavery is not abolished until the black man has the ballot. While the legislatures of the south retain the right to pass laws making any discrimination between the races, the negro is still a slave.”[^ch23-douglass-black-man-wants]

— Frederick Douglass, “What the Black Man Wants,” Boston, January 26, 1865.[^ch23-douglass-black-man-wants]

> “In fact, the distinction of race and color is by the bill made to operate in favor of the colored and against the white race.”[^ch23-app-veto]

— Andrew Johnson, veto message on the Civil Rights Act, Washington, D.C., March 27, 1866.[^ch23-app-veto]

> “The slave went free; stood a brief moment in the sun; then moved back again toward slavery.”[^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]

— W. E. B. Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America*, 1935.[^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]

- **Why these three.** A Black abolitionist argued that emancipation without the ballot was a mockery. The president who inherited Reconstruction insisted that federal protection for one race was discrimination against another. A historian writing seventy years later compressed the era into a brief interval of freedom. The three disagree about whether freedom was incomplete, whether defending it was favoritism, or whether it had already been reversed.[^ch23-check-epigraphs]
- **The attribution rule.** Douglass’s sentences must be collated against the Frederick Douglass Papers; the consulted transcription attributes the address to the American Anti-Slavery Society while many editions cite the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society, and the discrepancy must be resolved. Johnson’s message must be collated against the Senate Journal and the *Statutes at Large*; the American Presidency Project transcript is a convenient witness, not the final authority. Du Bois’s sentence is quoted from the widely reprinted 1935 text and must be collated against the consulted edition’s page.[^ch23-check-epigraphs][^ch23-douglass-black-man-wants][^ch23-app-veto][^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]
- **The historian’s voice.** Du Bois supplies the required historian’s voice and frames the chapter’s central disagreement: whether Reconstruction was a democratic experiment that failed, a constitutional revolution that was reversed, or a revolution that the nation never intended to complete.[^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction][^ch23-aaihs-dubois]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the ballot alone would have protected freedom, that Johnson spoke for a national majority, or that the reversal was inevitable. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and the date.** Under Andrew Johnson’s presidential Reconstruction, Mississippi’s newly elected legislature met in late 1865 and enacted what became known as the Black Code. The package included a vagrancy law and “An Act to Confer Civil Rights on Freedmen, and for other Purposes,” enacted in late November 1865.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]
- **The vagrancy law.** Section 2 declared that freedmen, free Negroes, and mulattoes over 18 found “with no lawful employment or business, or found unlawfully assembling themselves together” should “be deemed vagrants,” fined up to $150 rather than the $200 set for a White man, and imprisoned. Section 7 made failure to pay a tax “prima facie evidence of vagrancy” and required the sheriff to “hire … such delinquent taxpayer to anyone who will pay the said tax.”[^ch23-yawp-black-code]
- **The “civil rights” act.** Section 1 let freedmen sue and be sued and acquire personal property, but barred them from renting or leasing land outside incorporated towns. Section 7 authorized every civil officer, and any person, to arrest and carry back a worker “who shall have quit the service of his or her employer before the expiration of his or her term of service without good cause,” with a $5 reward and 10 cents per mile charged against the worker’s wages.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]
- **The penal code.** The code re-enacted the antebellum laws “defining offenses and prescribing the mode of punishment for crimes and misdemeanors committed by slaves, free Negroes, or mulattoes” against freedmen, and authorized hiring out convicts who could not pay their fines. The pattern repeats across the South, with South Carolina enacting its own code.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]
- **What the scene can show.** The scene can show the enacted text, the legislature’s setting in Jackson, and the contrast between the code’s grant of contract and property rights and its denial of land, mobility, and the ballot. It cannot show what individual legislators said or thought, and it must not stage floor debate or dialogue the record does not support.[^ch23-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A government defines freedom, argued from the record.** The scene’s claim is not that the Black Code restored slavery, but that a state elected under presidential Reconstruction tried to fix the meaning of emancipation as a labor discipline enforced by the criminal law and the courts. The freedpeople’s answer to that attempt is the chapter’s subject.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-mshistorynow]
- **The stakes.** The code appeared in the same months that Congress was deciding whether the federal government would guarantee the rights the states were curtailing. The scene sets up the collision between state power and federal responsibility that organizes §§1–2.[^ch23-check-scope][^ch23-mshistorynow]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how was freedom built, and how was its protection withdrawn?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from Mississippi to Washington, to the freedpeople’s schools and churches, and to the courthouse at Colfax.

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The statutes, their dates, and their provisions are documented. The chamber, the weather, and the members’ private reasoning must not be staged beyond what witnesses recorded.[^ch23-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The legislators of late 1865 did not know that Congress would override a presidential veto, pass the Reconstruction Acts, or impeach the president. The scene must preserve the uncertainty of the moment.[^ch23-check-scene][^ch23-check-dates]
- **No claim that the code “re-enslaved” Black Mississippians as a settled fact.** The chapter should say that the code sought to restore much of the old labor discipline and that its terms were contested and later partly struck down, not that slavery legally returned.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-yawp-black-code]

Three hundred to four hundred words. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows and supplies the years, scope, and limits.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes][^ch23-check-scene]

## 1. Presidential Reconstruction

### 1.1 Johnson’s assumptions and the restored states

- **The claim.** Andrew Johnson’s presidential Reconstruction rested on a narrow theory of the Union and a broad trust in White southern self-government, and it returned the former Confederate states to power without securing the rights of the people they had enslaved.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-senate-impeachment]
- **The plan.** In May 1865 Johnson issued guidelines modeled on Lincoln’s wartime plan: amnesty to those who took a loyalty oath, with individual pardon required of former high civil and military officers and of owners of property worth $20,000 or more in 1860; and readmission once 10 percent of a state’s voters had sworn loyalty and formed a government. Johnson appointed provisional governors, including William L. Sharkey in Mississippi, to organize conventions and elections.[^ch23-mshistorynow]
- **The result.** The restored governments were controlled largely by White southerners who had supported the Confederacy, and the new legislatures moved quickly to define the freedpeople’s status. Johnson pardoned many of the wealthiest and most prominent Confederates and used his veto against the earliest federal protections.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-constitutioncenter-civilrights1866]
- **The limits of the claim.** The chapter should present Johnson’s policy as a set of consequential choices made under real constraints, including his belief that the war had settled secession but not the racial order, rather than as pure malice.[^ch23-senate-impeachment][^ch23-check-scope]

### 1.2 The Black Codes

- **The claim.** The Black Codes sought to define emancipation as a racialized labor regime, granting a narrow civil capacity while denying land, mobility, and political rights.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]
- **The pattern.** Mississippi’s code is the exemplary case: the vagrancy law treated unemployment and assembly as crimes and hired out those who could not pay; the “civil rights” act let freedpeople contract and own property but not rent land; the penal code re-enacted the slave code. South Carolina and other states adopted comparable laws.[^ch23-yawp-black-code][^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes]
- **The purpose and the effect.** The codes gave employers a criminal-law remedy for labor discipline and gave the state a means of controlling a population it would not enfranchise. They also produced a documentary record of the freedpeople’s refusal to accept the terms, as workers left plantations, sought land, and demanded schooling.[^ch23-constitutioncenter-black-codes][^ch23-mshistorynow]
- **The consequence.** The codes, and the violence that accompanied them, persuaded many northern Republicans that federal protection was necessary, which leads to §1.3 and §2.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-memphis-massacre]

### 1.3 The break of 1866

- **The claim.** The veto fights, the massacres of 1866, and the Fourteenth Amendment turned Reconstruction from a presidential project into a congressional one.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-archives-14th]
- **The Freedmen’s Bureau.** Congress created the Bureau on March 3, 1865, and moved in 1866 to extend it over Johnson’s veto; the Bureau’s wartime and one-year mandate, its supervision of contracts, and its relief work made it a target of the president and of White southerners.[^ch23-senate-freedmens-bureau][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]
- **The Civil Rights Act.** Senator Lyman Trumbull’s bill declared all persons born in the United States, “excluding Indians not taxed,” citizens with the same contracting, property, and legal rights as White citizens. Congress overrode Johnson’s veto on April 9, 1866; the House vote was 122 to 41, the first time Congress legislated on civil rights.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-constitutioncenter-civilrights1866][^ch23-app-veto]
- **The massacres.** In May 1866, White police and mobs in Memphis attacked Black residents over three days; in July 1866, a White mob attacked a Black convention in New Orleans. The Memphis project documents the first large-scale racial massacre of the postwar South, and both events lent urgency to federal action.[^ch23-memphis-massacre][^ch23-mshistorynow]
- **The Fourteenth Amendment.** Congress passed the amendment on June 13, 1866, and submitted it to the states on June 16; it defined citizenship, guaranteed due process and equal protection, reduced representation for disfranchisement, and barred former Confederates from office. It was ratified on July 9, 1868.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-check-amendments]
- **The 1866 elections.** The Republican victory in 1866 gave Congress the votes to override Johnson and to build a statutory and constitutional reconstruction, which leads to §2.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-house-civilrights1866]

## 2. Congressional Reconstruction

### 2.1 The Fourteenth Amendment and the terms of citizenship

- **The claim.** The Fourteenth Amendment made national citizenship and equal protection constitutional, transferring a measure of authority over rights from the states to the federal government, but its enforcement provisions and its representation formula left much to Congress and to the courts.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-foner-second-founding]
- **The text.** Section 1 defined citizenship and prohibited states from abridging privileges or immunities, depriving persons of due process, or denying equal protection. Section 2 reduced representation where the male franchise was denied, and Section 5 gave Congress enforcement power.[^ch23-archives-14th]
- **The excluded.** The amendment’s representation formula counted “the whole number of persons,” “excluding Indians not taxed,” a phrase that left Native Americans within tribal structures outside the citizenship it declared, a question taken up in §5.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy]
- **The ratification problem.** The amendment’s passage on June 13, 1866, its submission to the states, and its ratification on July 9, 1868, must be kept distinct, and the Tennessee readmission and the 1868 elections that followed depend on the sequence.[^ch23-check-amendments][^ch23-archives-14th]

### 2.2 The Reconstruction Acts of 1867

- **The claim.** The Reconstruction Acts put the former Confederate states, except Tennessee, under military authority and made Black male suffrage and the Fourteenth Amendment conditions of readmission.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]
- **The first act.** Passed over Johnson’s veto on March 2, 1867, the act declared that “no legal State governments or adequate protection for life or property” existed in 10 named states, divided them into five military districts, and directed each commander to protect “all persons in their rights of person and property” and to suppress disorder.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]
- **The supplementary acts.** The acts of March 23 and July 19, 1867, required a registration of male citizens 21 and over under an oath of loyalty, called conventions to frame new constitutions, and made Congress’s approval of each constitution a condition of representation.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]
- **The limit.** The acts authorized force in principle but depended on the president and the army to enforce it, and Johnson’s administration used removals and instructions to restrain the commanders. This tension between authority and will runs through the chapter.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts][^ch23-senate-impeachment]
- **The map.** **Map 19 plan.** The registered visual `ch23-map-01` (Map 19, “Reconstruction, participation, and violence, 1865–1877”) belongs here and in §§3–4. It should show the five military districts, the areas of the heaviest Klan violence and the counties placed under martial law in 1871, and the pattern of Black political participation, without implying that geography determined the outcome. It needs a caption, source line, credit, alt text, and accessible description, and it must reconcile with the register and Appendix C.[^ch23-check-map]

### 2.3 Conventions, constitutions, and Black participation

- **The claim.** The Reconstruction conventions were the era’s most direct experiment in interracial self-government, and Black delegates helped write constitutions that established public schooling and expanded rights.[^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders][^ch23-mshistorynow]
- **The conventions.** The Mississippi convention that opened on January 7, 1868, included 16 Black and 78 White delegates and produced a constitution providing for Black male suffrage and a system of free public schools, among other provisions.[^ch23-zinn-ms-convention]
- **The scale.** Historians’ tables count roughly 1,500 Black officeholders in the southern states during Reconstruction, plus about 185 federal officeholders, including 267 Black delegates to the constitutional conventions of 1867–69 and 795 Black state legislators. The figures vary by source and definition and must be reconciled before publication.[^ch23-check-numbers][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]
- **The consequence.** The conventions created governments that depended on Black voters and on the willingness of the federal government to protect them, which made those governments the target of the counterrevolution in §4.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-mshistorynow]

### 2.4 The Fifteenth Amendment and the Grant years

- **The claim.** The Fifteenth Amendment barred disfranchisement on account of race, and the election of Ulysses S. Grant in 1868 put the presidency behind Reconstruction, but the amendment’s silence on gender and its enforcement gap limited its reach.[^ch23-archives-15th][^ch23-battlefields-1868]
- **The amendment.** Passed by Congress on February 26, 1869, and ratified on February 3, 1870, it protected the vote against denial “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude” and gave Congress enforcement power.[^ch23-archives-15th]
- **The election of 1868.** Held on November 3, 1868, it was the first presidential election in which newly freed Black men could vote in some states; Grant defeated Horatio Seymour with 214 electoral votes to 80.[^ch23-battlefields-1868]
- **The division.** The amendment split the women’s suffrage movement. The insertion of the word “male” in the Fourteenth Amendment and the Fifteenth Amendment’s silence on gender led Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony to oppose it, while others defended it as “the Negro’s hour”; this is an optional thread, not a section.[^ch23-nps-womens-suffrage-split]
- **The limits.** The amendment prohibited racial disfranchisement but left poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence available, and the Court’s later reading of the enforcement power narrowed what Congress could do.[^ch23-archives-15th][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]

### 2.5 Impeachment and the limits of federal will

- **The claim.** Johnson’s conflict with Congress culminated in impeachment, but his acquittal by one vote showed the limits of Reconstruction’s constitutional instruments and left enforcement dependent on the executive.[^ch23-senate-impeachment]
- **The impeachment.** The House voted to impeach on February 24, 1868, over Johnson’s removal of Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act. The Senate trial began on March 5.[^ch23-senate-impeachment]
- **The acquittal.** On May 16, 1868, the Senate fell one vote short of the two-thirds needed, 35 guilty and 19 not guilty; votes on further articles failed on May 26, and the court of impeachment adjourned. The outcome reflected concern for the office of the presidency as much as support for Johnson’s policies.[^ch23-senate-impeachment][^ch23-check-dates]
- **The consequence.** Johnson’s acquittal and the tenor of his administration showed that Reconstruction would rest on a fragile combination of congressional will and executive enforcement, a theme that runs through §§4 and 6.[^ch23-senate-impeachment][^ch23-check-scope]

## 3. Families, labor, schools, churches, and officeholding

### 3.1 Families and marriage

- **The claim.** Freedpeople made the family and the marriage contract the first arena of freedom, and the Freedmen’s Bureau became the agency through which many of those claims were legalized.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]
- **The work.** Bureau agents legalized marriages entered into during slavery, helped reunite families separated by sale and wartime displacement, and provided transportation to freedpeople seeking relatives. The Bureau recorded these acts, making its files a principal archive of Black family reconstruction.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]
- **The meaning.** By formalizing their marriages, freedpeople made claims on one another and on the state, and they sought federal recognition of relationships the slave regime had refused to protect.[^ch23-gilder-families]
- **The limits.** The record documents what the Bureau recorded; the interior lives and the separations that left no paperwork survive unevenly, and the chapter must name that asymmetry rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau]

### 3.2 Labor, contract, and sharecropping

- **The claim.** Freedpeople entered freedom with their labor as their main asset, and the contract system, the crop lien, and sharecropping converted legal freedom into economic dependence for many.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-gilder-families]
- **The contract system.** The Bureau negotiated written contracts between planters and freedpeople and supervised their terms. Many freedpeople resented the system and tried to avoid it.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-gilder-families]
- **Sharecropping.** Following the failure of land reform and the return of most land to its former owners, sharecropping became the dominant arrangement. In Georgia, 32 percent of farms were operated by sharecroppers by 1880, and 37 percent of the state’s 291,027 farms by 1910; a worker who could offer only labor often received as little as one-third of the crop, and crop liens kept many families in debt.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-check-numbers]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give the Georgia figures with their dates, denominators, and source, and it should not treat the debt cycle as uniform in time and place, since evidence shows substantial movement among arrangements.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping][^ch23-check-numbers]

### 3.3 In Focus: a Freedmen’s Bureau labor contract

- **The claim.** A single Bureau-approved contract from 1865 shows how the federal government tried to convert the master–servant relation into a written wage relation, and how thin the change could be.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract][^ch23-capitol-contract]
- **In Focus: the document.** **In Focus.** On August 28, 1865, James Sinclair, an agent of the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands for Robeson County, North Carolina, witnessed and approved an agreement in Lumberton between Robert McKenzie and Truss B. Hall. McKenzie agreed to pay Hall $4.00 a month until December 25 “for and in sideration of the said Truss B. Hall rendering the Robert McKenzie true and faithful service and obey all lawful commands as he use to when a slave.” Hall signed by his mark, and the Bureau agent’s approval made the instrument enforceable.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract]
- **How to read it.** The box should treat the contract as evidence of both the federal presence and its limits. The instrument gave Hall a wage and a term, but its language carried over the vocabulary of bondage, and its enforcement depended on the same Bureau agent and the same planters who dominated local power.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract][^ch23-capitol-contract]
- **The comparison.** A second contract from Kentucky, commencing January 19, 1866, between the employer Abraham Bledsoe and the freedman Henry Bledsoe, sets conditions and penalties for both parties and shows the same form. If reproduced, either document’s facsimile must clear rights and be registered separately.[^ch23-capitol-contract][^ch23-check-focus]
- **The evidence and its limits.** The Robeson County transcription is a single instrument in one county; the chapter should not generalize from it about all Bureau contracts, and it should present the language as the record gives it, including the original spelling.[^ch23-dpla-labor-contract][^ch23-check-focus]

### 3.4 Schools and churches

- **The claim.** Black schools and churches were the institutions freedpeople built first and kept, and they became the foundation of a distinct political and civic life.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches]
- **The schools.** The Freedmen’s Bureau did not hire teachers or operate schools itself, but assisted the aid societies and the freedpeople. In Georgia at least 8,000 students attended freedmen’s schools within a year of freedom, and nearly 20,000 within eight years; freedpeople paid tuition, raised funds, and built schoolhouses, and adults were often a third of the students, attending night and Sabbath schools.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education]
- **The churches.** Black Baptist and Episcopal congregations became sites where freedpeople worked out the terms of political participation, even as those churches often excluded women from formal leadership—an outcome that reinforced the era’s male-centered franchise politics.[^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches]
- **The consequence.** These institutions outlasted Reconstruction and carried the demand for rights into the long decades of Jim Crow, which is the chapter’s forward connection to Chapter 26.[^ch23-check-ch26][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education]

### 3.5 Officeholding and political life

- **The claim.** Black officeholding was real and consequential, and it was persistently exaggerated and vilified as “Negro rule” by those who sought to overturn it.[^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]
- **The national example.** Hiram Revels of Mississippi was seated on February 25, 1870, after the Senate voted 48 to 8 to admit him, becoming the first African American to serve in Congress; the vote turned on whether the Fourteenth Amendment had reversed *Dred Scott*.[^ch23-senate-revels]
- **The longer record.** Blanche K. Bruce, also of Mississippi, was elected to a full Senate term in 1874 and became the first African American to preside over the Senate in 1879. During Reconstruction, 17 African Americans served in Congress, 15 in the House and two in the Senate.[^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]
- **The measure.** The officeholders held federal, state, and local posts, including six lieutenant governors, nine secretaries of state, and one governor, P. B. S. Pinchback of Louisiana. The counts differ by source and should be presented as estimates with their definitions.[^ch23-check-numbers][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]
- **The falsity of “Negro rule.”** Black officeholders never held power proportionate to their numbers, and the caricature served the counterrevolution that §4 describes.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-senate-revels]

## 4. White supremacist counterrevolution

### 4.1 The Klan and paramilitary violence

- **The claim.** The counterrevolution was organized, armed, and aimed at the political capacity of Black communities and their White allies, not at crime or disorder.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-senate-enforcement]
- **The origins.** The Ku Klux Klan was founded in December 1865 by former Confederate officers and grew as a secret vigilante force targeting voters, officeholders, teachers, and jurors. It was one of several armed groups, alongside White Leagues and rifle clubs.[^ch23-eji-martial-law]
- **The pattern.** Violence concentrated in counties where Black majorities were entering politics. Federal investigators found in York County, South Carolina, alone evidence of 11 murders and more than 600 whippings and assaults; Attorney General Amos Akerman described the counties as “under the domination of systematic and organized depravity.”[^ch23-eji-martial-law]
- **The consequence.** The violence made the federal government’s enforcement power the decisive question of Reconstruction, which leads to §4.2.[^ch23-senate-enforcement][^ch23-house-kkk-act]

### 4.2 Enforcement and Grant’s response

- **The claim.** Congress answered the violence with a series of Enforcement Acts, and Grant used them, briefly, with real effect, before the will to enforce them receded.[^ch23-senate-enforcement][^ch23-eji-martial-law]
- **The acts.** The Enforcement Act of May 1870 targeted conspiracies to violate citizens’ rights, including going “in disguise upon the public highways.” The Second Force Act of February 1871 placed national elections under federal supervision. The Third Force Act, the Ku Klux Klan Act of April 20, 1871, made it a federal crime to deprive any person of the rights named in the Constitution and authorized the president to use the military and to suspend habeas corpus.[^ch23-senate-enforcement][^ch23-house-kkk-act]
- **The intervention.** After a warning on October 12, 1871, Grant declared martial law and suspended habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties on October 17. Roughly 600 Klansmen were jailed by December, more than 200 were indicted, 53 pleaded guilty, and five were convicted at trial; Klan terrorism in the state declined significantly.[^ch23-eji-martial-law]
- **The limits.** The gains depended on federal prosecutions and on local juries and officials. The violence subsided but did not end, and the legal foundation for enforcement was about to be narrowed by the courts.[^ch23-fjc-cruikshank][^ch23-senate-enforcement]

### 4.3 Colfax and the Supreme Court’s retreat

- **The claim.** The Colfax Massacre of 1873 and the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Cruikshank* in 1876 showed that the constitutional guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment would not reach the private violence that was overturning Reconstruction.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]
- **Colfax.** On Easter Sunday, April 13, 1873, in Colfax, the seat of Grant Parish, Louisiana, about 140 heavily armed White men, led by former Confederate officers, attacked a roughly equal number of Black men defending a Republican slate of parish officials after Louisiana’s disputed 1872 elections. Most of the Black men were killed while fleeing or after being taken captive. The death toll is contested, with claims ranging from about 50 to 400 and many recent accounts giving about 150.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-jcwe-colfax-count]
- **The prosecution.** Federal authorities secured convictions against three perpetrators in 1874 under the Enforcement Act, but the Supreme Court overturned the verdicts in *United States v. Cruikshank* on March 27, 1876, holding that the rights at issue were not enforceable against private individuals and that the Fourteenth Amendment applied only to state action.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]
- **The wider retreat.** The Court’s narrow reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, anticipated in the *Slaughter-House Cases* of 1873, eviscerated the federal enforcement that Congress had built. The Civil Rights Act of 1875, passed on March 1, 1875, would be struck down in 1883, a decision that belongs to Chapter 26.[^ch23-senate-civilrights1875][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank][^ch23-check-ch26]
- **The consequence.** After *Cruikshank*, the federal government could not easily prosecute the violence that was restoring White rule, and the counterrevolution accelerated.[^ch23-jcwe-colfax-count][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

### 4.4 The Mississippi Plan and Redemption

- **The claim.** “Redemption” was not a return to lawful order but a campaign of intimidation, fraud, and violence that dismantled Black political power state by state.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The method.** In Mississippi in 1875, Democrats organized armed clubs, disrupted Republican meetings, and drove Black voters from the polls, electing a Democratic legislature and governor and effectively ending Reconstruction in the state; the pattern was repeated across the South.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The name.** “Redemption” was the participants’ own term for the overthrow of the Reconstruction governments, and “the Mississippi Plan” became the name for the techniques later formalized in disfranchising constitutions.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-names]
- **The consequence.** By the mid-1870s the surviving Republican governments in the South depended on federal troops and on contested election returns, setting up the crisis of 1876–77 in §6.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-race]

## 5. Western citizenship and land

### 5.1 The bounded citizenship of the Fourteenth Amendment

- **The claim.** The Fourteenth Amendment defined citizenship in a way that included the formerly enslaved while leaving many Native Americans outside it, showing that the era’s redefinition of “the people” was both expansive and bounded.[^ch23-archives-14th]
- **The text.** Section 1 granted citizenship to all persons born or naturalized and subject to the jurisdiction of the United States, and Section 2 apportioned representation by “the whole number of persons in each State, excluding Indians not taxed,” a phrase that left those within tribal structures outside the count and, for many, outside the citizenship the amendment declared.[^ch23-archives-14th]
- **The consequence.** The amendment’s reach and its exclusions were settled later by statute and litigation, but the pairing of Black citizenship with Native non-citizenship was a defining feature of Reconstruction’s constitutional order.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-archives-14th]

### 5.2 Grant’s Peace Policy and the end of treaty-making

- **The claim.** The same federal government that reconstructed the South also reorganized its relations with Native nations, substituting a “peace policy” and unilateral legislation for treaty-making as the framework of western policy.[^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]
- **The policy.** Grant took office on March 4, 1869, and endorsed a plan advanced by Ely S. Parker, a Seneca leader and the first Native American Commissioner of Indian Affairs, and by the 1867 Indian Peace Commission, to reform the Bureau of Indian Affairs and distribute the management of reservations among Christian denominations while supporting western settlement and railroads.[^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy]
- **The end of treaties.** In 1871 Congress ended formal treaty-making, providing that no Indian nation or tribe “shall be acknowledged or recognized as an independent nation, tribe or power with whom the United States may contract by treaty.” Congress agreed to honor the roughly 368 treaties ratified between 1778 and 1868, but the diplomatic framework had changed.[^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]
- **The limit.** The policy’s reformers sought to end fraud and war, yet it accompanied and enabled the dispossession that Chapter 25 narrates; the chapter should present the reform and the dispossession together.[^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy][^ch23-check-ch25]

### 5.3 Railroads, land, and the western political economy

- **The claim.** The postwar West was being integrated into a national market by railroads and federal land policy at the same time that the South was being reconstructed, and the two projects shared a logic of federal subsidy and racialized belonging.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]
- **The measure.** Railroad construction boomed after the war: 35,000 miles of new track were laid between 1866 and 1873, and the first transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869. Railroads were the nation’s largest non-agricultural employer and the object of federal land grants and loans.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]
- **The connection.** The same Congress that imposed conditions on the southern states subsidized and organized the western territories, and the same racial logic that bounded citizenship in the Fourteenth Amendment shaped land and treaty policy in the West.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-archives-14th]
- **The hand-off.** The chapter keeps this connection concise; sustained conquest, wars, boarding schools, and allotment belong to Chapter 25, and the chapter should cross-reference rather than duplicate them.[^ch23-check-ch25][^ch23-check-scope]

### 5.4 The concise western frame

- **The claim.** Reconstruction was a national renegotiation of citizenship and land, not a southern episode alone, and the West is part of its history.[^ch23-check-scope][^ch23-nps-grant-indian-policy]
- **The frame.** Two sentences in the chapter should place the West beside the South: the West’s integration by railroad and subsidy, and the narrowing of Native sovereignty by the end of treaty-making, both belong to the same years as the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873][^ch23-aim-1871-treaties]
- **The exclusions.** The chapter does not narrate the Plains wars, the boarding schools, or the Dawes Act; it states the connection and hands it to Chapter 25.[^ch23-check-ch25]

## 6. The 1876–77 settlement

### 6.1 The disputed election of 1876

- **The claim.** The election of 1876 ended Reconstruction not by a clear verdict but by a disputed count resolved through a commission and an informal understanding.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The election.** Democrat Samuel J. Tilden ran against Republican Rutherford B. Hayes. The outcomes in South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana were unclear, and Republican-controlled returning boards in all three states threw out enough Democratic votes to award their electoral votes to Hayes; a dispute over an Oregon elector added a fourth contested state.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The count.** On December 6, 1876, conflicting electors met in Salem, Columbia, Tallahassee, and New Orleans. Tilden had 184 electoral votes and Hayes 165, with 20 disputed. The election saw turnout reported at about 82 percent, the highest in United States history.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-numbers]

### 6.2 The Electoral Commission and the count

- **The claim.** Congress resolved the crisis by creating an Electoral Commission whose composition and vote decided the presidency, not by a constitutional rule that all parties accepted.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The commission.** The Electoral Commission Act of January 29, 1877, created a commission of five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices. The independent justice refused to serve and was replaced by a Republican, and the commission voted 8 to 7 to award the disputed votes to Hayes.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The resolution.** After Democratic threats of filibuster, Speaker Samuel J. Randall forced the completion of the count in the early hours of March 2, 1877, and Hayes was declared the winner, 185 to 184, two days before his inauguration.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The limit.** The chapter must separate the disputed election, the commission’s vote, and the withdrawal of troops as three distinct decisions, not a single bargain.[^ch23-check-scope][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

### 6.3 The withdrawal of troops and the “compromise” question

- **The claim.** Hayes withdrew the remaining federal troops from the statehouses of Louisiana and South Carolina after Democratic pledges to protect the rights of Black and White Republicans, and the pledges were not kept; whether a formal “Compromise of 1877” existed is disputed.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The withdrawal.** When Hayes took office, federal troops protecting Republican governments were limited to small areas around the statehouses in New Orleans and Columbia. After the Democrats pledged to uphold civil and voting rights, Hayes pulled the troops out, and White southerners quickly disfranchised Black voters through poll taxes, literacy tests, and intimidation.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The historiographical caveat.** The Miller Center judges it “doubtful that Hayes, his supporters, and Democrats reached any sort of deal beyond what Hayes promised to do in his letter of acceptance.” The chapter should present the “Compromise of 1877” as a name historians have given to a contested cluster of events, not as a documented agreement with named terms.[^ch23-millercenter-1876][^ch23-check-scope]
- **The consequence.** The withdrawal removed the last federal shield from the surviving Reconstruction governments and made Redemption complete, which the Legacies take up.[^ch23-check-race][^ch23-millercenter-1876]

### 6.4 The Panic of 1873 and the national turn away

- **The claim.** The long depression that began in 1873 turned northern attention from civil rights to economic distress and helped make the withdrawal of federal protection politically acceptable.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]
- **The panic.** The banking firm Jay Cooke and Company, heavily invested in railroad construction, closed on September 18, 1873, and a major panic followed. By 1876, 89 of the country’s 364 railroads had collapsed, 18,000 businesses had failed in two years, and unemployment had risen to about 14 percent.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]
- **The political effect.** Workers and businesspeople fought over relief, Grant sided with eastern financial interests, and the North grew “less and less concerned with addressing racism in the South” as falling farm prices and labor strife dominated politics.[^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]
- **The connection.** The depression and the 1877 railroad strikes frame Chapter 24’s account of industrial conflict and the standardization of time, which §24 opens by naming.[^ch23-check-ch24][^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

- **Slavery was abolished and citizenship redefined.** The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments settled slavery’s legal status, national citizenship, equal protection, and racial disfranchisement, whatever their later enforcement.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-archives-15th]
- **Black political participation was real.** Hundreds of thousands voted, hundreds held office, and Black delegates helped write state constitutions; the “Negro rule” caricature is false.[^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders]
- **The counterrevolution was organized violence.** The Klan, White Leagues, and rifle clubs used terror and fraud to overturn Reconstruction governments, and federal enforcement was real but temporary.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-archives-colfax]
- **The constitutional guarantees were narrowed by the courts.** *Cruikshank* and related decisions limited the reach of the Fourteenth Amendment and undercut federal enforcement.[^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]
- **Land and labor were not settled.** The amendments did not distribute land or guarantee a livelihood, and sharecropping and the crop lien bound many freedpeople to dependence.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping]

### 7.2 What is open

- **Was Reconstruction a revolution, an occupation, a democratic experiment, or a counterrevolution?** Historians disagree about the era’s essential character and about which term best captures it.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-aaihs-dubois][^ch23-bensel-yankee-leviathan]
- **Did a “Compromise of 1877” exist?** The withdrawal of troops is certain; a formal bargain is not documented, and the chapter should say so.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **How much did federal will, as against southern violence and northern economics, decide the outcome?** The relative weight of enforcement, terror, and the depression remains debated.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-pbs-panic-1873]
- **What did the era settle for the long run?** The amendments’ later force, from the twentieth-century courts to the civil rights movement, is a legacy the era’s actors could not foresee.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-foner-second-founding]
- **Whose memory prevailed?** The Lost Cause account of Reconstruction as misrule competed with the emancipationist memory, and the chapter must not treat either as inevitable.[^ch23-blight-race-reunion][^ch23-aaihs-dubois]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which Black people built freedom under impossible conditions, Congress created real constitutional guarantees, and a violent counterrevolution combined with northern retreat to withdraw their protection; the era was neither a simple failure nor a completed revolution.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-aaihs-dubois]
- **The Du Bois position.** Du Bois argued that enslaved people had already struck for their freedom and that Reconstruction was a genuine democratic experiment, not the corrupt misrule of the Dunning School; his account restored Black agency and political economy to the center of the story.[^ch23-aaihs-dubois][^ch23-dubois-black-reconstruction]
- **The revolution position.** Eric Foner describes Reconstruction as an unfinished revolution and the amendments as a “second founding” that remade the Constitution and the federal government.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-foner-reconstruction]
- **The occupation and state-building position.** Accounts of Reconstruction as an occupation stress how few troops sustained it and how quickly northern will receded, while state-building accounts such as Bensel’s emphasize the durable federal capacities the era created even as its southern reach was uneven.[^ch23-bensel-yankee-leviathan][^ch23-millercenter-1876]
- **The counterrevolution position.** The Dunning School and its Lost Cause foundations treated Reconstruction as an error and Redemption as rescue; modern scholarship, including Blight’s, shows how reunion purchased a White republic and suppressed the emancipationist memory.[^ch23-blight-race-reunion][^ch23-foner-second-founding]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Freedom was built, constitutionalized, and then starved of protection; the chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not teams.[^ch23-check-table]

## Legacies

Reconstruction ended with slavery abolished, citizenship defined, and Black political participation demonstrated, yet with the guarantees of the amendments largely unenforced and the federal government withdrawing from the South. The freedpeople who had legalized their marriages, built schools, and sent delegates to state conventions had proved that they could make freedom real where they had power; what they lacked was protection from violence and a land base, and both were withheld.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping]

The amendments’ promise did not disappear when the troops left. The Fourteenth Amendment’s text, narrowed in the 1870s and 1880s, became the foundation on which twentieth-century courts and movements would build; the institutional life of Black churches and schools carried the demand for rights through the decades of Jim Crow; and the memory of Reconstruction as a failure or a betrayal shaped American politics for a century.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches][^ch23-blight-race-reunion]

The chapter’s lasting legacy is a set of questions that outlived the era: whether constitutional guarantees mean anything without enforcement, whether freedom requires land and a livelihood, and whether a nation that had abolished slavery would protect the people it had freed. Those questions passed to the industrial order that Chapter 24 describes.[^ch23-check-ch24][^ch23-check-ch26]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** Emancipation in the United States took place alongside abolition in the British Empire and the Atlantic world, and Reconstruction’s questions about labor, citizenship, and race were debated internationally. The chapter should place the amendments beside those debates without claiming that American events caused foreign reforms.[^ch23-foner-second-founding][^ch23-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** The amendments, the Reconstruction Acts, the Enforcement Acts, and the Bureau’s files survive in abundance, while the interior lives of the freedpeople and the undocumented violence of the Klan survive unevenly. The chapter should name that asymmetry without speaking for the silenced.[^ch23-archives-freedmens-bureau][^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 22 owns emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment; Chapter 25 owns sustained western conquest and allotment; Chapter 26 owns the Civil Rights Cases of 1883 and the making of Jim Crow. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch23-check-ch22][^ch23-check-ch25][^ch23-check-ch26]

## Summary

- Presidential Reconstruction under Andrew Johnson returned the former Confederate states to self-government without securing the freedpeople’s rights, and Mississippi’s late-1865 Black Code defined emancipation as a racialized labor regime.[^ch23-mshistorynow][^ch23-yawp-black-code]
- Congress overrode Johnson’s veto of the Civil Rights Act on April 9, 1866, and passed the Fourteenth Amendment on June 13, 1866; the amendment defined citizenship and equal protection and was ratified on July 9, 1868.[^ch23-house-civilrights1866][^ch23-archives-14th]
- The Reconstruction Acts of 1867 placed 10 former Confederate states under military authority and made Black male suffrage and the Fourteenth Amendment conditions of readmission.[^ch23-tsl-reconstruction-acts]
- Black delegates helped write new state constitutions, roughly 1,500 Black officeholders served in the southern states, Hiram Revels was seated in the Senate on February 25, 1870, and the Fifteenth Amendment was ratified on February 3, 1870.[^ch23-facinghistory-officeholders][^ch23-senate-revels][^ch23-archives-15th]
- The Freedmen’s Bureau legalized marriages and supervised labor contracts, and one Robeson County contract shows both the federal wage relation and the language of bondage that survived it.[^ch23-gilder-families][^ch23-dpla-labor-contract]
- Black schools and churches were built by freedpeople themselves; in Georgia at least 8,000 students attended freedmen’s schools within a year of freedom and nearly 20,000 within eight years.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-journal-civilwar-churches]
- Sharecropping replaced land reform for many freedpeople; by 1880, 32 percent of Georgia farms were sharecropped, and the crop lien kept many families in debt.[^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-sharecropping]
- The Klan and other armed groups used terror to overturn Reconstruction; Congress passed the Enforcement Acts, and Grant declared martial law in nine South Carolina counties on October 17, 1871, but the Supreme Court’s narrow reading of the Fourteenth Amendment, culminating in *Cruikshank*, undercut federal enforcement.[^ch23-eji-martial-law][^ch23-fjc-cruikshank]
- The Colfax Massacre of April 13, 1873, killed a contested number of Black men, and the subsequent prosecution failed in the Supreme Court.[^ch23-archives-colfax][^ch23-jcwe-colfax-count]
- The disputed election of 1876 was resolved by an Electoral Commission that awarded the presidency to Hayes, 185 to 184, and Hayes then withdrew the remaining federal troops; whether a formal “Compromise of 1877” existed is disputed.[^ch23-millercenter-1876]

> *A closing note.* Reconstruction gave the United States its first constitutional definition of a biracial citizenship and its first serious attempt at interracial democracy, and it withdrew the protection that made both possible. The amendments outlived the era; the land, the schools, and the votes did not, or not for long. What the freedpeople had built—families, churches, schools, and political organizations—proved more durable than the federal will to defend them.[^ch23-archives-14th][^ch23-georgiaencyclopedia-education][^ch23-millercenter-1876]
