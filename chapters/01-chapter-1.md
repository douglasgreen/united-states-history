## Epigraphs

> “a hidious & desolate wildernes, full of wild beasts …”
>
> — William Bradford, *Of Plymouth Plantation*, Book I, ch. 9, written 1630–51[^ch01-bradford]

> “The pristine image of 1492 seems to be a myth, then, an image more applicable to 1750, following Indian decline, although recovery had only been partial by that date.”
>
> — William M. Denevan, “The Pristine Myth: The Landscape of the Americas in 1492,” 1992, p. 381[^ch01-denevan]

> “For the Pueblos, then, the entire world is a special, sacred place.”
>
> — Rina Swentzell, “An Understated Sacredness,” 1985, p. 3[^ch01-swentzell]

- **Why these three.** Bradford supplies the colonial lens that turned an inhabited continent into “wilderness.” Denevan supplies the revision that makes the lens visible. Swentzell supplies a Pueblo voice that refuses both the empty wilderness and the merely “humanized” landscape: the land is sacred, not only managed. The epigraphs disagree about what kind of place the continent was, and that disagreement is the chapter’s problem.
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Bradford’s note flags the original spelling and the degrading phrase omitted from the quotation. The chapter does not modernize the spelling silently.
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that Bradford’s view was universal, that Denevan’s estimate is correct, or that Swentzell speaks for all Pueblo peoples. They stage the questions.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **Place.** Fajada Butte rises 135 meters above the floor of Chaco Canyon, in the San Juan Basin of present-day New Mexico. It is the site of the Sun Dagger: three stone slabs, two to three meters tall, leaning against a cliff face with two carved spiral petroglyphs. Visitors can see the butte from an overlook off the park road, but the site itself is closed.[^ch01-fajada-nps]
- **The spirals.** The larger spiral is 34 centimeters high and 41 centimeters wide. It makes nine and a half turns, moving outward and counterclockwise from its center. The smaller petroglyph is a coiled snake form, higher and to the left of the large spiral.[^ch01-fajada-hao]
- **The light.** At the summer solstice, shortly after 11:00 a.m. local solar time, a spot of sunlight appears above the large spiral. It lengthens into a narrow, downward-pointing dagger. By about 11:15, it bisects the spiral almost over its entire height. The overhang then cuts off the upper end, and the dagger moves downward until it slips off the cliff. The whole event lasts a little over 20 minutes. At the winter solstice, two shafts bracket the spiral. At the equinoxes, a long shaft illuminates the spiral off center, and a second, smaller shaft bisects the snake.[^ch01-fajada-hao]
- **The change.** The National Park Service restricted public access in 1982. In 1989, observers noticed that two slabs had shifted; the summer-solstice pattern was altered. The dagger is now thicker, and it no longer crosses the center of the spiral as it once did. The site is no longer open to the public.[^ch01-fajada-nps-archeoastronomy]
- **The people.** The butte lies within the Chacoan world. The great houses of Chaco Canyon were built from the mid-800s into the 1100s. They were oriented to solar, lunar, and cardinal directions, connected by roads, and surrounded by water-control devices and formal mounds. Pueblo peoples and the Diné (Navajo) are among the descendant communities associated with the park.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The absence.** No written account from the makers survives. The record does not name an observer, date the carvings, record a ceremony, or preserve a prayer. The slabs may have been shaped by people, but the High Altitude Observatory notes that the supposed pecking marks are effectively impossible to distinguish from natural weathering.[^ch01-fajada-hao] The scene must not supply what the record lacks.

### The scene’s argument

- **A place where time is made visible.** The light does not “tell time” in the modern clock sense. It marks the turning points of the year, the moments when planting, ceremony, and season are ordered. The knowledge is embodied in stone and sky, not in a written table.
- **The people are not a mystery.** The makers are not anonymous “mound builders” or “prehistoric Indians.” They are Ancestral Pueblo people whose descendants live in the Pueblo nations of the Southwest. The scene should name the continuity without pretending that a modern Pueblo person’s beliefs can be read back into the tenth century.
- **The record is a relationship, not a transcript.** The butte, the slabs, the spirals, and the light are evidence. The meaning of the event for the people who used it is inferred from archaeology, astronomy, and descendant communities. The scene should hold the evidence and the inference apart.
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: What can be known about the continent before European written accounts, and how did diverse societies shape their environments without leaving a single story?
- **The turn.** The light is precise. The history is not. The chapter moves from a phenomenon that can be observed today to a past that must be reconstructed.

### What must not be invented

- No imagined observer, priest, astronomer, child, or ceremony. The scene check records this limit and requires a final comparison with the documented light sequence before drafting.[^ch01-check-scene]
- No invented dialogue, thoughts, clothing, weather, or sensory detail beyond what the official descriptions report.
- No claim that the slabs were deliberately shaped. The evidence is ambiguous.
- No claim that the markings are a calendar that predicted eclipses or that the builders knew the exact length of the solar year.
- No claim that the site was abandoned in a single moment or that the Ancestral Pueblo “disappeared.” Their descendants are living peoples.
- No claim that the carvings date to a specific year. The Chacoan great houses have dates; the petroglyphs do not carry a secure date in the consulted record.
- No use of “Anasazi” in the book’s prose. The term is older and contested; use “Ancestral Pueblo” and explain the shift if a quotation requires it.

### Drafting target

Three hundred fifty to four hundred words. Open with a concrete detail: the butte, the slabs, the spiral, the first spot of light. Establish the place and the phenomenon in the first two sentences. Use present tense for the documented place and the recurring light, and past tense for the Chacoan construction. Let the light bisect the spiral. Then turn: the people who watched it are known through descendants and archaeology, not through a written record. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows immediately and supplies the years, scope, and limits.

## Framing paragraph and roadmap

The framing paragraph (150–200 words) should say: this chapter covers the territory that became the United States from the peopling of the Americas to about 1500 CE, but its narrative ends before the Norse landfall, which Chapter 2 owns. It is centered on the lands and peoples that became the fifty states, but it follows Beringian, Pacific, Arctic, and hemispheric connections. It uses archaeology, oral histories, museum and park records, and the published sciences of dating and ecology. It does not pretend that “First Americans” names a single people or a single arrival. It treats dates as estimates or minimums, distinguishes evidence from inference, and names the silences. Dates appear as calibrated radiocarbon ages in years before present (BP, with 1950 CE as the reference year), as years ago where the source uses that form, or as BCE/CE where the source permits.[^ch01-check-naming] The paragraph should end by distinguishing the chapter’s question from a search for a single origin.

## 1. First arrivals and competing routes

### 1.1 The evidence and the old story

- **The claim.** People were living in North America long before the Clovis culture, and the old story of a single migration through an ice-free corridor is no longer adequate. The evidence is a set of sites, dates, and models, not a single origin point.
- **White Sands.** Fossil human footprints at White Sands National Park in New Mexico were made between about 23,000 and 21,000 calibrated years ago. The National Park Service gives calibrated seed dates of 22,860 ±320 and 21,130 ±250 years ago. The prints are mostly those of teenagers and children, which the researchers interpret as evidence of a division of labor at a lakeshore.[^ch01-white-sands-nps]
- **The dating.** The ages rest on radiocarbon dating of *Ruppia cirrhosa* seeds above and below the footprints. The U.S. Geological Survey investigated the hard-water effect, which can make aquatic plants appear older than they are, and concluded that the ages were robust. The study excavated 61 footprints and placed them in multiple track horizons.[^ch01-white-sands-usgs]
- **Cooper’s Ferry.** At Cooper’s Ferry, also called Nipéhe, on the lower Salmon River in western Idaho, archaeologists found stone tools and hearth remains dated to about 16,500–16,300 years ago. The site is in Nez Perce country, and the excavators argue that it fits a Pacific coastal migration followed by an upriver move through the Columbia corridor.[^ch01-coopers-ferry-smithsonian]
- **Monte Verde.** Monte Verde, in southern Chile, was dated in the 1990s to about 14,500 years ago and became the leading evidence against Clovis-first. In 2026, a team led by Todd Surovell re-dated the site to between 4,200 and 8,200 years ago, arguing that the older wood had been redeposited by a creek and that volcanic ash below the occupation dates to about 11,000 years ago. Tom Dillehay, who has studied the site since the 1970s, rejected the re-dating as methodologically flawed.[^ch01-monte-verde-reuters]
- **The state of the question.** The Clovis-first model has been badly shaken, but not every early date is secure. White Sands and Cooper’s Ferry are strong evidence for a pre-Clovis presence. Monte Verde is now a live dispute. The chapter should present the pattern, not a single triumphant site.[^ch01-check-dates]

### 1.2 Routes and the peopling of a continent

- **The claim.** The peopling of the Americas was not one journey. It was a long process of movement, settlement, and adaptation, with several plausible routes and many pauses.
- **Beringia.** The Bering Land Bridge connected northeast Asia and Alaska during periods of lowered sea level. The National Park Service notes that North America has been inhabited for at least 16,500 years and that the land-bridge hypothesis has a long history. Beringia was not only a crossing; it was a region where people lived, hunted, and moved for thousands of years.[^ch01-beringia-nps]
- **The coastal route.** The “Kelp Highway” hypothesis holds that people moved south along the Pacific coast, using a chain of marine resources that stretches from the Kuril Islands to the Channel Islands. NOAA describes the coastal route as the widely accepted hypothesis and stresses that migration was thousands of small, punctuated moves, not a single walk from Siberia to Tierra del Fuego.[^ch01-kelp-highway-noaa]
- **The interior route.** An ice-free corridor through present-day Canada opened later than the earliest coastal sites. It may have carried later migrants, but it cannot explain the oldest dated sites south of the ice sheets. The chapter should distinguish “a route” from “the first route,” and it should source the corridor’s opening date before asserting it.[^ch01-check-dates]
- **Boats and coastlines.** A coastal route implies watercraft, or at least the ability to move along shorelines and across river mouths. Direct evidence of the earliest boats is scarce in most of the region, so the inference is strong but not direct.
- **The consequence.** “First Americans” is a misleading singular. The continent was peopled by many groups, over many generations, by more than one route. The chapter’s argument does not depend on finding the oldest site; it depends on taking the diversity of the evidence seriously.

### 1.3 What the evidence cannot settle

- **The claim.** The exact date of first arrival, the number of migrations, and the relative weight of coastal and interior routes remain open. The chapter should say so plainly.
- **The limits of dating.** Radiocarbon dates carry ranges and assumptions. Contamination, redeposition, and hard-water effects can shift results. A date is not a fact until the sample’s context and the laboratory’s method are understood.
- **The limits of the record.** Many early coastal sites are underwater. Many inland sites have been destroyed by erosion, development, or agriculture. The absence of a site is not proof of absence.
- **The political stakes.** Dates and routes are not only technical questions. They bear on Indigenous claims to presence and on national origin stories. The chapter should not use a date to settle a political question, and it should not ignore the politics.
- **Transition.** Once people were here, they did not stop moving or changing. §2 turns to the economies that made the continent’s later societies possible.

## 2. Archaic economies and independently domesticated eastern crops

### 2.1 Mounds before agriculture

- **The claim.** Monumental earthworks in eastern North America were built by hunter-fisher-gatherers before the arrival of maize agriculture. The old ladder of “hunting, then farming, then civilization” does not fit the evidence.
- **Watson Brake.** Watson Brake, in northeast Louisiana, is a complex of eleven earthen mounds connected by ridges, enclosing an area about 984 by 656 feet. It dates to about 3,500–2,800 BCE. The builders were fisher-hunter-gatherers who used the site in all seasons, perhaps not year-round. Mound construction continued for six or seven centuries and then stopped.[^ch01-watson-brake]
- **Poverty Point.** Poverty Point, also in Louisiana, was built between about 3,700 and 3,100 years BP, with 1950 as the reference year. It consists of five mounds, six concentric semi-elliptical ridges, and a central plaza. UNESCO calls it a remarkable achievement of earthen construction by hunter-fisher-gatherers, unsurpassed in North America for at least 2,000 years.[^ch01-poverty-point-unesco]
- **The speed of construction.** One analysis of Mound A at Poverty Point estimated that it was built in 90 days or fewer by at least 3,000 laborers. The estimate is a model, not a counted workforce, but it challenges the idea that foragers could not organize large projects.[^ch01-poverty-point-archaeology]
- **The interpretation.** A 2025 Washington University release describes Poverty Point as a temporary gathering place of egalitarian hunter-gatherers, not a permanent chiefdom. The site may have drawn thousands of people for trade, ceremony, and labor. The interpretation is debated, but it shows that complexity does not require agriculture or a king.[^ch01-poverty-point-washu]
- **The consequence.** Eastern North America had monumental architecture before it had domesticated crops. The chapter should not treat mounds as the product of farming, nor farming as the product of mounds. The two histories overlapped without forming a single staircase.

### 2.2 An independent center of domestication

- **The claim.** Eastern North America was one of the world’s independent centers of plant domestication. Its first crops were not maize; they were local plants domesticated over thousands of years.
- **The four plants.** Bruce D. Smith’s synthesis identifies four indigenous domesticates: marshelder (*Iva annua*), chenopod or goosefoot (*Chenopodium berlandieri*), squash (*Cucurbita pepo* ssp. *ovifera*), and sunflower (*Helianthus annuus*). The calibrated ages are about 5,025 BP for squash, 4,840 BP for sunflower, 4,400 BP for marshelder, and 3,700 BP for chenopod.[^ch01-smith-domestication]
- **The dates in prose.** In calibrated radiocarbon years BP, the four domesticates appear between about 5,000 and 3,700 BP, or roughly 3,000–1,750 BCE. The chapter should use BCE/CE where clarity permits.
- **Maize was late.** Maize arrived in the East at about 2,200 BP, roughly 2,500 years after the first local domesticates. The Eastern Agricultural Complex was not a failed precursor to maize; it was a working food system in its own right.[^ch01-smith-domestication]
- **The debate.** Some genetic and archaeological studies have argued that eastern domesticates were introduced from Mexico. Smith’s synthesis rejects that claim and defends the independence of all four. The chapter should present the evidence and note that the debate is technical and ongoing.[^ch01-smith-domestication]
- **The consequence.** Agriculture did not arrive from a single center. It was invented in several places, with different plants and different social consequences. That fact undermines both the diffusionist story and the idea of a single American origin.

### 2.3 The consequences of abundance

- **The claim.** The Archaic economies of eastern North America supported large gatherings, long-distance trade, and elaborate ritual without the centralized states that older models expected.
- **Storage and surplus.** Domesticated seeds and wild foods could be stored. Storage supported seasonal aggregation and made it possible to feed labor crews. But storage also created risk: a failed harvest or a raided cache could be catastrophic.[^ch01-poverty-point-washu]
- **Trade.** Poverty Point’s stone, copper, and shells came from distant regions. The site’s location in the Lower Mississippi Valley made it a crossroads. Trade was not a minor add-on; it was part of the economy that built the mounds.[^ch01-poverty-point-unesco]
- **Social difference.** The evidence does not show a single hereditary ruling class at Poverty Point. It shows a society capable of coordinating thousands of workers and feeding them. The chapter should not invent a king to explain the earthworks.[^ch01-poverty-point-washu]
- **Transition.** In the Southwest, people built towns in a dry landscape. Their history shows how water, architecture, and astronomy could create a regional center without a single agricultural formula.

## 3. Southwest towns, water, and calendars

### 3.1 Chaco as a regional center

- **The claim.** Chaco Canyon was a planned regional center, not a simple farming village. Its great houses, roads, and astronomical alignments show a society capable of large-scale coordination in a marginal environment.
- **The built landscape.** The Chacoan cultural flowering began in the mid-800s and lasted more than 300 years. Builders used masonry techniques to construct great houses of multiple stories with hundreds of rooms. The buildings were planned from the start, unlike the usual practice of adding rooms as needed. Construction on some buildings spanned decades or centuries.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The center.** By 1050, Chaco was the ceremonial, administrative, and economic center of the San Juan Basin. Dozens of great houses in the canyon were connected by roads to more than 150 great houses across the region. Lines of sight between great houses allowed communication. The buildings were oriented to solar, lunar, and cardinal directions.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The function.** The great houses may not have been traditional farming villages occupied by large populations. They may have been public architecture used periodically for ceremony, commerce, and trade. The park service presents this as an open question, not a settled fact.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The consequence.** Chaco was a center of coordination, not necessarily a city of residents. The distinction matters: it shows that power could be expressed through architecture, ritual, and roads without a dense urban population.

### 3.2 Water and agriculture

- **The claim.** Chacoan society depended on careful water management in a landscape with long winters, short growing seasons, and marginal rainfall. Water was a condition of survival, not a backdrop.
- **The environment.** The park service describes Chaco Canyon as an unlikely place for a major center: high desert, long winters, short growing seasons, and marginal rainfall. Yet the valley supported a thriving culture a thousand years ago.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **Water control.** The park service describes water-control devices around the great houses. The chapter should call this “water management,” not “irrigation,” unless a specific source documents canal irrigation. The distinction matters because the evidence supports management of episodic water, not necessarily a permanent canal system.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **Agriculture.** The Chacoans depended on agriculture in a marginal environment and supplemented it with wild plants and game. The chapter should not imply that Chaco was self-sufficient. The evidence points to a hinterland of farms, villages, and labor.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The limit.** The evidence for water management is architectural and sedimentary. It does not tell us who decided where the water went, how labor was organized, or how conflicts over water were resolved.

### 3.3 Calendars and the Sun Dagger

- **The claim.** The Fajada Butte markings show that Chacoan people tracked the sun’s seasonal movement with precision. They do not show a single “calendar” or a single system of meaning.
- **The mechanics.** Three slabs collimate sunlight onto two spiral petroglyphs. At the summer solstice, a dagger of light bisects the large spiral. At the winter solstice, two shafts bracket it. At the equinoxes, a shaft illuminates the spiral off center and a smaller shaft bisects the snake.[^ch01-fajada-hao]
- **The date problem.** The carvings are not directly dated. The park service says the Sun Dagger is “believed” to have marked the solstices and equinoxes. The observatory page notes that the supposed shaping of the slabs cannot be distinguished from natural weathering. The chapter should not assign the carvings to a specific century.[^ch01-fajada-nps-archeoastronomy]
- **The cultural context.** Chacoan architecture was oriented to solar, lunar, and cardinal directions. The Sun Dagger is one expression of a broader interest in celestial order, not an isolated wonder.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The limit.** The markings show that people attended to the sun. They do not tell us what the solstice meant to them, which ceremonies accompanied it, or whether the same person watched it every year. The chapter should name the silence.

### 3.4 Towns, hierarchy, and dispersal

- **The claim.** Chaco’s influence did not end in a single collapse. In the 1100s and 1200s, construction slowed, the regional center shifted, and people moved to new areas. The descendants of Chacoan society are the Pueblo peoples and other nations of the Southwest.
- **The shift.** Chaco’s role as a regional center declined, and its influence continued at Aztec, Mesa Verde, the Chuska Mountains, and other centers. People migrated, reorganized their world, and interacted with new peoples.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The descendants.** The park service lists Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, Laguna, Jemez, and many other Pueblo nations, along with the Diné (Navajo), as peoples traditionally associated with Chaco. Their presence is not a footnote; it is the living context of the archaeology.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- **The lesson.** “Collapse” is often the wrong word. Chaco did not vanish; it changed. The chapter should describe dispersal, reorganization, and continuity, not extinction.
- **Transition.** In the East, earthworks and Cahokia produced a different kind of center. §4 turns to the mound-building world and to the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico.

## 4. Eastern earthworks and Cahokia

### 4.1 Earlier earthworks and Map 1

- **The claim.** Eastern North America contained several distinct earthwork traditions, separated in time and space. They were not all built by the same people, for the same purpose, or in the same century.
- **The sequence.** Watson Brake and Poverty Point belong to the Archaic period. The Adena and Hopewell traditions followed. The Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks were built between about 2,000 and 1,600 years ago, roughly 1–400 CE, along the central tributaries of the Ohio River. Cahokia’s peak came later, between about 1050 and 1150 CE.[^ch01-hopewell-unesco]
- **Hopewell form.** The Hopewell earthworks include precise squares, circles, and octagons, with alignments to the cycles of the sun and the far more complex cycles of the moon. They were built by dispersed, non-hierarchical groups whose economy mixed foraging, fishing, farming, and cultivation. Artifacts from Yellowstone and Florida show a continent-wide sphere of interaction.[^ch01-hopewell-unesco][^ch01-hopewell-nps]
- **Newark.** The Newark Earthworks in Ohio include a Great Circle enclosing 30 acres and an Octagon with a 20-acre circle and a 50-acre octagon. The Ohio History Connection calls Newark the largest set of geometric earthen enclosures in the world.[^ch01-newark-ohc]
- **Map 1.** Map 1, “Cultural complexes around 1200 CE,” must distinguish earlier earthwork traditions and abandoned centers from communities active around 1200. Hopewell and Poverty Point were long finished. Chaco’s regional role had shifted. Cahokia was still occupied, though its peak had passed. The map must not imply that all earthworks were contemporary or that a single “mound builder” culture covered the continent. Map 1 is registered as `ch01-map-01`; no asset exists yet, and its caption, source line, and alt text remain production tasks.[^ch01-check-map]
- **The consequence.** The eastern earthworks are a sequence of experiments in landscape, ceremony, and labor. They are not a single tradition, and they are not the prelude to a single nation.

### 4.2 Cahokia and Monks Mound

- **The claim.** Cahokia was the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico and a planned center of political, ceremonial, and economic power. Monks Mound is its most concentrated material evidence of organized labor.
- **The scale.** Cahokia was occupied mainly between about 800 and 1350 CE. It covered nearly 1,600 hectares and included about 120 mounds. At its peak between 1050 and 1150, UNESCO estimates a population of 10,000–20,000; the National Park Service says “Greater Cahokia,” including East St. Louis and St. Louis, may have had 40,000–50,000. The ranges are estimates, not counts.[^ch01-cahokia-unesco][^ch01-cahokia-nps][^ch01-check-population]
- **The plan.** The state historic site preserves 51 platform, ridgetop, and conical mounds, along with residential, public, and specialized activity areas, a reconstructed palisade, and a circle of wooden posts aligned with the sun, often called Woodhenge. The central plaza and the mounds defined the settlement’s symmetry.[^ch01-cahokia-unesco]
- **Monks Mound.** Monks Mound is the largest indigenous earthen structure north of Mexico. It was constructed in fourteen stages, covers six hectares, rises in four terraces to a height of 30 meters, and dominates the site. It is not a single dump of earth; it is a sequence of planned additions.[^ch01-cahokia-nps]
- **The labor.** The mound’s size implies a labor force capable of moving enormous volumes of soil, sustaining itself, and coordinating across seasons. The evidence does not tell us whether the laborers were conscripted, enslaved, paid, or performing ritual obligations. The mound establishes capacity and coordination; it does not establish a constitution.[^ch01-cahokia-nps]

**In Focus.** Monks Mound is a close reading in soil. Its four terraces and fourteen stages are not decoration; they are a record of repeated decisions about height, access, and surface. The mound covers six hectares and rises 30 meters above the plain, which means that its builders moved and packed enough earth to create a landform visible from a great distance. The stages show that the work was not a single outburst of labor but a sequence of projects, each requiring planning, provisioning, and coordination. The mound rises above the central plaza, and the site’s other mounds, palisade, and Woodhenge belong to the same planned landscape. What the mound proves is organizational capacity: someone could assemble labor, schedule work, and sustain a project across time. What it does not prove is who held power, how labor was obtained, or what the builders believed. Those questions require written records that Cahokia did not leave, and the chapter should say so. The close reading should use the mound’s materiality to argue for organized labor without turning the mound into a portrait of a king.[^ch01-cahokia-unesco][^ch01-cahokia-nps]

### 4.3 Cahokia’s decline

- **The claim.** Cahokia’s decline had multiple causes, and no single factor explains it. Environmental stress, political fragmentation, population movement, and the costs of complexity all played a role.
- **The environmental evidence.** Researchers have documented flooding, drought, and deforestation around Cahokia. A flood deposit around 1200 CE and a warm-season drought occurred at roughly the same time. Deforestation in the bluff zone may have increased runoff. But the evidence does not show that environment alone caused the collapse.[^ch01-tainter-cahokia]
- **The social evidence.** Cahokia’s population declined, public construction continued, and the hierarchy became more differentiated. People left the American Bottom, and those who remained faced a higher labor burden. Tainter argues that the collapse is best understood as diminishing returns to complexity, combined with population movement and political fragmentation.[^ch01-tainter-cahokia]
- **The caution.** Environmental explanations are popular because they are dramatic. The chapter should present them as hypotheses, not verdicts. A society that weathered more than a century of repeated drought may have adapted to it; a flood does not explain a political order by itself.[^ch01-tainter-cahokia]
- **The consequence.** Cahokia did not “fall” in a day. It dispersed. Its people moved into the surrounding countryside and beyond, and the center lost its regional role. The chapter should describe that process without turning it into a morality tale about ecological sin.

### 4.4 What earthworks do and do not show

- **The claim.** Earthworks are evidence of labor, ceremony, astronomy, and exchange. They are not evidence of a single race, a single state, or a single belief system.
- **Serpent Mound.** Serpent Mound in Ohio is the largest documented surviving ancient effigy mound in the world. The National Park Service says it was “probably built several hundred years before or after the Hopewell-era sites,” and it is not part of the Hopewell World Heritage inscription.[^ch01-serpent-nps]
- **The dating dispute.** Edward Herrmann and colleagues argued in 2014 that Serpent Mound was initially constructed about 2,300 years ago, during the Adena period, and later modified by Fort Ancient people. Bradley Lepper and colleagues dispute that conclusion, arguing that radiocarbon dates on bulk sediment and soil humates can be several hundred to a thousand years too old. The mound’s date remains open.[^ch01-serpent-lepper]
- **What the mound shows.** Whatever its date, Serpent Mound shows a people willing to shape a ridge into a serpent effigy at a scale visible from the air. It shows a relationship between landscape, animal imagery, and ceremony. It does not show which culture built it, and it does not show what the serpent meant.[^ch01-serpent-nps]
- **The rule.** The chapter should say “earthworks” in the plural and name the tradition, the date range, and the region whenever possible. It should not use “the mound builders” as a collective noun.
- **Transition.** The eastern woodlands were not the whole continent. §5 turns to the coasts, the Great Basin, the Plains, and the Arctic, where different economies produced different kinds of history.

## 5. Coasts, basins, Plains, and Arctic

### 5.1 Pacific coasts and marine economies

- **The claim.** The Pacific Northwest supported permanent and semi-permanent communities based on marine resources, especially salmon, long before agriculture became dominant. Complexity did not wait for farming.
- **Namu.** At Namu, on the central coast of British Columbia, ancient DNA analysis of salmon remains shows a consistent emphasis on pink salmon over the past 7,000 years. The researchers argue that Namu was a permanent village with a salmon-storage economy by at least 5,000 BCE, several millennia before substantial population growth or social differentiation in the region.[^ch01-namu-cannon]
- **The exception.** The authors caution that Namu is exceptional for the length and detail of its record. It is not yet clear how widespread the pattern was. The chapter should present it as a strong case, not a template for the whole coast.
- **Ozette.** At Ozette, a Makah village on the Olympic Peninsula, a mudslide about 500 years ago buried six longhouses and their contents. The Makah oral history told of a “great slide,” and archaeology confirmed it. The eleven-year excavation recovered more than 55,000 artifacts, which the tribe kept on the reservation.[^ch01-ozette-makah]
- **The point.** Ozette is not “prehistory.” It is an ancestral village whose oral history and archaeology agree. The chapter should use it to show that Indigenous knowledge and excavation can corroborate each other without collapsing into one voice.
- **The consequence.** Marine resources could support dense, settled communities without maize. The old story that complexity required farming does not fit the Northwest Coast.

### 5.2 The Great Basin

- **The claim.** Great Basin peoples developed flexible, mobile economies that used wetlands, caves, and seasonal resources. Their history is not a story of deprivation or failed agriculture.
- **Lovelock Cave.** Lovelock Cave in Nevada was used for thousands of years for shelter and storage. It produced more than 10,000 artifacts, including baskets, textiles, and tools. The famous tule duck decoys are about 2,000 years old and are among the oldest known in the world.[^ch01-lovelock-blm]
- **Human remains.** Human remains in the cave are up to 4,000 years old. Some artifacts have been repatriated to Native tribes. The chapter should treat the cave as a burial and heritage site, not only an artifact cache.[^ch01-lovelock-blm]
- **The economy.** The Great Basin was not a barren desert. Wetlands, lakes, and springs supported fish, waterfowl, seeds, and game. Mobility was a strategy, not a failure. The duck decoys show sophisticated knowledge of bird behavior and craft.[^ch01-lovelock-blm]
- **The limit.** The cave’s dry conditions preserved objects that rarely survive elsewhere. The richness of Lovelock is an accident of preservation, not proof that the Great Basin was richer than other regions.

### 5.3 The Plains

- **The claim.** Plains peoples developed communal hunting systems that required coordination, knowledge of animal behavior, and large-scale labor. The bison jump is a technology of abundance, not a primitive leftover.
- **Head-Smashed-In.** Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump in southern Alberta was used from about 5,800 BP until about 1850 CE. Hunters used drive lanes marked by stone cairns to funnel bison toward a 10-meter cliff. The carcasses were butchered in camps below.[^ch01-head-smashed-in-unesco]
- **The knowledge.** The jump required knowledge of topography and bison behavior. It also required social organization: enough people to build and maintain the lanes, drive the herd, and process the meat. The Blackfoot-speaking First Nations are the traditional users and interpreters of the site.[^ch01-head-smashed-in-unesco]
- **The geography.** Head-Smashed-In is in Canada, outside the future United States. It belongs in the chapter because the Plains bison economy crossed the border and because the book’s fifty-state frame is not a natural cultural boundary. The chapter should say this explicitly.[^ch01-head-smashed-in-unesco]
- **The caution.** The chapter should consider failure and waste, not only efficiency. A communal hunt was a system of risk management, not a guarantee of abundance.

### 5.4 The Arctic

- **The claim.** The Arctic was not an empty edge. It was a series of maritime worlds, linked by boats, dogsleds, and whale hunting, with a history of migration and exchange.
- **The Thule.** About 1,000 years ago, Thule people in northern and western Alaska were successful whalers, artists, and traders. They expanded across the North American Arctic to Greenland in one of the longest and most rapid human migrations known.[^ch01-thule-nps]
- **The technology.** Thule hunters used toggling harpoons, drag floats, umiat, kayaks, and dogsleds. They hunted bowhead whales and other sea mammals. Their winter houses were semi-subterranean, built of stone, whalebone, and sod.[^ch01-thule-uaf]
- **The encounter.** The Thule replaced the Dorset culture in the Canadian Arctic and interacted with the Norse. The migration was not a single event; it was a process of expansion, adaptation, and conflict.[^ch01-thule-uaf]
- **The descendants.** The Thule are the ancestors of modern Inuit and Yupik peoples. “Thule” is an archaeological label, not a nation. The chapter should use the descendants’ names where known and explain the label.[^ch01-thule-uaf]
- **The consequence.** The Arctic was connected to the rest of the continent by trade, migration, and conflict. It was not a frozen isolation. The chapter should end §5 with the range of societies, not with a single “American” story.

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **People were here before Clovis.** White Sands and Cooper’s Ferry place people in North America well before the Clovis culture. The old Clovis-first model is no longer adequate.[^ch01-white-sands-usgs][^ch01-coopers-ferry-smithsonian]
- **Eastern North America domesticated plants independently.** Squash, sunflower, marshelder, and chenopod were domesticated locally, not imported from Mexico. Maize arrived later.[^ch01-smith-domestication]
- **Hunter-fisher-gatherers built monuments.** Watson Brake and Poverty Point are earthworks built by people who did not depend on maize agriculture. Complexity did not require farming.[^ch01-watson-brake][^ch01-poverty-point-unesco]
- **Indigenous peoples managed landscapes.** The evidence for fire, clearing, cultivation, and water control is extensive. The “pristine wilderness” is not a neutral description.[^ch01-denevan]
- **Earthworks are not one tradition.** They were built by different peoples, in different centuries, for different purposes. The chapter must name the tradition and the date.[^ch01-hopewell-unesco][^ch01-serpent-nps]

### 6.2 What is open

- **The first arrival.** The exact date, the number of migrations, and the relative weight of coastal and interior routes remain open. Monte Verde’s 2026 re-dating shows that even famous sites can be reopened.[^ch01-monte-verde-reuters]
- **The dating of White Sands.** The hard-water effect has been investigated, but the dates are still debated. The chapter should state a minimum age, not a single arrival.[^ch01-white-sands-usgs]
- **Population sizes.** Cahokia’s population is estimated at 10,000–20,000, or 40,000–50,000 for Greater Cahokia. Chaco’s population is not known. The ranges are wide because the evidence is fragmentary.[^ch01-cahokia-unesco][^ch01-cahokia-nps]
- **Serpent Mound.** The mound’s date is disputed between an Adena origin and a later Fort Ancient construction. The radiocarbon methods are themselves in dispute.[^ch01-serpent-lepper]
- **Cahokia’s decline.** Environmental stress, political fragmentation, and the costs of complexity all have evidence. No single cause has been proved.[^ch01-tainter-cahokia]
- **The degree of landscape management.** Denevan argues for a humanized landscape; Krech warns against ecological romanticism; Mann’s synthesis has been criticized by Denevan himself for overstatement. The debate is about scale, intent, and evidence, not about whether Indigenous peoples loved nature.[^ch01-denevan][^ch01-krech-review][^ch01-denevan-jsw]
- **The pairing to avoid.** The README explicitly drops the unsupported “Keegan versus Mann” pairing. This outline does not stage it. Keegan’s work is not the same argument, and it has not been consulted for this chapter. The real disagreement is among the humanized-landscape synthesis, the critique of ecological romanticism, and regional empirical caution.[^ch01-check-table]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The choice is not between a pristine wilderness and a universal garden. The evidence shows a continuum of human modification, varying by region, population, and time. The chapter’s position is that the continent was inhabited, managed, and unevenly transformed.
- **Denevan’s correction.** Denevan’s 1992 essay argued that the “pristine” image of 1492 was a myth, more applicable to 1750 after Indigenous decline. He did not argue that every acre was cultivated. He argued that the landscape was humanized almost everywhere people lived.[^ch01-denevan]
- **Denevan’s self-critique.** In a 2004 forum, Denevan criticized Mann’s Atlantic article for saying the hemisphere was “thoroughly dominated by humankind” and for calling it the “world’s largest garden.” Denevan insisted on “a continuum of degrees of human modification, from mostly invisible or ephemeral to a fully cultural landscape.” The correction is important: a strong thesis can become an overstatement.[^ch01-denevan-jsw]
- **Mann’s synthesis.** Mann’s *1491* brought the revision to a wide audience and challenged the sparse-population and primitive-agriculture stereotypes. It is a journalistic synthesis, not a primary authority; its reliance on interviews and its dramatic formulations are part of why Denevan criticized it.[^ch01-mann-review]
- **Krech’s correction.** Krech’s *The Ecological Indian* argues that Indigenous peoples were not uniformly conservationist. They sometimes overhunted, set fires that escaped control, and wasted animals. The book is a corrective to a romantic stereotype, not a denial that Indigenous peoples managed land.[^ch01-krech-review]
- **The double edge.** The “ecological Indian” stereotype and the “pristine wilderness” stereotype are related. Both deny Indigenous people a full, changing, sometimes destructive history. The chapter should reject both.
- **The lesson.** The best history states its position, gives the strongest version of the rival, and shows what the evidence can bear. The chapter should not replace one origin myth with another.

## Legacies

The deep past is not a prelude to the nation; it is the first chapter of a continental history in which the nation is one episode. The chapter’s evidence matters to later chapters because it establishes that land was already homeland, ecology, and jurisdiction, and in many places property, before the United States existed. It also establishes that “natural” landscapes are often post-collapse landscapes: the forests and grasslands that Europeans described were shaped by Indigenous management and then by Indigenous decline.[^ch01-denevan]

The “pristine” myth had consequences. If the continent was empty wilderness, settlement could be framed as improvement; if it was already managed, the same settlement looks like dispossession. Denevan’s argument that environmental protection does not need a wilderness foundation is a direct challenge to that framing.[^ch01-denevan-jsw] The chapter should state the connection without turning the deep past into a modern policy brief.

The earthworks and the butte are living heritage, not abandoned ruins. Pueblo peoples and the Diné are among the peoples traditionally associated with Chaco; the park service lists them as associated nations.[^ch01-chaco-nps] The Makah kept the Ozette collection on the reservation and built a museum to interpret it.[^ch01-ozette-makah] These are not decorative details. They show that the people of the deep past are still here, and that archaeology is one voice among several.

The chapter’s legacies are therefore double. The continent was humanized, and that history is a resource for thinking about land and belonging. The continent was also conquered, and that history is a warning against using deep time to escape the violence of the later record. The chapter ends with the butte, not with a verdict.

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** Beringia spans Russia and Alaska; Head-Smashed-In is in Canada; the Thule migration crosses Canada and Greenland. The book’s fifty-state frame is a political geography, not a cultural one. The chapter should say so once and then return to the center.
- **Archives and silences.** For the peoples of the future United States, the deep past has no written archive from the makers. The evidence is archaeological, oral, linguistic, and ecological. Some sites are closed to protect them. The chapter should name the silence without treating it as emptiness, and it should let a Pueblo voice such as Swentzell’s remind the reader that the land is sacred, not only data.[^ch01-fajada-nps-archeoastronomy][^ch01-swentzell]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 2 begins at L’Anse aux Meadows and takes up the Norse landfall, contact, and demographic collapse. Chapter 3 owns the political worlds of the contact era. Chapter 25 returns to conquest and the West; Chapter 49 returns to the recent reckoning; Chapter 50 weighs the book’s through-lines, including land and environment. This chapter’s job is to establish the depth and diversity that those later chapters presuppose.

## Summary

- People were living in North America by at least 23,000 years ago, and perhaps earlier; the old Clovis-first story is no longer adequate.[^ch01-white-sands-nps][^ch01-coopers-ferry-smithsonian]
- The peopling of the Americas was a long process with multiple routes, including a Pacific coastal route and a later interior corridor.[^ch01-beringia-nps][^ch01-kelp-highway-noaa]
- Eastern North America was an independent center of plant domestication; squash, sunflower, marshelder, and chenopod were domesticated locally, and maize arrived later.[^ch01-smith-domestication]
- Hunter-fisher-gatherers built monumental earthworks at Watson Brake and Poverty Point before maize agriculture.[^ch01-watson-brake][^ch01-poverty-point-unesco]
- Chaco Canyon was a planned regional center that coordinated great houses, roads, water, and astronomy; its decline was a dispersal, not an extinction.[^ch01-chaco-nps]
- Cahokia was the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico; Monks Mound is material evidence of organized labor, not a portrait of a king.[^ch01-cahokia-unesco][^ch01-cahokia-nps]
- Eastern earthworks belong to distinct traditions and dates; Serpent Mound’s date remains disputed.[^ch01-hopewell-unesco][^ch01-serpent-lepper]
- The Pacific coast, Great Basin, Plains, and Arctic each produced distinct societies and economies; complexity did not follow a single ladder.[^ch01-namu-cannon][^ch01-head-smashed-in-unesco][^ch01-thule-uaf]
- The debate over landscape management is a debate about evidence, scale, and intent, not about whether Indigenous peoples were “close to nature.”[^ch01-denevan][^ch01-krech-review]

## Further Reading

This is a selective guide, not a citation ledger. The groups move from primary voices and official descriptions to syntheses and specialized studies, then to the historiographical debate. Unless the annotation says otherwise, the works below were consulted for this outline and are cited in the combined notes. Krech and Mann are recommendations pending full reading; their entries say so.

### Primary voices

- **William Bradford, *Of Plymouth Plantation* (written 1630–51).** The colonial lens that turned an inhabited continent into “wilderness.” Read it as evidence of how newcomers saw, not as a description of an empty continent.
- **Rina Swentzell, “An Understated Sacredness” (1985).** A short Pueblo essay on sacredness, place, and Chaco. It is one Santa Clara Pueblo voice, not a collective statement.

### Origins and routes

- **National Park Service, “Fossilized Footprints,” White Sands National Park.** A clear, official summary of the footprints and the dating; read it with the USGS newsletter on the hard-water effect. It is a starting point, not the final scientific report.
- **National Park Service, “History of the Bering Land Bridge Theory.”** Useful for the history of the idea and for the distinction between Beringia as a region and as a crossing. It does not settle the timing of migration.
- **Jason Daley, “Idaho Site Shows Humans Were in North America 16,000 Years Ago,” *Smithsonian Magazine* (2019).** A readable account of Cooper’s Ferry that includes a skeptic. It is valuable for showing how a site becomes a controversy.

### Agriculture, earthworks, and towns

- **Bruce D. Smith, “Eastern North America as an Independent Center of Plant Domestication,” *PNAS* 103 (2006).** The clearest short synthesis of the four eastern domesticates and their dates. Technical but accessible to a careful reader.
- **UNESCO, “Monumental Earthworks of Poverty Point” and “Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site.”** Authoritative summaries of scale, date, and significance. They are institutional descriptions, not excavation reports.
- **National Park Service, “History & Culture,” Chaco Culture National Historical Park.** The best short introduction to Chaco’s great houses, roads, astronomy, and descendant communities. It presents the function of the great houses as an open question.
- **James A. Tainter, “Cahokia: Urbanization, Metabolism, and Collapse,” *Frontiers in Sustainable Cities* 1 (2019).** An open-access argument for complexity and diminishing returns. It is one interpretation among several, not a consensus.

### Coasts, Plains, and Arctic

- **Aubrey Cannon and Dongya Y. Yang, “Early Storage and Sedentism on the Pacific Northwest Coast,” *American Antiquity* 71 (2006).** A technical but important case for salmon storage and permanent settlement at Namu. Read the authors’ caution that Namu may be exceptional.
- **UNESCO, “Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump.”** A concise account of the drive lanes, cliff, and butchering camps, with Blackfoot interpretation. It explains the technique better than it explains the people.
- **University of Alaska Museum of the North, “Thule Culture.”** A clear chronology of the Thule migration and its technology. It uses “Thule” as an archaeological label, which the chapter should explain.

### The debate

- **William M. Denevan, “The Pristine Myth: The Landscape of the Americas in 1492,” *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* 82 (1992).** The foundational revision. Read it with Denevan’s later self-critique in the *Journal of the Southwest* forum.
- **W. George Lovell et al., “1491: In Search of Native America,” *Journal of the Southwest* 46 (2004).** A forum in which Denevan, Dobyns, Woods, and Mann argue about population, landscape, and overstatement. Excellent for teaching the difference between a thesis and a slogan.
- **Shepard Krech III, *The Ecological Indian: Myth and History* (1999).** The strongest critique of ecological romanticism; read it with Adrian Tanner’s critical review and with Native critics. It was not consulted in full for this outline, so its page locators must be supplied before publication.[^ch01-check-sources]
- **Charles C. Mann, *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus* (2005).** A widely read synthesis that brought the revision to general readers. It is journalism, not a primary authority; use it to orient, not to settle.

> *A closing note.* The butte still stands above the canyon. The slabs have shifted, and the dagger no longer crosses the center of the spiral as it once did. The site is closed to protect it.[^ch01-fajada-nps-archeoastronomy] The light still comes, but the pattern is not the one recorded when the site was first studied. That is the chapter’s condition: the past is not a fixed image. It is a relationship between a place, a record, and the people who keep asking what the record means. The Ancestral Pueblo did not vanish into a mystery. Their descendants live in the Pueblo nations, and the Diné and other peoples also hold Chaco in their histories.[^ch01-chaco-nps]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at L’Anse aux Meadows, on the northern tip of Newfoundland, where Norse builders raised turf walls and iron-working debris about a thousand years ago. Their buildings are among the earliest known European buildings in the Americas, but they are not the beginning of American history. They are a brief landing on a continent that was already old.[^ch01-check-ch2]
