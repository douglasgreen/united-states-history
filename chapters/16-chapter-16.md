## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and time.** In 1820 Thomas Jefferson, seventy-six years old and three years into retirement, lived at Monticello in Albemarle County, Virginia. He had withdrawn from public affairs and had, by his own account, “long ceased to read the newspapers.”[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- **The correspondent.** John Holmes was a congressman from Maine who had voted for the compromise and who sent Jefferson a copy of a letter to his constituents defending it. Jefferson thanked him for the copy and answered with his reading of the crisis.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- **The alarm.** Jefferson wrote that the Missouri question had come “like a fire bell in the night” and had “awakened and filled me with terror.” He called it “the knell of the Union,” hushed only for the moment.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- **The line.** He predicted that a “geographical line, coinciding with a marked principle, moral and political, once conceived and held up to the angry passions of men, will never be obliterated; and every new irritation will mark it deeper and deeper.” The line was real: Congress drew it at 36°30′ in the act approved March 6, 1820.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes][^ch16-missouri-act]
- **The dilemma.** Jefferson called slavery a “heavy reproach” and wrote that “we have the wolf by the ear, and we can neither hold him, nor safely let him go. Justice is in one scale, and self-preservation in the other.” He argued that diffusion would make enslaved people “individually happier” and facilitate eventual emancipation, and he insisted that the condition of persons within a state was that state’s exclusive right.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]

### The scene’s argument

- **A founder reading his fears.** The scene should open on Jefferson at his writing table in the spring of 1820, a man who had helped build the republic now watching its sections divide over slavery’s extension. The point is not that Jefferson was antislavery; he was a slaveholder who wrote of the “wolf by the ear.” The point is that the crisis made a leading founder describe the Union’s future in the language of terror.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- **The claim and the contradiction.** Jefferson’s letter holds the chapter’s central contradiction in one hand: a moral admission that slavery was a “reproach” and a practical argument for diffusion and state rights. The chapter should let that contradiction stand rather than resolve it, because it is the contradiction of the age itself.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes][^ch16-check-race]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: who gained a political voice as mass democracy expanded?
- **The turn.** The answer is not a story of simple widening. The same decades that brought more White men to the polls drew a sharper line around the enslaved, the dispossessed, and the disfranchised. The Missouri line was the first modern proof that the republic’s expansion and its exclusions advanced together.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- **The widening.** The scene stands in 1820. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader through the elections, the party battles, the revivals, and the penny press that together made the age Jackson’s.[^ch16-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, room, or dialogue.** The letter, its date, the correspondent, and its words are documented. Monticello’s weather, the room, the servants present, and any spoken exchange are not attested in the cited sources; do not stage them.[^ch16-check-scene]
- **No claim that Jefferson freed anyone or acted on his fears.** Jefferson remained a slaveholder and took no public role in the crisis. The scene must not convert his private alarm into public leadership.[^ch16-check-race]
- **No modern phrasing in his mouth.** Quotations come from the consulted text character by character; do not modernize or paraphrase inside quotation marks.[^ch16-check-epigraphs]
- **No claim that the crisis settled the question.** The compromise drew a line; it did not resolve the principle. Jefferson said so himself, and John Quincy Adams called the crisis only the “title page to a great tragic volume.”[^ch16-jefferson-holmes][^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

## 1. Missouri and the Era of Good Feelings

### 1.1 The Era of Good Feelings and the appearance of consensus

- **The claim.** The years after the War of 1812 produced a one-party nationalism that looked like consensus, and the appearance was deceptive.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]
- **The mood.** With the Federalist Party collapsed after the Hartford Convention and the peace of Ghent, James Monroe won the presidency in 1816 and again in 1820 with little organized opposition. A Boston newspaper gave the period its name, the “Era of Good Feelings.” National policy turned toward a tariff, a national bank, and internal improvements—the program Henry Clay later called the American System.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- **The pressure beneath.** The Panic of 1819, the young republic’s first nationwide depression, broke the mood. Unemployment rose, banks failed, mortgages were foreclosed, and agricultural prices fell by half; debtors demanded stay laws and the abolition of debtors’ prisons, while others attacked banks and “privileged” corporations. The panic’s politics fed the demand for wider suffrage and the hostility to privilege that the 1820s would carry into national elections.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri][^ch16-check-ch15]
- **The consequence.** The Era of Good Feelings was less a settled consensus than a pause between party systems. The Missouri crisis and the panic together ended it.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

### 1.2 The Tallmadge amendments and the sectional debate

- **The claim.** Missouri’s application for statehood forced Congress to decide, for the first time since 1787, whether it could regulate slavery’s spread, and the answer divided the sections.[^ch16-house-tallmadge]
- **The amendments.** On February 13, 1819, Representative James Tallmadge Jr., a first-term New York Republican, offered two amendments to the Missouri statehood bill: one prohibited “the further introduction of slavery or involuntary servitude” into Missouri, and the other freed every child born there at the age of twenty-five.[^ch16-yawp-missouri][^ch16-house-tallmadge]
- **The text.** The first amendment read: “And provided, that the further introduction of slavery or involuntary servitude be prohibited, except for the punishment of crimes, whereof the party shall have been fully convicted; and that all children born within the said State, after the admission thereof into the Union, shall be free at the age of twenty-five years.”[^ch16-yawp-missouri]
- **The vote.** The House approved both amendments, voting “along ominously sectional lines,” but the Senate defeated them, and the 15th Congress ended without resolving Missouri’s statehood.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri][^ch16-house-tallmadge]
- **The argument.** Tallmadge rested his case on Article IV, Section 3 of the Constitution, which gave Congress power over the territories, and on the precedent that Congress had excluded slavery from the Northwest Territory in 1787. He said he was representing his constituents’ “hatred to slavery in every shape,” and answered southern threats of disunion: “If a dissolution of the Union must take place, let it be so! If civil war, which gentlemen so much threaten, must come, I can only say, let it come!” He also conceded that his amendment did nothing about slavery where it already existed.[^ch16-house-tallmadge]
- **The consequence.** The debate exposed the sectional fault line that would define the next forty years of legislative conflict, even though the immediate bill failed.[^ch16-house-tallmadge]

### 1.3 The Compromise of 1820 and the line at 36°30′

- **The claim.** Congress resolved the immediate crisis by pairing Missouri’s admission as a slave state with Maine’s as a free state and by drawing a line across the Louisiana Purchase beyond which slavery would be prohibited.[^ch16-missouri-act]
- **The pairing.** Congress admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a non-slave state at the same time, so that the balance between slave and free states would remain equal.[^ch16-missouri-act]
- **The line.** Section 8 of the act declared that in the territory ceded by France as Louisiana lying “north of thirty-six degrees and thirty minutes north latitude,” not included in Missouri, “slavery and involuntary servitude … shall be, and is hereby, forever prohibited,” with a fugitive-recapture proviso. The act was approved March 6, 1820.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-yawp-missouri]
- **The sequel.** Missouri then adopted a constitution barring free Black settlement, and Congress admitted the state only after a second compromise requiring Missouri not to deprive any citizen of the privileges and immunities guaranteed by the Constitution; Missouri entered the Union in 1821. The line held until the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 repealed it, and the Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional in *Dred Scott v. Sandford* (1857).[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-check-dates]
- **The consequence.** The compromise preserved the sectional balance and postponed the conflict, but it also established the principle that Congress could draw a boundary around slavery. The South accepted a limit it had denied Congress the power to impose.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

### 1.4 What the crisis exposed

- **The claim.** The Missouri crisis ended the Era of Good Feelings on a note of foreboding and revealed that expansion would keep returning the slavery question to national politics.[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]
- **The founder’s fear.** Jefferson’s “fire bell in the night” and his prediction that the geographical line would “never be obliterated” captured what many leaders feared: that the Union contained a division that no compromise could close.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- **The price southerners paid.** The historian account reproduced by Digital History argues that southerners won the immediate fight but paid a high price: if the South was to defend its political power against an antislavery majority, it would have to forge new alliances with the North and West or acquire new territory in the Southwest, “inevitably reigniting northern opposition to the further expansion of slavery.”[^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]
- **The consequence.** The crisis did not create the sectional conflict; it made it visible and gave it a line on the map. The chapter should treat the compromise as a settlement that changed the terms of conflict without settling them—the book’s central claim about American settlements.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-digitalhistory-missouri]

## 2. Elections and parties

### 2.1 The widening White male electorate and its contractions

- **The claim.** More White men could vote in the 1820s and 1830s than ever before, but the expansion began before Jackson, advanced for reasons of party strategy as much as democratic conviction, and was accompanied by new exclusions.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- **How early it began.** Stuart M. Blumin’s survey argues that the most “significant progress toward a more inclusive white male suffrage occurred in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, well before the Jacksonian presidency.” New states from Vermont (1791) to Missouri entered mostly without economic qualifications, and historians’ estimates suggest that as many as 80 percent of adult White males were eligible to vote nationally by 1800. The Jacksonian era saw changes in party organization and campaign style more than a legal expansion of the franchise.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- **The stragglers and the strategy.** Maryland dropped its property qualification in 1802; New Jersey, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and New York switched to a taxpaying qualification by 1821; Rhode Island and Virginia were last to eliminate property qualifications, in 1842 and 1850. Blumin argues the expansion was usually driven not by popular protest but by party strategists who expected a broader electorate to help them win, or addressed as a secondary matter when delegates met on other business.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- **The contractions.** As economic qualifications fell, other limits stiffened. New Jersey women lost the vote in 1807. New York’s 1821 constitutional convention expanded eligibility without addressing race, and a new state constitution restricted the franchise to “white freemen” only in 1838, taking the vote from free Black men who had held it. Pennsylvania ratified a constitution limiting the franchise to “white freemen” in 1838; New York’s amendment eliminating Black voting came in 1826. Free Black men lost legal access to the ballot in nearly every state that widened White male suffrage.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-merritt-voting]
- **The consequence.** The widening electorate was real and consequential, and it was bounded by race and gender at the very moment it expanded. The chapter must hold the expansion and the contraction together.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-check-race]

### 2.2 The election of 1824 and the “corrupt bargain”

- **The claim.** The election of 1824 was a political turning point in which the old rules failed: no candidate won an electoral majority, and the House of Representatives chose a president whom the plurality of voters had not chosen.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- **The new electorate.** For the first time the popular vote mattered broadly: eighteen states chose electors by popular vote while six still left the choice to their legislatures, and a swath of newly enfranchised regional voters replaced congressional caucuses with state conventions and assemblies as the nominating machinery. Jackson’s support, based on his military fame and his outsider standing, drew the largest rallies and the only support beyond a regional base.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- **The tally.** Four Democratic-Republicans ran. Andrew Jackson polled about 151,271 popular votes (41.3 percent) and 99 electoral votes; John Quincy Adams about 113,122 (30.9 percent) and 84; William H. Crawford about 40,856 and 41; Henry Clay about 47,531 and 37. Jackson led both counts but fell short of an electoral majority.[^ch16-ucsb-1824][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- **The House vote.** Under the Twelfth Amendment the House chose among the top three, eliminating Clay. As Speaker, Clay was the pivotal figure; in February 1825 Adams won with a one-vote margin as most of Clay’s supporters and several old Federalists swung to him, and Adams then named Clay secretary of state.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- **The charge.** Jackson called the result a “corrupt bargain” and said Clay had sold his influence. The available evidence suggests behind-the-scenes maneuvering by Clay but also Clay’s genuine doubts about Jackson’s qualifications and his preference for Adams’s support of the American System; the defection of three states Jackson had won in the popular vote (Illinois, Maryland, and Louisiana) indicates more was involved than one man’s maneuvering. The chapter should present the charge as Jackson’s claim and the record as ambiguous.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- **The consequence.** Jackson resigned his Senate seat and rebuilt a grassroots campaign to defeat Adams in 1828, turning the “corrupt bargain” into the founding grievance of the new Democratic Party.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]

### 2.3 Party organization, the second party system, and Figure 3

- **The claim.** Between 1824 and 1840 the mass party became the central institution of American politics, and the results are visible in votes and turnout.[^ch16-feller-franchise]
- **The machinery.** Under Jackson and Van Buren the Democrats perfected a pyramidal structure of local, state, and national committees, caucuses, and conventions, ensuring coordinated action that “supposedly reflected opinion at the grass roots, though their movements in fact were often directed from Washington.” The “spoils system” of patronage gave party ranks both activity and discipline.[^ch16-feller-franchise]
- **The competition.** Once the popular Jackson left the scene, the Democrats and the newly formed Whigs were “very nearly equal in their bases of popular support”; presidential elections through the 1840s were among the closest in history, and control of Congress passed back and forth. Close competition and near-universal White male suffrage turned campaigns into “a combination of spectator sport and participatory street theater,” with barbeques, rallies, and stump speeches.[^ch16-feller-franchise]
- **The numbers.** Voter participation rose from about 26.9 percent of the voting-age population in 1824 to about 57.6 percent in 1828, 55.4 percent in 1832, 57.8 percent in 1836, and 80.2 percent in 1840, the highest of the century to that point. These percentages are often attributed to Walter Dean Burnham’s series and depend on how the voting-age population is defined; the denominator excludes women and, in most states, free Black men, and it must be stated.[^ch16-infoplease-turnout][^ch16-ucsb-turnout][^ch16-check-numbers]
- **Figure 3.** Figure 3 (`ch16-figure-01`) plots the electorate from 1824 to 1840 and carries three caveats: the denominator and its exclusions; the fact that South Carolina still chose electors by its legislature and that other states used mixed methods; and that eligibility varied by state even among White men. The chapter should not read the 1840 spike as proof that democracy itself had suddenly broadened; the franchise had mostly widened earlier, and the jump reflects mobilization and counting as much as admission. Underlying returns and definitions belong in App. B, Table 2.[^ch16-check-figure][^ch16-infoplease-turnout][^ch16-ucsb-turnout]

### 2.4 Campaigns as spectacle: 1828, 1832, 1836, and 1840

- **The claim.** By 1840 the parties campaigned as never before, selling candidates as folk heroes and reaching ordinary voters through cheap print and mass rallies.[^ch16-feller-franchise]
- **Jackson’s campaigns.** The 1828 rematch, fought against Adams as “the people” against an elitist, drew more than twice the voters of 1824, and Jackson won decisively. Personal attacks on Jackson’s wife, Rachel, cast as an “American Jezebel,” only enhanced his image as an authentic outsider. The 1832 contest, fought over the Bank veto, Jackson won over Clay.[^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain][^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The 1840 log cabin.** The Whigs answered “Old Hickory” with “Old Tippecanoe,” William Henry Harrison, and ran a “log cabin and hard cider” campaign that painted the incumbent Martin Van Buren as a wealthy sybarite while presenting the well-born Harrison as a man of the people. Charles Ogle’s three-day “Gold Spoon” speech attacked Van Buren’s supposed luxury; both sides flooded the country with cheap memorabilia, and Harrison, breaking the custom that candidates should not campaign, appeared at enormous rallies. Harrison carried nineteen of twenty-six states and defeated Van Buren 234 to 60 in the electoral college.[^ch16-nps-1840]
- **The irony.** The campaign’s imagery inverted the candidates’ origins: the “log cabin” candidate was born to a prominent family and educated at Hampden-Sydney and the University of Pennsylvania, while Van Buren had been raised at his father’s tavern and farm. The chapter should use the inversion to show that the new politics manufactured images as well as counted votes.[^ch16-nps-1840]
- **The consequence.** The second party system gave ordinary White men a real stake in national politics and turned that stake into a business of organization, image, and turnout. Whether it gave them power or only the feeling of power is the chapter’s central question.[^ch16-feller-franchise]

## 3. Jackson’s governing conflicts

### 3.1 Rotation in office and the spoils system

- **The claim.** Jackson entered office promising reform of a corrupt bureaucracy and created instead the system of partisan patronage that his opponents named the “spoils system.”[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The removals.** Charging the Adams bureaucracy with fraud, Jackson initiated “sweeping removals among high-ranking government officials—Washington bureau chiefs, land and customs officers, and federal marshals and attorneys,” claiming to restore opportunity through “rotation in office.” In practice, many offices were doled out as rewards for political service, and his appointees included newspaper editors who had championed him. His most notorious appointee, Samuel Swartwout, collector of the New York customhouse, absconded in 1838 with more than $1 million.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The phrase.** Senator William L. Marcy of New York defended the removals in 1832 by saying that in politics as in war “to the victor belong the spoils of the enemy.” Jackson denied political criteria motivated him, but he treated an officeholder’s support for Adams as evidence of unfitness and relied on his own partisans for replacements.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The consequence.** The spoils system gave parties discipline but tied office to faction and invited corruption. It became one of the lasting grievances the Whigs mobilized against Jacksonian rule.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-feller-franchise]

### 3.2 The Eaton affair and the “kitchen cabinet”

- **The claim.** A Washington social scandal over the wife of the secretary of war entangled Jackson’s cabinet, estranged him from Vice President John C. Calhoun, and elevated Martin Van Buren.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The scandal.** Jackson’s secretary of war, John Henry Eaton, married Margaret “Peggy” O’Neale Timberlake just before the inauguration, amid rumors that her first husband had died under scandalous circumstances. Cabinet wives, including Calhoun’s wife Floride, shunned her. Jackson saw in the snubbing the persecution he believed had hounded his own Rachel, and he came to see a plot by Calhoun to control his administration.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The rupture.** The one cabinet officer apart from the snubbing was the widower Martin Van Buren, whose courtliness to Peggy Eaton and whose policy views drew Jackson closer. By 1831 the affair threatened to paralyze the administration; Eaton and Van Buren resigned to give Jackson an occasion to demand the other secretaries’ resignations and appoint a new cabinet. Calhoun cast the tie-breaking vote against Van Buren’s nomination as minister to Great Britain, and Van Buren returned as a political martyr and Jackson’s choice for vice president.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-tocqueville-hanover]
- **The consequence.** The “kitchen cabinet” of informal advisers and the Eaton affair together showed how personal and factional the new politics had become. They also widened the breach between Jackson and Calhoun that the tariff crisis would turn into a constitutional struggle.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]

### 3.3 Nullification and the Union

- **The claim.** South Carolina’s attempt to nullify the federal tariff forced Jackson to define the Union as indissoluble and the states as bound by federal law.[^ch16-loc-nullification]
- **The ordinance.** In November 1832 a South Carolina convention declared the tariff acts of 1828 and 1832 “unauthorized by the constitution of the United States, and … null, void, and no law, nor binding upon this State,” and the legislature moved to block the collection of federal customs at the state’s ports.[^ch16-loc-nullification]
- **The proclamation.** On December 10, 1832, Jackson issued a Proclamation to the People of South Carolina disputing a state’s right to nullify federal law. Drafted largely by Secretary of State Edward Livingston, the proclamation questioned the Carolinians’ obsession with the tariff, dismissed the constitutional theory behind nullification, and warned that “disunion by armed force is treason.” At a political dinner in 1830 he had already signaled his position, staring at Calhoun as he toasted, “Our federal Union: It must be preserved.”[^ch16-loc-nullification][^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The Force Bill and the compromise.** Congress passed the Force Act in 1833 authorizing military force against a resisting state, and at the same time Henry Clay and Calhoun broker a Compromise Tariff that lowered rates over nine years. South Carolina, claiming victory, rescinded its nullification but nullified the Force Bill in a final gesture of defiance.[^ch16-loc-nullification][^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The consequence.** The crisis ended tariff agitation until the 1840s, but it also joined the defense of slavery to the doctrine of state sovereignty. Nullification’s logic would return in the secession crisis that Chapter 20 owns.[^ch16-loc-nullification][^ch16-check-race]

### 3.4 The Bank War and the veto of 1832

- **The claim.** Jackson’s war on the Second Bank of the United States destroyed the country’s central financial institution and made the Bank the defining issue of his second term.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The institution.** The Second Bank, chartered in 1816, was a quasi-public corporation that managed the federal government’s finances and provided a national currency; the government owned one-fifth of the stock and appointed one-fifth of the directors. It had fallen into disrepute in the Panic of 1819 and been repaired by its president, Nicholas Biddle, but Jackson attacked it in his first message to Congress in 1829 as unconstitutional and a threat to popular liberty.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The veto.** On the advice of Clay and Daniel Webster, Biddle sought recharter in 1832, calculating Jackson would not dare veto before the election. Congress passed the bill, and on July 10, 1832, Jackson vetoed it. His message, intended for the public, attacked the “special privilege enjoyed by private stockholders in a government-chartered corporation” and declared that the “rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes.” The chapter should quote the message and note that Jackson’s market position was contested: he also opposed charters for manufacturing and transportation companies.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The removal of the deposits.** After his reelection, Jackson determined to withdraw the government’s deposits. When Treasury Secretary William John Duane refused, Jackson fired him and put Roger Taney in his place; the removal was largely complete by December 1833. Biddle retaliated by contracting the Bank’s lending to force a recharter, but the contraction discredited the Bank and vindicated Jackson’s charges.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The censure and the consequence.** The Senate, in Whig hands, rejected Taney and adopted a resolution of censure against Jackson in March 1834; Jackson’s defenders expunged it from the journal in 1837. The state banks, cut loose and gorged with federal funds, fed the speculative boom that collapsed in the Panic of 1837, the economic sequel Chapter 15 owns.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-check-ch15]

### 3.5 Removal’s national political role

- **The claim.** Indian removal was not the work of one man but a national policy with broad consensus, and it became the only major legislation passed at Jackson’s behest in eight years.[^ch16-ostler-removal]
- **The act.** Congress passed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830, authorizing the president to grant lands west of the Mississippi in exchange for Indigenous homelands and to pay removal costs. The vote was close in the House, 102 to 97, and comfortable in the Senate, 28 to 19. Jackson, who regarded tribes within the states “not as independent sovereign entities but as wards of the government and tenants-at-will,” considered removal the “happy consummation” of a policy “pursued for nearly 30 years,” reaching back to Jefferson’s proposal to settle eastern nations in Louisiana.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-ostler-removal]
- **The Cherokee cases.** The Cherokee Nation sued Georgia over its assertion of jurisdiction. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) the Supreme Court called the Cherokee a “domestic dependent nation” without standing to challenge Georgia’s laws; in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) it held Georgia’s laws undermining Cherokee sovereignty unconstitutional. The victories gave the Cherokee no practical means of resistance, and Georgia, tacitly encouraged by Jackson, ignored the rulings.[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The scope.** Removal affected dozens of nations across the East and Midwest, not only the five southeastern nations; about 88,000 people indigenous to the eastern United States were forcibly moved west in the 1830s and 1840s, and between 12,000 and 17,000 died in the roundups, detention, and westward journeys, a death toll of 14 to 19 percent. The chapter should name that scale, which belongs to this chapter’s political account, while the routes, the treaties, and the consequences belong to Chapter 17.[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-check-ch17]
- **The boundary with Chapter 17.** This chapter states the national political role: Jackson’s priority, the close 1830 vote, the Cherokee cases, and the consensus behind removal. It stops before the Treaty of New Echota of December 1835 and the removal of 1838–39, which Chapter 17 owns and to which this chapter bridges.[^ch16-check-ch17][^ch16-ostler-removal]

## 4. Revival and reform

### 4.1 The Second Great Awakening and the burned-over district

- **The claim.** A wave of revivals in the 1820s and 1830s reshaped American religion, drew women and families into public religious work, and supplied the moral energy for reform.[^ch16-finney-rochester]
- **The revival.** Charles Grandison Finney, a former lawyer turned evangelist, held a powerful revival in Rochester, New York, from September 1830 to March 1831, using a preaching style he had honed for years. Daily preaching and revival services produced public conversions, often of leading community members, and women and families spread the revival through home visits and prayer groups; Protestants traveled up to a hundred miles to hear him. Converts sat on the “anxious bench” near his pulpit to be made the focus of public conversion.[^ch16-finney-rochester]
- **The context.** Rochester was a rapidly growing commercial and manufacturing town where the old mutuality of masters and apprentices had given way to sharper class stratification; it stood at the center of the “Burned-Over District,” the belt of intense revivals across upstate New York. According to the historian Paul Johnson, the revival attracted primarily the emerging middle class, who then evangelized the working class.[^ch16-finney-rochester]
- **The turn to reform.** Finney’s emphasis on perfectionism and free moral choice fed social reform, especially temperance; halfway through the revival he invited the temperance activist Theodore Dwight Weld to organize temperance work in Rochester. The revivals thus linked personal conversion to public reform, the engine of the era’s benevolent societies.[^ch16-finney-rochester]
- **The consequence.** The Second Great Awakening made religion a public and organized force in the North, and it gave women a legitimate, if contested, role in moral work that would widen into antislavery and women’s-rights activism.[^ch16-finney-rochester][^ch16-check-reform]

### 4.2 Temperance, common schools, and asylums

- **The claim.** The revivals produced a cluster of reforms aimed at perfecting the individual and the society: temperance, public schooling, and humane treatment of the mentally ill.[^ch16-lumen-temperance]
- **Temperance.** In 1825 the Presbyterian minister Lyman Beecher preached six sermons on intemperance, published the next year; the American Temperance Society was formed in 1826, and by the early 1830s thousands of similar societies had sprung up. Members first pledged to shun hard liquor; by 1836 leaders including Beecher advocated total abstinence, or teetotalism, which split the movement and slowed it. A new working-class phase began with the Washington Temperance Society in 1840, which dramatized the drunkard’s plight and enrolled as many as 600,000 pledge-takers by 1844. The early movement fit the emerging middle-class ethic of a sober workforce.[^ch16-lumen-temperance]
- **Common schools.** Horace Mann, appointed secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education in 1837, became the leading advocate of the common school—free, universal, non-sectarian, and public—as a means of moral and socioeconomic uplift. He argued it would create the virtuous republican citizenry needed to sustain self-government and the disciplined workforce needed for the economy, and he edited *The Common School Journal* to spread the message. His legacy is mixed: some historians see a step toward a more open society, others a tool of social control that universalized the values of the Protestant middle class, and deaf advocates have criticized his role in promoting oral over sign education.[^ch16-mann-common-school]
- **Asylums.** Dorothea Dix’s *Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts* (January 1843) reported on her visits to jails and almshouses and demanded state provision for the “pauper insane.” She catalogued a woman “in a cage,” a person chained for seventeen years, and the “use of cages all but universal,” documenting the mixing of the mentally ill with criminals and paupers. Her appeal—“I tell what I have seen”—helped drive the creation of state asylums.[^ch16-dix-memorial]
- **The consequence.** The reforms were genuine improvements and instruments of order at once. The chapter should present them as efforts to perfect a society the market was transforming, not as simple benevolence.[^ch16-lumen-temperance][^ch16-mann-common-school]

### 4.3 Abolitionism and the printed word

- **The claim.** The 1830s saw the rise of immediate abolitionism, a movement that demanded an uncompensated end to slavery now and used the press to reach a national audience.[^ch16-garrison-liberator]
- **The Liberator.** William Lloyd Garrison, who had earlier advocated Black colonization and gradual abolition, changed his mind under the influence of Black northerners such as David Walker and launched *The Liberator* in Boston on January 1, 1831. In its first issue he renounced gradual abolition as “timidity, injustice, and absurdity” and demanded the “immediate enfranchisement of our slave population,” declaring: “I am in earnest—I will not equivocate—I will not excuse—I will not retreat a single inch—AND I WILL BE HEARD.”[^ch16-garrison-liberator][^ch16-nps-liberator]
- **The movement and its base.** Garrison’s tenets included an immediate uncompensated end to slavery, attacks on the Church and the Constitution as tools of the oppressors, and change through peaceful moral suasion alone; he also championed women’s rights, pacifism, and the fight against capital punishment. The paper drew some of its strongest support from Boston’s free Black community: Black leaders endorsed it, a committee of Black women led fundraisers, and Black subscribers made up roughly 75 percent of subscribers in the early years. Garrison published writings by Black authors including Maria Stewart and William Cooper Nell.[^ch16-nps-liberator]
- **The backlash.** The movement provoked furious opposition. In 1835 abolitionists mailed thousands of antislavery tracts to southern clergy and officials; southern postmasters and mobs intercepted many, and Jackson and Postmaster General Amos Kendall approved their action and recommended federal suppression of “incendiary publications.” Congress began adopting annual “gag rules” to keep abolition petitions off the floor.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-check-reform]
- **The consequence.** Abolitionism remained a minority movement, but it changed the terms of national debate and forced the question of slavery into the mails, the petitions, and the floor of Congress—where the gag rule only proved its disruptive power.[^ch16-nps-liberator]

### 4.4 Women, moral reform, and the limits of “the people”

- **The claim.** Women entered public reform through benevolence and antislavery work, and their participation sharpened the era’s central question about who belonged to “the people.”[^ch16-grimke-appeal]
- **The appeal.** Angelina Grimké, a South Carolinian who became a Philadelphia abolitionist, published her *Appeal to the Christian Women of the South* in 1836, written as an evangelical appeal to southern women to act against slavery. She grounded the case in the Declaration’s “self-evident truth that all men are created equal” and in Scripture, and she answered the objection that women made no laws: “I know you do not make the laws, but I also know that you are the wives and mothers, the sisters and daughters of those who do.” She urged women to read, pray, speak, and act, and defended the doctrine that if a law commands one to sin, it should be broken.[^ch16-grimke-appeal]
- **The Tocquevillian frame.** Tocqueville, writing in 1835, described American democracy as separating the sexes into “two clearly distinct lines of action” and held that American women never managed business or took part in political life—a claim the Grimkés’ public activism was already straining. The chapter should set Tocqueville’s description against the women who were entering the public sphere.[^ch16-tocqueville-hanover]
- **The labor movement.** In the same years artisans and journeymen organized. Philadelphia’s carpenters struck for a ten-hour day in 1827; out of the failed strike William Heighton formed the Mechanics’ Union of Trade Associations, the nation’s first citywide coalition of local unions, and in 1828 the Working Men’s Party, the nation’s first labor party, whose platform demanded a ten-hour day, free public education, the abolition of imprisonment for debt, and a more equitable tax system. The party elected twenty of its fifty-four candidates in 1829 before collapsing, but Pennsylvania abolished imprisonment for debt in 1833, passed the Free School Act in 1834, and granted city workers a ten-hour day in 1835.[^ch16-phila-workingmen][^ch16-dol-pessen]
- **The boundary.** Seneca Falls and the organized campaign for women’s suffrage belong to 1848 and to later context; this chapter should name the connection without absorbing it. The boundary is a forward pointer, not a subplot inside an 1840 endpoint.[^ch16-check-reform][^ch16-merritt-voting]

## 5. Print, popular culture, and exclusion

### 5.1 The penny press and a national audience

- **The claim.** A new cheap daily press in the 1830s drew a mass urban readership and transformed how Americans learned about politics, crime, and the wider world.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun]
- **The innovation.** The *New York Sun*, founded in 1833, was among the inexpensive “penny press” that began publication during the 1830s and “drew a large new readership with lower prices and a more narrative style of journalism than earlier newspapers.” By selling for a penny and depending on advertising and circulation rather than party subsidy, the penny papers built an audience that included the working class.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun]
- **The audience.** The historian framework reproduced by the Lost Museum treats the penny press as the creation of a large, previously untapped urban audience; the *Sun* made crime, scandal, and sensation into front-page material and in doing so helped invent the modern idea of “the news.”[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun]
- **The consequence.** The new press extended the public sphere beyond the party organ and the mercantile paper, but it also showed how easily a mass audience could be manipulated—a lesson the 1835 moon hoax delivered with a straight face.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-lindahall-locke]

### 5.2 In Focus: a *New York Sun* page from the moon hoax, 1835

- **In Focus: the August 25, 1835, front page of the *New York Sun*.** **In Focus.** The box reads a single page: the first column of the six-day “Great Moon Hoax” series that began in the *Sun* on August 25, 1835, under the headline “Great Astronomical Discoveries lately made by Sir John Herschel.” The column announced that the famous English astronomer, working in South Africa with a telescope “of vast dimensions and an entirely new principle,” had discovered life on the moon, and it attributed the account to a reprint from a *Supplement* to the *Edinburgh Journal of Science*—credibility borrowed from a respectable journal that the *Sun* did not disclose had ceased publishing under that title in 1833. The page then described the telescope itself, reported as some twenty-four feet in diameter and able to magnify objects 42,000 times, before reaching the lunar creatures: unicorns, bipedal beavers, and finally the flying human-like “man-bat,” which the paper named *Vespertilio-Homo*.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-lindahall-locke]
- **How to read it.** The page is an instrument for reading the new public sphere. It shows the mechanics of borrowed authority, the appetite of a mass readership, and the entertainment economy of the penny press. Its author was the *Sun* writer Richard Adams Locke, who intended to satirize the popular belief in extraterrestrial life (the object of fun was the Scottish writer Thomas Dick) and to boost circulation; the series ran six days, was republished as pamphlets in several languages, and was never publicly confessed by the *Sun*. Locke admitted he had invented it only in a private letter five years later. The chapter should read the page as evidence of how a mass audience was made and managed, not as a curiosity.[^ch16-lindahall-locke][^ch16-lostmuseum-sun]
- **The text.** A reading can quote from the archived transcript: “We were thrilled … to perceive four successive flocks of large winged creatures, wholly unlike any kind of birds,” and the description of beings who “were like human beings, for their wings had now disappeared.” The chapter must collate any quotation against the *Sun* page or a scholarly edition.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-check-focus]
- **The limits.** The box must fix the exact issue and page, locate a digitized copy, and note that the often-reproduced “lunar animals” lithograph was sold separately and was not part of the original columns. If a rights-cleared facsimile cannot be obtained, the box reads the page from the archived transcript and citation alone and does not reproduce a third-party scan.[^ch16-check-focus][^ch16-lindahall-locke]

### 5.3 The gag rule, the mails, and the boundaries of debate

- **The claim.** The era’s widening public sphere had a hard boundary, and the boundary was slavery.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The petitions.** Antislavery societies bombarded Congress with petitions for the abolition of slavery and the slave trade in the District of Columbia. Southern defenders demanded that criticism of slavery be silenced in the interest of public safety, and Congress adopted annual “gag rules” to keep abolition petitions off the floor.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The mails and the mobs.** The 1835 postal campaign provoked violence: southern postmasters and mobs intercepted antislavery tracts, and in Charleston a mob broke into the post office and burned mail. Jackson and Kendall approved the interception and recommended federal suppression of “incendiary publications,” and Jackson’s Farewell Address of 1837 warned of sectional fanaticism and a “money power” that threatened liberty.[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
- **The consequence.** The gag rule made the suppression of debate itself a political issue and gave abolitionists a platform for arguing that the South was muzzling the North. The chapter should show that the same public sphere that widened for White men narrowed around slavery.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-nps-liberator]

### 5.4 Racial exclusion and the narrowing republic

- **The claim.** The age of the “common man” narrowed citizenship for free Black people and Native nations even as it widened it for White men.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- **The disfranchisement.** As White male suffrage expanded, free Black men lost the vote in state after state. New York’s 1821 convention expanded eligibility without addressing race, and the state restricted the franchise to “white freemen” in 1826; Pennsylvania did so in 1838, the same year its voters ratified a constitution confining the franchise to “white freemen.” During the Jacksonian era “nearly every free state in the North and Midwest extended voting rights to all White men … as economic qualifications were lowered or removed,” while state legislators “remembered … to confine the franchise explicitly to white men.” The chapter should present the widening and the narrowing as one process.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-merritt-voting]
- **The southern limits.** In the South and Southwest, voting restrictions for White men persisted because enslavers wanted to limit the franchise to men who would defend slavery; poll taxes, intimidation, and fraud held the electorate in check, and some states refused to let men cast ballots for Lincoln in 1860.[^ch16-merritt-voting]
- **The dispossession.** Removal and the cotton frontier’s dispossession of Native nations ran on the same national political consensus. Chapter 17 owns those routes; this chapter owns the political fact that the expanding democracy rested on the exclusion of the enslaved, the dispossessed, and the disfranchised.[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-check-ch17]
- **The consequence.** The chapter’s guiding question has its answer here: the political voice that widened for White men was purchased in part by the narrowing of the republic for everyone else. The “common man” was not a universal category but a racial and gendered one.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The electorate widened.** More White men voted in the 1820s and 1830s than before, the mass party became the central institution of national politics, and turnout rose dramatically by 1840.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-infoplease-turnout]
- **The expansion preceded and outran Jackson.** Historians’ estimates put White male eligibility as high as 80 percent by 1800; the Jacksonian era saw changes in party organization and campaign style as much as in law.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-feller-franchise]
- **The widening was accompanied by exclusions.** Free Black men lost the vote in state after state, women had no vote, and Native nations faced removal; the democracy expanded inside the category of White manhood.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal]
- **Jackson’s conflicts were real and consequential.** The Bank veto, the nullification crisis, the force bill, and the removal act defined his presidency and produced the Whig opposition.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-loc-nullification]
- **Revival and reform reshaped the North.** The Second Great Awakening generated organized reform, and abolitionism used print and petition to force slavery back onto the national agenda.[^ch16-finney-rochester][^ch16-nps-liberator]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the widening democratic or anti-democratic?** Democrats and historians alike have claimed Jackson as the champion of the common man; others point to his hostility to abolitionism, his removal policy, and the racial limits of his democracy. The chapter should weigh the evidence rather than declare a verdict.[^ch16-feller-franchise][^ch16-glencoe-historians]
- **Who made the expansion?** Blumin argues party strategists and demographic change drove suffrage expansion more than popular protest. If so, the era’s democracy was as much a product of elite calculation as of popular demand.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- **Did the parties give voters power?** Close competition and near-universal White male suffrage made politics participatory and theatrical, but the substance of party difference and the role of class are still disputed.[^ch16-feller-franchise][^ch16-glencoe-historians]
- **How central was slavery to the age?** Some historians read the era through the market revolution and class; others argue the Democratic Party was above all a defender of slavery and White supremacy, and that the age’s democracy was built on racial exclusion and dispossession.[^ch16-glencoe-historians][^ch16-ostler-removal]
- **How should Tocqueville be used?** Tocqueville is the era’s most quoted witness, and he is also a foreign aristocrat who observed for nine months and wrote for a French audience. The chapter should use him as a source and a subject, not as an unqualified witness to equality.[^ch16-tocqueville-hanover][^ch16-feller-franchise]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which Jacksonian democracy was genuinely expansive and genuinely exclusive: it mobilized White men into national politics and did so by narrowing the republic for the enslaved, the dispossessed, and the disfranchised—and it cannot be told apart from either the market revolution that fed it or the slavery and removal it protected.
- **The progressive synthesis.** The early twentieth-century “progressive” historians and their culmination in Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr.’s *The Age of Jackson* (1945) saw Jacksonian democracy as an early battle against economic privilege, a coalition of farmers and laborers East, West, and South against Eastern capital.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]
- **The entrepreneurial and consensus criticisms.** Richard Hofstadter’s 1948 essay read Jackson as the spokesman of rising entrepreneurs blocked by entrenched elites; Bray Hammond called the Jacksonian cause “one of enterpriser against capitalist”; and Marvin Meyers’s *The Jacksonian Persuasion* (1957) saw a nostalgic effort to restore an imagined agrarian past.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]
- **The quantitative and social-historical turn.** Lee Benson’s *The Concept of Jacksonian Democracy* (1961) stressed religion and ethnicity over class in voting; Edward Pessen’s *Jacksonian America* (1969) found rhetoric about equality disguising an increasingly stratified society; Sean Wilentz’s *Chants Democratic* (1984) and *The Rise of American Democracy* (2005) traced a working-class identity and a broadly democratizing politics; John Ashworth and Harry Watson read party conflict as a reflection of larger social change.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]
- **The critical reassessment.** Michael Rogin’s *Fathers and Children* (1975) and Alexander Saxton’s *The Rise and Fall of the White Republic* (1990) foreground Jackson’s violence toward Native nations and the Democratic Party’s commitment to slavery and White supremacy, while Daniel Walker Howe’s *What Hath God Wrought* (2007) portrays the Jacksonians as champions of White male supremacy and the Whigs as in some ways more truly democratic. Robert Remini’s biography still defends Jackson as, in his time, a genuine “man of the people.”[^ch16-glencoe-historians]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions show that the era’s democratic energy and its exclusions were one history. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not as teams, and should test each against the suffrage record, the removal vote, and the gag rule.[^ch16-glencoe-historians][^ch16-check-table]

## Legacies

The party system that took shape between 1824 and 1840 outlasted the men who built it. Mass parties, national conventions, patronage, and the campaign as spectacle became the permanent machinery of American politics, and the twice-a-decade mobilization of a large White male electorate became a defining feature of the republic.[^ch16-feller-franchise]

The age left a divided inheritance. It widened participation, made ordinary White men a force in national politics, and created institutions through which they could demand attention. It did so while free Black men lost the vote in state after state, while women remained outside the formal political nation, and while removal cleared Native nations from the path of the expanding cotton frontier.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal]

And the chapter’s central question endured. The Missouri line that Jefferson feared would never be erased stayed on the map until 1854; nullification’s doctrine of state sovereignty returned in the secession crisis; and the gag rule’s suppression of antislavery petitions foreshadowed the sectional conflict over slavery’s extension. The widening democracy and the deepening sectional conflict were not opposites. They were two faces of the same expanding republic, and the next chapters follow the removal and the war that carried the contradiction toward its crisis.[^ch16-missouri-act][^ch16-check-ch17][^ch16-loc-nullification]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** Tocqueville observed the United States for a French audience comparing democracy with aristocracy; the British antislavery movement and the British and German enthusiasm for the moon hoax show that American print and reform circulated in an Atlantic world. The chapter should treat American democracy as one part of a wider argument about the future of self-government.[^ch16-tocqueville-hanover][^ch16-lindahall-locke]
- **Archives and silences.** The election returns, the party platforms, and the newspaper pages record the voices of White male voters most fully; the enslaved, the disfranchised, the removed, and women appear in those records as property, as exclusions, or as dependents. The chapter should name that unevenness and seek the excluded in petitions, narratives, and the records of the reform and removal debates.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal]
- **Distant connections.** The Missouri crisis, nullification, and the gag rule set up the sectional conflict (Chs. 18–20); the market revolution that fed the political crisis belongs to Chapter 15; and the removal policy that this chapter treats politically becomes the substance of Chapter 17.[^ch16-check-ch15][^ch16-check-ch17]

## Summary

- The Missouri crisis ended the Era of Good Feelings; Tallmadge’s February 1819 amendments divided Congress, and the Compromise of 1820 paired Missouri and Maine and prohibited slavery north of 36°30′.[^ch16-house-tallmadge][^ch16-missouri-act]
- Jefferson heard the crisis as a “fire bell in the night” and predicted the geographical line would “never be obliterated,” while conceding he had no practicable remedy.[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
- White male suffrage widened mainly before Jackson—perhaps 80 percent of adult White males were eligible by 1800—and free Black men lost the vote in state after state as it widened.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-merritt-voting]
- In 1824 Jackson won the most popular and electoral votes but lost in the House, which chose Adams and prompted Jackson’s charge of a “corrupt bargain.”[^ch16-ucsb-1824][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
- The mass party, with conventions, patronage, and spectacle, drove turnout from about 26.9 percent in 1824 to about 80.2 percent in 1840, winning the era its reputation as the age of the common man.[^ch16-infoplease-turnout][^ch16-ucsb-turnout]
- Jackson’s administration created the spoils system, survived the Eaton affair, faced down nullification with a proclamation and the Force Bill, vetoed the Second Bank in 1832, and pushed the Indian Removal Act of 1830 through a closely divided Congress.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-loc-nullification]
- The Second Great Awakening’s revivals fed temperance, common-school, and asylum reform, and immediate abolitionism used the press to force slavery onto the national agenda.[^ch16-finney-rochester][^ch16-garrison-liberator]
- The penny press built a mass audience in the 1830s, and the 1835 moon hoax showed how easily that audience could be manipulated.[^ch16-lostmuseum-sun][^ch16-lindahall-locke]
- The gag rule, the interception of antislavery mail, and the disfranchisement of free Black men bounded the era’s public sphere even as it widened for White men.[^ch16-jackson-domestic][^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
- The debate over Jacksonian democracy turns on whether the widening electorate was a democratic victory, an elite-managed expansion, or a White male supremacy built on exclusion, and the honest answer holds several of these together.[^ch16-glencoe-historians]

## Study Questions

1. Read Jefferson’s April 22, 1820, letter to John Holmes. What does the “fire bell in the night” reveal about his fears, and how does the “wolf by the ear” justify his inaction?[^ch16-jefferson-holmes]
2. Read the Tallmadge amendment and Section 8 of the Missouri act. On what constitutional ground did Tallmadge rest Congress’s power over slavery in the territories, and how did the compromise answer him?[^ch16-yawp-missouri][^ch16-missouri-act]
3. Blumin argues that White male suffrage expanded mainly before Jackson and was driven by party strategy. Using his account, explain why the Jacksonian era gets credit for a change that began earlier.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage]
4. The 1824 election gave Jackson the most votes but not the presidency. Explain the House procedure and assess the evidence for and against the “corrupt bargain” charge.[^ch16-ucsb-1824][^ch16-miller-corrupt-bargain]
5. Use Figure 3. What does the rise in turnout between 1824 and 1840 measure, and what does the chart leave out about who could vote?[^ch16-check-figure]
6. Compare Jackson’s Bank Veto message with his removal policy. How does the same president argue for the “humble members of society” and against the “incendiary publications” of the abolitionists?[^ch16-jackson-domestic]
7. Read Dorothea Dix’s 1843 *Memorial* and Angelina Grimké’s 1836 *Appeal*. How did each woman convert the era’s domestic and religious ideals into public argument?[^ch16-dix-memorial][^ch16-grimke-appeal]
8. The 1835 moon hoax was intended as satire, not deception. What does its success suggest about the new penny-press audience and about how public opinion could be shaped?[^ch16-lindahall-locke]
9. Tocqueville wrote that there was “so little independence of mind and real freedom of discussion” in America. Is his the “tyranny of the majority,” and how does the gag rule bear on his claim?[^ch16-tocqueville-hanover][^ch16-jackson-domestic]
10. Use Table 1. Which interpretation best explains why the widening electorate coexisted with racial exclusion, and what does each position leave unexplained?[^ch16-check-table]
11. The removal vote of 1830 was 102 to 97 in the House. What does its closeness reveal about the politics of removal, and why did the policy proceed anyway?[^ch16-ostler-removal]
12. The contingency question: if the House had defeated the Indian Removal Act in 1830, or if New York had not restricted the vote to “white freemen” in 1826, how might the age of Jackson have looked different? What evidence would you need to answer?[^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-blumin-suffrage]

> *A closing note.* The age of the common man was real, and so was its price. Between 1815 and 1840 more White men voted than ever before, parties competed for their support, and an ordinary person could rise to the presidency. That same era drew a line across the continent at 36°30′, took the vote from free Black men in state after state, removed tens of thousands of Native people from their homelands, and silenced antislavery petitions in Congress. The chapter’s question is not whether the era was democratic; it is how a widening democracy and a hardening exclusion could be the same history, and who decided where the line between the two would fall.[^ch16-blumin-suffrage][^ch16-ostler-removal][^ch16-jackson-domestic]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at New Echota in December 1835, where a small faction of Cherokees signed the treaty that would lead to their removal. This chapter has explained why removal was a national political question—Jackson’s priority, the close vote of 1830, the Cherokee cases, and the consensus behind dispossession. Chapter 17 takes up the treaty, the routes, and the making of Indian Country.[^ch16-check-ch17][^ch16-ostler-removal]
