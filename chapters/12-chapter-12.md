## Epigraphs

> “The unhappy time is come in which we have been obliged to shed blood.”[^ch12-shepard-letter]

— William Shepard to James Bowdoin, Springfield, January 26, 1787.[^ch12-shepard-letter]

> “This Government will commence in a moderate Aristocracy; it is at present impossible to foresee whether it will, in it’s Operation, produce a Monarchy, or a corrupt oppressive Aristocracy…”[^ch12-constitutioncenter-mason]

— George Mason, “Objections to the Constitution of Government formed by the Convention,” 1787.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-mason]

> “Twenty years will produce all the mischief that can be apprehended from the liberty to import slaves. So long a term will be more dishonorable to the American character than to say nothing about it in the Constitution.”[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise]

— James Madison, debated in the Federal Convention, August 25, 1787, as reported in his notes.[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise]

> “It was a momentous change, and one not at all anticipated in 1776.”[^ch12-amrev-power-liberty]

— Gordon S. Wood, *Power and Liberty: Constitutionalism in the American Revolution* (2021).[^ch12-amrev-power-liberty]

- **Why these four.** A Massachusetts general, ordered to defend a federal arsenal, opened his report with the fact of bloodshed. An Anti-Federalist who had helped write Virginia’s Declaration of Rights predicted that the new government would begin as an aristocracy and end as a monarchy or an oligarchy. A framer conceded the slave trade’s evil and then accepted twenty more years of it as the price of union. A modern historian insists that no one in 1776 imagined the government of 1787. The four disagree about what the Constitution was for: order, suspicion of power, the defense of slavery, or the unplanned product of a decade of conflict.[^ch12-check-epigraphs]
- **The attribution rule.** Each epigraph carries its own named note with a precise locator. Shepard’s sentence is quoted from the Gilder Lehrman Institute’s transcription of his letter, sourced to the Springfield Technical Community College Shays’s Rebellion project; collate it against the original in the Bowdoin papers. Mason’s sentence is quoted from the National Constitution Center’s excerpt of his objections; collate it against the *Documentary History of the Ratification of the Constitution* text and note that the manuscript date is disputed between September and October 1787. Madison’s sentence is quoted from the National Park Service day-by-day account of August 25, 1787, which reproduces his notes; collate it against a scholarly edition of the Convention debates. Wood’s sentence is quoted from the Museum of the American Revolution’s excerpt of *Power and Liberty*; locate it in the printed book.[^ch12-check-epigraphs][^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-constitutioncenter-mason][^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-amrev-power-liberty]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the militia fired without provocation, that the Constitution was designed as an aristocracy, that all the framers reasoned as Madison did, or that the outcome was random. They stage the chapter’s questions: whose power did the Constitution secure, and at what cost was it obtained.

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The arsenal at Springfield.** Springfield, Massachusetts, held the United States Arsenal, with barracks, an infirmary, a powder house, an artillery park, and stores. In the autumn of 1786 the state militia began gathering there to protect the state supreme court and the arsenal from the indebted farmers and veterans who were closing courts across western Massachusetts.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays]
- **The column approaches.** On January 25, 1787, a force under Captain Daniel Shays marched toward the public buildings at the arsenal. In his report the next day, General William Shepard wrote that Shays “was at the head of about twelve hundred men,” that he “marched yesterday afternoon about four o’Clock, towards the public buildings in battle array,” and that he “marched his men in an open column by plattoons.”[^ch12-shepard-letter]
- **The demand and the warning.** Asked what he wanted, Shays answered “he wanted barracks, & barracks, he would have & Stores.” The reply was “he must purchase them dear, if he had them.” Shays halted about 250 yards from the arsenal; Shepard sent an aide to warn him “not to march his troops any nearer the Arsenal on his peril,” and that he would “surely fire on him & his men” if Shays advanced. Shays then “put his troops in motion, & marched on rapidly near one hundred yards.”[^ch12-shepard-letter]
- **The artillery.** Shepard ordered Major Stephens, commanding the artillery, to fire. “The two first shott he endeavoured to overshoot them, in hopes they would have taken warning without firing among them, but it had no effect on them. Major Stephens then directed his shot thro’ the center of his column. The fourth or fifth shot put their whole column into the utmost confusion.” A howitzer loaded with grapeshot added to the damage. “There was not a single musket fired on either side,” Shepard reported. He found “three men dead on the spot, & one wounded, who is since dead,” and one artilleryman badly wounded when a gun was mishandled.[^ch12-shepard-letter]
- **The aftermath.** The rebel force broke and scattered; state troops under General Benjamin Lincoln surprised its camp in early February and ended the organized resistance. Shays fled and was later pardoned.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-abt-shays-bio]
- **The numbers.** The accounts differ. Shepard reported about 1,200 men under Shays; the American Battlefield Trust gives Shays’s force as about 1,500 and the militia as 1,200; the Springfield Technical Community College reconstruction describes a column of more than 1,400 Regulators against more than 1,200 militia, and puts the dead at three and the wounded at more than a dozen. The battle’s casualties are given as four dead and twenty wounded by the American Battlefield Trust. The scene must give the range and not settle it.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene][^ch12-check-numbers]
- **The weather and the missing regiment.** The Springfield Technical Community College scene says January 25 “dawned cold,” with four feet of snow, and that a supporting regiment under Luke Day missed the attack because of a miscommunication. These details come from a reconstructed public-history scene, not from Shepard’s letter, and must be qualified or attributed if used.[^ch12-stcc-scene][^ch12-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A government defending itself.** The scene should open on the militia’s position at the arsenal and let the reader watch the column approach, the warnings fail, and the cannon fire. The point is not that the farmers were criminals; it is that a state government, and a federal installation it garrisoned, had reached the point of firing on its own people.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays]
- **Both sides invoked the Revolution.** The Regulators were debt-ridden farmers, many of them veterans who had not been paid, and they described themselves as defending the liberties won in the Revolution; the militia, including former officers, understood themselves as defending government against anarchy. The scene should let both claims stand.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: What problems did the Constitution solve, and whose power did it secure?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that the rebellion produced the Constitution. The Convention had already been authorized when the cannon fired at Springfield. The deeper story is the fiscal crisis, the contested meaning of the Revolution, and the bargains over representation and slavery that shaped the frame.[^ch12-check-scene][^ch12-avalon-feb21][^ch12-check-ch11]
- **The widening.** The scene sits at the arsenal in January 1787. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader back to the Articles of 1781 and forward to the Convention and the ratification contest.[^ch12-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- No invented dialogue, weather, or interior thought beyond what the sources describe. The demand for barracks and stores, the warnings, and the artillery fire are documented; the day’s fine weather and the crowd’s feelings are not.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-check-scene]
- No settled count of the rebel force or the casualties; the accounts differ, and the scene must give the range with its sources.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene][^ch12-check-numbers]
- No claim that the militia fired without warning. Shepard’s report describes repeated warnings and two deliberately overshot shots before the aimed fire.[^ch12-shepard-letter]
- No claim that Shays’s Rebellion alone called the Convention into being. Annapolis and Congress’s February 21, 1787 resolution came first or independently; the rebellion strengthened an argument already under way.[^ch12-avalon-feb21][^ch12-check-scene]
- No claim that all the Regulators were poor or all the militia were wealthy; the two forces drew from overlapping communities of neighbors and veterans.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene]

## 1. Confederation government

### 1.1 The Articles as a league of states

- **The claim.** The Articles of Confederation created a Congress of sovereign states, not a government over individuals, and its structural limits were the problem the Constitution was meant to solve.
- **What the Articles said.** The Articles were adopted by the Continental Congress on November 15, 1777, and took effect when Maryland ratified them on March 1, 1781. They established a “league of friendship” in which each state retained “its sovereignty, freedom and independence,” and every power “which is not by this confederation expressly delegated to the United States.” Congress had one vote per state, and the document’s own terms made amendment require the agreement of every state.[^ch12-archives-articles]
- **What Congress could not do.** Congress under the Articles lacked authority to levy taxes; it could only request that the states contribute their share to the common treasury, and the requested sums did not come. It could negotiate treaties, but the states had to ratify them, and Congress could not compel obedience. It had no authority to regulate foreign or interstate commerce, so state tariffs and reprisals multiplied. Important legislation required nine states, and amendments required all thirteen, which let a single state block a fiscal amendment.[^ch12-congress-confederation]
- **The fiscal design.** Article VIII defrayed war and general expenses from a common treasury supplied by the states “in proportion to the value of all land within each state,” and Article IX apportioned troop requisitions “in proportion to the number of white inhabitants in such state.” The Articles thus counted people for requisitions while leaving the money to the states—the fiscal weakness that the Constitution’s framers set out to repair.[^ch12-archives-articles][^ch12-check-slavery-terms]
- **What the Confederation achieved.** The chapter should not reduce the 1780s to failure. The Confederation concluded the peace, received the states’ western land cessions, passed the Land Ordinance of 1785 and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, and created the framework for admitting new states. The debate is over whether its weaknesses were fatal or merely inconvenient.[^ch12-archives-northwest-ordinance][^ch12-amrev-power-liberty]
- **The consequence.** By the mid-1780s, nationalists argued that the Confederation could not pay its debts, defend its commerce, or hold the union together, and that amendment was impossible under the Articles’ own rules. That argument is the chapter’s starting point.[^ch12-congress-confederation][^ch12-archives-articles]

### 1.2 War debt, paper money, and the fiscal crisis

- **The claim.** The war’s debt, the states’ refusal to fund it, and the resulting paper money and foreclosures made the Confederation’s weakness concrete for creditors, soldiers, and the public.
- **The debt.** The Confederation owed foreign and domestic creditors for the war. Congress attempted to fund the debt through an impost and other requisitions; the need for a revenue amendment was widely conceded even before Maryland ratified the Articles in 1781, and twelve states agreed to an amendment allowing duties—until Rhode Island refused, defeating it.[^ch12-congress-confederation]
- **Soldiers paid in IOUs.** Many Revolutionary soldiers were paid in government debt—effectively IOUs—so the paper had little value at the time, and cash-strapped veterans sold it cheaply to investors. The Convention later debated whether the new government should pay these securities at face value; George Mason objected to rewarding “those who purchased fraudulently of the ignorant and distressed,” while Elbridge Gerry, who owned at least $50,000 worth of the securities, defended the market that kept their value up. The decision on when and how to pay the war debt was left to Congress.[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise]
- **Paper money and inflation.** The central government had a depleted treasury while paper money flooded the country, creating inflation; the states, meanwhile, pursued their own monetary and debtor-relief policies. The result was a contest over whether to honor debts in hard money or to inflate them away.[^ch12-archives-articles]
- **The critical-period question.** Nineteenth-century writers such as John Fiske called the 1780s “the most critical moment in all the history of the American people,” a time of near-ruin and disorder. Later historians, including Merrill Jensen, argued that the decade also showed growth and political vitality, and that the “crisis” was partly constructed after the fact. The chapter should present the fiscal problem as real and the claim of total collapse as contested.[^ch12-amrev-power-liberty][^ch12-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** Creditors wanted a government that could pay; soldiers and farmers wanted relief; merchants wanted commercial regulation. The Constitution answered the first and third and threatened the second—which is why the debt question ran through both the Convention and the ratification fight.[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-federalist-10]

### 1.3 From Annapolis to Philadelphia

- **The claim.** The movement for a stronger government began before Shays’s Rebellion, as states tried to solve commerce problems that the Confederation could not.
- **The steps.** A Maryland–Virginia conference at Mount Vernon in 1785 produced an interstate compact on Potomac navigation. Virginia then called for a broader commercial convention, which met at Annapolis in September 1786 with delegates from only five states; its report, sent to Congress and the states, asked that another convention meet at Philadelphia the following May to amend the Articles.[^ch12-wikipedia-timeline][^ch12-loc-convention]
- **Congress’s call.** On February 21, 1787, Congress, having considered the Annapolis report, “strongly recommend[ed] to the different legislatures to send forward delegates to meet the proposed convention on the second Monday in May next at the city of Philadelphia,” to revise the Articles. The authorization thus predated the Springfield fight by a month, though the fighting strengthened the case for action.[^ch12-avalon-feb21]
- **Washington and Madison.** Washington had retired to Mount Vernon but remained engaged; James Madison and Henry Knox persuaded him that “a thorough reform of the present system is indispensable” and that his presence was essential. Washington attended and was unanimously elected president of the Convention.[^ch12-mountvernon-convention][^ch12-loc-convention]
- **The consequence.** The delegates were authorized to “revise” the Articles but quickly decided to replace them—a decision the chapter must mark as a deliberate break with the mandate, not an obvious step. That decision is the subject of §3.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]

## 2. State crises and Shays’s Rebellion

### 2.1 Massachusetts: taxes, foreclosures, and the Regulators

- **The claim.** In Massachusetts, hard-money taxes to pay the state’s war debt fell on indebted farmers, many of them unpaid veterans, who organized to stop the foreclosures.
- **The tax burden.** To pay its creditors, Massachusetts merchants persuaded the state government to raise taxes; the hardest hit were western farmers with little hard currency, who relied on barter and credit. When they could not pay, their farms were seized through foreclosure and some were sent to debtors’ prisons.[^ch12-abt-shays]
- **The veterans.** Many of the farmers were Revolutionary veterans who had received less than their full pay, sometimes none, and whose pensions went only to the disabled or to soldiers’ widows. Captain Daniel Shays, who had fought at Bunker Hill and Saratoga and had guarded the British major John André, sold an ornamental sword that Lafayette had given him to pay his debts and met a storm of criticism for it.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-abt-shays-bio]
- **The courthouse closings.** When the legislature adjourned in August 1786 without acting on petitions for debt relief, a group of more than 1,000 farmers marched on Northampton and prevented the court from convening. The farmers called themselves “Regulators,” a name borrowed from an earlier North Carolina uprising. Shays was among them, and the next month he helped lead armed farmers who intimidated the Springfield court into adjourning.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-abt-shays-bio]
- **The state’s response.** Governor James Bowdoin condemned the actions, and Benjamin Lincoln, a former Continental general, raised money from merchants to recruit and equip a militia of about 3,000 men to suppress the rebels. The national government had no power to intervene, so the crisis fell to the state.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-abt-shays-bio]
- **The consequence.** The Massachusetts crisis shows the fiscal problem at the level where it was felt: a state taxing in hard money to pay war creditors while its own veterans went unpaid. It is the context for the arsenal fight.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-congress-confederation]

### 2.2 The Springfield arsenal, January 25, 1787

- **The claim.** At Springfield, the state militia defended the federal arsenal and opened fire on the Regulators, ending the rebellion’s main force in a matter of minutes and generating the evidence for the argument that the Confederation could not keep order.
- **The assault.** Shays and other leaders marched on the arsenal hoping to seize the weapons stored there and to force the government to listen; Shepard’s militia, some of them armed from the arsenal’s own stores, held the buildings and turned two cannon to rake the road. When Shays’s column came on after the warnings, the artillery fired.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene]
- **The record.** Shepard’s letter of January 26, 1787, is the central primary source for the action: the size of the column, the demand for “barracks … & Stores,” the warning and the advance, the overshot warning shots, the aimed shot into the column’s center, the howitzer’s grapeshot, the absence of musket fire, and the dead and wounded.[^ch12-shepard-letter]
- **The cost.** Shepard counted three killed outright and one mortally wounded among the Regulators, plus one artilleryman hurt in a loading accident; the American Battlefield Trust gives four killed and twenty wounded. The Springfield project describes three killed instantly and more than a dozen wounded. Present these as ranges with their sources.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene][^ch12-check-numbers]
- **The ending.** The rebel column broke and fled; most of the men scattered north. On the night of February 3, Lincoln’s militia surprised the rebel camp and captured about 150 men; Shays and others escaped over the border into Vermont or New Hampshire. The state convicted and sentenced eighteen men, including Shays, to death, but most were pardoned, and Shays was pardoned in 1788.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-abt-shays-bio]
- **The consequence.** The fight at the arsenal was the most violent clash in the Massachusetts conflict and the one that made the rebellion a national argument. Whether it proved the need for a stronger government or revealed the government’s willingness to shed its citizens’ blood is the question the chapter must hold open.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-stcc-scene]

### 2.3 The aftermath and the argument over causes

- **The claim.** The rebellion was suppressed, but its meaning was contested: nationalists read it as proof that the Confederation could not maintain order, while critics read the state’s response as evidence of hard-money government against its own people.
- **Contemporary reactions.** George Washington wrote nervously that “commotions of this sort, like snow-balls, gather strength as they roll.” Abigail Adams called the Regulators “ignorant, restless desperadoes … under the pretense of grievances which have no existence but in their imaginations.” Thomas Jefferson, writing from Paris, took the other side: “I like a little rebellion now and then. It is like a storm in the Atmosphere.” The chapter should let the disagreement appear rather than resolving it.[^ch12-abt-shays][^ch12-stcc-scene]
- **The historiographical question.** Late-twentieth-century accounts treated Shays’s Rebellion as an agrarian insurrection that exposed the Articles’ weakness and helped lead to the Constitution. More recent work emphasizes the movement’s largely nonviolent courthouse tactics, its basis in Revolutionary grievance, and the degree to which the rebellion was suppressed by state rather than national power. Whether it “caused” the Constitution is an open question, since the Convention had already been authorized.[^ch12-check-shays-sources][^ch12-avalon-feb21][^ch12-check-scene]
- **The constitutional effect.** What can be established is that the rebellion, and the debt crisis behind it, became part of the case the nationalists made in Philadelphia and in the ratification argument: that a government unable to pay its debts or keep order needed to be replaced.[^ch12-battlefields-ratify][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]
- **The consequence.** The rebellion did not create the Convention; it changed the terms of the argument inside it. That argument is the subject of §3.[^ch12-avalon-feb21][^ch12-check-scene]

## 3. Philadelphia

### 3.1 The Convention assembles

- **The claim.** Delegates gathered in Philadelphia with a mandate to revise the Articles and instead produced a new frame of government, meeting in secret and treating the change as a response to a real crisis.
- **The meeting.** The Convention assembled at the Pennsylvania State House (Independence Hall) in May 1787, with delegates from twelve states; Rhode Island sent none. Washington presided, though he spoke rarely and saw his role as nonpartisan. The delegates shuttered the windows and swore secrecy so they could speak freely.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-mountvernon-convention][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]
- **The decision to replace.** The Library of Congress records that the delegates “quickly resolved to replace rather than merely revise the Articles of Confederation”; by mid-June they had decided to redesign the government. This departure from the Convention’s mandate is a turning point the chapter must mark explicitly.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]
- **The record.** Madison’s notes, which he refined nightly, became the most important contemporary record of the debates; other delegates kept their own notes and drafts. The chapter should treat the notes as a reconstruction filtered through Madison’s purposes, not a verbatim transcript.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** Once the delegates chose to replace the Articles, the question became structural: what powers a new government would hold, and how they would be distributed among states and branches. The plans that answered it are the next subsection.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-mountvernon-convention]

### 3.2 The Virginia Plan, the New Jersey Plan, and the structure

- **The claim.** Small-state resistance and large-state ambition produced the bicameral compromise and the structure of the new government.
- **The Virginia Plan.** Presented by Governor Edmund Randolph on May 29, 1787, and prepared chiefly by Madison and Washington, the Virginia Plan provided for proportional representation in a bicameral legislature and a strong national government with veto power over state laws. It became the basis for debate.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-mountvernon-convention]
- **The New Jersey Plan.** On June 15, William Paterson presented an alternative designed to protect the small states: a unicameral legislature with equal votes for states, as under the Articles, plus the power to raise revenue and regulate commerce and foreign affairs. It would have kept most of the Articles’ structure.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-mountvernon-convention]
- **The Great Compromise.** By mid-July the representation issue had brought the Convention near dissolution. On July 16 the delegates agreed to a bicameral legislature with equal state representation in the Senate and proportional representation in the House, with money bills originating in the House. This “great compromise,” or Connecticut Compromise, saved the Convention.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-mountvernon-convention]
- **The executive and judiciary.** The delegates debated whether the executive should be one person or a board, and eventually chose a single executive—with many expecting Washington to be the first president, which shaped the office. The judiciary, representation, trade, taxes, and the method of electing a president were all contested and compromised.[^ch12-mountvernon-convention][^ch12-loc-convention]
- **From draft to text.** A Committee of Detail, chaired by John Rutledge, produced a draft constitution in early August; a Committee of Style and Arrangement, working with Madison, Rufus King, and Alexander Hamilton, gave the document its final form in September. Gouverneur Morris is credited with the preamble’s “We the people of the United States,” which anchored the government in the people rather than in the states.[^ch12-loc-convention]
- **The signing.** On September 17, 1787, thirty-eight delegates signed; a delegate signed for the absent John Dickinson, bringing the total to thirty-nine. Three delegates present—Edmund Randolph and George Mason of Virginia and Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts—refused to sign.[^ch12-archives-constitution-how][^ch12-loc-convention]

### 3.3 Representation, slavery, and the bargains

- **The claim.** The Convention bargained over how to count enslaved people for representation and taxation and how long to permit the slave trade, and those bargains built slaveholding power into the frame.
- **Counting the enslaved.** The three-fifths rule, recommended by Madison, counted three-fifths of “all other Persons” for apportioning both House seats and direct taxes. Southern states wanted to count the whole enslaved population; Northern states wanted to count none; the compromise gave the South most of the political benefit while imposing little of the tax liability in practice.[^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript]
- **The slave-trade clause.** Delegates agreed that Congress could not prohibit the “Migration or Importation of such Persons” until 1808, though it could impose a tax of up to ten dollars per person on such importation. Charles Cotesworth Pinckney moved to extend the deadline from 1800 to 1808, and the motion passed 7–4 over Madison’s objection; Madison recorded that “twenty years will produce all the mischief that can be apprehended from the liberty to import slaves” and that so long a term would be “more dishonorable to the American character than to say nothing about it in the Constitution.”[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript]
- **Avoiding the word.** Gouverneur Morris proposed naming the imported people “slaves” and identifying Georgia and the Carolinas; Roger Sherman and George Clymer objected that the word was “not pleasing to some people,” and Morris withdrew the change. The Constitution thus described slavery without using the words “slave” or “slavery.”[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-check-slavery-terms]
- **The fugitive clause and entrenchment.** Article IV, Section 2, provided that no person “held to Service or Labour” who escaped into another state should be discharged from that service but “shall be delivered up on Claim of the Party to whom such Service or Labour may be due.” Article V barred any amendment before 1808 that would affect the first and fourth clauses of Article I, Section 9, protecting the slave-trade bargain. The chapter should read these clauses together with the three-fifths clause as one system.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-cornell-article-v][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]
- **The Northwest Ordinance.** Passed by the Confederation Congress in July 1787, even as the Convention met, the Northwest Ordinance banned slavery in the territory north of the Ohio River while also providing for the return of people who escaped into it. It is evidence that antislavery and proslavery measures advanced together, and that the founding did not have a single position on slavery.[^ch12-archives-northwest-ordinance][^ch12-check-slavery-terms]
- **The consequence.** The compromises did not settle slavery; they postponed the conflict and increased the slaveholding states’ power in the new government. That doubled effect is the chapter’s central point about whose power the Constitution secured.[^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

### 3.4 In Focus: the Three-Fifths Clause

- **In Focus: the Three-Fifths Clause.** **In Focus.** Article I, Section 2, Clause 3, apportions seats in the House and direct taxes among the states “according to their respective Numbers,” found by adding “the whole Number of free Persons, including those bound to Service for a Term of Years, and excluding Indians not taxed, three fifths of all other Persons.”[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript] The clause never says “slave.” “All other Persons” was the Convention’s circumlocution for the enslaved, and the delegates chose it deliberately: Sherman and Clymer objected that the word “slave” was “not pleasing to some people,” and Madison and Morris debated whether to name the thing outright.[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise] Read carefully, the clause did not declare an enslaved person to be three-fifths of a human being; it was a formula for distributing political power and tax liability. Its practical effect was to count sixty percent of the enslaved population toward a state’s representation while counting far less of it, in practice, toward its taxes—a bargain that gave slaveholding states additional seats and, through the Electoral College, additional presidential electors. After the 1790 census, four Southern states gained eleven representatives from the rule.[^ch12-csac-three-fifths] The clause is the point at which the Constitution’s silence about slavery did its most concrete work.[^ch12-check-focus]

## 4. Federalist and Anti-Federalist arguments

### 4.1 The Federalist case: faction, union, and separated powers

- **The claim.** Writing as Publius, Madison, Hamilton, and Jay argued that an extended republic and separated powers would control faction and secure the union against foreign and domestic danger.
- **The essays.** The Federalist was a series of eighty-five newspaper essays published anonymously in defense of the Constitution, later collected in two volumes in 1788; Madison, Hamilton, and Jay were the authors. The essays aimed first at New York but had a national intended scope.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-federalist-10]
- **Federalist No. 10.** Published November 22, 1787, Madison’s tenth essay defines a faction as a group “united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adversed to the rights of other citizens,” argues that the “most common and durable source of factions has been the various and unequal distribution of property,” and contends that an extended republic will multiply interests and make oppressive majorities harder to form.[^ch12-federalist-10][^ch12-loc-convention]
- **Federalist No. 51.** Madison’s fifty-first essay defends the separation of powers: because “men are not angels,” the departments must check one another, and “ambition must be made to counteract ambition.” The essay is the clearest statement of the Constitution’s theory of auxiliary precautions.[^ch12-federalist-51]
- **The argument for union.** The Federalists contended that the Confederation could not pay its debts, defend its commerce, or secure its borders, and that only a government operating directly on individuals could do so. They also argued that the new government’s powers were enumerated and that the states would retain the rest.[^ch12-federalist-10][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]
- **The consequence.** The Federalist case turned economic conflict into a reason for a large republic rather than a small one—a striking answer to the Anti-Federalist claim that a large consolidated government would be tyrannical.[^ch12-federalist-10][^ch12-check-sources]

### 4.2 The Anti-Federalist case: consolidation, rights, and the states

- **The claim.** Opponents argued that the plan would consolidate power, absorb the states, and lacked a declaration of rights to constrain the new government.
- **Mason’s objections.** George Mason, who had refused to sign, wrote that “There is no Declaration of Rights,” that in the House there was “not the Substance, but the Shadow only of Representation,” that the federal judiciary would “absorb & destroy the Judiciarys of the several States,” and that the president had no constitutional council. He predicted the government would “commence in a moderate Aristocracy” and end in monarchy or oligarchy. His objections circulated widely and became a template for Anti-Federalist argument.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-mason][^ch12-encyclopediava-mason]
- **“Brutus.”** The Anti-Federalist essays of “Brutus” argued that “to reduce the thirteen States into one government, would prove the destruction of your liberties,” and warned that a constitution designed “for generations yet unborn” needed “the most express and full declaration of rights.” “Brutus” rejected as “specious” the claim that a bill of rights was unnecessary because everything not given was reserved.[^ch12-brutus-ii]
- **Patrick Henry.** At the Virginia ratifying convention, Henry attacked the preamble’s “We, the people” in place of “We, the states,” and warned that “The rights of conscience, trial by jury, liberty of the press, all your immunities and franchises, all pretensions to human rights and privileges, are rendered insecure, if not lost, by this change.” He argued that liberty, not power, was the proper end of government.[^ch12-teachinghistory-henry]
- **Without a bill of rights.** For many opponents, the Constitution’s failure to guarantee specific rights was the decisive objection: a government “complete to every object” could reach life, liberty, and property, and the state declarations would not bind it. The demand for amendments became the ratification bargain.[^ch12-brutus-ii][^ch12-archives-bor]
- **Who the Antifederalists were.** They were not a single group: some worried about aristocracy and distant power, some about state sovereignty, some about commerce and creditors, and some about slavery, though few spoke for the enslaved. The chapter should present them as critics of consolidation, not as one party with one motive.[^ch12-check-sources][^ch12-check-slavery-terms]

### 4.3 Who counted as “the people”?

- **The claim.** Both sides argued about who constituted “the people,” and the debate excluded enslaved people, Native nations, women, and the propertyless from the decision.
- **“We the people.”** The preamble’s phrase, credited to Gouverneur Morris, anchored the new government in the people rather than in the states—and Henry attacked it precisely for that reason. But the people who ratified the Constitution were the voters qualified under state law, overwhelmingly White men with property or other qualifications, choosing delegates to state conventions; the Constitution left voting qualifications to the states.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-teachinghistory-henry][^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript]
- **The enslaved and Native nations.** The three-fifths clause counted the enslaved for representation while denying them any voice, and the Constitution excluded “Indians not taxed” from that count while treating tribal nations as separate powers in the commerce clause. Neither group took part in ratification.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-csac-three-fifths]
- **Women.** Women were excluded from the ratifying conventions and from the franchise in every state, though they participated in the political argument. The chapter should name the exclusion rather than let “the people” stand unexamined.[^ch12-check-names]
- **The evidence problem.** Enslaved people, Native nations, and women appear in these records mainly through the documents of those who governed, owned, or excluded them. The chapter should name the archive’s angle of vision.[^ch12-check-names][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]
- **The consequence.** The ratification contest was a real argument about power, but it took place within a narrowed public. The Constitution’s promise and its exclusions were written in the same document.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## 5. Ratification

### 5.1 The Article VII process

- **The claim.** The framers bypassed the state legislatures and required nine state ratifying conventions, making the Constitution rest on popular consent—narrowly defined—rather than on the existing governments.
- **The rule.** Article VII provided that “The Ratification of the Conventions of nine States, shall be sufficient for the Establishment of this Constitution between the States so ratifying the Same.” The framers chose conventions rather than legislatures because legislators would be reluctant to give up power to a national government.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]
- **The theory.** Madison later described the Constitution as “of no more consequence than the paper on which it was written” unless ratified by the people; the ratification process was the framers’ attempt to make popular sovereignty real. Ratification thus became a second contest, separate from the Convention.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]
- **The starting position.** At the time the Constitution was signed, only six of the thirteen states had a pro-Constitution majority, so the Federalists needed to convert at least three. The outcome was not inevitable, and the Anti-Federalists nearly won.[^ch12-archives-constitution-how][^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]
- **The consequence.** Because ratification ran state by state, the contest turned on particular local balances of interest—commerce, debt, frontier, and slavery—rather than on a single national verdict. The state-by-state results are the next subsection.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification][^ch12-check-ratification-dates]

### 5.2 The Federalists win, state by state

- **The claim.** Ratification was close and contested; the Massachusetts compromise and the promise of recommended amendments secured the key states.
- **The first five.** Delaware ratified unanimously on December 7, 1787; Pennsylvania followed on December 12 by 46–23 after a bitter fight; New Jersey on December 18; Georgia on January 2, 1788; and Connecticut on January 9 by 128–40.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]
- **Massachusetts and the compromise.** The Massachusetts convention was closely divided, and critics such as Samuel Adams and John Hancock pressed for amendments. To reach “yes,” the Federalists brokered the “Massachusetts Compromise”: the convention ratified on February 6, 1788, while recommending a set of amendments. Every subsequent convention except Maryland’s recommended amendments as part of ratification.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification][^ch12-archives-bor]
- **The ninth and tenth states.** Maryland ratified on April 28, 1788 (63–11); South Carolina on May 23 (149–73); New Hampshire on June 21 (57–47), the ninth state to do so; and Virginia on June 25 (89–79) in a close fight, after Federalists agreed to recommend a bill of rights. New York followed on July 26, 1788 (30–27), another bitter contest; a New York City parade of artisans and professionals had pressed the convention days earlier.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification][^ch12-loc-convention]
- **The late states.** With eleven states in by the end of July 1788, the new government could begin. North Carolina and Rhode Island held out until after the new government was established, North Carolina ratifying in November 1789 and Rhode Island not until May 1790.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-check-ratification-dates]
- **The consequence.** The margin in Virginia (89–79) and New York (30–27) shows how contingent ratification was: a few shifted votes could have changed the result. The chapter should present the outcome as a near thing, not a verdict.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification][^ch12-check-ch13]

### 5.3 What ratification did and did not settle

- **The claim.** The new government began with the Bill of Rights promised but not yet secured and with the protections of slavery intact.
- **The promised amendments.** The recommended amendments were not legally binding, but they created a political commitment that the First Congress would take up. The compact between ratification and rights is the bridge to §6.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]
- **Slavery untouched.** None of the recommended amendments touched the three-fifths clause, the fugitive clause, or the slave-trade clause; the Constitution’s protections of slavery survived ratification intact and were reinforced by the settlements’ new power.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]
- **Who was represented.** State population and representation comparisons should use Appendix B, Table 1, not the presidential-election series, and must state the observation year and the treatment of the enslaved population.[^ch12-check-table][^ch12-csac-three-fifths]
- **The consequence.** Ratification created a new national government and a new political arena, but it settled the question of slavery’s place by not settling it. That open question is the chapter’s largest legacy.[^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## 6. The Bill of Rights

### 6.1 Madison’s amendments in the First Congress

- **The claim.** Madison, once a leading opponent of a bill of rights, introduced amendments in the First Congress to fulfill the ratification bargain and to head off more drastic structural changes.
- **The introduction.** Few members of the First Congress wanted to make amending the Constitution a priority, but Madison introduced a list of amendments on June 8, 1789, and pressed his colleagues to pass them. He had come to value the protections voters demanded, their role in educating people about rights, and the chance that adding them would prevent opponents from making larger changes.[^ch12-archives-bor]
- **The process.** The House passed a joint resolution with seventeen amendments; the Senate reduced it to twelve; a conference committee settled the differences, and on October 2, 1789, President Washington sent the twelve amendments to the states. By December 15, 1791, three-fourths of the states had ratified ten of them, now called the Bill of Rights.[^ch12-archives-bor]
- **The two that were not ratified then.** Of the twelve sent to the states, two were not ratified in 1791: one on the apportionment of representatives and one barring a congressional pay raise from taking effect before the next election. The pay amendment was finally ratified in 1992 as the Twenty-Seventh Amendment; the apportionment amendment remains unratified. The chapter should distinguish the twelve proposed from the ten adopted.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-check-bor-two]
- **The consequence.** The Bill of Rights was a product of the ratification struggle and of Madison’s political calculation, not a gift from the framers. Its adoption completed the bargain that had secured ratification in the key states.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]

### 6.2 The guarantees and their limits

- **The claim.** The Bill of Rights constrained the new federal government but not the states, and it left slavery untouched.
- **What the ten do.** The amendments protect speech, press, assembly, and religion; the right to bear arms; protection against quartering; search and seizure; grand jury, double jeopardy, self-incrimination, due process, and property; speedy trial, jury, and counsel; civil jury trial; and protection against excessive bail, cruel and unusual punishment, and reserved powers. They are limits on federal power.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript]
- **What they do not do.** As originally understood, the amendments bound only the federal government, not the states; the First Amendment begins “Congress shall make no law,” and it would take later constitutional development to apply the guarantees to the states. Nor did the amendments abolish slavery or give the vote to those excluded; those changes came with the Civil War and after. The chapter must not read later outcomes back into 1791.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-check-sources]
- **The silence.** No amendment in 1791 addressed the three-fifths clause, the fugitive clause, or the slave trade. The Bill of Rights protected the rights of citizens while leaving the Constitution’s protections of slavery in place—an omission its authors knew and largely accepted.[^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic][^ch12-csac-three-fifths]
- **The consequence.** The Bill of Rights shows both the Constitution’s capacity for amendment and the limits of what the founding generation would amend. It is the chapter’s last piece of evidence about whose power the Constitution secured.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## 7. The Debate

### 7.1 What is settled

- **The Articles’ weaknesses were real.** Congress could not tax, regulate commerce, or compel the states, and the amendment rule let one state block fiscal reform.[^ch12-congress-confederation][^ch12-archives-articles]
- **The Convention exceeded its mandate.** Authorized to revise the Articles, the delegates instead replaced them and created a government operating directly on individuals.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-archives-constitution-how]
- **The compromises protected slavery and built in slaveholding power.** The three-fifths rule, the fugitive clause, and the 1808 slave-trade guarantee worked as a system to entrench slavery and increase the slave states’ weight.[^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]
- **Ratification was close and contingent.** Virginia and New York were decided by narrow margins, and Massachusetts required the promise of amendments.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]
- **The Bill of Rights was a ratification bargain.** It responded to Anti-Federalist demands and did not touch slavery.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-brutus-ii]
- **The founding excluded most of the people.** Enslaved people, Native nations, women, and the propertyless did not participate in the decision about the government that claimed to speak for “the people.”[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-check-names]

### 7.2 What is open

- **Whether the 1780s were a genuine crisis.** Were the Articles’ defects fatal, or did later historians construct a “critical period” to justify the Constitution? Fiske called the decade the most critical in American history; Jensen argued there was no stagnation and decay, and Wood insists the change to a national republic was real and radical.[^ch12-amrev-power-liberty][^ch12-check-sources]
- **How much economic interest drove the outcome.** Did the framers and ratifiers act mainly from property interests, as Beard argued, or from many interests, ideas, and bargaining, as his critics replied?[^ch12-claremont-beard][^ch12-wikipedia-beard][^ch12-check-sources]
- **What Shays’s Rebellion caused.** Did the rebellion produce the Constitution, or did it merely strengthen an argument already under way? The authorization of the Convention preceded the arsenal fight.[^ch12-avalon-feb21][^ch12-check-shays-sources]
- **Whether the Constitution was pro-slavery or merely silent.** Did the document protect slavery operationally, as Waldstreicher and others argue, or did its refusal to name property in human beings leave an opening that later antislavery politics used, as Wilentz contends?[^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic][^ch12-check-sources]
- **What the archives can and cannot show.** Enslaved people, Native nations, and women appear mainly through the documents of those who governed or owned them; Madison’s notes are a mediated reconstruction; and the ratification debates were recorded unevenly. The chapter should name those limits.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic][^ch12-check-names]
- **How to weigh the near-miss.** If Virginia or New York had voted no, what would have happened? The narrow margins make the outcome look contingent, but counterfactual reasoning needs evidence, not assumption.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification]

### 7.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the Constitution was neither simply a democratic advance nor simply a counterrevolution: it created a more capable national government and, at the same time, built slaveholding power and racial and gender exclusions into its frame.
- **The economic interpretation.** Charles A. Beard’s *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution of the United States* (1913) argued that the framers were a cohesive elite protecting personal property, especially public securities, against debtor-majority politics, and that the Constitution was a “counter-revolution.” The thesis dominated Progressive-era scholarship and is the origin of the chapter’s central question about whose power the Constitution secured.[^ch12-claremont-beard][^ch12-wikipedia-beard]
- **The critics.** Beginning in the 1950s, Robert E. Brown and especially Forrest McDonald attacked Beard’s evidence and categories, arguing that the framers and ratifiers held many interests and that the Constitution cannot be reduced to bondholders’ self-interest; Peter Novick concluded that Beard’s Progressive account had been “decisively refuted.” Later work, including Robert McGuire’s statistical studies, found that class interests did matter but as one factor among several.[^ch12-claremont-beard][^ch12-wikipedia-beard][^ch12-check-sources]
- **The crisis debate.** Beard’s critics also questioned the “critical period” itself. John Fiske’s 1888 book gave the decade its name and its picture of near-ruin; Merrill Jensen’s *The New Nation* (1950) described the 1780s as years of hopeful striving, not chaos; Gordon Wood argues that the change to a national republic was nonetheless real, radical, and unanticipated. The chapter should hold both the fiscal crisis and the historians’ debate in view.[^ch12-amrev-power-liberty][^ch12-check-sources]
- **The democratic and slavery-centered readings.** Jackson Turner Main’s account of the Antifederalists restored the losers to the story, while Staughton Lynd, Paul Finkelman, and David Waldstreicher placed slavery at the center, arguing that the Constitution’s clauses protected slave property and increased slaveholding power. Sean Wilentz reads the document’s silence about property in human beings as an antislavery opening; Waldstreicher answers that the silence was strategic and the operation proslavery.[^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic][^ch12-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions say that the Constitution answered a real crisis of debt, commerce, and order, that its framers bargained among many interests rather than acting as one class, and that the bargains secured both a stronger republic and the slaveholding power that would nearly destroy it. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch12-claremont-beard][^ch12-amrev-power-liberty][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## Legacies

The Constitution created a government that could tax, borrow, regulate commerce, raise armies, and act directly on individuals, and it has proved durable and adaptable. It also embedded a contradiction at the center of the republic. The three-fifths clause, the fugitive clause, and the slave-trade guarantee made the new government a protector of slavery, and the slaveholding states used the House seats and electoral votes the three-fifths rule gave them to shape national policy for decades.[^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

The Bill of Rights gave the national government’s opponents real protections and became the foundation of American civil liberties, but as originally understood it bound only the federal government, and it said nothing about slavery—or about the rights of women, Native nations, or the enslaved. Those omissions would be addressed, incompletely and after enormous conflict, by later amendments and later generations.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-brutus-ii]

And it left the question the chapter’s epigraphs raise. The Constitution secured the power of those who made it, and it created a frame broad enough for later Americans to claim rights it did not yet grant. Both things are true at once, and the book returns to them whenever it asks who could claim membership, freedom, land, and authority in the republic.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The American Constitution was written in the same decade as the French Revolution’s opening and the revolution in Saint-Domingue; its framers watched events abroad and debated how a republic should guard against faction and foreign war. The chapter should place the founding inside the Atlantic revolutionary moment, not outside it.[^ch12-federalist-10][^ch12-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Enslaved people, Native nations, and women appear in these records mainly through the documents of those who governed, owned, or excluded them. Madison’s notes are a filtered reconstruction, and the state conventions’ debates survive unevenly; the chapter should name those limits.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 13 carries the new government into the 1790s; later chapters return to the three-fifths clause and the “slave power” (Chs. 16 and 20), to the amendment process (Ch. 23), and to the struggle over federal and state power that this chapter’s compromises set in motion.[^ch12-check-ch13][^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## Summary

- The Articles of Confederation created a league of sovereign states whose Congress could not tax, regulate commerce, or compel obedience, and whose amendment rule let one state block fiscal reform.[^ch12-archives-articles][^ch12-congress-confederation]
- The war debt, paper money, and foreclosures made the Confederation’s weakness concrete, while unpaid veterans and hard-money taxes pressed on farmers in states such as Massachusetts.[^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-abt-shays]
- In January 1787, militia under William Shepard fired cannon into Daniel Shays’s column at the Springfield arsenal, killing several and breaking the rebellion’s main force.[^ch12-shepard-letter][^ch12-abt-shays]
- The movement for a stronger government began before the rebellion, with the Annapolis Convention and Congress’s call, on February 21, 1787, for a convention at Philadelphia.[^ch12-wikipedia-timeline][^ch12-avalon-feb21]
- The Philadelphia Convention replaced the Articles with a bicameral government, resolving large- and small-state conflict through the Great Compromise and bargaining over slavery through the three-fifths, fugitive-slave, and slave-trade clauses.[^ch12-loc-convention][^ch12-nps-slavery-compromise][^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript]
- Federalists argued that an extended republic and separated powers would control faction, while Anti-Federalists such as Mason, “Brutus,” and Henry warned of consolidation and demanded a bill of rights.[^ch12-federalist-10][^ch12-constitutioncenter-mason][^ch12-brutus-ii][^ch12-teachinghistory-henry]
- Ratification was a close, state-by-state contest; the Massachusetts compromise and the promise of amendments secured the key states, and Virginia and New York passed by narrow margins.[^ch12-constitutioncenter-ratification][^ch12-loc-convention]
- Madison’s amendments became the Bill of Rights on December 15, 1791, but the ten amendments bound only the federal government and left slavery untouched.[^ch12-archives-bor][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

## Study Questions

1. Read Shepard’s letter of January 26, 1787, and the American Battlefield Trust’s account of Shays’s Rebellion. Where do they disagree about the size of the forces and the casualties, and what does that tell you about counting an eighteenth-century confrontation?
2. Using the Articles of Confederation’s text, identify three powers Congress lacked and give the article that withholds each one. How did those limits shape the 1780s?
3. Read the three-fifths clause slowly. What exactly does it count, for what two purposes, and why does the chapter argue that it did not define an enslaved person as three-fifths of a human being?
4. Compare Federalist No. 10 with “Brutus” II. Where do Madison and “Brutus” agree about human nature, and where do they part?
5. Read George Mason’s objections beside Patrick Henry’s Virginia speech. What do they fear most: a standing army, a distant government, an aristocracy, or the loss of a bill of rights?
6. The Massachusetts Compromise made ratification possible by recommending amendments. Why did the Federalists agree to it, and what does the bargain reveal about the strength of the Anti-Federalists?
7. Virginia ratified 89–79 and New York 30–27. If either had voted no, what would the new government have looked like? What evidence would you need to answer?
8. Read Article VII of the Constitution and the account of ratification. Why did the framers use state conventions rather than state legislatures?
9. Use Table 1. Which interpretation best explains the making of the Constitution, and what does each position leave unexplained?
10. The contingency question: Shays’s Rebellion is often said to have caused the Constitution. Given that Congress called the Convention on February 21, 1787, before the arsenal fight, how would you state the relationship between the two?
11. Read the Bill of Rights and the account of its adoption. Which two of the twelve proposed amendments were not ratified in 1791, and what happened to them later?
12. Choose one source cited in this chapter. Identify its author, audience, purpose, and silences. What can it establish about the founding, and what can it not?

> *A closing note.* The Constitution answered real problems: a government that could not pay its debts, regulate commerce, or keep order could not hold a union together. But the answer was given by a convention that exceeded its mandate, in a contest that excluded most of the people it claimed to represent, and through bargains that made slavery stronger rather than weaker. The document did not say an enslaved person was three-fifths of a human being; it did something more consequential, counting the enslaved for power while denying them any voice, and promising rights to citizens without touching the bondage that denied the promise to millions. That contradiction, not a single motive, is what the founding left to the republic.[^ch12-archives-constitution-transcript][^ch12-csac-three-fifths][^ch12-waldstreicher-atlantic]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on April 30, 1789, when George Washington took the oath of office on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York and the government the Constitution described began to operate. This chapter has followed the frame as it was written, fought over, and amended; Chapter 13 takes up the machinery once it ran—the fiscal program, the frontier, the Atlantic revolutions, and the birth of party conflict—and asks how a written plan became a working and partisan government.[^ch12-check-ch13]
