# Appendix D. Chronology

*Stage 3 researched outline — November 22, 2026. Page allowance: 6 pages. Appendices. Source: [README](../README.md), Appendix D. This file specifies seven chronology tables, their row designs, candidate entries, source targets, and production checks. It is not a draft of the appendix and does not certify every date it lists.*

## 1. How Appendix D Works

### 1.1 Purpose and reader-facing form

- Appendix D is a selective chronology, not a daybook and not a second narrative. It gives the reader a dated spine for the book’s five through-lines and a way to compare developments that the chapters treat separately.[^app-d-plan]
- The appendix has seven tables. Each table covers one period and uses the same five tracks: politics and law; war and foreign affairs; economy and technology; society and culture; land, environment, and demography.
- A row is a period or a turning point, not a claim that every event in the row shares one cause. The period column carries the row’s dates and a short label. The five track cells carry dated entries selected for their consequence, their prominence in the chapters, or their usefulness as a comparison.
- Entries are compressed to a date, an event, and, only where needed, a result. A cell may hold one to three entries. A full sentence belongs in a chapter, not in a chronology cell.
- An em dash in a cell means that no entry was selected for that track in that row. It does not mean that nothing happened, that the track is unimportant, or that the evidence is absent.
- The appendix is written for a reader who wants to locate a period, check a sequence, or compare tracks. It should remain usable without reading the chapters first, but it does not replace them.[^app-d-plan]
- The appendix has no maps, figures, or images. Its only display items are the seven tables and their captions and source lines.

### 1.2 Numbering, boundaries, and cross-references

- The stable display numbers are App. D, Table 1 through App. D, Table 7. The planning keys `app-d-table-01` through `app-d-table-07` identify the same tables in the README and in production records; they are not reader-facing citations.[^app-d-plan]
- The seven date ranges are inclusive at both ends: deep past–1606; 1607–1699; 1700–1799; 1800–1899; 1900–1945; 1946–2000; 2001–October 29, 2026.
- An event that falls on a boundary year goes in one table only. The 1607 Virginia settlement belongs to Table 2; the 1700 turn belongs to Table 3; 1800 belongs to Table 4; 1900 belongs to Table 5; 1945 belongs to Table 5; 1946 belongs to Table 6; 2001 belongs to Table 7. When an event needs the earlier context, the later table cross-references the earlier table instead of repeating the row.
- The chapter boundaries and the appendix boundaries do not have to coincide. The appendix is a reference grid, while a chapter is an argument with its own opening and closing dates. The reconciliation points are 1551, 1859, 1902, 1910, 1953, and the October 29, 2026, cutoff.[^app-d-plan]
- **1551.** Chapter 2 ends in 1551. Table 1 carries a 1521–1551 row and a 1551–1606 row. The 1551 boundary is a change of emphasis from the first mainland invasions to the Florida, New Mexico, and Roanoke years, not a claim that Spanish expansion stopped.
- **1859.** Chapter 19 ends in 1859. Table 4 carries a 1855–1859 row. Harpers Ferry (October 16–18, 1859) is the last entry in that row; the 1860 election opens the next row.
- **1902.** Chapter 27 ends with the Philippine-American War in 1902. Table 5’s 1900–1903 row carries the war’s formal end and the 1903 Panama intervention, so the reader can see the imperial sequence without a separate chapter boundary.
- **1910.** Chapter 26 ends in 1910 and Chapter 29 begins in the same year. Table 5’s 1910–1914 row owns the date; both chapters cross-reference it rather than each creating a separate 1910 entry.
- **1953.** Chapter 37 carries the early Cold War into 1953, and Chapter 38 begins the Eisenhower years. Table 6’s 1950–1953 row owns the Korean armistice and the end of the Truman presidency; the next row begins with 1954.
- **October 29, 2026.** Table 7 ends on the book’s research cutoff. No event after that date, including the November 2026 election, appears as completed history.[^app-d-cutoff]
- Cross-references inside the appendix use “see Table 4” or “Table 5, 1945 row.” Cross-references to the chapters use the book’s standard form, “Ch. 19, §5.3,” and are checked against the actual section before publication.[^app-d-rules]
- Appendix A and Appendix D use different date conventions for documents. Appendix A dates a document by its adoption or signing; Appendix D records the same event once, at the point where its consequence is clearest, and notes the instrument’s later ratification or entry into force only when the distinction matters. Appendix B’s observation years are data years, not event dates, and are not repeated here.[^app-d-rules]

### 1.3 Tracks, row design, and word budget

- **Politics and law.** Elections, offices, constitutions, statutes, court decisions, administrative acts, and the formal acts of legislatures and executives.
- **War and foreign affairs.** Declared and undeclared wars, campaigns, occupations, alliances, treaties, diplomatic crises, and the use of force.
- **Economy and technology.** Production, finance, credit, transport, communication, energy, labor organization, and technological change.
- **Society and culture.** Migration, religion, education, media, arts, associational life, social movements, public health, and the organization of everyday life.
- **Land, environment, and demography.** Dispossession and treaty cessions, settlement, agriculture, extraction, conservation, disasters, climate, and population change.
- An event is placed in the track of its principal action. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 belongs to politics and law; the sit-ins that produced it belong to society and culture; the enforcement of the act belongs to politics and law again. If an event has two inseparable aspects, one cell carries the event and the other track carries a cross-reference such as “see politics,” not a duplicate full entry.
- The row caps are 10 for Table 1, nine for Table 2, 12 for Table 3, 16 for Table 4, 12 for Table 5, 13 for Table 6, and 10 for Table 7, for 82 rows. The caps are chosen to fit the six-page allowance at a compact reference setting, not to imply that the periods are equal in importance.[^app-d-pages]
- The writing rule is six words per cell where possible, eight at the outside, and never a full sentence. The arithmetic is 82 rows times five cells times about six words, or roughly 2,460 words, plus period labels, captions, and source lines. At the book’s 500-word measure, the tables should occupy about six pages. The rule is a compression target, not a license to drop a necessary date.
- A compressed row would read, for example: “1607–1614 | Virginia Company; Powhatan diplomacy | — | tobacco, glass, iron | Starving Time | Chesapeake drought.” The example illustrates the format; it is not a finished row, and the final wording must come from the cited sources.
- A model caption is: “Table 1. Chronology, deep past–1606: selected turning points in politics and law, war and foreign affairs, economy and technology, society and culture, and land, environment, and demography.” A model source line is: “Selected and compressed from the sources in the combined notes; dates marked ‘c.’ are approximate, and contested dates are identified in the notes.”
- If the finished tables cannot be set legibly within the cap, cut a row or shorten cells before cutting definitions, source lines, or the notes. If the typesetter still cannot fit the tables, request eight pages and update the README budget rather than shrinking the type below the book’s minimum.[^app-d-pages]
- The preferred setting is a matrix with a period column and five track columns, possibly rotated on the page. If the page geometry cannot hold six columns, stack the five tracks under each period as a labeled list. Stacking changes the layout, not the table number or the row content. Do not split one table into two numbered tables.
- Each table has a caption and a source line. The caption names the period and the five tracks; the source line says that the table is a selection compiled from the combined notes and that dates marked “c.” or “disputed” are approximations or contested. The source line does not replace the notes.
- The appendix’s prose is limited to short table introductions and the note on conventions. It does not summarize the chapters or carry a debate.

### 1.4 Date, name, and entry conventions

- Dates through September 2, 1752, are Julian (Old Style); dates from September 14, 1752, are Gregorian (New Style). The appendix does not convert dates or supply dual dates merely to modernize them.[^app-d-rules]
- A January–March date before 1752 that the source records under the old legal year keeps the double year, for example “February 9, 1649/50.” The appendix does not silently modernize it.[^app-d-rules]
- Approximate dates use “c.”; a date known only to a season or year uses the season or year without an invented day. Archaeological dates use BCE and CE. “Years before present” is avoided unless the reference year is stated.[^app-d-rules]
- Date ranges use the en dash: 1607–1699, 1861–65, 1939–45. A hyphen is not used between numbers to mean a range.[^app-d-rules]
- The appendix distinguishes signing, ratification, and entry into force. The Treaty of Paris was signed on September 3, 1783, and ratified by Congress on January 14, 1784; the Louisiana Purchase was signed on April 30, 1803, and the transfer of Upper Louisiana took place on March 9–10, 1804. The chronology uses the date that matches the row’s argument and puts the other date in the note when the distinction matters.
- War dates distinguish the first hostile action, the declaration or authorization, and the peace instrument. The Korean War begins with the North Korean attack on June 25, 1950, not with the American authorization; the armistice of July 27, 1953, is not a peace treaty.
- “First” is defined. “First permanent English settlement” means a settlement that survived; “first European settlement” in North America includes Norse and Spanish sites; “first Africans” in Virginia means the first recorded Africans in the surviving colonial record, not the first Africans in the Americas.[^app-d-rules]
- Names follow the book’s house style: specific Indigenous nations where known, “Native American” and “Indigenous” capitalized, “Black” and “White” capitalized for people, “enslaved people” in the book’s prose, and historical proper names retained.[^app-d-rules]
- Place names use the historical name with the present name in parentheses where useful. A contested name is explained once, at its first appearance, and not reargued in every row.[^app-d-rules]
- Statistics do not belong in chronology cells except where a number is part of the event’s name, such as the Fourteenth Amendment or the 54th Massachusetts. Population, prices, and casualty figures belong in Appendix B or the chapter, with their definitions.[^app-d-rules]
- No event after October 29, 2026, is included. A scheduled event is not a completed event.[^app-d-cutoff]

### 1.5 Source hierarchy and verification status

- Primary and official records come first: the *Statutes at Large*, treaty texts, proclamations, executive orders, court opinions, the *Congressional Record*, census publications, agency statistics, and the records of the National Archives and Records Administration.
- Institutional chronologies come second: the National Park Service, the Library of Congress, the Office of the Historian at the Department of State, the Senate and House historical offices, the Smithsonian Institution, the Federal Reserve, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the National Archives’ milestone-document pages.
- Scholarly monographs and peer-reviewed articles are used for contested dates and for the interpretation of an event’s significance. A tertiary summary may establish that a date is disputed; it does not settle the dispute.
- “Consulted” means that the source was opened and read for this outline. “Discovered” means that the item was located but not read. The notes mark the difference. A date in this outline is a candidate value to be collated before drafting, not a certified table value.
- A note attached to a row identifies the principal source for the row’s anchor event, not every cell. A cell whose date is not covered by that source is a candidate entry and must be sourced separately before drafting. The “Used for” field says which claims the source actually supports; it does not turn the whole row into verified text.
- Several dates are genuinely contested or depend on the instrument chosen. The most important are the Peopling of the Americas; the founding of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy; the first Africans in Virginia; the date of the Mayflower Compact under the Old Style calendar; the identity of the first landfall in 1492; the route of the De Soto expedition; the date of the Declaration’s adoption versus its printing; the Treaty of Paris’s signature versus ratification; the transfer of Louisiana; the start of the Civil War at Fort Sumter; the end of the Civil War in the Trans-Mississippi; the ratification dates of the Reconstruction amendments; the Wounded Knee death toll; the entry into force of the Nineteenth Amendment; the date of the Japanese surrender; the Tonkin Gulf resolution; the Tet Offensive’s start; the Paris Accords; the Iran hostage release; the 2000 election’s resolution; the 2020 election’s certification; the January 6 attack; and every 2025–2026 event in Table 7.[^app-d-date-checks]
- The 2025–2026 rows are the most fragile. They are checked against dated official records where those records exist, but some are still contested, incomplete, or subject to litigation. The appendix does not inherit a date from a news summary without checking the instrument or record.[^app-d-cutoff]
- The notes record what each source can establish and what it cannot. A page that gives a date does not thereby establish the event’s cause, its consequences, or its typicality.

### 1.6 What the chronology must not do

- It must not imply that a date is a cause. The chronology can show that the Stamp Act followed the Seven Years’ War; it cannot show that the war caused the Stamp Act without an argument and evidence.
- It must not treat a period label as a single event. “The Gilded Age,” “the Progressive Era,” “the New Deal,” and “the Cold War” are interpretive labels with contested boundaries.
- It must not let a boundary year duplicate an event. The same event does not appear at the end of one table and the start of the next.
- It must not make 1492 or 1607 the beginning of American history. Table 1 begins in the deep past and treats Indigenous history as history, not as a prologue.
- It must not use “first” without a definition or “discovered” for a place that was already inhabited.
- It must not print a casualty figure, a population, or a price without its source, units, and date. Those belong in Appendix B or the chapter.
- It must not use the chronology to settle a historiographical dispute. A date can be disputed; the table says so.
- It must not turn a scheduled event into a completed one, and it must not extend the record past October 29, 2026.
- It must not use a tertiary encyclopedia as the sole support for a contested date when an official record or a scholarly edition is available.
- It must not let a silent track imply that nothing happened. The em dash is a selection decision, not a finding.

## 2. Table 1. Deep Past–1606

### 2.1 Analytical job and claim

- Table 1 establishes that the continent was inhabited, managed, and connected long before European arrival. It carries the book’s first through-line, land and environment, and its corrective to a contact-centered narrative.[^app-d-plan]
- The table’s claim is that 1607 is an episode in a longer history, not the beginning of American history. The rows move from the peopling of the Americas through Poverty Point, Hopewell, Cahokia, and Chaco to the Norse, Caribbean, and Spanish invasions, and end with the English failure at Roanoke and the 1606 Virginia charter.
- The table also shows that Indigenous political and economic worlds were not static. Urban centers rose and were abandoned; trade networks spanned the continent; agriculture and fire management changed landscapes; and the arrival of Europeans was one shock among many.
- The table must not imply that the deep past is a single migration or a single culture. Archaeologists divide the past into periods for comparison, while Indigenous nations have their own chronologies and histories.[^app-d-native-timeline]

### 2.2 Table design and candidate rows

- **c. 15,000–10,000 BCE — peopling.** Land/environment: Beringia, coastal routes, glacial retreat; society/culture: small mobile bands, kinship; economy/technology: hunting, foraging, fluted points. Politics/law and war/foreign affairs: —. The date is a range, not one migration.[^app-d-deep-history]
- **c. 10,000–3000 BCE — Archaic worlds.** Land/environment: warming, rising seas, river valleys, fire management; society/culture: larger seasonal camps, cemeteries; economy/technology: fishing, nuts, early cultivation, ground stone. Politics/law and war/foreign affairs: —.[^app-d-native-timeline]
- **c. 3000–1000 BCE — Poverty Point.** Land/environment: Mississippi floodplain; society/culture: earthwork labor, ceremonial center; economy/technology: long-distance exchange, stone and copper. Politics/law: —; war/foreign affairs: —.[^app-d-poverty-point]
- **c. 1000 BCE–500 CE — Woodland and Hopewell.** Land/environment: Ohio Valley and Eastern Woodlands; society/culture: Adena and Hopewell mounds, burial, craft; economy/technology: pottery, copper, mica, obsidian exchange; politics/law: emerging chiefdoms. War/foreign affairs: —.[^app-d-native-timeline]
- **c. 500–1200 CE — Mississippian and Chaco florescence.** Land/environment: Mississippi Valley, Four Corners, terraces and irrigation; society/culture: Cahokia, Chaco, urbanism, hierarchy; economy/technology: maize, turquoise, roads, trade; politics/law: regional centers. War/foreign affairs: —.[^app-d-cahokia][^app-d-chaco]
- **c. 1200–1450 CE — abandonment and reorganization.** Land/environment: drought, deforestation, soil exhaustion; society/culture: migrations, new confederacies; economy/technology: —; politics/law: —; war/foreign affairs: —. Cahokia and Chaco are not “lost”; their peoples and descendants moved and reorganized.[^app-d-cahokia][^app-d-chaco]
- **c. 1000–1350 CE — Norse voyages.** War/foreign affairs: Norse expeditions from Greenland; economy/technology: North Atlantic trade; land/environment: L’Anse aux Meadows; politics/law and society/culture: —. The site is evidence of a short-lived encampment, not a permanent colony.[^app-d-lanse]
- **1492–1521 — Caribbean conquest and the Columbian exchange.** Politics/law: Tordesillas line, 1494; war/foreign affairs: conquest and resistance; economy/technology: mining, plantations, livestock; society/culture: epidemic, slavery, mission; land/environment: disease, horses, cattle, weeds. The Tordesillas line was a paper division between two crowns, not a border recognized by Indigenous nations.[^app-d-tordesillas]
- **1521–1551 — mainland invasions.** Politics/law: New Spain and vice-regal rule; war/foreign affairs: De Soto 1539–42, Coronado 1540–42; economy/technology: silver, encomienda, missions; society/culture: mestizaje, mission towns; land/environment: epidemic, livestock, land grants. The routes are reconstructed and disputed.[^app-d-desoto]
- **1551–1606 — Florida, New Mexico, and Roanoke.** Politics/law: St. Augustine 1565, New Mexico 1598, Virginia charter 1606; war/foreign affairs: Spanish-English rivalry; economy/technology: missions, ranching, trade; society/culture: missions, Pueblo resistance; land/environment: settlement, disease, livestock. The 1606 Virginia charter closes this table; the 1607 landing opens Table 2.[^app-d-staugustine][^app-d-roanoke][^app-d-loc-colonial]

### 2.3 Source spine and exact locators

- The archaeological sequence and the caution about periodization come from the National Park Service’s “Deep History & Archeological Periods” and “Timeline of Native American Cultures.”[^app-d-deep-history][^app-d-native-timeline]
- Poverty Point’s dates of roughly 1650–700 BCE, its earthworks, and its exchange network come from the National Park Service’s site article.[^app-d-poverty-point]
- Cahokia’s florescence between about 1000 and 1350 CE, its population range, and Monks Mound come from the National Park Service’s World Heritage article. The population is a range, not a census.[^app-d-cahokia]
- Chaco’s occupation between about 850 and 1250 CE, its height around 1020–1110, and its roads and great houses come from the UNESCO World Heritage description.[^app-d-chaco]
- The Norse site and its UNESCO designation come from Parks Canada. The page says “over 1000 years ago” and does not supply a single year for the encampment.[^app-d-lanse]
- The Tordesillas treaty’s date and terms come from the Avalon Project transcription. The text is a translation; the appendix uses it for the date and the line, not for a philological claim.[^app-d-tordesillas]
- De Soto’s 1539 landing, his death on May 21, 1542, and the expedition’s range come from the National Park Service’s expedition page. The route remains reconstructed.[^app-d-desoto]
- St. Augustine’s 1565 founding comes from the National Park Service’s town-plan page. The page also records the earlier French settlement at Fort Caroline, which the Spanish destroyed.[^app-d-staugustine]
- The 1587 Roanoke expedition and the “CROATOAN” evidence come from the National Park Service’s “1587: The Lost Colony” page. The word “lost” is a later label, not a contemporary description.[^app-d-roanoke]
- The 1606 Virginia Company expedition and the 1607 settlement come from the Library of Congress’s colonial overview and the National Park Service’s Jamestown comparison. The Library of Congress dates the London Company’s expedition to December 20, 1606.[^app-d-loc-colonial][^app-d-jamestown]

## 3. Table 2. 1607–1699

### 3.1 Analytical job and claim

- Table 2 covers the formation of English, Dutch, and French colonial societies inside Indigenous homelands. It carries the book’s through-lines of land and environment, liberty and unfreedom, and pluralism and belonging.[^app-d-plan]
- The table’s claim is that the seventeenth century is not a prelude to the Revolution. It is the period in which racial slavery, colonial law, and Indigenous dispossession became institutions, and in which the first sustained critiques of slavery appeared.
- The table also shows that the colonies were not a single society. Virginia’s tobacco economy, New England’s family farms and towns, New Netherland’s fur trade, Carolina’s plantation frontier, and Pennsylvania’s proprietary experiment produced different labor systems and different relations with Indigenous nations.
- The table must not treat “colonial America” as English only. It includes Dutch, Swedish, French, and Spanish presence where those powers shaped the English colonies, and it names the Indigenous nations whose land and diplomacy made the colonies possible.

### 3.2 Table design and candidate rows

- **1607–1614 — Jamestown and the Powhatan world.** Politics/law: Virginia Company, 1606 charter, 1607 landing; war/foreign affairs: Powhatan diplomacy and conflict; economy/technology: tobacco, glass, iron; society/culture: Starving Time, 1610; land/environment: Chesapeake, 1609–10 drought. The “first permanent English settlement” claim is a definition, not a natural fact.[^app-d-jamestown][^app-d-loc-colonial]
- **1619–1624 — assemblies, Africans, and the first New England colony.** Politics/law: House of Burgesses, 1619; war/foreign affairs: 1622 Powhatan uprising; economy/technology: tobacco boom; society/culture: first documented Africans, 1619; land/environment: Virginia becomes a royal colony, 1624. The Africans’ legal status is disputed.[^app-d-first-africans][^app-d-jamestown]
- **1625–1640 — Massachusetts Bay and Pequot War.** Politics/law: Massachusetts Bay charter, 1629; war/foreign affairs: Pequot War, 1637; economy/technology: fur, fish, shipbuilding; society/culture: Great Migration, 1630–40; land/environment: New England town settlement. The Pequot War was a war of dispossession, not a frontier skirmish.[^app-d-rhode-island][^app-d-jamestown]
- **1640–1660 — English Civil War and Atlantic slavery.** Politics/law: English Civil War, 1642; war/foreign affairs: —; economy/technology: Barbados sugar, enslaved labor; society/culture: Maryland Toleration Act, 1649; land/environment: Navigation Acts, 1651. The Toleration Act protected Trinitarian Christians, not religious liberty in general.[^app-d-maryland][^app-d-rhode-island]
- **1660–1674 — Restoration empires.** Politics/law: Restoration, 1660; war/foreign affairs: English seizure of New Netherland, 1664; economy/technology: mercantilism, fur trade; society/culture: Carolina charter, 1663; land/environment: New York, Hudson Valley. The Dutch surrender was negotiated; it was not a clean transfer of a unified colony.[^app-d-new-netherland]
- **1675–1678 — King Philip’s War and Bacon’s Rebellion.** Politics/law: —; war/foreign affairs: King Philip’s War, 1675–76; economy/technology: —; society/culture: Bacon’s Rebellion, 1676; land/environment: frontier dispossession. The two conflicts were distinct, but both exposed the fragility of colonial authority.[^app-d-king-philip][^app-d-bacon]
- **1679–1689 — Pennsylvania and the Dominion.** Politics/law: Pennsylvania charter, 1681; war/foreign affairs: —; economy/technology: —; society/culture: Germantown Quaker protest, 1688; land/environment: Dominion of New England, 1686. The Germantown protest is the first recorded North American petition against slavery, not a successful movement.[^app-d-penn][^app-d-germantown]
- **1689–1697 — revolution, war, and Salem.** Politics/law: Glorious Revolution, 1688–89; war/foreign affairs: King William’s War, 1689–97; economy/technology: —; society/culture: Salem witch trials, 1692; land/environment: —. The Salem trials were a product of local conflict and legal procedure, not a simple outbreak of superstition.[^app-d-rhode-island][^app-d-salem]
- **1697–1699 — settlement and regulation.** Politics/law: Treaty of Ryswick, 1697; war/foreign affairs: —; economy/technology: Wool Act, 1699; society/culture: —; land/environment: royalization and border disputes. The Wool Act is an imperial regulation, not a colonial law.[^app-d-wool-act]

### 3.3 Source spine and exact locators

- The Virginia and Plymouth sequence, including the 1606 charter, the 1607 landing, the 1619 assembly, and the 1622 uprising, comes from the Library of Congress’s colonial overview and the National Park Service’s Jamestown comparison.[^app-d-loc-colonial][^app-d-jamestown]
- The first documented Africans in Virginia are recorded on the National Park Service’s “First Africans in Virginia” marker page. The page gives 1619, Point Comfort, a Dutch warship, and “twenty and odd” Africans. It does not settle their legal status, and the table must not either.[^app-d-first-africans]
- The Massachusetts Bay, Providence, Rhode Island, and New England chronology comes from the National Park Service’s Roger Williams timeline. It gives 1620 for the Mayflower, 1630 for the Massachusetts Bay settlement, 1636 for Providence, 1638 for Anne Hutchinson’s Portsmouth, 1642 for the English Civil War, 1651 for the first Navigation Act, 1663 for the Rhode Island charter, 1675 for King Philip’s War, 1686 for the Dominion of New England, 1689 for the Glorious Revolution, and 1692 for the Salem trials.[^app-d-rhode-island]
- The Maryland Toleration Act’s date and text come from the Maryland State Archives. The act is “An Act Concerning Religion,” April 21, 1649; it protected belief in the Trinity and did not extend toleration to all faiths.[^app-d-maryland]
- The New Netherland surrender in 1664 comes from the National Park Service’s “The Rise and Fall of New Netherland.” The page says Stuyvesant surrendered without a shot; the table uses the date and does not treat the surrender as a transfer of an intact Dutch society.[^app-d-new-netherland]
- King Philip’s War’s outbreak in June 1675, the Great Swamp fight in December 1675, and Metacom’s death in 1676 come from the National Park Service’s Roger Williams pages.[^app-d-king-philip]
- Bacon’s Rebellion’s burning of Jamestown on September 19, 1676, comes from the National Park Service’s Historic Jamestowne page.[^app-d-bacon]
- Pennsylvania’s 1681 charter comes from the Pennsylvania Historical and Museum Commission’s page on Penn’s charter. The page is a state historical account; the charter itself is the authority.[^app-d-penn]
- The Salem trials’ 1692 date and the museum’s collections come from the Peabody Essex Museum’s account. The page does not give the death toll; the count of the executed varies in the literature, and the table should print the range or a court-record figure with its definition.[^app-d-salem]
- The Germantown protest is a candidate entry, not a verified one. The 1688 petition is widely dated to April 18, 1688; the table should use a scholarly edition or the surviving manuscript, not a general timeline.[^app-d-germantown]

### 3.4 Open checks before drafting

- Editorial check — decide how to describe the first Africans’ status. The surviving record does not use “slave” for the 1619 arrivals, and the legal transition from servitude to racial slavery was gradual. ## 4. Table 3. 1700–1799

- Table 3 covers the consolidation of colonial societies, the great imperial wars, the American Revolution, and the constitutional settlement. It carries all five through-lines, with the Revolution as the hinge.[^app-d-plan]

## 6. Table 5. 1900–1945

### 6.1 Analytical job and claim

### 9.1 Boundary concordance

- The boundary years are 1606/1607, 1699/1700, 1799/1800, 1899/1900, 1945/1946, and 2000/2001. Each boundary has one owner and one cross-reference:

  - 1945: the end of the Second World War is in Table 5; Table 6 begins in 1946.
  - 2001: September 11 is in Table 7; Table 6 ends in 2000.
- The chapter/appendix reconciliation points are 1551, 1859, 1902, 1910, 1953, and October 29, 2026. Each is checked against the actual chapter section before publication.[^app-d-boundary-checks]
- The appendix’s dates must agree with Appendix A’s document dates and Appendix B’s observation years. A document’s adoption date is not always the event date; a data year is not always the policy date.[^app-d-boundary-checks]
- The appendix’s dates must agree with Appendix C’s map dates. A map that shows a boundary in 1763 cannot use a 1774 boundary without saying so.[^app-d-boundary-checks]

### 9.2 Recurring period labels

- “Colonial America” is not a single period. The appendix uses 1607–1699 for the seventeenth century and 1700–1799 for the eighteenth, while the chapters use their own arguments.
- “Revolution” can mean 1763–1783 (the imperial crisis and war) or 1775–1783 (the war). The table uses 1763–1783 for the crisis and 1775–1783 for the war, and says which is which.
- “Civil War” is 1861–65. The table uses April 1861 for the start and April–June 1865 for the end, with the Trans-Mississippi surrender noted.
- “Reconstruction” is usually 1865–77. The table uses 1865–1870 for the amendments and 1871–1883 for the industrial order; the period label is not a single row.
- “Gilded Age,” “Progressive Era,” “New Deal,” “Cold War,” “War on Terror,” and “Great Recession” are interpretive labels with contested boundaries. The table uses them in captions only when the dates are also given.
- “The present” is not a period. Table 7 ends at the cutoff, and the notes say what remains unsettled.[^app-d-boundary-checks]

### 9.3 Statistics, tables, and source lines

- The chronology does not print population, prices, casualty figures, or election percentages. Those belong in Appendix B or the chapter, with their definitions.
- If a number is part of an event’s name (the Fourteenth Amendment, the 54th Massachusetts, the 19th Amendment), the table prints the name and does not add a statistic.
- Each table’s source line names the principal source families and points to the combined notes. It does not list every source in the cell.
- Each table’s caption says “selected events” and names the five tracks. A caption that says “complete chronology” is false.[^app-d-table-format]
- The appendix’s tables are not images; they do not need alt text. They do need captions, source lines, and textual references from the appendix’s prose.[^app-d-table-format]

### 9.4 Page budget and production

- The allowance is six text pages, or about 3,000 words at the book’s 500-word measure. The row cap and the six-word cell rule are designed to fit that allowance.[^app-d-pages]
- The arithmetic is 82 rows times five cells times about six words, or roughly 2,460 words, plus 82 period labels, seven captions, seven source lines, and short introductions. The total is about 2,900–3,100 words.
- If the typeset table cannot be read at the book’s minimum type size, request eight pages and update the README budget. Do not solve a layout problem by deleting definitions or sources.
- The appendix has no images and no maps. It does not need an image-rights review. It does need a table-format review, a caption review, and a source-line review.
- The production sequence is: freeze the row list; verify every date; write the cells; set the tables; check the page count; check the cross-references; then reconcile the README register and the chapter dates.[^app-d-table-format]
