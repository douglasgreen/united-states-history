## Epigraphs

> “The threat is nearly invisible in ordinary ways. It is a crisis of confidence. It is a crisis that strikes at the very heart and soul and spirit of our national will.”[^ch43-carter-confidence]

— Jimmy Carter, address to the nation on energy and national goals, July 15, 1979.[^ch43-carter-confidence]

> “Preachers are not called to be politicians, but soul winners. …”[^ch43-falwell-pbs]

— Jerry Falwell, sermon “Ministers and Marches,” 1964, a position he later reversed as he organized the Religious Right.[^ch43-falwell-pbs]

> “The Seventies … marked a great shift in American culture, society, and politics, the moment when the postwar order gave way to the world we now inhabit.”[^ch43-schulman-seventies]

— Bruce J. Schulman, *The Seventies: The Great Shift in American Culture, Society, and Politics* (2001), paraphrase of the book’s argument; exact sentence to be collated.[^ch43-schulman-seventies]

- **Why these three.** Carter states the diagnosis he offered the nation: that its deepest problem was a loss of confidence and purpose. Falwell supplies the voice of a movement that rejected that diagnosis and located the problem in a secular, permissive culture it meant to reclaim; his 1964 line is the position he abandoned, which is precisely why it belongs here. Schulman argues that the decade’s meaning is structural — the passing of the postwar order — rather than a matter of mood. The three disagree about what the nation’s problem was, whether it was spiritual or economic, and whether the decade was a decline or a hinge.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-schulman-seventies]
- **The attribution rule.** Carter’s sentence is quoted from the American Presidency Project’s transcript of the July 15, 1979, address and must be collated against the *Public Papers of the Presidents of the United States: Jimmy Carter, 1979*, Book II. Falwell’s 1964 sentence is quoted from the PBS *God in America* profile and must be collated against the sermon text or a documentary edition before publication. Schulman’s book was identified through publisher and course records and was not read for this outline; a sentence must be collated from the published text or the epigraph must be replaced. Do not quote an unread book.[^ch43-check-epigraphs][^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-schulman-seventies]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date, the time, and the occasion.** Carter delivered the address at 10 p.m. on July 15, 1979, from the Oval Office; it was broadcast live on radio and television. He began by noting that exactly three years earlier, on July 15, 1976, he had accepted his party’s nomination for the presidency.[^ch43-carter-confidence]
- **The ten-day preparation.** Carter told the audience that ten days earlier he had planned yet another speech on energy, and that he had instead “decided to reach out and listen to the voices of America,” inviting to Camp David “people from almost every segment of our society — business and labor, teachers and preachers, Governors, mayors, and private citizens,” and then traveling to listen to others. The Washington Post described the same period as an “extraordinary 10-day ‘domestic summit conference’ at Camp David.”[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-wapo-confidence]
- **The speech’s own diagnosis.** Carter said the nation’s “true problems” were “deeper than gasoline lines or energy shortages, deeper even than inflation or recession,” and that the country faced a “crisis of confidence” visible in “the growing doubt about the meaning of our own lives and in the loss of a unity of purpose.” He cited “paralysis and stagnation and drift,” a majority believing the next five years would be worse than the last, two-thirds not voting, declining productivity, and “a growing disrespect for government and for churches and for schools, the news media, and other institutions.”[^ch43-carter-confidence]
- **The energy program.** In the same address Carter set a goal that the nation would never use more foreign oil than in 1977, announced import quotas for 1979 and 1980, proposed an “energy security corporation” and a “solar bank,” asked Congress to mandate that utilities cut oil use by half within a decade, proposed a conservation program and standby gasoline rationing, and asked for a windfall profits tax.[^ch43-carter-confidence]
- **The aftermath.** The address was, by the account of Carter’s own speechwriter Hendrik Hertzberg, initially a popular success, and Carter never used the word “malaise” in it; the label was applied afterward. Carter’s domestic record in these months also included the April 18, 1977, energy address he called the “moral equivalent of war” and, in July 1979, a cabinet reshuffle in which he replaced several cabinet officers and asked for the resignations of his Cabinet.[^ch43-newyorker-malaise][^ch43-miller-carter-keys][^ch43-fed-volcker]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the setting, the broadcast, the preparation method Carter described, the text of the speech, the energy proposals, and the later reputation of the address. It cannot show what every listener felt, how representative the Camp David guests were, or that the speech “caused” any later political outcome. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-newyorker-malaise][^ch43-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In July 1979, no one in the White House knew that the hostages would be taken in November, that the Soviets would invade Afghanistan in December, or that Carter would lose in 1980. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch43-check-scene][^ch43-check-dates-appD]

## 1. Energy, inflation, and deindustrialization (1973–1980)

### 1.1 The claim: three supports of the postwar boom weakened at once

- **The claim.** The long postwar expansion had rested on cheap energy, rising productivity, and the dollar’s fixed value; in the 1970s all three eroded together, producing “stagflation” — high inflation alongside high unemployment — that confounded the policy tools of the postwar settlement.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-bls-cpi][^ch43-fed-volcker]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the oil shock of 1973–74, which Chapter 42 owns at its onset, through the second oil shock that followed the Iranian Revolution, the inflation of 1979–80, the Federal Reserve’s turn in October 1979, and the deindustrialization of the old industrial belt, including the closing of the Youngstown mills in 1977.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-belt-youngstown]

### 1.2 The second oil shock and the energy crisis

- **The shock.** Oil prices began to rise rapidly in mid-1979 and more than doubled between April 1979 and April 1980. Iranian oil output declined by 4.8 million barrels per day — about 7 percent of world production — by January 1979, as the revolution ended the reign of Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi; the Federal Reserve’s history argues that fear of further disruption and speculative demand may have mattered more than the lost barrels.[^ch43-fed-oil1979]
- **The gasoline lines.** Carter’s July 1979 address named the “long lines which have made millions of you spend aggravating hours waiting for gasoline” and called the dependence on imported oil “the direct cause” of “the increased inflation and unemployment that we now face.”[^ch43-carter-confidence]
- **The nuclear accident.** On March 28, 1979, the Unit 2 reactor at Three Mile Island, near Middletown, Pennsylvania, suffered a partial meltdown — the most serious accident in United States commercial nuclear power history, though its small radioactive releases were judged to have had no detectable health effects on workers or the public. The accident deepened public unease about energy policy in the same spring as the gasoline lines.[^ch43-nrc-tmi]
- **The caution.** The chapter should distinguish the physical supply disruption, the speculative price response, and the political reaction, and should not treat any single cause as the whole explanation.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-check-numbers]

### 1.3 Inflation and the productivity slowdown

- **The inflation series.** Consumer inflation had already begun to accelerate, rising from below 5 percent in early 1976 to nearly 7 percent by March 1979; by July 1979 it was running at about 9 percent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics records the largest 12-month increase of the era as March 1979–March 1980, at 14.8 percent.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-bls-cpi]
- **The productivity puzzle.** The era’s growth in output per hour slowed sharply by comparison with the quarter-century after 1948; one widely cited estimate puts the fall in nonmanufacturing labor-productivity growth from about 2.95 percent a year in 1948–72 to about 1.29 percent a year in 1972–96. The causes remain disputed and the chapter should present the slowdown as an estimate with competing explanations.[^ch43-gordon-productivity][^ch43-check-numbers]
- **The measurement caution.** Give each figure with its index, base years, units, and the population or sector it describes; the personal consumption expenditure index and the consumer price index are not interchangeable, and a 12-month change is not an annual average.[^ch43-check-numbers]

### 1.4 The Volcker turn

- **The appointment.** In July 1979, as part of a cabinet reshuffle, Carter moved Federal Reserve Chairman G. William Miller to the Treasury and nominated Paul A. Volcker to replace him; inflation was then running at about 9 percent, and Volcker pledged to make fighting inflation his top priority.[^ch43-fed-volcker]
- **The October reform.** In October 1979 the Federal Open Market Committee announced new operating procedures aimed at controlling the growth of money and bank reserves rather than targeting the federal funds rate directly — a change that led to sharply higher interest rates. The reform is often dated to the weekend of October 6, 1979.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-sf-fed-1979]
- **The consequence and the framing.** The full effect — the recession of the early 1980s and the eventual disinflation — belongs chiefly to Chapter 44; this chapter should present the turn as a decision whose costs and outcome were not yet known in 1979, and should not attribute to it knowledge of the later result.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-dates-appD]

### 1.5 Deindustrialization: Map 34

- **Youngstown.** On September 19, 1977, Youngstown Sheet & Tube announced it was closing its Campbell Works, putting about 5,000 people out of work; within five years roughly 50,000 jobs disappeared from the Mahoning Valley. Local accounts call the day “Black Monday,” and the chapter should present the closing as one documented instance of a wider regional change rather than the whole of it.[^ch43-belt-youngstown]
- **The map.** Map 34, “Where the work went, 1969–1979,” is the registered visual for this section. It should show where employment and population moved, distinguishing absolute employment change, percentage change, and population change so that the reader cannot mistake a small-count area with high percentage growth for the largest gain, or decline for stasis.[^ch43-check-map34]
- **The map’s limits.** The map describes regional outcomes; it cannot establish why any plant closed, attribute motive to any firm, or prove that deindustrialization alone caused the politics that followed. Its source task is County Business Patterns supported by App. B, Table 7, and the chapter should present it as a description of change, not a causal claim.[^ch43-check-map34][^ch43-check-numbers]
- **The human dimension and the silence.** The record is fuller for firms, unions, and officials than for the workers who lost their jobs and the communities that absorbed the loss; the chapter should name that asymmetry rather than speak for the displaced.[^ch43-check-silences]

## 2. Cities, taxpayers, and Proposition 13 (1975–1979)

### 2.1 The claim: the fiscal crisis moved from Washington to the cities and the voters

- **The claim.** As inflation and slow growth squeezed public budgets, the fiscal crisis of the mid-1970s was fought out at the level of cities and states, and the taxpayer revolts that followed rewrote the terms of urban governance and public finance.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-ppic-prop13]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the near-default of New York City in 1975 through the creation of state oversight machinery, to the California ballot measure of June 1978 and the wider taxpayer reaction, with the In Focus on the text of Article XIII A.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-prop13-art13a]

### 2.2 The New York City fiscal crisis

- **The strain.** New York City’s finances had come under increasing strain from rising demand for social programs, job loss, middle-class flight to the suburbs, and heavy reliance on short-term borrowing; in 1975 the strain became a crisis when the Urban Development Corporation defaulted on some of its bonds and the city’s own bonds came under scrutiny.[^ch43-nys-fcb]
- **The state response.** In April 1975 the state advanced $800 million to keep the city operating; the Legislature created the Municipal Assistance Corporation in June 1975, and in September 1975 passed the Financial Emergency Act, creating the Emergency Financial Control Board, which took control of the city’s finances and would not release money without approving the city’s budget.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-fcb-about]
- **The federal reversal.** In December 1975 President Gerald R. Ford signed the Seasonal Financing Act, authorizing the Treasury to make almost $6 billion available to the city over the next two and a half years — after first having signaled his refusal to bail out the city, a refusal captured in the *New York Daily News* headline “Ford to City: Drop Dead,” which ran on October 30, 1975. The chapter should verify the headline’s exact date and wording against the newspaper’s own archive.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-check-nyc-headline]
- **The caution.** The chapter should present the crisis as the product of the city’s revenue structure, its borrowing practices, its service obligations, and the national economy, not as evidence that municipal government was simply profligate.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 In Focus: the text of Article XIII A (Proposition 13)

- **The object.** The In Focus reads the adopted text of Article XIII A of the California Constitution, the constitutional amendment voters approved as Proposition 13 on June 6, 1978, with about 65 percent support.[^ch43-prop13-art13a][^ch43-ppic-prop13]
- **The close reading.** Section 1(a) provides: “The maximum amount of any ad valorem tax on real property shall not exceed One percent (1%) of the full cash value of such property.” Section 1(b) exempts from that limit indebtedness and bonded indebtedness in specified cases. Other sections require two-thirds voter approval for special taxes and reassess property on a change of ownership, with the base-year value increasing by no more than 2 percent a year. The box should read these clauses as a set of limits on the power to tax, and should connect each clause to its documented consequence.[^ch43-prop13-art13a][^ch43-prop13-sierra]
- **The distinction the box must make.** It must distinguish the initiative as a political act from the constitutional text as a legal instrument, and it must not repeat the draft’s unsupported claim that the full measure was only 150 words; the adopted article is longer than that, and Section 1 alone contains multiple subdivisions. Verify the final adopted text against the official California Constitution before quoting.[^ch43-check-focus][^ch43-check-prop13-words]
- **The rights and register record.** The text is public law and freely available, but a reproduction of a facsimile, ballot, or newspaper page would still need an exact object locator, caption, source line, and rights record. The README lists no image for this chapter, so the box is a textual close reading unless such a reproduction is registered.[^ch43-check-focus][^ch43-check-rights]

### 2.4 The taxpayer revolt beyond California

- **The wider reaction.** Proposition 13 was the best-known of a series of tax-limitation measures; Massachusetts voters approved Proposition 2½ in 1980. The chapter should present the California measure as one case within a national movement whose scope, timing, and causes require their own evidence.[^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-check-debate-works]
- **The consequence.** The limits shifted the cost of local services and reduced the growth of the property tax base, with effects that fell unevenly on schools, cities, and the residents who depended on them. The chapter should describe those effects from evidence, not as a foregone conclusion.[^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-check-numbers]

## 3. Helsinki, Saigon, and détente’s critics (1973–1979)

### 3.1 The claim: détente was both an achievement and a provocation

- **The claim.** Détente’s architecture — arms control, the Helsinki framework, and the partial opening to China — produced real agreements, but it also gave its critics a target, and by 1979 the critics had the initiative in the Senate and in the emerging foreign-policy debate.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-state-salt2]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the Helsinki Final Act of 1975 through the fall of Saigon and its aftermath, to the Committee on the Present Danger and the SALT II agreement of June 1979.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-state-saigon][^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-state-salt2]

### 3.2 The Helsinki Final Act

- **The agreement.** The Helsinki Final Act was signed by 35 nations on August 1, 1975, concluding the Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe. It was not a treaty and not legally binding; it dealt with its subjects in four “baskets,” the first on political and security principles, the second on economic and trade relations, the third on human rights and freedom of movement, and the fourth on procedures for future meetings.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-ford-helsinki]
- **The stake.** The Soviet Union sought recognition of the postwar borders in Eastern Europe; Western governments accepted the borders in exchange for the human-rights and freedom-of-movement language of Basket III. Gerald R. Ford later cited the accords among his greatest foreign-policy achievements, and dissidents and monitoring groups used Basket III in the years that followed.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-ford-helsinki]
- **The caution.** The chapter should present the accords as an ambiguous instrument: it neither ended the Cold War nor was simply a defeat, and its consequences were uneven across the signatory states.[^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 3.3 The fall of Saigon and the war’s end

- **The end of the war.** The Paris settlement of January 1973 belongs to Chapter 42; the war continued, and North Vietnamese forces took Saigon on April 30, 1975, ending the Republic of Vietnam. The chapter should mark the fall as the close of the American war and as a shock to the postwar claim that American power could shape outcomes abroad at will.[^ch43-state-saigon][^ch43-check-ch42]
- **The aftermath.** The May 1975 *Mayagüez* incident and the evacuation of Americans and Vietnamese allies fall in this period; the chapter should verify the dates and figures from official histories before asserting them, and should name the Vietnamese allies and refugees whose fate the record often leaves to the margin.[^ch43-state-saigon][^ch43-check-silences]

### 3.4 Détente’s critics and the Committee on the Present Danger

- **The critics.** The Committee on the Present Danger was formed in 1976, announcing itself soon after the presidential election of that year, to promote a strong defense policy. Its creation was prompted especially by skepticism about the arms-control negotiations of the détente era, and its founders included former senior officials such as Paul H. Nitze and Eugene V. Rostow.[^ch43-cpd-oac]
- **The intellectual contest.** The “Team B” exercise of 1976, in which outside analysts were asked to produce an alternative assessment of Soviet strategic objectives, gave the critics an official venue for their claim that the Soviet Union was seeking strategic superiority; the chapter should present the contest as one about the interpretation of evidence, not only about policy preference.[^ch43-team-b-frus][^ch43-cpd-oac]
- **The caution.** The chapter should state the critics’ argument at its strongest — that verified arms control required a defensible military balance and that the Soviet buildup was real — before testing it against the record.[^ch43-cpd-oac][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 SALT II and the limit of détente

- **The agreement.** Carter and Soviet General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev signed the SALT II treaty in Vienna on June 18, 1979. It capped strategic nuclear delivery vehicles and MIRVed systems and continued the framework begun at Vladivostok in 1974, but it did not achieve the deep reductions some had sought.[^ch43-state-salt2]
- **The unratified treaty.** Carter transmitted the treaty to the Senate, but after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 the Senate chose not to ratify it, and Carter withdrew it from consideration on January 2, 1980. The chapter should present ratification as a decision taken within the changed international circumstances, not as a verdict on the treaty’s merits.[^ch43-state-salt2][^ch43-politico-salt2][^ch43-check-ch44]

## 4. Ford, Carter, Camp David, Panama, Iran, and Afghanistan (1974–1980)

### 4.1 The claim: notable settlements in a constrained presidency

- **The claim.** The Ford and Carter presidencies produced lasting diplomatic settlements — the Egypt-Israel treaty, the Panama Canal treaties, and the SALT II framework — while appearing unable to control energy prices, inflation, or events abroad, and the gap between the diplomatic record and the political perception is the section’s subject.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
- **The structure.** The section runs from Ford’s succession and the 1976 election through Carter’s human-rights diplomacy, Camp David and the Panama treaties, the Iranian Revolution and the hostage crisis, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.[^ch43-miller-carter-keys][^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-iran-hostages][^ch43-state-afghanistan]

### 4.2 Ford and the interregnum

- **The succession.** Gerald R. Ford became president in August 1974 and pardoned Richard M. Nixon in September 1974, both events owned by Chapter 42; this chapter takes up the Ford presidency as a period of transition, including the “Whip Inflation Now” campaign, the Helsinki signing, the New York fiscal crisis, and the 1976 election, in which Carter narrowly defeated Ford.[^ch43-check-ch42][^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-miller-carter-keys]
- **The caution.** The chapter should not present the Ford years as a mere interval; the Helsinki accords, the fiscal-crisis response, and the 1976 campaign set terms Carter inherited.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-nys-fcb]

### 4.3 Carter, human rights, and the Panama Canal treaties

- **The foreign-policy frame.** Carter took office in January 1977 promising a foreign policy that emphasized human rights; he pardoned Vietnam War draft evaders, wrote in support of the Soviet dissident Andrei Sakharov, and spoke at Notre Dame in May 1977 of a new direction that put fundamental human rights ahead of anti-communism.[^ch43-miller-carter-keys]
- **The Panama treaties.** Carter and Panama’s Brigadier General Omar Torrijos signed two canal treaties on September 7, 1977. The Senate ratified the Neutrality Treaty on March 16, 1978, by a vote of 68 to 32, and the Panama Canal Treaty on April 18, 1978, by the same margin; the treaties entered into force on October 1, 1979, and provided for Panamanian control of the canal by the end of 1999.[^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-un-panama]
- **The caution.** The treaty fight was a domestic political ordeal as well as a diplomatic settlement; the chapter should present both the agreement and the opposition it provoked.[^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 4.4 Camp David and the Egypt-Israel treaty

- **The summit.** After Anwar Sadat’s November 1977 announcement that he would go to Jerusalem and the deadlock that followed, Carter called a summit at Camp David, which met from September 5 to 17, 1978. It produced two “Framework” documents rather than a formal peace agreement, laying out the principles of an Egyptian-Israeli peace and a formula for Palestinian self-government in Gaza and the West Bank.[^ch43-state-campdavid]
- **The treaty.** After further negotiations, including Carter’s March 1979 trip to Israel, the Egyptian-Israeli Peace Treaty was formally signed on March 26, 1979. The State Department’s milestone dates the signing to March 26; other accounts give March 29. The discrepancy must be resolved against the treaty text and the department’s own record before a date is asserted.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-check-campdavid-date]
- **The caution.** The chapter should present Camp David as a genuine achievement that left the Palestinian question unresolved, and should not treat the treaty as a comprehensive Middle East settlement.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 Iran: from the “island of stability” to the hostage crisis

- **The revolution.** The Iranian Revolution toppled the Shah, whose regime Carter had visited and described in December 1977 as “an island of stability” in the Middle East; by early 1979 the Shah had fallen and Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini’s Islamic republic was taking shape, and the disruption helped drive the second oil shock.[^ch43-miller-carter-keys][^ch43-fed-oil1979]
- **The seizure.** On November 4, 1979, Iranian students seized the United States embassy in Tehran and detained more than 50 Americans, from the chargé d’affaires to junior staff; the State Department records that the diplomats were held for **444 days**. The crisis dominated the news, made the administration look weak, and sapped its vitality.[^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
- **The frame.** The chapter owns the seizure and the first year of the crisis; the release in January 1981 and therefore the full 444-day span read as a completed episode belong to Chapter 44. The chapter should present the crisis as it appeared to Americans in 1979–80, without the knowledge of its ending.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-dates-appD]

### 4.6 Afghanistan and the end of détente

- **The invasion.** At the end of December 1979 the Soviet Union sent thousands of troops into Afghanistan and assumed control of Kabul and large portions of the country, beginning a decade-long war and marking the only time the Soviet Union invaded a country outside the Eastern Bloc.[^ch43-state-afghanistan]
- **The American response.** In an address to the nation on January 4, 1980, Carter said that “fifty thousand heavily armed Soviet troops have crossed the border” and “are now dispersed throughout Afghanistan,” called the invasion “an extremely serious threat to peace,” and linked it to the ongoing hostage crisis. The administration responded with measures including a grain embargo and a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, and the invasion led Carter to withdraw SALT II from the Senate.[^ch43-app-afghanistan-address][^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-politico-salt2]
- **The caution.** The chapter should present the invasion and the American response as events whose consequences — the longer Afghan war, the renewed Cold War, the eventual Soviet withdrawal — were not known in 1980.[^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-check-dates-appD]

### 4.7 The constrained presidency and the 1980 campaign

- **The frame.** By the spring of 1980 the administration faced inflation, the unresolved hostage crisis, and the political challenge of the coming campaign; the chapter ends on the eve of that contest, not on its result.[^ch43-check-scope][^ch43-check-ch44]
- **The hand-off.** The bridge names Chapter 44’s planned first scene, Reagan’s July 17, 1980, acceptance of the Republican nomination, and must not import the wording of Reagan’s 1981 inaugural address.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-bridge]

## 5. Rights, the ERA struggle, evangelical organizing, and the New Right (1972–1982)

### 5.1 The claim: rights widened and a countermovement organized

- **The claim.** The same decade that widened rights for women and gay people produced an organized countermovement that fused religious conviction with a political program and reshaped the Republican coalition; the gains and the backlash are two parts of one story.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-falwell-pbs]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1972 ERA passage, owned by Chapter 42, through the ratification struggle and the 1977 National Women’s Conference, the gay-rights advances and Harvey Milk’s career and murder, the founding of the Moral Majority, and the Supreme Court’s reinstatement of the death penalty in *Gregg v. Georgia*.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-justia-gregg]

### 5.2 The Equal Rights Amendment and its defeat

- **The amendment.** The proposed Equal Rights Amendment was fifty-one words long; it was first introduced in 1923 in a form suggested by Alice Paul, and Congress passed it in March 1972 and sent it to the states. The ratification struggle, the extension, and the defeat belong to this chapter.[^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-check-ch42]
- **The campaign.** Phyllis Schlafly, a conservative activist and author, founded the Eagle Forum in 1972 and led the STOP ERA campaign, arguing that the amendment would abolish protections for women and threaten the family. She helped organize a counter-rally to the 1977 National Women’s Conference in Houston, which had infused the ratification effort with new energy.[^ch43-nwhm-schlafly][^ch43-loc-era]
- **The defeat.** The original deadline was extended to 1982, but no new states ratified during the extension, and the amendment failed, three states short. On the night of June 30, 1982, supporters held vigils, while opponents celebrated what Schlafly called “a great victory for women” and called for “a mighty movement” to “set America on the right path.”[^ch43-loc-era]
- **The caution.** The chapter should present the ERA fight as a contest between two organized camps of women, not as a simple story of progress delayed, and should give the anti-ERA argument at its strongest before answering it.[^ch43-check-debate-works][^ch43-nwhm-schlafly]

### 5.3 Gay rights, Harvey Milk, and the Briggs Initiative

- **Milk’s election.** Harvey Milk, born in 1930, was elected to the San Francisco Board of Supervisors in 1977 and became the first openly gay elected official in California. He was assassinated on November 27, 1978, together with Mayor George Moscone, by a disgruntled former supervisor, Dan White.[^ch43-glbt-milk]
- **The initiative and the aftermath.** The statewide Briggs Initiative, which would have barred gay and lesbian teachers and their supporters from public-school employment, was defeated at the polls in November 1978; after White was convicted of manslaughter and given a light sentence, mass protests known as the White Night Riots followed in San Francisco.[^ch43-nara-milk][^ch43-glbt-milk]
- **The caution.** The record of the period’s gay-liberation history is uneven and retrospective; the chapter should attribute statements to the witnesses who made them, date later recollections, and note the roles of people of color and gender-nonconforming people whom early accounts often minimized.[^ch43-glbt-milk][^ch43-check-silences]

### 5.4 Evangelical organizing and the New Right

- **The turn to politics.** Jerry Falwell, a fundamentalist preacher who had once declared that “preachers are not called to be politicians, but soul winners,” changed course in the 1970s, dismayed by the 1973 *Roe v. Wade* decision and influenced by Francis Schaeffer; he staged “I Love America” rallies that attacked the ERA, homosexuality, pornography, and women’s liberation, and in 1979 he helped found the Moral Majority, which mobilized conservative Christian voters.[^ch43-falwell-pbs]
- **The coalition.** The Moral Majority joined tax, race, and family-values organizing to a religious appeal, and it became one of the vehicles by which the New Right entered the Republican coalition. The chapter should present the organizing as the work of identifiable people and institutions, not as a spontaneous national mood.[^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The movement’s reach and its effect on the 1980 election are contested; the chapter names the 1980 contest but leaves its outcome to Chapter 44.[^ch43-check-ch44][^ch43-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 *Gregg v. Georgia* and the death penalty

- **The holding.** In *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, decided July 2, 1976, the Supreme Court upheld Georgia’s revised death-penalty statute against an Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment challenge, holding that the new procedures guided the sentencer’s discretion and thus avoided the arbitrary imposition the Court had condemned in *Furman v. Georgia* (1972). The decision allowed executions to resume.[^ch43-justia-gregg][^ch43-check-gregg]
- **The frame.** *Furman* and *Roe* belong to Chapter 42; *Gregg* and the death-penalty debate of the later 1970s belong here. The chapter should present the decision as a legal holding with a contested aftermath, not as a verdict on the morality of capital punishment.[^ch43-check-gregg][^ch43-check-ch42]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The second oil shock raised prices sharply.** Oil more than doubled between April 1979 and April 1980, and consumer inflation reached a 12-month peak of 14.8 percent from March 1979 to March 1980.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-bls-cpi]
- **The Federal Reserve changed course in October 1979.** The FOMC adopted new operating procedures, and Volcker had been installed as chairman that year with a mandate to fight inflation.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-sf-fed-1979]
- **New York City required state and then federal help.** The state created the Municipal Assistance Corporation and the Emergency Financial Control Board in 1975, and the federal Seasonal Financing Act followed in December 1975.[^ch43-nys-fcb][^ch43-fcb-about]
- **Proposition 13 passed in June 1978 with about 65 percent support.** Its text is Article XIII A of the California Constitution.[^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-prop13-art13a]
- **The Helsinki Final Act was signed on August 1, 1975.** It was a non-binding statement among 35 nations organized in four baskets, the third on human rights and freedom of movement.[^ch43-state-helsinki][^ch43-ford-helsinki]
- **Camp David produced frameworks, and the Egypt-Israel treaty followed in March 1979.** The Panama Canal treaties were ratified in 1978 and entered into force in 1979.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-un-panama]
- **The embassy in Tehran was seized on November 4, 1979, and the hostages were held for 444 days.** The State Department records more than 50 Americans held.[^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
- **The Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan at the end of December 1979, and Carter withdrew SALT II on January 2, 1980.**[^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-politico-salt2]
- **The ERA failed to be ratified by its extended 1982 deadline.**[^ch43-loc-era]
- ***Gregg v. Georgia* reinstated the death penalty in 1976.**[^ch43-justia-gregg]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the decade a failure or a pivot?** The “malaise” reading treats the 1970s as a crisis of confidence and decline; the structural-pivot reading treats the decade as the hinge between the postwar order and the present. Schulman and Stein emphasize rupture and transition; accounts closer to the period’s own diagnosis emphasize decline. The chapter should state each at its strongest and test the claims against the record.[^ch43-schulman-seventies][^ch43-stein-pivotal][^ch43-check-debate-works]
- **How much was structure and how much was choice?** One position locates the decade’s outcomes in oil, productivity, and the end of Bretton Woods; another locates them in decisions — Volcker’s turn, the tax revolts, deregulation, and détente’s management — that could have gone otherwise. The chapter should separate the precipitating shocks from the contingent choices made within them.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-check-debate-works]
- **Did conservative organizing cause the realignment or reflect it?** The movement account treats grassroots conservative organization around taxes, race, and family values as a cause of the political turn; the structural account treats it as a response to economic and social change. The chapter should not reduce the question to one causal arrow.[^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-check-debate-works]
- **Was “malaise” an interpretation or a measurement?** Carter never used the word; the label was applied afterward, and the speech was initially well received. The chapter should treat “malaise” as a contested interpretation, not a description of a universal national mood.[^ch43-newyorker-malaise][^ch43-miller-carter-keys][^ch43-check-malaise]
- **What did the decade’s diplomatic achievements accomplish?** The Egypt-Israel treaty, the Panama treaties, and SALT II were real agreements with contested legacies; the chapter should distinguish what they settled from what they left open.[^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-salt2]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the postwar settlement was eroded by structural forces — energy, productivity, and the dollar — and by contingent choices — a monetary turn, a taxpayer revolt, a diplomatic opening, a revolution, and an invasion — and in which no participant knew how the decade would end.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
- **Contingency.** The Volcker turn, the Panama ratification, the Camp David summit, the decision to admit the Shah, and the response to the Afghan invasion were decisions that could have gone otherwise; the chapter should identify the alternatives that were actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-state-panama][^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-check-debate-works]
- **Structure and choice together.** The synthesis should hold the international and economic conditions together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation over the other.[^ch43-stein-pivotal][^ch43-check-scope]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that between 1973 and 1980 the United States discovered that the abundance on which the postwar settlement rested was not guaranteed, that its government could negotiate lasting settlements and still appear powerless, and that the rights it widened for some of its people were the grievances around which others organized.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-state-campdavid]

## Legacies

The years from 1973 to 1980 left a more constrained federal government and a more contested presidency. The taxpayer revolts and the New York fiscal crisis began a long renegotiation of who pays for local government and public services; Proposition 13 and the measures that followed fixed limits that still shape state and local budgets, and the fiscal crisis of a great city became an argument about the reach of government itself. The chapter should connect these developments to the present as a documented change in public finance, not as a political verdict.[^ch43-ppic-prop13][^ch43-prop13-art13a][^ch43-nys-fcb]

The decade also left the market turn and the religious right. The October 1979 monetary reform began the long disinflation that would define the following decade, and the organizing around taxes, family, and faith built a movement that the 1980 campaign would test. Neither outcome was foreordained in 1979, and the chapter should present them as contingent results of choices made under pressure.[^ch43-fed-volcker][^ch43-falwell-pbs][^ch43-check-ch44]

And it left the hostage crisis as a template and a wound. The seizure of the Tehran embassy and the long captivity that followed fixed a way of talking about American power and vulnerability that outlasted the individuals, and the Afghan invasion reopened a Cold War the decade had tried to soften. The chapter should present those connections from the evidence, and the questions they raised about who the nation was and what it could do as the question the book keeps asking.[^ch43-state-iran-hostages][^ch43-state-afghanistan][^ch43-falwell-pbs]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The decade’s politics were made in Riyadh, Tehran, Cairo, and Kabul as well as Washington; the oil shocks, the Camp David process, the Panama treaties, and the Afghan invasion all show how international decisions bounded American choices. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate the diplomatic history.[^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-state-campdavid][^ch43-state-afghanistan]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, officials, and journalists and under-represents working people, the poor, women outside the political class, and the Iranian and Afghan peoples. The displaced workers of the Mahoning Valley, the gasoline-line motorists, and the ordinary Iranians and Afghans appear mostly through the eyes of officials and reporters. The chapter should name these asymmetries rather than speak for the silenced.[^ch43-check-silences][^ch43-belt-youngstown][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 42 owns the 1973 embargo’s onset, the elections of 1968 and 1972, and the China opening; Chapter 44 owns the hostages’ release, the 1980 election, the Reagan presidency, and the Cold War’s end; Chapter 45 owns the post-Cold War order. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch43-check-ch42][^ch43-check-ch44]

> *A closing note.* Between 1973 and 1980 the United States discovered that the energy, productivity, and confidence on which the postwar settlement rested were not guaranteed. Its government negotiated lasting settlements in the Middle East and Panama and still appeared powerless before gasoline lines, inflation, and a hostage crisis; it widened rights for women and gay people while a countermovement organized around the family and the flag. The chapter’s question — what made the postwar settlement harder to sustain? — is answered by a decade in which structural forces and human choices pressed at once, and in which no participant knew how the pressure would resolve.[^ch43-carter-confidence][^ch43-fed-oil1979][^ch43-loc-era][^ch43-state-iran-hostages]
