# Chapter 38. The Fifties: Affluence, Conformity, and Resistance (1945–1960)

> “The elimination of the clause has changed absolutely nothing.”[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]
>
> — William J. Levitt, statement in the *Levittown Tribune*, 1949, on dropping the racial clause after *Shelley v. Kraemer*.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]

> “Wants are increasingly created by the process by which they are satisfied.”[^ch38-galbraith]
>
> — John Kenneth Galbraith, *The Affluent Society*, 1958.[^ch38-galbraith]

> “Our most powerful visions of traditional families derive from images that are still delivered to our homes in countless reruns of 1950s television sit-coms.”[^ch38-coontz]
>
> — Stephanie Coontz, “What We Really Miss About the 1950s,” in *The Way We Never Were*, 1992.[^ch38-coontz]

A builder said that ending a racial clause had changed nothing. An economist said that an economy which manufactured the wants it then satisfied was living on a false account. A historian said that the decade’s most cherished picture of family life was a rerun. The three statements disagree about what postwar abundance was: a private liberty, a public failure, or a comforting myth. They also disagree about whom it served. That disagreement frames the chapter’s question: for whom was postwar abundance secure?[^ch38-check-epigraphs]

Levittown, New York, in 1950 was the paradigmatic mass-produced suburb. Between 1947 and 1951, Levitt & Sons put up 17,447 houses on the former potato fields of Hempstead, Long Island, and *Time* put the developer William J. Levitt on its cover on July 13, 1950.[^ch38-levitt-hales] The houses were small, the lots were small, and the construction was industrial: crews moved from house to house, and buyers chose among a few models.[^ch38-levitt-hales] The firm advertised low down payments and long mortgages for veterans, and the development became the model of the postwar suburb.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]

The lease for rental properties contained a sentence that made race a term of occupancy. It read: “The tenant agrees not to permit the premises to be used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race.”[^ch38-levitt-lease] On May 3, 1948, the Supreme Court held in *Shelley v. Kraemer* that racially restrictive covenants were not themselves unconstitutional, but that state courts could not enforce them, because enforcement would be state action barred by the Equal Protection Clause.[^ch38-shelley-oyez] Levitt dropped the clause. In 1949 he said the change “changed absolutely nothing,” and he continued to refuse to sell or rent to Black families.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]

The exclusion outlasted the clause. In 1953, when Levittown’s population reached about 70,000, the historian Lizabeth Cohen wrote that it was the largest community in the United States with no Black population.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo] The 1960 census counted 65,276 residents, 57 of them Black.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo] A local Committee to End Discrimination in Levittown organized, and in February 1952 hundreds of supporters gathered at the home of a White tenant facing eviction; Levitt backed down.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo] A Black family that gained occupancy in 1952 was evicted a year later.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]

In 1950, *Brown v. Board of Education* was four years away, the sit-ins a decade away, and the Fair Housing Act eighteen years away. The record shows the lease, the decision, the developer’s statement, the census counts, and the organizing. It does not show what most residents privately thought, how many wanted integration, or how much of the exclusion was Levitt’s alone rather than an industry and a federal practice. For whom was postwar abundance secure?[^ch38-check-scene][^ch38-check-levittown][^ch38-check-silences]

The security state that Chapter 37 describes did not recede as the fighting in Korea ended. It became the backdrop for a consumer society at home, and the question was no longer whether the nation could deter an enemy but who would share in the prosperity the confrontation helped to produce.[^ch38-check-ch37]

This chapter covers the years from 1945 to 1960 and asks for whom postwar abundance was secure. It draws on statutes and leases, census and labor data, court opinions, executive orders, television and press records, and the secondary literature. It treats “the affluent society,” “conformity,” “the silent generation,” “suburbia,” “the organization man,” “termination,” “relocation,” and “the movement” as contested period terms. “The affluent society” was John Kenneth Galbraith’s name for an economy of private plenty and public neglect, not a neutral description. “Conformity” named a set of anxieties about the new middle class, and “the organization man” was one of its caricatures. “The silent generation” was a label applied to the young adults of the 1950s by commentators who thought them politically cautious; it described a style of commentary as much as a generation. “Suburbia” described a built environment and a way of life that were new, not traditional. “Termination” was the federal policy of ending federal recognition and trust protection for tribes; “relocation” was the program that moved Native people to cities. “The movement” was the freedom struggle, and its participants did not agree on its boundaries or its aims.[^ch38-check-scope][^ch38-check-names][^ch38-check-silences]

The record over-represents homeowners, network executives, and federal officials and under-represents the excluded, the displaced, the terminated, the relocated, and the rural poor. The decade’s statistics often measure different things — household ownership, program participation, or labor-force participation — and the figures are given with their definitions. The decade’s apparent consensus is treated as a surface to be explained rather than a fact to be assumed.[^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-check-numbers][^ch38-check-scope]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the politics of prosperity and anticommunism. §2 follows housing, highways, and migration, and reads a Levittown lease in the In Focus. §3 turns to television, music, and the making of national audiences. §4 examines gender, family, employment, and federal termination and relocation policy. §5 follows the freedom struggle from *Brown* to the eve of the sit-ins. §6 asks how historians have weighed the affluent-society and conformity frameworks against histories of exclusion and resistance. The sections are thematic rather than strictly chronological: §§1 and 2 overlap in the late 1940s and early 1950s, and §§3–5 all run through the middle of the decade. The chapter begins in 1950 in Levittown, New York.[^ch38-check-scope][^ch38-check-dates-appD]

## 1. Politics, prosperity, and anticommunism

### 1.1 The claim: growth and its limits

**The claim.** Between 1945 and 1960 the American economy grew on a scale that lifted millions into a new middle class, and national politics narrowed around that growth and around anticommunism. The chapter presents prosperity and the politics of loyalty as connected, because both rested on choices about who belonged.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-mccarthy-censure]

The boom was produced, not natural. Wartime savings, federal spending, the GI Bill, and consumer credit built it, and its benefits were distributed unevenly by race, gender, and region.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining] The federal government did not simply get out of the way after the war. It insured mortgages, paid tuition, built highways, and bought goods, and those choices shaped the kind of prosperity that followed.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-highway-act]

Programs, statutes, and aggregate growth are documented. They do not show that everyone shared in the gains. The chapter therefore presents “the affluent society” as a claim to test rather than a description, and it keeps the distribution of the gains in view.[^ch38-galbraith][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 1.2 The GI Bill and the prosperity it helped build

The Servicemen’s Readjustment Act, signed on June 22, 1944, offered World War II veterans federally financed education, training, unemployment insurance, and home loans. It helped convert a feared postwar depression into a generation of growth.[^ch38-gi-bill] Within seven years about eight million veterans received educational benefits; roughly 2.3 million attended colleges and universities, and millions more received school or on-the-job training. The number of degrees granted by American colleges more than doubled between 1940 and 1950.[^ch38-gi-bill]

The housing benefits were as important as the classrooms. By 1955, 4.3 million home loans had been granted with a face value of about $33 billion, and veterans bought about 20 percent of all new homes built after the war.[^ch38-gi-bill] The program was a universal promise in form and a selective benefit in practice. Black veterans were often unable to obtain mortgages in Black neighborhoods and were excluded from suburban developments reserved for White buyers, and the GI Bill’s administration belongs to the argument about unequal opportunity.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-check-levittown]

### 1.3 Galbraith and the affluent-society critique

In 1958 the Harvard economist John Kenneth Galbraith published *The Affluent Society*, arguing that an economy devoted to producing and consuming private goods had neglected public needs and manufactured the wants it then satisfied.[^ch38-galbraith] He warned that an economy in which “wants are increasingly created by the process by which they are satisfied” was unsound and, in his view, immoral. The phrase “private opulence and public squalor” became the shorthand for his charge that the private sector flourished while public provision lagged.[^ch38-galbraith]

Galbraith’s book was an argument of the period, not a measured finding. Its forecasts were contestable, and it is treated here as a period critique rather than a verified description of the economy.[^ch38-affluent-works][^ch38-check-debate-works] The critique deserves its strongest statement: it named an imbalance that the growth figures alone could not show, and it asked what abundance was for.[^ch38-galbraith][^ch38-affluent-works]

### 1.4 Anticommunism after McCarthy

The second Red Scare reached its most visible phase in the early 1950s and then broke, and the break came through television and the Senate rather than through the courts alone.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure] On April 22, 1954, the Senate’s Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, led by Senator Joseph R. McCarthy of Wisconsin, opened hearings into security in the United States Army. The Army–McCarthy hearings ran to June 17, 1954, and were carried on national television. McCarthy “badgered witnesses while ignoring parliamentary procedures and the rules of common courtesy,” and by the time they ended he had damaged his standing with the public.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure] On July 30, 1954, Senator Ralph Flanders of Vermont introduced a resolution to censure him; on December 2, 1954, the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure McCarthy for abuse of a Senate committee.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure]

Chapter 37 owns McCarthy’s 1950 Wheeling speech, the Smith Act prosecutions, and the Rosenberg execution. This chapter takes up the televised hearings, the censure, and the political aftermath.[^ch38-check-ch37][^ch38-mccarthy-censure] The censure did not end the broader politics of accusation, which continued in loyalty programs, blacklists, and local campaigns. It did show that the most reckless style of accusation could be made to cost its user, and it made the Senate, not the courtroom, the stage on which the spell broke.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure][^ch38-check-silences]

### 1.5 Who was left out of the boom

The comfortable picture of the decade rested on exclusions the next sections examine: segregated suburbs, segregated schools, women pushed out of or confined within the paid labor force, and Native nations pressed to dissolve their political status.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-termination][^ch38-womens-labor] The “consensus” is best read as a surface and the exclusions as the structure beneath it, without treating either as total. The suburbs were not all White, the family was not all contained, and the movement was not only a later response to the decade; it was already present within it.[^ch38-check-scope][^ch38-exclusion-works]

Policies and their distribution are documented. They do not add up to a single national experience, and the prose avoids speaking for the excluded as if they left a single voice. Where the archive is thin, the gap is named rather than filled with a composite witness.[^ch38-check-silences]

## 2. Housing, highways, and migration

### 2.1 The claim: a federally built, racially sorted metropolis

**The claim.** Federal housing and highway policy after the war subsidized a largely White suburban middle class, sorted the metropolis by race and income, and reshaped where Americans lived.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-act] The chapter shows the mechanism — insurance, underwriting guidance, covenants, zoning, and expressway routing — because the outcome was produced by policy, not by an invisible market.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-segregation]

Program rules and patterns of construction and displacement are documented. They cannot prove individual motive, and the account distinguishes a racially neutral rule with a racial effect from a rule designed to exclude.[^ch38-check-housing-terms] That distinction matters because the two kinds of policy call for different explanations and different remedies. A zoning rule that kept lots large could sort a suburb by income without naming race; an underwriting manual that warned against “inharmonious racial groups” named the result it expected.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-check-housing-terms]

### 2.2 Levittown and the In Focus

Levittown made the mass-produced suburb the model of postwar success and, at the same time, made racial exclusion a condition of access to it.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-levitt-lease] The development’s 17,447 houses were marketed to veterans, and its rental leases carried the Caucasian-occupancy clause; after *Shelley v. Kraemer* the clause was dropped while the exclusion continued; and by 1960 the community had almost no Black residents. Local organizers challenged the policy, and Levitt defended it as a private business judgment.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-shelley-oyez]

**In Focus.** The sentence reads as boilerplate: “The tenant agrees not to permit the premises to be used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race.”[^ch38-levitt-lease] Its power is in its grammar. It is a tenant’s promise to a landlord, not a statute, and it makes race a term of occupancy like rent or repairs. A restrictive covenant was a similar private arrangement written into a deed; a lease clause was a term of rental. Both could do a statute’s work without being one. It borrows property law’s authority to say who may live in a house. In *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) the Supreme Court held that such covenants were not themselves unconstitutional but that state courts could not enforce them, because judicial enforcement would be state action violating the Equal Protection Clause.[^ch38-shelley-oyez] The decision made the clause unenforceable without making the exclusion illegal. Levitt dropped the clause and kept the practice, saying in 1949 that the change “changed absolutely nothing,” and the community remained almost entirely White a decade later.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo] The sequence is the argument in miniature: the law withdrew its hand from enforcement while private power and federal subsidy carried the exclusion forward.[^ch38-fha-redlining] The distinction is between a covenant and a lease clause, an unenforceable covenant and a lawful refusal, and a private act and the federal underwriting that made the suburb possible.[^ch38-check-housing-terms] It is quoted from Martha Biondi’s *To Stand and Fight* (2003), p. 230.[^ch38-levitt-lease] The box is textual; no image is reproduced.[^ch38-check-image][^ch38-check-focus]

### 2.3 GI Bill loans, FHA redlining, and Shelley v. Kraemer

The GI Bill’s mortgage guarantees and the Federal Housing Administration’s insurance made suburban homeownership possible for millions, and both operated within a system that steered Black borrowers away from new suburbs and older urban neighborhoods.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining] Redlining meant rating a neighborhood as a poor credit risk; in practice, the rating followed race. The Federal Reserve’s history records that the FHA “began redlining at the very beginning of its operations in 1934.” Its 1938 *Underwriting Manual* emphasized the negative effect of the “infiltration of inharmonious racial groups” on credit risk and recommended restrictive covenants prohibiting “the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are intended.”[^ch38-fha-redlining] Federal policy reflected the segregation of American society, and public and private redlining were intertwined.[^ch38-fha-redlining]

*Shelley v. Kraemer* barred judicial enforcement of racial covenants in 1948. It did not bar private discrimination, and it left the FHA’s underwriting practice untouched, so the decision was a real limit on state power that did not by itself open the suburbs.[^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-check-housing-terms] The distinction between the two is the difference between a court refusing to enforce a private rule and a government deciding not to insure a loan. The first withdrew state power from the covenant; the second continued to direct federal money toward the developments the covenant had made White.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-check-housing-terms]

### 2.4 The highway act and the built environment

The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, signed on June 29, 1956, and popularly known as the National Interstate and Defense Highways Act, authorized the largest public-works program in American history and helped remake both the suburbs and the cities.[^ch38-highway-act] The act expanded the interstate system to 41,000 miles and authorized $25 billion for fiscal years 1957 through 1969, with the federal government paying 90 percent of interstate construction costs.[^ch38-highway-act] The National Archives records that “in urban areas, massive superhighways cut through neighborhoods and destroyed housing, much of it in poorer sections of the city.”[^ch38-highway-act]

One journalistic account drawing on Department of Transportation estimates reports that more than 475,000 households and more than a million people were displaced nationwide by federal roadway construction, disproportionately in Black and other communities of color.[^ch38-highway-segregation] The chapter treats that figure as an estimate with a method and a cutoff, and it does not present expressway routing as an accident. The routes were chosen by officials, defended by business groups, and opposed by residents whose neighborhoods stood in the way; the pattern of who lost a home was a political outcome, not a traffic fact.[^ch38-highway-segregation][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 2.5 Migration and metropolitan growth, with Map 29

The postwar decades saw a vast internal migration: Black migration out of the South into northern and western cities, White migration to the suburbs, and regional shifts in population and industry that the map is planned to show.[^ch38-check-map29] The two movements were connected. The same federal mortgage rules that made a suburban house affordable for a White veteran helped make the older urban neighborhood he left behind a place that lenders valued less.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining]

Map 29, “Migration and metropolitan growth, 1940–1960,” is the chapter’s registered visual. Its caption and source line must state the observation years and distinguish interstate migration, metropolitan growth, and the Great Migration, and it must not imply that all movement ran in one direction.[^ch38-check-map29] Its evidence targets are Census regional and county series and Appendix B, Table 1, and its production record is `repo-map29`. The map must distinguish total population change from Black population change and must not infer individual motives from aggregate movement.[^ch38-check-map29] A map can show direction and scale. It cannot show why any individual moved, and causation stays in the prose.[^ch38-check-map29]

## 3. Television, music, and national audiences

### 3.1 The claim: one nation, many audiences

**The claim.** The 1950s invented a genuinely national mass audience, and that audience was also a battlefield over race, gender, region, and youth.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-elvis] Television, popular music, and the Beats belong together, because each both standardized taste and provoked conflict about the terms of belonging.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-howl]

The record can show distribution, ratings, and censorship. It cannot measure what a viewer or listener took from a program, and that limit is marked here. A rating estimates the percentage of all television households tuned to a program; a share estimates the percentage of households actually watching television. Neither tells us what a family argued about afterward.[^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 3.2 Television: diffusion and the freeze

Television spread with extraordinary speed. The Library of Congress notes that in 1950 only 9 percent of American households had a television set, and that by 1960 the figure had reached 90 percent.[^ch38-television-loc] On September 30, 1948, the Federal Communications Commission announced a freeze on new television licenses that lasted until 1952, a pause that helped consolidate the dominance of the national networks. The chapter states the freeze and its dates without claiming that it alone caused the network system.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-check-tv-stat]

Ownership and licensing are documented. They cannot settle how many households watched a given program, and the various diffusion figures differ by measure and year and must be reconciled.[^ch38-check-tv-stat] The Library of Congress series is used here because it states household ownership, and the measure is labeled rather than treated as a single uncontested number.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-check-numbers]

### 3.3 Rock and roll, Elvis, and youth culture

Rock and roll made youth a national market and a national anxiety. When Elvis Presley first appeared on *The Ed Sullivan Show* on September 9, 1956, about 60 million viewers watched, and the program reported about 82 percent of the television audience — a share, not a rating.[^ch38-elvis] Presley’s blend of rhythm and blues, country, and gospel drew on Black musical traditions while the industry marketed him as a White star. His performances were attacked in the press, and by his final appearance on January 6, 1957, CBS censors required that he be filmed from the waist up.[^ch38-elvis]

The “gyrations” controversy was a debate about race, respectability, and the young. The performances that made Presley a national star also made him a symbol of what adults feared in the new youth culture, and the censorship tried to keep his body out of the picture while keeping his voice on the air.[^ch38-elvis][^ch38-check-silences] The appearances, audience figures, and censorship are documented. They cannot show that Presley alone caused a cultural change, and he belongs within the broader traffic between Black and White music.[^ch38-elvis][^ch38-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 The Beats and the Howl trial

The Beat writers offered the decade’s sharpest literary dissent from conformity, and their reputation was made in part by a censorship trial. Allen Ginsberg wrote *Howl* in the summer of 1955, read it publicly at the Six Gallery in San Francisco in October 1955, and published it in 1956 with City Lights.[^ch38-howl] When copies printed in London arrived through United States Customs, City Lights’s Lawrence Ferlinghetti and Shigeyoshi Murao were arrested on obscenity charges in San Francisco. At the trial poets, critics, and academics testified to the poem’s redeeming social value, and the court ruled *Howl* not obscene, a decision that widened the boundary of what American literature could print.[^ch38-howl]

The record can show the reading, the publication, and the trial. It cannot establish that the verdict alone liberalized American culture, and the Beats are best presented as a small avant-garde whose trial became a national test case.[^ch38-howl][^ch38-check-debate-works] The verdict mattered because it gave publishers a precedent, but the larger change came from the willingness of writers, readers, and courts to treat a poem about sex, drugs, and madness as literature rather than as evidence of a crime.[^ch38-howl]

## 4. Gender, family, employment, and Native termination/relocation

### 4.1 The claim: an ideal that excluded

**The claim.** The decade’s settled picture of the male breadwinner and the suburban housewife rested on a labor market, a welfare state, and a federal policy toward Native nations that pushed many people outside the picture.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-check-names] The chapter holds the ideal and its exclusions together, because the same prosperity that made the housewife possible depended on underpaid domestic and service work and on the dissolution of Native political status.[^ch38-womens-labor][^ch38-termination]

Marriage, birth, and employment patterns and federal statutes are documented. They cannot show that the ideal was universally believed, and it is presented here as a widely promoted norm, not a consensus of conduct.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-check-silences] The gap between the norm and the conduct is the story: women worked, families varied, and Native nations did not disappear when the federal government changed the name of its policy.[^ch38-womens-labor][^ch38-termination]

### 4.2 Domesticity and the baby boom

The years after the war produced a marriage and baby boom that made the family the center of national life and the suburb its setting.[^ch38-coontz] The historian Stephanie Coontz records that the birth rate rose from a low of 18.4 per 1,000 women during the Great Depression to a high of 25.3 per 1,000 in 1957. She also records that “the birth rate for third children doubled between 1940 and 1960, and that for fourth children tripled.” The divorce rate and the rate of births outside marriage were about half their later levels.[^ch38-coontz]

Rates and the cultural promotion of domesticity are documented. They cannot show that the 1950s family was the traditional norm rather than an aberration, and the nostalgic picture is treated as an argument Coontz and others contest.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-exclusion-works] The baby boom was not a return to an older pattern. It was a departure, produced by a particular combination of young marriages, federal housing policy, rising incomes, and a cultural confidence that the future would be better than the past.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-gi-bill]

### 4.3 Women, work, and the labor market

The domestic ideal coexisted with rising numbers of women in the paid labor force, most of them in sex-segregated and low-paid work.[^ch38-womens-labor] About one in three women was in the labor force in 1950 — 33.9 percent in the series the Institute for Women’s Policy Research cites — and married women’s participation rose steadily through the decade.[^ch38-womens-labor] The chapter gives the series, its definition, and its source, and does not confuse the total participation rate with the rate for married women or for women with young children.[^ch38-check-womens-work]

The bracero program brought Mexican laborers north under contract, and the 1954 enforcement campaign known by the period slur “Operation Wetback” removed Mexican workers in a drive whose totals remain disputed. The name is reproduced only because it was the campaign’s official designation.[^ch38-wetback][^ch38-check-wetback] The program and the deportation drive belonged to the same labor system: parts of the economy depended on Mexican labor that could be brought in when it was wanted and removed when it was not.[^ch38-wetback][^ch38-check-wetback]

Participation and earnings are documented. They cannot show why any woman worked or left work, and the housewife and the working woman are not opposites. Many households contained both, and the woman who worked for pay might also be the woman who kept the suburban house running.[^ch38-womens-labor][^ch38-check-silences]

### 4.4 Native termination and relocation

Federal policy in the 1950s tried to end the special legal relationship between the United States and Native nations and to move Native people into cities, with damaging results.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-relocation] On August 1, 1953, Congress adopted House Concurrent Resolution 108, calling for termination of federal supervision “at the earliest possible time” of named tribes and those in California, Florida, New York, and Texas. On August 15, 1953, Public Law 280 transferred criminal and civil jurisdiction on many reservations in California, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon, and Wisconsin to state authorities.[^ch38-termination] From 1953 to 1970 Congress began 60 termination proceedings, and more than three million acres of tribal land were relinquished.[^ch38-termination]

The Bureau of Indian Affairs ran a voluntary urban relocation program from the early 1950s, offering help with housing and employment in Chicago, Denver, Los Angeles, Cleveland, and Seattle.[^ch38-relocation] The Indian Relocation Act of 1956, Public Law 959, authorized vocational training for Native people living on or near reservations, and it extended the program’s reach.[^ch38-relocation-act] Many who moved faced unemployment, low-wage work, discrimination, and the loss of cultural ties.[^ch38-relocation] The National Archives records that when relocation began nearly 8 percent of Native Americans lived in cities, and that the 2000 Census found about 64 percent did.[^ch38-relocation]

The record can show the resolutions, the statutes, and the program’s design. It cannot show that relocation was uniformly forced or uniformly chosen, and individual Native decisions are presented as decisions made under heavy pressure.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-check-silences] “Voluntary” described the formal offer, not the range of choices. A family that could not find work on the reservation, or whose training took them to a distant city, was choosing within a policy that had already narrowed the alternatives.[^ch38-relocation][^ch38-check-silences]

## 5. The freedom struggle through the 1960 sit-ins’ background

### 5.1 The claim: legal victory and mass movement

**The claim.** The 1950s combined a legal campaign against segregation with mass protest, and the two built on each other; the decade ended with a partly dismantled legal order, a national movement, and strong resistance.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery] The chapter presents the decade’s movement as foreshadowing the 1960 sit-ins without absorbing them, because Chapter 39 owns the sit-ins and what followed.[^ch38-check-ch39]

The record can show the cases, the campaigns, and the violence. It cannot show that *Brown* caused the movement, and the distinction is between the decision’s effect and its symbolic role.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-check-debate-works] The legal campaign gave the movement a language of rights and a set of court victories. The mass campaigns gave those victories a public meaning and exposed the distance between a ruling and a changed school.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery]

### 5.2 Brown and “all deliberate speed”

On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court unanimously held in *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka* that segregating children in public schools solely on the basis of race deprived them of equal protection, and that “separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.” It overruled the “separate but equal” doctrine of *Plessy v. Ferguson*.[^ch38-brown] The NAACP’s Legal Defense and Educational Fund, led by Thurgood Marshall, had built the case through a series of graduate-school and school-finance suits; Marshall argued *Brown* before the Court in 1952 and 1953.[^ch38-marshall]

A year later, on May 31, 1955, the Court returned in *Brown II* and instructed the states to proceed “with all deliberate speed,” a formula that left the pace to lower courts and local officials and produced years of delay.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-little-rock] The decision was a victory and a limit at once. It declared segregation unconstitutional and then handed the remedy to the officials who had built it.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 Emmett Till and the galvanizing of a movement

The murder of Emmett Till in 1955 and his mother’s decision to open his casket turned a private grief into a national image of Jim Crow violence.[^ch38-till] On August 28, 1955, Till, a 14-year-old from Chicago visiting family in Money, Mississippi, was abducted and killed after an encounter with a White store clerk; his body was recovered from the Tallahatchie River.[^ch38-till] Two White men, Roy Bryant and J. W. Milam, were tried and acquitted by an all-White, all-male jury; they later sold their account of the killing to *Look* magazine.[^ch38-till]

Mamie Till-Mobley insisted on an open-casket funeral in Chicago, attended by more than 50,000 people and pictured in *Jet*, and the photographs carried the violence to a national audience.[^ch38-till] About three months later, Rosa Parks, a Montgomery activist, refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery city bus.[^ch38-montgomery] Participants and later accounts drew the connection; it is not treated as a demonstrated cause.[^ch38-till][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-check-till-record]

### 5.4 Montgomery and Browder v. Gayle

The Montgomery bus boycott transformed a local grievance into a mass protest and made a new leader, the Reverend Martin Luther King Jr., nationally visible.[^ch38-montgomery] The boycott began on December 5, 1955, after the arrest of Rosa Parks on December 1; on the first day, about 90 percent of Montgomery’s Black residents stayed off the buses.[^ch38-montgomery] The Montgomery Improvement Association coordinated a carpool of some 300 cars, the city indicted more than 80 boycott leaders, and King’s home and the home of E. D. Nixon, the former leader of the Montgomery NAACP, were bombed.[^ch38-montgomery] The federal district court ruled in *Browder v. Gayle* on June 5, 1956, that bus segregation was unconstitutional, and the Supreme Court affirmed in November 1956; the boycott ended on December 20, 1956.[^ch38-montgomery]

The organizers were not only the ministers who became famous. The Women’s Political Council, the MIA’s women volunteers, and earlier challengers including Claudette Colvin and Mary Louise Smith were central, and the campaign is not reduced to one leader or one arrest.[^ch38-montgomery] The boycott was a mass mobilization that depended on thousands of ordinary decisions — to walk, to share a ride, to keep a car running, to refuse a seat — and its success was made by the network that sustained those decisions.[^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-check-silences]

### 5.5 Little Rock and federal power

The Little Rock crisis of 1957 tested whether the federal government would enforce *Brown*, and it showed both the reach and the limits of federal action.[^ch38-little-rock] Nine Black students enrolled at Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, in September 1957. Governor Orval Faubus deployed the Arkansas National Guard to bar them; on September 4 a mob threatened one of the nine, Elizabeth Eckford, as she tried to enter alone.[^ch38-little-rock] After Faubus withdrew the Guard, a full-scale riot followed. On September 23, 1957, President Dwight D. Eisenhower issued Proclamation 3204; the next day he signed Executive Order 10730, federalizing the Arkansas National Guard and sending about 1,000 Army paratroopers of the 101st Airborne Division to restore order.[^ch38-little-rock][^ch38-eo10730]

The record can show the orders, the troops, and the students’ entry. It cannot show that federal force desegregated the schools, since Little Rock’s schools closed rather than integrate and reopened under a different plan.[^ch38-little-rock][^ch38-check-silences] The crisis showed that the president would use force to uphold a court order, and it showed how much force was needed to get nine children through a door. It did not show that the law had changed the community.[^ch38-little-rock][^ch38-eo10730]

### 5.6 The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 and the eve of the sit-ins

Congress passed the first civil rights legislation since Reconstruction, and it was weak; the gap between the statutes and enforcement set the stage for the direct-action phase that began in 1960.[^ch38-cra1957] The Civil Rights Act of 1957, signed on September 9, 1957, created a civil rights unit in the Justice Department and a federal Civil Rights Commission. It empowered federal prosecutors to seek injunctions against interference with voting, but it was weakened in Congress.[^ch38-cra1957] A second act, signed on May 6, 1960, required election officers to preserve voting records, authorized federal courts to use voting referees, and made obstruction of federal court orders a crime.[^ch38-cra1960]

On February 1, 1960, four Black students sat down at a segregated lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and refused to leave. The chapter names that date and stops there, leaving the sit-ins and what followed to Chapter 39.[^ch38-check-ch39][^ch38-check-bridge] The legal victories and the unmet demands of the 1950s did not settle the movement. They defined the ground on which the next phase would be fought.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-cra1957]

## 6. The Debate

The chapter’s narrative has raised more questions than it can settle. This section separates what the evidence establishes from what historians argue about, and it states the rival positions at their strongest.

### 6.1 What is settled

The economy grew and consumption reshaped daily life. Federal programs, the GI Bill, consumer credit, and rising incomes produced broad, uneven growth.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-galbraith] The suburb was federally made and racially sorted; the GI Bill’s mortgage guarantees, the FHA’s underwriting practice, and restrictive covenants and leases shaped who could buy new housing.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-levitt-lease]

The interstate highway program was authorized in 1956 and displaced large numbers of people; the act’s scope and the urban effects are documented.[^ch38-highway-act][^ch38-highway-segregation] Television became a mass medium and a site of conflict; diffusion, the FCC freeze, and the Elvis and *Howl* controversies are documented.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-elvis][^ch38-howl]

Federal policy toward Native nations pursued termination and relocation; HCR 108 and Public Law 280 are documented, as is the urban relocation program.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-relocation] The decade’s movement dismantled parts of the legal order; *Brown*, *Browder v. Gayle*, Little Rock, and the 1957 and 1960 acts are documented.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-cra1957]

These facts establish the sequence, the institutions, and the outcomes. They do not establish why the sequence happened or how to weigh its costs.[^ch38-check-debate-works]

### 6.2 What is open

Was the decade a period of prosperity and consensus or of exclusion and incipient revolt? The affluent-society and conformity frameworks, from Galbraith to Whyte, Riesman, and Mills, emphasize growth, affluence, and social pressure; histories of exclusion and resistance, from Coontz to Cohen, Sugrue, and Hall, emphasize who was left out and who fought back. The chapter states each at its strongest.[^ch38-affluent-works][^ch38-exclusion-works][^ch38-check-debate-works]

Did the Cold War advance civil rights or confine them? Mary L. Dudziak argues that international criticism of American racism pressured the federal government to reform but limited reform to formal equality and away from “a broader critique of the American economic and political system.” Others center Black activism and southern politics. The chapter weighs both.[^ch38-dudziak][^ch38-exclusion-works]

Was segregation a matter of law, custom, or federal subsidy? The evidence supports a combined explanation: covenants and clauses, unenforceable after *Shelley* but effective through custom, rested on federal insurance and underwriting. The chapter does not choose one cause.[^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-levitt-lease]

What caused the urban crisis? One position locates it in 1960s riots and White flight; another, exemplified by Thomas J. Sugrue, locates it in deindustrialization, discrimination, and housing policy in the 1940s and 1950s. The timing remains a question.[^ch38-exclusion-works]

Was the 1950s family traditional or exceptional? Coontz and others argue that the era was an aberration rather than a norm; the demographic evidence is presented with the contested framing.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-exclusion-works]

How much did policy displace, and how many were terminated? Displacement and termination figures rest on different methods and cutoffs, and the figures are given as ranges with their methods.[^ch38-check-numbers][^ch38-highway-segregation][^ch38-termination]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which broad growth and durable exclusion were produced together, and in which the excluded repeatedly acted. Neither a story of affluence and consensus nor a story of exclusion alone explains the record.[^ch38-affluent-works][^ch38-exclusion-works]

The GI Bill’s administration, the FHA’s underwriting rules, the route of an expressway, the wording of a lease, and the choice to terminate a tribe were decisions people made and could have made otherwise; none was dictated by the outcome.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-segregation] The counterfactuals are not decorations. They identify where the evidence would have to be strongest to support a claim of inevitability.

Structure and choice belong together. Federal subsidy, labor demand, regional politics, and the Cold War set the pressures; the choices made within those pressures determined who got a house, who got a road, and who got a lawyer.[^ch38-exclusion-works][^ch38-check-debate-works] A history that emphasizes only structure cannot explain why the FHA wrote the manual it did or why a developer kept a clause after the courts had stopped enforcing it. A history that emphasizes only choice cannot explain why so many of the choices pointed in the same direction.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-check-debate-works]

The synthesis for this chapter is that the United States after 1945 built a mass middle class on federal foundations and drew its boundaries with law, contract, and violence. The people drawn outside those boundaries began the movement that would dominate the next decade.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery] That is the answer to the chapter’s question, and it is a double answer: abundance and exclusion were not opposites. They were produced together.[^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-exclusion-works]

## Legacies

The years from 1945 to 1960 left the United States a suburban nation and a metropolitan crisis. The mortgage guarantees, underwriting rules, and expressway contracts of those years fixed a pattern of race and residence that outlasted the decade and shaped the geography of opportunity for generations.[^ch38-gi-bill][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-highway-segregation]

The decade left a movement and a legal framework. *Brown*, the Montgomery boycott, and Little Rock made segregation a national question and produced the first civil rights statutes since Reconstruction, while leaving enforcement and economic justice unresolved; the sit-ins of 1960 built directly on that base.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-cra1957]

The decade also left a memory. The television family, the suburban house, and the “silent generation” became a widely recycled picture of American normality, and historians from Coontz to Sugrue have argued that the picture obscured the exclusions that made it possible.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-exclusion-works]

The dissatisfaction that the picture could not contain became a public issue in 1963, when Betty Friedan published *The Feminine Mystique* and gave the phrase “the problem that has no name” to the discontent of educated suburban wives. The book was a later chapter in the history this chapter describes, and its focus on White, middle-class women was one of its limits.[^ch38-friedan] The 1950s did not end in 1960, and the questions it raised about work, family, and belonging did not stay inside the decade’s picture of itself.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-check-dates-appD]

- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents homeowners, network executives, and federal officials and under-represents the excluded, the displaced, the terminated, the relocated, and the poor. The chapter names these asymmetries rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch38-check-silences][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]

## Summary

- The postwar economy grew broadly on federal foundations: the GI Bill of 1944 provided education and training benefits to about eight million veterans and, by 1955, had backed 4.3 million home loans worth about $33 billion.[^ch38-gi-bill]
- In 1958 John Kenneth Galbraith’s *The Affluent Society* argued that an economy which manufactured its own wants neglected public needs.[^ch38-galbraith]
- The Army–McCarthy hearings of April–June 1954 were televised, and on December 2, 1954, the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure Senator Joseph R. McCarthy.[^ch38-mccarthy-censure]
- Levittown’s rental leases barred occupancy by any but members of “the Caucasian race”; after *Shelley v. Kraemer* made such covenants unenforceable in 1948, Levitt dropped the clause but said the change “changed absolutely nothing,” and the community remained almost entirely White.[^ch38-levitt-lease][^ch38-shelley-oyez][^ch38-levitt-tougaloo]
- The FHA’s 1938 *Underwriting Manual* warned of the “infiltration of inharmonious racial groups” and recommended restrictive covenants, embedding discrimination in federal housing policy.[^ch38-fha-redlining]
- The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 authorized 41,000 miles and $25 billion, and urban expressways displaced more than 475,000 households by one federal estimate.[^ch38-highway-act][^ch38-highway-segregation]
- Television ownership rose from 9 percent of households in 1950 to 90 percent in 1960, and Elvis Presley’s first *Ed Sullivan Show* appearance on September 9, 1956, drew about 60 million viewers.[^ch38-television-loc][^ch38-elvis]
- Allen Ginsberg read *Howl* at the Six Gallery in October 1955; City Lights published it in 1956, and the 1957 obscenity trial ended with the poem ruled not obscene.[^ch38-howl]
- The birth rate rose to 25.3 per 1,000 in 1957, and third and fourth children became far more common, even as about a third of women were in the labor force.[^ch38-coontz][^ch38-womens-labor]
- House Concurrent Resolution 108 of August 1, 1953, sought termination of federal supervision, Public Law 280 shifted jurisdiction to the states, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs relocated Native people to cities.[^ch38-termination][^ch38-relocation]
- *Brown v. Board of Education* of May 17, 1954, held school segregation unconstitutional; *Brown II* set “all deliberate speed”; Emmett Till was murdered in 1955; the Montgomery boycott ran from December 1955 to December 1956 and ended with *Browder v. Gayle*; and the Little Rock crisis of 1957 brought federal troops.[^ch38-brown][^ch38-till][^ch38-montgomery][^ch38-little-rock]
- The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 were the first two such statutes since Reconstruction, and both were weak, leaving the direct-action phase to begin at Greensboro on February 1, 1960.[^ch38-cra1957][^ch38-cra1960][^ch38-check-ch39]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1945 and 1960 the United States built the most prosperous mass society it had yet built, and it drew the boundaries of that society with leases, underwriting rules, expressway routes, and statutes. The decade gave millions of families a house, a car, and a television, and it gave the people it excluded a movement. The chapter’s question — for whom was postwar abundance secure? — is answered by that double record of provision and exclusion, and by a freedom struggle that was already, by 1960, about to become a mass phase.[^ch38-levitt-tougaloo][^ch38-fha-redlining][^ch38-brown][^ch38-montgomery]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on February 1, 1960, when four Black students sat down at a segregated lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and refused to leave. Chapter 39 takes up the civil rights revolution from the sit-ins through the freedom rides, Birmingham, the March on Washington, Mississippi, Selma, and Black Power. The legal victories and the unmet demands that Chapter 38 describes did not settle the movement; they defined the ground on which the next phase would be fought.[^ch38-check-bridge][^ch38-check-ch39]
