## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date and the occasion.** On the morning of September 11, 2001, two hijacked airliners struck the World Trade Center towers, a third struck the Pentagon, and a fourth crashed in Somerset County, Pennsylvania; the towers collapsed within about 90 minutes. Survivors streamed to the southern tip of Manhattan, and vessels in New York Harbor began moving people off the island by water.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-cgf-boatlift]
- **The radio call.** A Coast Guard lieutenant, Michael Day, later recalled a small boat at the lower tip of Manhattan taking on “ten deep” crowds; the Coast Guard put out a call over the radio: “All available boats, this is the U.S. Coast Guard aboard the Pilot Boat *New York*. Anyone wanting to help with the evacuation of lower Manhattan, report to Governor’s Island.”[^ch46-cgf-boatlift]
- **The response.** Within 15 to 20 minutes, more than 100 boats responded — ferries, tugboats, party boats, and private boats — and one engineer aboard the vessel *Mary Gellatly* recalled, “If it floated and it could get there, it got there.” In under nine hours, the boats evacuated more than 500,000 people from lower Manhattan.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift]
- **The Coast Guard’s own frame.** The Coast Guard Foundation describes the response as among the largest maritime evacuations in history, and notes that the Coast Guard was moved from the Department of Transportation to the Department of Homeland Security in 2003. The Memorial’s account records that the attacks killed 2,977 people and caused the deaths of 441 first responders.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift][^ch46-911memorial-events]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the radio call, the vessels, and the number evacuated. It cannot show what each passenger felt, how individual decisions were made, or that the evacuation prevented a specific number of deaths. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift][^ch46-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** On the morning of September 11, 2001, no one on the water knew that Congress would pass an authorization for the use of force three days later, that the PATRIOT Act would pass in six weeks, that the United States would invade Afghanistan in October and Iraq in 2003, or that a financial collapse would occur in 2008. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch46-check-scene][^ch46-check-dates-appD]

### The scene’s argument

- **A city’s improvisation, and a state’s reorganization.** The scene’s claim is not that the boat lift caused any later policy, but that on the first day the response was improvised by mariners and first responders while the state’s institutions, as the 9/11 Commission later put it, were “unprepared.” The improvisation on the water and the failure and then reorganization of the national security state are the chapter’s subject.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift][^ch46-911report-exec]
- **The stakes.** The tension between an improvised, decentralized rescue and the centralized emergency powers that followed — the authorization, the surveillance statute, the new department, the detention program — is the chapter’s central problem.[^ch46-check-scope][^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did emergency reorganize state power and public trust, and by what distinct steps?[^ch46-check-scope]
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the water off lower Manhattan to the Capitol, the intelligence agencies, the mountains of Afghanistan, the streets of Baghdad, the flooded wards of New Orleans, and the trading floors of 2008.[^ch46-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No invented crowd or weather detail.** The chapter may not stage a fabricated crowd count, weather, dialogue, or interior thought. It may use the documented radio call, the named participants, and the recorded fact of the evacuation; the number evacuated rests on the Coast Guard account and must be given as an estimate with its source.[^ch46-check-scene][^ch46-cgf-boatlift]
- **No borrowing later words.** The scene must not import Bush’s September 20 formulation (“Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists”) or the 2004 “mission accomplished” claim, which belong to §1 and §4.[^ch46-check-scene][^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]
- **No hindsight.** The scene must not give its participants knowledge of the AUMF, the PATRIOT Act, the Afghan and Iraq wars, the detention program, or the 2008 collapse.[^ch46-check-scene][^ch46-check-dates-appD]

Where those voices do not survive, the chapter should say so rather than inventing them.[^ch46-check-silences]

## 1. Attacks and response (2001)

### 1.1 The claim: a transformation and the choices it opened

- **The claim.** The attacks of September 11, 2001, killed 2,977 people and transformed American security policy; the responses that followed — war, statute, a new department, and a surveillance and detention program — were contingent choices made under emergency, and they must be told as distinct institutional decisions rather than one causal story.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the attacks and their toll through the rescue and the 9/11 Commission’s account of national unpreparedness to the president’s framing of a global war, with the maritime evacuation as the opening scene.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]

### 1.2 The attacks and the toll

- **The sequence.** The 9/11 Commission’s executive summary records the timeline: the first airliner struck the World Trade Center’s North Tower at 8:46 a.m., the second hit the South Tower at 9:03, a third hit the Pentagon at 9:37, and a fourth crashed in southern Pennsylvania at 10:03; the towers collapsed less than 90 minutes after the impacts.[^ch46-911report-exec]
- **The toll.** The Memorial’s account records that the attacks killed 2,977 people, the single largest loss of life from a foreign attack on American soil, including 441 first responders; the Commission’s summary gives the distribution as more than 2,600 at the World Trade Center, 125 at the Pentagon, and 256 on the four planes, and notes that the deaths surpassed those at Pearl Harbor in December 1941.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec]
- **The caution.** The counts differ slightly by source and by date of compilation and must be given with their source and cutoff; the 2,977 total and the Commission’s component figures should not be presented as interchangeable without explanation.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-check-numbers]

### 1.3 The rescue and the Commission’s accounting

- **The rescue.** Rescue workers, mariners, and civilians improvised the response, including the waterborne evacuation of more than 500,000 people from lower Manhattan; the 441 first responders who died were the greatest single-day loss of emergency responders in American history.[^ch46-cgf-boatlift][^ch46-911memorial-events]
- **The Commission.** The National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States, created by Congress and reporting on July 22, 2004, produced a bipartisan account that ran “without dissent” and concluded that “the nation was unprepared,” identifying failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management.[^ch46-911report-exec]
- **The finding to carry forward.** The Commission wrote that “the most important failure was one of imagination” and that “it is therefore crucial to find a way of routinizing, even bureaucratizing, the exercise of imagination.” The chapter should quote this precisely and use it as the retrospective hinge between the rescue of §1 and the reorganization of §2.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-check-focus]
- **The caution.** The Commission is an official retrospective, not a neutral verdict; its findings are evidence of what a bipartisan panel concluded and should be distinguished from the events themselves.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The framing of a global war

- **The address.** In his September 20, 2001, address to a joint session of Congress, Bush said the evidence pointed to al Qaeda, demanded that the Taliban turn over its leaders, warned that “Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists,” and announced the creation of a Cabinet-level Office of Homeland Security under Tom Ridge.[^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]
- **The scope of the claim.** Bush framed the conflict as broader than one organization: “Our war on terror begins with al Qaeda, but it does not end there. It will not end until every terrorist group of global reach has been found, stopped and defeated.” The chapter should treat this as the administration’s own statement of an open-ended campaign, not as a description the evidence settles.[^ch46-bush-joint-address-2001]
- **The dissent, stated fairly.** Feingold and other critics argued that the response should be measured by whether it preserved the liberties it claimed to defend; the chapter should state that position at its strongest in §1.4 and test it in §2 and §6.[^ch46-feingold-2001][^ch46-check-silences]
- **The caution.** The chapter must not treat September 11 as a single cause of the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, the PATRIOT Act, or the later surveillance programs; it should identify the specific decisions and decision-makers for each.[^ch46-check-scope][^ch46-check-debate-works]

## 2. AUMF, PATRIOT Act, DHS, detention, torture, and surveillance (2001–2008)

### 2.1 The claim: emergency delegated power across statutes, agencies, and courts

- **The claim.** In the six years after the attacks, Congress and the executive branch created a legal and institutional architecture of emergency — an authorization for the use of force, a surveillance statute, a new department, and a detention and interrogation program — that delegated broad authority and moved contested decisions into the executive branch and the courts.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-ssci-2014]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the authorization through the PATRIOT Act and the Section 215 In Focus, the creation of the Department of Homeland Security, the detention and interrogation program and Abu Ghraib, and the surveillance expansion and the courts.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-ssci-2014]

### 2.2 The Authorization for Use of Military Force

- **The text.** Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force on September 14, 2001, and President Bush signed it on September 18 as Public Law 107-40, 115 Stat. 224. Section 2 authorizes the president “to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks that occurred on September 11, 2001, or harbored such organizations or persons.”[^ch46-aumf-2001]
- **The votes.** The Senate agreed to the resolution 98–0, and the House passed it without objection; the chapter should give the votes and note that the authorization did not name a country or an enemy and left the determination to the president.[^ch46-aumf-votes]
- **The delegation.** The chapter should explain the constitutional question — how far Congress delegated its war power and how the executive construed that delegation — rather than treating the resolution as a settled grant or a usurpation.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 2.3 The PATRIOT Act and the In Focus

- **The statute.** The Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism (USA PATRIOT) Act became Public Law 107-56 on October 26, 2001, six weeks after the attacks.[^ch46-patriot-act]
- **The expanded powers.** The statute expanded surveillance powers in several areas, including records searches under Section 215, delayed-notice “sneak and peek” searches under Section 213, the “significant purpose” standard for foreign-intelligence searches under Section 218, and pen-register and trap-and-trace authority under Section 214. The chapter should give each provision from the statute, not from a later characterization.[^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-patriot-aclu]
- **In Focus.** The close reading reads Section 215, which replaced the prior “records” standard for certain businesses with authority to order production of “any tangible things (including books, records, papers, documents, and other items)” relevant to an authorized investigation, and restricted disclosure of the order. It should quote the statutory language, explain what “tangible things” and “relevant to an authorized investigation” put in dispute, and distinguish the statutory text from the later implementation — including the foreign-intelligence court’s orders and the post-2013 disclosures — rather than collapsing text and implementation into one claim.[^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-patriot-aclu][^ch46-check-focus]
- **The dissent.** The ACLU and others argued that Section 215 allowed the government to compel records from anyone, on a certification the judge could not reject, and that the nondisclosure requirement undercut the ability to challenge the order; the chapter should present that argument at its strongest before testing it against the statute’s text and the later interpretations.[^ch46-patriot-aclu][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 2.4 The Department of Homeland Security

- **The statute.** The Homeland Security Act of 2002 became Public Law 107-296 on November 25, 2002, establishing the Department of Homeland Security as an executive department to prevent terrorist attacks, reduce vulnerability, and coordinate recovery.[^ch46-homeland-security-act]
- **The reorganization.** The act consolidated functions from many agencies into the new department; the Coast Guard, which had improvised the maritime evacuation of September 11, was moved there in 2003. The chapter should describe the reorganization from the statute and note its scale and its coordination problems.[^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-cgf-boatlift]

### 2.5 Detention, interrogation, and Abu Ghraib

- **The program.** After the attacks, the administration established a detention and interrogation program that held suspected terrorists, including at the Guantánamo Bay naval base, and authorized interrogation techniques that critics called torture. The chapter should distinguish the detention of fighters captured in Afghanistan from the CIA’s separate detention program and the military’s interrogation practices in Iraq.[^ch46-ssci-2014][^ch46-hrw-abu-ghraib]
- **The Senate study.** The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence completed a study of the CIA’s detention and interrogation program, approved by the committee and released in a declassified executive summary on December 9, 2014, finding that the agency used “enhanced interrogation techniques” and detailing the program’s operation and its intelligence claims. The chapter should cite the study’s findings with their locators and present the program as documented, while noting the minority views appended to the report.[^ch46-ssci-2014]
- **Abu Ghraib.** In April 2004, photographs of abuse of detainees at Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq became public; officials called the abuse the work of a few “bad apples,” while Human Rights Watch and others argued it followed from administration decisions to set aside interrogation rules. The chapter should present both framings and use the investigations, not only the photographs.[^ch46-hrw-abu-ghraib][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The record of coercion is contested in its legal characterization; the chapter should separate what the memos authorized, what the interrogations did, and what the official investigations concluded.[^ch46-ssci-2014][^ch46-hrw-abu-ghraib][^ch46-check-silences]

### 2.6 Surveillance and the courts

- **The expansion.** Beyond the PATRIOT Act, the administration conducted warrantless surveillance of international communications, disclosed in 2005, and the foreign-intelligence court supervised orders under the amended Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act; the chapter should describe the sequence and the legal dispute from the record.[^ch46-patriot-aclu][^ch46-check-sources]
- **The courts.** The Supreme Court’s decisions of 2004 and 2006 on the detention of United States citizens and foreign nationals held as enemy combatants constrained and redefined parts of the program; the chapter should give the holdings and distinguish them from the political claims about them.[^ch46-check-sources][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **The stakes for §2.** The section’s question is whether emergency justified these delegations and whether the courts and Congress recovered their role; the chapter should hold the security claims and the liberty claims together rather than choosing a side.[^ch46-feingold-2001][^ch46-check-scope]

## 3. Afghanistan (2001–2008)

### 3.1 The claim: a war that toppled a regime and left an unfinished state

- **The claim.** The war in Afghanistan quickly removed the Taliban from power and disrupted al Qaeda, but the political settlement and the redirection of resources to Iraq left the war unfinished; the section asks what the United States accomplished and what it left undone by 2008.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the October 2001 campaign through the Bonn settlement, the failure to capture or kill Osama bin Laden at Tora Bora, and the shift of attention and resources to Iraq.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-bonn-agreement]

### 3.2 Operation Enduring Freedom

- **The campaign.** On October 7, 2001, the United States and Britain began air strikes against al Qaeda training camps and Taliban military installations; Bush said the strikes were “designed to disrupt the use of Afghanistan as a terrorist base of operations, and to attack the military capability of the Taliban regime.”[^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement]
- **The coalition and the Northern Alliance.** The United States supported the Northern Alliance, and after a series of victories its forces entered Kabul on November 13, 2001; the chapter should describe the campaign from the naval and defense histories and the Bush library guide.[^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement][^ch46-cfr-afghanistan]
- **The caution.** The chapter should give the coalition’s composition and the Taliban’s collapse as documented, and should not present the quick victory as a settled outcome of a long war.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 3.3 Bonn and the political settlement

- **The agreement.** In December 2001, Afghan factions meeting in Bonn under United Nations auspices signed the Agreement on Provisional Arrangements in Afghanistan Pending the Re-establishment of Permanent Government Institutions, which created an interim authority and set a path to a new constitution and elections.[^ch46-bonn-agreement]
- **The exclusion and the stakes.** The settlement produced a government under Hamid Karzai but rested on a narrow set of factions and depended on continuing foreign military support; the chapter should describe the settlement’s terms and its limits.[^ch46-bonn-agreement][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 Tora Bora and the redirection

- **The missed opportunity.** In December 2001, al Qaeda forces, including possibly Osama bin Laden, were cornered at Tora Bora; the reliance on Afghan militias and the failure to block escape routes became a symbol of the war’s limits. The chapter should describe what the record establishes and what remains uncertain.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **The shift to Iraq.** By 2002 the administration’s attention and resources moved toward Iraq; the chapter should show the diversion as a documented reallocation rather than a retrospective judgment alone.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-check-ch47]
- **The caution.** The claim that Tora Bora could have captured bin Laden is a counterfactual; the chapter should present the evidence and mark it as inference.[^ch46-check-debate-works]

## 4. Iraq, occupation, insurgency, and the surge (2002–2008)

### 4.1 The claim: a war justified by claims the evidence did not support

- **The claim.** The invasion of Iraq in March 2003 was justified principally by claims that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and posed a threat; the weapons were not found, the occupation and insurgency produced years of violence, and the 2007 surge changed the war’s course without producing the political settlement it was meant to enable.[^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-isg-2006]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the WMD case and the Iraq authorization through the invasion and the occupation to the insurgency, the Iraq Study Group, and the 2007 surge.[^ch46-powell-un-2003][^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-isg-2006][^ch46-bush-surge-2007]

### 4.2 The case for war

- **The authorization.** Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force Against Iraq Resolution of 2002, Public Law 107-243, 116 Stat. 1498, which President Bush signed on October 16, 2002; the House passed it 296–133 (Republicans 215–6, Democrats 81–126) and the Senate 77–23.[^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-iraq-aumf-votes]
- **The Powell presentation.** On February 5, 2003, Secretary of State Colin Powell presented the administration’s case to the United Nations Security Council, asserting that Iraq had concealed weapons of mass destruction in breach of Resolution 1441. The chapter should quote the presentation from the State Department record and note that Powell later called parts of it a blot on his record.[^ch46-powell-un-2003]
- **The caution.** The chapter must distinguish the claims used to justify the war from the later verified findings; the presentation is evidence of what the administration asserted, not evidence that the weapons existed.[^ch46-powell-un-2003][^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 4.3 The invasion and the occupation

- **The invasion.** The United States and a coalition invaded Iraq in March 2003 and quickly removed Saddam Hussein’s government; on May 1, 2003, Bush delivered what became known as the “mission accomplished” address aboard the USS *Abraham Lincoln*, while major combat operations were declared over even as an insurgency was forming.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-check-dates-appD]
- **The occupation.** The Coalition Provisional Authority governed Iraq and made early decisions — including the disbanding of the Iraqi army and the de-Baathification order — that critics argue deepened the insurgency. The chapter should describe the authority’s orders from the record and present the debate about their consequences.[^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **The insurgency and Abu Ghraib.** The insurgency grew through 2003 and 2004 amid sectarian violence, and the abuse of detainees at Abu Ghraib deepened the war’s political costs. The chapter should hold the military campaign and the detention record together.[^ch46-hrw-abu-ghraib][^ch46-check-silences]

### 4.4 The Iraq Study Group and the surge

- **The assessment.** The bipartisan Iraq Study Group, co-chaired by James A. Baker III and Lee H. Hamilton, issued its report on December 6, 2006, calling for new diplomatic and political efforts and a change in the primary mission of United States forces.[^ch46-isg-2006]
- **The surge.** On January 10, 2007, Bush announced a “New Way Forward” in Iraq, sending additional troops, replacing commanders, and endorsing a counterinsurgency strategy; the chapter should quote the address and give the troop figures with their sources.[^ch46-bush-surge-2007]
- **The results.** Violence fell after 2007, and the chapter should give the casualty and violence figures with their definitions and note the competing explanations — the surge, the Sunni Awakening, the sectarian separation of neighborhoods, and the cease-fire of the Mahdi Army — rather than assigning the decline to one cause.[^ch46-bush-surge-2007][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **The cost.** The war’s human and financial costs are large and method-dependent; the chapter should give the American and Iraqi casualty estimates and the fiscal cost with their sources and ranges, and should not present a single contested number as settled.[^ch46-costsofwar][^ch46-iraqbodycount][^ch46-check-numbers]

### 4.5 The weapons reckoning

- **The finding.** The Iraq Survey Group’s report, released on September 30, 2004, and presented by Charles Duelfer to the Senate Armed Services Committee on October 6, 2004, concluded that Iraq had destroyed its chemical and biological weapons stockpiles and ended its nuclear program after 1991, while seeking to preserve the capacity to reconstitute them if sanctions were lifted.[^ch46-duelfer-report]
- **The stakes.** The finding that the weapons did not exist as claimed is central to the chapter’s judgment that the war was justified by claims the evidence did not support; the chapter should present the finding and the administration’s response, not only the later verdict.[^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-check-debate-works]

## 5. Taxes, education, Medicare, Katrina, and the financial breakdown (2001–2008)

### 5.1 The claim: a domestic program and two failures of response

- **The claim.** The administration’s domestic record combined large tax reductions, an expanded federal role in education and prescription drugs, and a response to Hurricane Katrina widely judged inadequate, while the housing and credit boom ended in the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression; the section asks how a decade of war and tax reduction ended in collapse.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-fcic-report]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the tax cuts through the education law and the Medicare benefit, the federal response to Katrina, and the housing and financial breakdown, with Map 37.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-medicare-partd-2003][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-fcic-report]

### 5.2 The tax cuts and the fiscal turn

- **The first cut.** The Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001, H.R. 1836, became Public Law 107-16 on June 7, 2001, creating a new 10 percent bracket and phasing in reductions of the higher marginal rates.[^ch46-egtrra-2001]
- **The second cut.** A further reconciliation act in 2003 accelerated the rate reductions and reduced taxes on dividends and capital gains; the chapter should give both statutes and their Congressional Budget Office cost estimates, and should distinguish annual deficits from the accumulated debt.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-check-numbers]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give revenue and deficit figures from the Office of Management and Budget or the Congressional Budget Office, in current and inflation-adjusted terms, and should not attribute the fiscal turn to any single cause.[^ch46-check-numbers][^ch46-check-debate-works]

### 5.3 No Child Left Behind and Medicare Part D

- **The education law.** The No Child Left Behind Act of 2001, H.R. 1, became Public Law 107-110 on January 8, 2002, revising and reauthorizing the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and expanding federal testing and accountability requirements.[^ch46-nclb-2002]
- **The prescription-drug benefit.** The Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003, H.R. 1, became Public Law 108-173 on December 8, 2003, creating a voluntary prescription-drug benefit under Medicare (Part D), the largest expansion of the program since its creation.[^ch46-medicare-partd-2003]
- **The measure.** The chapter should give the benefit’s costs and the enrollment figures with their sources, and should note the debate over the drug-benefit provision that limited the government’s ability to negotiate prices.[^ch46-medicare-partd-2003][^ch46-check-numbers]

### 5.4 Hurricane Katrina and the federal response

- **The storm and the flood.** Hurricane Katrina made landfall on August 29, 2005; the federally built hurricane protection system failed, and significant levee failures occurred on the 17th Street Canal, the Industrial Canal, and the London Avenue Canal, flooding most of New Orleans.[^ch46-katrina-lessons]
- **The toll.** Katrina caused an estimated 1,833 deaths, and the National Weather Service attributes the loss of life and property largely to the levee breaks. The chapter should give the death toll and the damage figures with their sources and geographic scope.[^ch46-katrina-deaths][^ch46-katrina-lessons]
- **The response and the judgment.** The White House’s own review concluded that the magnitude of the storm “does not excuse our inadequate preparedness and response,” and the failure fell hardest on those who could not leave; the chapter should present the response as a documented institutional failure and name the silences in the record.[^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-check-silences]

### 5.5 The housing boom and the financial breakdown, with Map 37

- **The boom and the bust.** Rising house prices and the growth of subprime mortgage lending produced a boom that reversed in 2007; the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, created by Congress and reporting in 2011, examined the causes of the crisis and concluded that it was avoidable and that the failures included widespread failures in financial regulation and corporate governance.[^ch46-fcic-report]
- **The mechanism.** Federal Reserve Chairman Ben S. Bernanke distinguished the “triggers” of the crisis — most prominently expected losses on subprime mortgages — from the structural “vulnerabilities” that propagated them, including the too-big-to-fail problem; the chapter should present both registers and not reduce the crisis to a single cause.[^ch46-bernanke-2010]
- **The collapse.** Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008, and short-term funding markets seized; the chapter should give the sequence and the market and unemployment figures with their series and dates, and must not present the 2008 outcome as known to the actors of 2006.[^ch46-fcic-report][^ch46-check-numbers]
- **Map 37.** The registered visual for this section is `ch46-map-01` (Map 37, “Housing boom and crisis, 2000–2008”). It must show lending geography and mortgage exposure over a restricted 2000–2008 window, state its dates, geographic units, and data series, and distinguish measured lending from areas the data do not cover; it must not present a 2010 outcome before the chapter’s narrative endpoint or read a causal story off the map.[^ch46-check-map37]

### 5.6 TARP and the response

- **The statute.** The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008, H.R. 1424, became Public Law 110-343 on October 3, 2008, authorizing the Troubled Assets Relief Program to purchase troubled assets from financial institutions; the chapter should give the statute’s title, date, and authorized amount from the record.[^ch46-eesa-2008]
- **The hand-off.** The collapse and TARP are the chapter’s ending and the immediate prehistory of Chapter 47, which owns the recovery response; the chapter should end there and must not narrate the Obama administration’s policies.[^ch46-check-ch47][^ch46-check-bridge]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The attacks.** The September 11, 2001, attacks killed 2,977 people, including 441 first responders; the four hijacked airliners struck the World Trade Center, the Pentagon, and a field in Pennsylvania.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-911report-exec]
- **The authorizations and statutes.** The AUMF became Public Law 107-40 on September 18, 2001, after passing the Senate 98–0 and the House without objection; the USA PATRIOT Act became Public Law 107-56 on October 26, 2001; the Homeland Security Act became Public Law 107-296 on November 25, 2002.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-aumf-votes][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-homeland-security-act]
- **The wars.** Operation Enduring Freedom began on October 7, 2001, and the Iraq authorization became Public Law 107-243 on October 16, 2002; Bush announced the “New Way Forward” in Iraq on January 10, 2007.[^ch46-bush-afghanistan-statement][^ch46-iraq-aumf][^ch46-bush-surge-2007]
- **The weapons finding.** The Iraq Survey Group concluded in 2004 that Iraq had destroyed its chemical and biological stockpiles and ended its nuclear program after 1991.[^ch46-duelfer-report]
- **The domestic record and the collapse.** The tax cut became Public Law 107-16 on June 7, 2001, the education law became Public Law 107-110 on January 8, 2002, and the Medicare benefit became Public Law 108-173 on December 8, 2003; Katrina made landfall on August 29, 2005; and the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act became Public Law 110-343 on October 3, 2008.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-nclb-2002][^ch46-medicare-partd-2003][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-eesa-2008]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Did the emergency justify the delegations?** One account holds that the threat was novel and that broad authority was necessary; another holds that the statutes and the detention program exceeded what the law allowed and damaged the nation’s standing. The chapter should present each at its strongest and test it against the statutes and the courts.[^ch46-feingold-2001][^ch46-ssci-2014][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **Was the Iraq war a necessary response, a strategic blunder, or a deliberate misrepresentation?** Accounts stress ideology, credibility, intelligence failure, and the choices of individuals; the chapter should compare them and not present any as settled.[^ch46-leffler-2023][^ch46-powell-un-2003][^ch46-duelfer-report]
- **Did the surge work?** The decline in violence after 2007 is read by supporters as proof the strategy worked and by critics as the product of the Awakening, sectarian separation, and other factors; the chapter should state each and use the violence figures.[^ch46-bush-surge-2007][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **Did the tax cuts cause the deficits and the collapse?** One account links the fiscal turn and the crisis to the tax reductions and deregulation; another links the crisis to global capital flows and private risk-taking; the chapter should compare their methods and years.[^ch46-egtrra-2001][^ch46-fcic-report][^ch46-check-debate-works]
- **What did Katrina prove about the state?** The flood is read as evidence of a federal failure to protect, of a natural disaster that exceeded any response, or of the inequality the storm exposed; the chapter should present the alternatives fairly.[^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-check-silences]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the emergency produced real institutional changes whose security value and legal cost are both contested, in which the wars were justified by claims the evidence did not support, and in which a domestic program of tax reduction and expanded benefits ended in a financial collapse whose causes were multiple.[^ch46-911report-exec][^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-fcic-report]
- **Contingency.** The AUMF, the PATRIOT Act, the choice for war in Iraq, the Iraq Study Group’s alternatives, the surge, the tax cuts, and the TARP vote were all contingent; the chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch46-aumf-votes][^ch46-iraq-aumf-votes][^ch46-isg-2006][^ch46-eesa-2008]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter should hold the structural conditions — the altered threat, the global financial system, the fiscal position — together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation.[^ch46-check-scope][^ch46-fcic-report]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Between 2001 and 2008 the United States suffered the deadliest attack on its soil, fought two wars, created a new legal and institutional architecture of emergency, expanded federal education and health benefits while cutting taxes, failed in its response to a catastrophic flood, and ended the decade in a financial collapse; the chapter’s question — how emergency reorganized state power and public trust — is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch46-911memorial-events][^ch46-homeland-security-act][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-eesa-2008]

## Legacies

The emergency of 2001 left a legal and institutional architecture that outlasted the administration that built it. The authorizations, the surveillance statutes, the new department, and the detention program fixed frameworks that later governments would defend, revise, or abandon, and the question of how far emergency may expand executive power became a permanent feature of American constitutional debate. The chapter should connect these to the present as documented changes, not as a verdict.[^ch46-aumf-2001][^ch46-patriot-act][^ch46-ssci-2014]

The wars left two countries and two American publics changed. The invasion of Iraq, undertaken on claims the evidence did not support, and the unfinished war in Afghanistan shaped a generation of American foreign policy and the lives of Iraqis and Afghans; the human and financial costs are large and method-dependent, and the chapter should trace them to their sources.[^ch46-duelfer-report][^ch46-costsofwar][^ch46-iraqbodycount]

And the decade of tax reduction and expanded benefits ended in a financial collapse whose causes and whose response shaped the politics that followed. The housing boom and the credit crisis, the federal response to Katrina, and the program of tax reduction set terms that Chapter 47 takes up as it examines the recovery and the years after 2008.[^ch46-fcic-report][^ch46-katrina-lessons][^ch46-check-ch47]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The decade’s outcomes were made in Afghanistan, Iraq, Pakistan, and the global credit markets as well as in Washington. The wars, the detention program, and the financial crisis all show how international decisions bounded American choices; the chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch46-cfr-afghanistan][^ch46-isg-2006][^ch46-fcic-report]
- **Archives and silences.** The record over-represents presidents, officials, commanders, and journalists and under-represents the evacuees, the detainees, the Iraqi and Afghan civilians, and the displaced of the Gulf Coast. The chapter should name these asymmetries in the framing paragraph and here rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch46-check-silences][^ch46-ssci-2014][^ch46-katrina-lessons]
- **Distant connections.** Chapter 45 owns the 1990s and the 2000 election dispute; Chapter 47 owns the recovery response and the Obama administration. The chapter should cross-reference these rather than re-narrate them.[^ch46-check-ch45][^ch46-check-ch47]
