## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place and the date.** On Sunday, November 18, 1883, the railroads of the United States and Canada reset their clocks at the new standard-time noon, a day that came to be called “the Day of Two Noons,” because the clock struck noon once by the old local time and again by the new standard time.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- **The problem.** Before 1883, each city set its clocks by the sun across its own meridian, and hundreds of local times existed. Railroads kept their own company time, and stations often displayed several clocks for the lines they served; travelers could not rely on a single time.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-loc-two-noons]
- **The choice of a private standard.** On October 11, 1883, the General Time Convention met at the Grand Pacific Hotel in Chicago, where William F. Allen, secretary of the convention and publisher of the *Traveler’s Official Railway Guide*, presented a plan for standardized railway time. The railroads adopted five zones named Intercolonial, Eastern, Central, Mountain, and Pacific, set to the mean sun-time on the 75th, 90th, 105th, and 120th meridians west of Greenwich, one hour apart.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-loc-two-noons]
- **The mechanism.** Both the United States Naval Observatory and the Allegheny Observatory in Pittsburgh sent telegraph signals to help the railroads synchronize their clocks, and the railroad companies persuaded city governments to adopt the new standard.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- **The dissent.** Contemporary opinion was divided. The *Indianapolis Sentinel* published a condemnation of railroad time: “People will have to marry by railroad time and die by railroad time. Ministers will be required to preach by railroad time — banks will open and close by railroad time — in fact, the Railroad Convention has taken charge of the time business.”[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- **What the scene can show.** The scene can show a private association of railroad officials setting a standard that governed the nation’s public life, a city resetting its clocks, and a newspaper objecting that a business convention had taken charge of time itself. It cannot show what an individual New Yorker thought or said on that Sunday.[^ch24-check-scene]

### The scene’s argument

- **A private body sets a public standard, argued from the record.** The scene’s claim is not that the railroads “invented time,” but that they standardized the nation’s clocks and then had their standard adopted by cities and, decades later, by Congress. The chapter’s later sections show the same pattern in steel, oil, finance, and labor: private enterprises making decisions that organized public life.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-check-scope]
- **The stakes.** The federal government did not make standard time law until the Standard Time Act of 1918, which also created daylight saving time; for thirty-five years the railroads’ private standard governed the nation’s clocks. The scene sets up the chapter’s question about how private enterprises acquired public power.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-check-ch25]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did private enterprises acquire public power?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the clocks to the railroads, the trusts, the immigrant districts, the steelworks, and the Pullman town.

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The convention, the plan, the observatories, and the newspaper reaction are documented. The weather, the crowd, and the private thoughts of the officials must not be staged beyond what witnesses recorded, and no New Yorker’s voice may be manufactured.[^ch24-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** In November 1883, no one knew that Congress would enact standard time in 1918 or that time zones would remain the railroads’ legacy after passenger trains declined. The scene must preserve the uncertainty of the moment.[^ch24-check-scene][^ch24-check-dates]
- **No claim that the nation adopted standard time uniformly and at once.** Many cities adopted the new standard immediately and many more within a year, but the change was contested and uneven, and the exact number of local times is reported differently across sources. The chapter should give a range with its source.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-check-numbers]

## 1. Railroads, Crédit Mobilier, and standard time

### 1.1 The railroad as the first big business

- **The claim.** The railroad was the first American business to require large fixed capital, professional management, and coordinated operation at a distance, and it became the model for the corporation that followed.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]
- **The scale.** By 1865 the United States had an estimated 35,000 miles of track, and construction continued at great speed through the 1870s and 1880s. The railroads were the nation’s largest nonagricultural employer and the largest consumers of steel, coal, and timber.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- **The measure.** **Figure 4 plan.** The registered visual `ch24-figure-01` (Figure 4, “Industry and urbanization, 1865–1900”) belongs here. It should use separate panels for rail mileage, steel output, and the urban share of population, because the three series use different units and cannot be plotted on one scale. It needs a caption, source line, credit, alt text, and accessible description, and it must reconcile with the register and Appendix B, Tables 1 and 7. The rail series should name the consistent source series and year of observation; the steel panel should define units and distinguish Bessemer from total steel; the urban-share panel should state the Census threshold used in each decade.[^ch24-check-figure]
- **The consequence.** The railroad’s need for capital, timetables, and management produced both the modern corporation and the standardization of time, which leads to §1.3.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand][^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]

### 1.2 Public subsidy and the Crédit Mobilier

- **The claim.** The transcontinental railroads were built with extraordinary federal and state subsidy, and the Crédit Mobilier scandal showed how that public money could be diverted to private profit and political corruption.[^ch24-house-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- **The subsidy.** The Pacific Railroad Acts of 1862 and 1864 chartered the Union Pacific and authorized federal land grants and bonds for the transcontinental line. Because no railroad yet existed to generate revenue, the money was made in constructing the road.[^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- **The mechanism.** Union Pacific officials created Crédit Mobilier of America to build the road; it billed Union Pacific more than the work cost, and the profits went to the same men who controlled both companies. One of them, Representative Oakes Ames of Massachusetts, distributed shares at bargain rates to roughly a dozen high-ranking colleagues, including Speaker Schuyler Colfax, to buy political protection.[^ch24-house-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- **The exposure.** On September 4, 1872, the *New York Sun* exposed the scheme on its front page under the headline “THE KING OF FRAUDS.” The House appointed a select committee chaired by Representative Luke Poland in December 1872, and on February 27, 1873, the House censured Ames and Representative James Brooks for using their political influence for personal financial gain.[^ch24-house-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- **The consequence.** The scandal tied corporate promotion to political corruption in the public mind and helped discredit the railroad securities market, which contributed to the Panic of 1873 and the long depression that followed.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-check-ch23]

### 1.3 Standard time and the standardization of the nation

- **The claim.** The standardization of time was a private achievement with public consequences, and it shows how corporate decisions organized the daily life of citizens before the federal government acted.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog][^ch24-loc-two-noons]
- **The proposal.** Charles F. Dowd proposed a system of national time zones in the late 1860s and 1870; William F. Allen adapted the idea, and the General Time Convention adopted it on October 11, 1883, setting new standard noon for November 18, 1883.[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- **The reaction.** Contemporary opinion divided. Many welcomed the convenience; some objected to what they considered a violation of the natural order, and the *Indianapolis Sentinel*’s editorial (quoted in the opening scene) mocked the Railroad Convention for having “taken charge of the time business.”[^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- **The exception to remember.** The federal government did not codify standard time until the Standard Time Act of 1918, and it placed the authority to set zone boundaries with the Interstate Commerce Commission. The chapter should not date federal authority earlier than it existed.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-check-dates]

### 1.4 Regulation: *Wabash*, the Interstate Commerce Act

- **The claim.** The first federal regulation of a private industry was itself a product of corporate power: railroads charged discriminatory rates, states tried to regulate, the Supreme Court struck the state laws down, and Congress responded with the Interstate Commerce Act and the Interstate Commerce Commission.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]
- **The problem.** Railroads set prices, excluded competitors, and favored large shippers with rebates while charging more for short hauls than for long ones, which fell hardest on farmers and small shippers. Granger-controlled state legislatures in the West and South passed regulatory laws upheld in *Munn v. Illinois* (1877).[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce]
- **The opening and the closing.** In *Wabash, St. Louis & Pacific Railway Co. v. Illinois* (1886), the Supreme Court held that states could not regulate interstate railroad rates, which left the federal government as the only regulator. Congress responded with the Interstate Commerce Act, approved on February 4, 1887, creating the five-member Interstate Commerce Commission and requiring “reasonable and just” rates and an end to rebates and to the long-haul/short-haul discrimination.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]
- **The limit.** The act did not give the commission the power to set rates directly, and the railroads often contested its authority; the chapter should present the act as a first step, not a solution.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]

## 2. Steel, oil, finance, and trusts

### 2.1 Steel and the Bessemer process

- **The claim.** Cheap steel made from the Bessemer process transformed construction, transportation, and manufacturing, and Andrew Carnegie built the most efficient integrated steel enterprise in the country.[^ch24-loc-homestead][^ch24-nps-pullman-story]
- **The technology.** The Bessemer process, patented in the 1850s, made steel cheap enough for rails and structures. Carnegie’s Edgar Thomson Steel Works, built near Pittsburgh with the process at its center, poured its first steel in the 1870s and grew into a system that controlled ore, coke, furnaces, mills, and shipping.[^ch24-loc-homestead][^ch24-check-sources]
- **The strategy.** Carnegie’s “vertical integration” and relentless cost cutting let him undersell competitors and expand in downturns. The chapter should distinguish the technological achievement from the labor regime that made it profitable, which §4 takes up at Homestead.[^ch24-loc-homestead][^ch24-yawp-carnegie]
- **The measure.** The steel panel of Figure 4 should show the growth of American steel output and the fall in its price, with units, source series, and observation years named.[^ch24-check-figure]

### 2.2 Rockefeller and Standard Oil

- **The claim.** John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil on refining, cost control, and railroad rebates, and by the late 1880s it controlled about 90 percent of American refining.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-archives-sherman]
- **The company.** Rockefeller formed the Standard Oil Company on January 10, 1870, and within the first three months of 1872 he had bought out, shut down, or bankrupted 22 of his 26 Cleveland competitors. Standard Oil’s size let it negotiate railroad rebates in exchange for guaranteed volume, which smaller refiners could not match.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil]
- **The trust.** On January 2, 1882, Standard Oil’s attorney Samuel Dodd devised the trust: stockholders of the component companies transferred their shares to a board of nine trustees and received trust certificates in return, and the trustees ran all the companies as one. The form spread to sugar, tobacco, and other industries.[^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-loc-standard-oil]
- **The consequence.** Standard Oil’s control of the market helped prompt the antitrust laws of the 1880s and 1890s, which leads to §2.4.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-archives-sherman]

### 2.3 Finance and the investment banker

- **The claim.** As the trusts grew, the investment banker became the organizer and disciplinarian of American industry, and J. Pierpont Morgan’s interventions made finance a form of private government over the corporations.[^ch24-check-sources][^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]
- **The mechanism.** Morgan and other bankers consolidated railroads, imposed reorganization plans on failing lines, placed their partners on corporate boards, and underwrote the securities that financed mergers. This power rested on access to capital and on the dependence of companies on the banker’s judgment.[^ch24-check-sources]
- **The limit.** The chapter should present Morgan’s rise as a documented development of the 1880s and 1890s and note that the great merger into United States Steel and the Northern Securities episode fall at or after 1900, so they belong as clearly labeled consequence rather than as the chapter’s narrative. This point needs a direct source and is an open check.[^ch24-check-sources][^ch24-check-dates]

### 2.4 The trust, the holding company, and the Sherman Act

- **The claim.** The trust was a legal device for combining competitors, and the Sherman Anti-Trust Act of 1890 was Congress’s first attempt to forbid it, but the statute was too loosely worded and too narrowly read to stop consolidation before 1900.[^ch24-archives-sherman]
- **The statute.** Section 1 declared illegal “every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States,” and Section 2 forbade monopolization. The Senate passed it 51–1 on April 8, 1890; the House passed it 242–0 on June 20, 1890; President Benjamin Harrison signed it on July 2, 1890.[^ch24-archives-sherman]
- **The weakness.** The act did not define “trust,” “combination,” “conspiracy,” or “monopoly,” and in *United States v. E. C. Knight Company* (1895) the Supreme Court held that a company controlling about 98 percent of sugar refining had not violated the law, because control of manufacture was not control of trade. The ruling seemed to end federal trust regulation until the twentieth century.[^ch24-archives-sherman]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should show that the antitrust impulse existed and that the courts and the statute’s wording limited it, rather than claiming that the law broke the trusts before 1900.[^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-check-dates]

## 3. Immigration and cities

### 3.1 Old and new immigration

- **The claim.** The sources and character of European immigration shifted in the 1880s and 1890s, and the change fed both the industrial labor force and a nativist reaction.[^ch24-loc-immigration]
- **The numbers and the shift.** Nearly 12 million immigrants arrived in the United States between 1870 and 1900. In the 1870s and 1880s the great majority came from Germany, Ireland, and England; by the 1890s, rising numbers came from southern and eastern Europe, and the “new immigration” became a contested phrase.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-check-sources]
- **The gateways.** More than 70 percent of immigrants entered through New York City, most of them at the Castle Garden depot until the federal government opened a new processing center on Ellis Island on January 1, 1892. Ellis Island operated until 1954 and processed over 12 million steamship passengers.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-nps-ellis]
- **The limit.** The chapter should give the decade totals with their source and should not treat “old” and “new” immigration as a clean contrast, since both continued and the categories were invented partly by nativists.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-check-names]

### 3.2 Chinese exclusion as labor and immigration policy

- **The claim.** Chinese exclusion began as a labor and immigration policy of the West and became federal law in 1882, and it is part of this chapter’s industrial story rather than a separate topic.[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion]
- **The exclusion act.** Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act, officially “An act to execute certain treaty stipulations relating to Chinese,” and President Chester A. Arthur signed it on May 6, 1882. It suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years, and it rested on the declared premise that “the coming of Chinese laborers to this country endangers the good order of certain localities.”[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion]
- **The extension.** Congress extended exclusion for another ten years in the Geary Act of 1892 and made it permanent in 1902. The Library of Congress guide records the sequence and the 1943 repeal.[^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-state-chinese-immigration]
- **The labor context.** Chinese immigrants had worked in the gold mines, agriculture, and the garment industry and were “particularly instrumental in building railroads in the American west.” As their numbers grew, anti-Chinese sentiment among other workers grew with them, and exclusion followed.[^ch24-state-chinese-immigration][^ch24-loc-immigration]
- **The ownership rule.** Chinese exclusion belongs to this chapter as labor and immigration policy. The later Angel Island immigration station, which opened in 1910, belongs to Chapter 29, and the chapter must not date it here.[^ch24-check-ch29][^ch24-check-scope]

### 3.3 The tenement and the industrial city

- **The claim.** Industrial cities grew faster than their housing and sanitation, and the tenement became the characteristic form of working-class urban life.[^ch24-yawp-riis][^ch24-census-urbanization]
- **The growth.** The share of the population living in places the Census classified as urban rose from 35.1 percent in 1890, and by 1900 about four in ten Americans lived in cities. The Census threshold for “urban” changed across the period, which any comparison must state.[^ch24-census-urbanization][^ch24-check-figure]
- **The tenement.** Jacob Riis, a Danish immigrant and police reporter, published *How the Other Half Lives* in 1890. He wrote that three-fourths of New York’s people lived in tenements, and that “the fifteen thousand tenant houses that were the despair of the sanitarian in the past generation have swelled into thirty-seven thousand, and more than twelve hundred thousand persons call them home.”[^ch24-yawp-riis]
- **The evidence and its limits.** Riis’s account combines documented figures with reform rhetoric and staged photographs; the chapter should read it as a reformer’s argument with real data, not as a neutral description.[^ch24-yawp-riis][^ch24-check-race]

### 3.4 The urban political economy

- **The claim.** The industrial city was also a political order, in which machines, ward bosses, and employers governed immigrant and working-class communities, and in which workers built unions, churches, and mutual institutions.[^ch24-loc-organized-labor][^ch24-yawp-riis]
- **The frame.** Two sentences should place the city beside the factory: the same decades that produced the trust produced the ethnic ward, the settlement house’s forerunners, and the mass-circulation press. This section should hold the connection without duplicating Chapter 28’s account of mass culture.[^ch24-check-scope]
- **The limit.** The chapter should name the racial and religious hierarchy inside the immigrant city, including the exclusion of Chinese and other Asian immigrants and the segregation of Black migrants, rather than describing “the immigrants” as one population.[^ch24-check-race][^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion]

## 4. Labor from 1877 through Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman

### 4.1 The Great Railroad Strike of 1877

- **The claim.** The first nationwide strike began among railroad workers over wage cuts and became a general uprising of industrial workers, suppressed by militia and federal troops.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-check-sources]
- **The course.** The strike began on the Baltimore & Ohio at Martinsburg, West Virginia, on July 16, 1877, after another wage cut, and spread along the rail lines through West Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, New York, Missouri, and Illinois. In Pittsburgh the militia fired into a crowd and the city’s rail yards burned.[^ch24-loc-1877]
- **The toll.** Estimates of the dead vary; the Library of Congress guide records Pittsburgh’s official death toll as 26 but likely higher, and other accounts give about 100 killed nationwide and roughly 1,000 jailed. The chapter should give a range with sources rather than one number.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-check-numbers]
- **The consequence.** The strike won almost no immediate concessions and showed both employers and the state that industrial conflict would be met with force, which leads to §4.2.[^ch24-loc-1877]

### 4.2 Haymarket and the eight-hour movement

- **The claim.** The Haymarket affair of 1886 grew out of the eight-hour-day movement, produced a bomb and a wave of repression, and damaged the Knights of Labor and the broader labor movement.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-loc-organized-labor]
- **The context.** On May 1, 1886, Chicago unionists, socialists, anarchists, and ordinary workers made the city the center of the national movement for an eight-hour day; 35,000 workers walked off their jobs, and police clashed with strikers repeatedly. At the McCormick reaper plant on May 3, police fired on strikers and killed at least two.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket]
- **The bombing.** At a protest meeting on Des Plaines Street on the evening of May 4, someone threw a bomb at police; one officer died instantly, sixty were injured, and eight eventually died. The police fired into the crowd, and an undetermined number of the crowd were killed or wounded.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket]
- **The trial and the aftermath.** Eight anarchists were tried and convicted; four were hanged on November 11, 1887, one died by suicide in prison, and three were pardoned by Governor John Peter Altgeld in 1893 after he criticized the trial. The Knights of Labor, associated with Haymarket in the public mind, declined sharply after 1886.[^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-loc-organized-labor]

### 4.3 The Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor

- **The claim.** The two leading organizations of the 1880s represented different strategies: the Knights of Labor tried to organize all workers, skilled and unskilled, while the American Federation of Labor organized skilled craft unions and bargained for concrete gains.[^ch24-loc-organized-labor][^ch24-check-sources]
- **The Knights.** Founded in 1869 and led after 1879 by Terence V. Powderly, the Knights welcomed workers across trades and, at least in principle, across race and gender, and reached roughly 700,000 members by 1886. The Library of Congress guide records the founding and Powderly’s leadership, and the decline after Haymarket.[^ch24-loc-organized-labor]
- **The AFL.** In 1886 the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions became the American Federation of Labor, led by Samuel Gompers and other craft-union officials, and it grew by concentrating on wages, hours, and working conditions for skilled workers.[^ch24-loc-organized-labor]
- **The limit.** The AFL’s craft strategy excluded most Black, immigrant, and women workers from its core unions, an exclusion the chapter should state rather than pass over. This claim needs a direct source and is an open check.[^ch24-check-race][^ch24-check-sources]

### 4.4 In Focus: a Pullman lease

- **The claim.** One identified Pullman lease shows how the company town turned housing, water, and gas into instruments of dependence, and how the company’s property right reached into every household.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-nps-pullman-story]
- **In Focus: the document.** **In Focus.** In 1885 the economist Richard T. Ely described the town of Pullman and the lease on which every tenant held a house. Ely wrote that ‘every tenant holds his house on a lease which may be terminated on ten days’ notice,’ and he transcribed the form: “From \_\_\_ to \_\_\_, unless sooner cancelled in accordance with the conditions of the lease,” adding that “it is not necessary that any reason be assigned for the notice,” and that “it is expressly agreed that the fact that rent may have been paid at any time in advance shall not be a waiver of the right to put an end to the term and tenancy under this lease by such notice.”[^ch24-ely-pullman]
- **How to read it.** The box should treat the lease as evidence of the company’s power in the town. Ely recorded that rents of the dwellings varied from $14 to $25 a month, exclusive of the water charge, and that a five-room cottage rented for $17 a month; the company retained title to “every square rod of ground,” and no church or other organization could occupy anything but rented quarters.[^ch24-ely-pullman]
- **The evidence and its limits.** Ely’s article is a documented 1885 observer’s account that transcribes the lease form but does not identify a named tenant or reproduce a signed and dated lease. The chapter should present it as the recorded lease form and add that an original executed lease is the target for the In Focus reproduction. If a facsimile is reproduced, it must clear rights and be registered separately; otherwise the box reads the form in prose only.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-check-focus]
- **The historical connection.** Rents were set to yield a return on the company’s investment in the town, while wages were not guaranteed, and workers could not buy their homes. When wages fell in 1893 and 1894, rents did not, which is the grievance that leads to §4.5.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-nps-pullman-story]

### 4.5 Pullman, 1894, and the injunction

- **The claim.** The Pullman strike of 1894 joined the grievances of a company town to a nationwide railroad boycott, and the federal injunction that broke it established the courts as the principal instrument of industrial discipline.[^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman][^ch24-fjc-debs]
- **The grievance.** After the depression of 1893, Pullman cut wages by about a quarter while rents held steady. The workers’ statement to the American Railway Union convention in June 1894 testified that five reductions in wages had swept the shops between May and December 1893, the last nearly 30 percent, and “our rents had not fallen,” and that “no man or woman of us all has ever owned or can ever hope to own one inch of George M. Pullman’s land.”[^ch24-pullman-workers-statement]
- **The strike and the boycott.** The workers walked off the job on May 11, 1894, and in late June the ARU, led by Eugene V. Debs, called a boycott of Pullman cars; by late June at least 125,000 ARU members had walked out in support, halting freight and mail traffic across much of the country.[^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]
- **The injunction.** When Governor John P. Altgeld refused to request federal troops, U.S. Attorney General Richard Olney sought the first federal injunction against a strike, and President Grover Cleveland sent about 2,000 troops to Illinois to enforce it; violence followed and Debs and other leaders were arrested. Debs served six months for violating the injunction.[^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman][^ch24-fjc-debs]
- **The legal result.** In 1895 the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the injunction in *In re Debs*, holding that the federal government could act to protect interstate commerce and the mails. The chapter should quote the Court’s own words, including Justice David Brewer’s statement that the United States “may remove everything put upon highways, natural or artificial, to obstruct the passage of interstate commerce, or the carrying of the mails.”[^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

## 5. Inequality and its defenders and critics

### 5.1 The distribution question

- **The claim.** The second industrial revolution produced both unprecedented wealth and visible poverty, and Americans argued about whether the inequality was the price of progress, the result of unjust law, or a problem to be fixed by organization.[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-yawp-carnegie]
- **The frame.** The chapter should state the argument plainly: defenders treated competition and concentration as natural and beneficial; critics treated land monopoly and the wage system as the causes of poverty; organizers treated collective action as the remedy. The section should not reduce the debate to “robber barons versus reformers.”
- **The measure.** The discussion should use the era’s own terms and, where it uses statistics, give the source series and the observation year. Concrete distribution figures for the period remain an open check.[^ch24-check-numbers]

### 5.2 The defenders

- **The claim.** Defenders of the new order, from Herbert Spencer’s social philosophy to William Graham Sumner’s essays and Andrew Carnegie’s “Gospel of Wealth,” argued that competition and inequality were the engine of progress and that the wealthy held their surplus as trustees for the community.[^ch24-yawp-carnegie][^ch24-check-sources]
- **Carnegie.** In “Wealth” (1889), Carnegie wrote that the concentration of business “in the hands of a few” was “not only beneficial, but essential for the future progress of the race,” and that the rich should administer their surplus as “trustee for the poor.”[^ch24-yawp-carnegie]
- **The limit.** The chapter should present the defenders’ arguments in their own strongest terms rather than as hypocrisy, and should identify Spencer’s and Sumner’s works, which were consulted only through descriptions and remain open checks.[^ch24-check-sources][^ch24-check-epigraphs]

### 5.3 The critics

- **The claim.** Critics proposed structural remedies: Henry George a single tax on land values, and Edward Bellamy a cooperative commonwealth, and both found large audiences in the 1880s and 1890s.[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-check-sources]
- **George.** In *Progress and Poverty* (1879), George called the association of poverty with progress “the great enigma of our times,” blamed it on the monopoly of land, and proposed a “single tax” on land values. In 1886 he ran for mayor of New York City as the candidate of the United Labor Party, showing how far the critique had entered politics.[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-check-sources]
- **Bellamy.** Edward Bellamy’s novel *Looking Backward, 2000–1887* (1888) imagined a cooperative future and sold hundreds of thousands of copies; this work was consulted only through descriptions and remains an open check.[^ch24-check-sources][^ch24-check-scope]

### 5.4 The measure

- **The claim.** The chapter should give the inequality debate a quantitative footing without overclaiming, naming the source series for wages, prices, and the distribution of income and wealth.[^ch24-check-numbers][^ch24-check-figure]
- **The frame.** Where the chapter uses a figure, it should come from the same series used in Figure 4 or from Appendix B, Table 7, with the observation year and the price index named for any inflation adjustment.[^ch24-check-figure][^ch24-check-numbers]
- **The limit.** Period inequality estimates are contested, and the chapter should present them as estimates with their methods rather than as settled facts.[^ch24-check-numbers]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The scale and concentration of industry are documented.** Railroad mileage, steel output, and the urban share of the population all rose sharply between 1865 and 1900, and firms such as Standard Oil and Carnegie Steel dominated their industries.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-loc-homestead]
- **Public subsidy and public standardization were real.** The transcontinental railroads received federal land grants and bonds, and the railroads set a standard of time that the federal government adopted only in 1918.[^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-two-noons]
- **Labor conflict was pervasive.** The great strikes of 1877, 1886, 1892, and 1894 were national events, and the state and the courts intervened on the side of employers.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket][^ch24-loc-homestead][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]
- **The law lagged the corporations.** The Interstate Commerce Act and the Sherman Act were real federal efforts, but both were limited in wording and narrowed by the courts before 1900.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-archives-sherman]
- **Immigration restriction was racialized.** The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 singled out one nationality for exclusion, and its extension in 1892 and permanence in 1902 fixed the principle for decades.[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Why did corporate consolidation happen?** Historians disagree about whether it was mainly an efficiency gained through management, a product of public subsidy and the law, or an outcome of the struggle between capital and labor.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand][^ch24-white-republic]
- **How much did the state build the market?** The weight of land grants, tariffs, courts, and coercion in creating the corporate order remains debated.[^ch24-white-republic][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- **How should the labor upheavals be explained?** Whether the great strikes were defensive reactions to wage cuts, expressions of a working-class republicanism, or the birth pangs of modern unionism is contested.[^ch24-check-sources][^ch24-loc-organized-labor]
- **How much did “new immigration” change the country, and how new was it?** The categories were partly nativist inventions, and their analytic value is debated.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-check-names]
- **When should the era be said to end?** The chapter’s 1900 endpoint is a planning choice; the merger movement, U.S. Steel, and the great antitrust actions fall at or just after it, and the chapter must label them as consequence rather than as the main narrative.[^ch24-check-dates][^ch24-check-scope]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which corporate power was built at once by organizational innovation, by public subsidy and law, and by the defeat of labor’s counter-organization; no single explanation accounts for the era, and the chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand][^ch24-white-republic]
- **The managerial position.** Chandler argued that the modern business enterprise and its salaried managers lowered costs and coordinated production and distribution more efficiently than markets, and that this “visible hand” of management replaced the “invisible hand” of market forces in many sectors.[^ch24-chandler-visible-hand]
- **The political-economy position.** Richard White treats the Gilded Age as a period in which the state, the courts, and public subsidy were constitutive of corporate power, and in which the costs fell on workers, immigrants, and Indigenous nations.[^ch24-white-republic]
- **The labor position.** Labor historians emphasize workers’ own organization, the “great upheaval” of the 1880s, and the reconstruction of workplace authority, and they read Homestead and Pullman as defeats of an alternative order rather than as mere riots. These works were discovered but not yet consulted.[^ch24-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Corporate power was acquired, not simply earned: through management, through law and subsidy, and through the suppression of labor’s claims, and the chapter should state each mechanism with its evidence.[^ch24-check-table][^ch24-white-republic]

## Legacies

The second industrial revolution gave the United States the productive capacity and the corporate institutions that would define the twentieth century. It also concentrated power over time, work, and place in private hands, and it left a set of questions that outlived the era. The railroads set the nation’s clocks before Congress did; the trusts organized whole industries before the antitrust laws could reach them; and the courts made the injunction the instrument that ended the great strikes.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]

The era’s exclusions were as durable as its achievements. Chinese exclusion fixed a racial principle in immigration law that lasted until 1943; the craft unions of the AFL largely excluded Black, immigrant, and women workers; and the tenement and the company town organized the lives of millions who had come for the promise of the “Golden Door.”[^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-loc-organized-labor][^ch24-yawp-riis]

The chapter’s lasting legacy is a set of questions that passed to the twentieth century: whether a republic could govern corporations it had chartered and subsidized, whether a wage was enough to make a worker free, and whether the standardization of time and the concentration of capital had made a new kind of citizenship. Those questions passed to the West that Chapter 25 describes, and to the reform movements that Chapter 29 takes up.[^ch24-check-ch25][^ch24-check-ch29]

## Summary

- On November 18, 1883, the railroads standardized the nation’s time on their own authority, a change Congress did not make law until 1918, and the day was called “the Day of Two Noons.”[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-loc-standard-time-blog]
- The transcontinental railroads were built with federal land grants and bonds, and the Crédit Mobilier scandal of 1872 diverted construction profits to private and political hands; the House censured Oakes Ames and James Brooks in 1873.[^ch24-house-credit-mobilier][^ch24-loc-credit-mobilier]
- Rockefeller formed Standard Oil in 1870, organized the Standard Oil Trust in 1882, and by the late 1880s controlled about 90 percent of American refining; the Sherman Anti-Trust Act of 1890 was narrowed by *E. C. Knight* in 1895.[^ch24-loc-standard-oil][^ch24-archives-sherman]
- The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 created the first federal independent regulatory commission after *Wabash v. Illinois* (1886) struck down state rate regulation.[^ch24-archives-interstate-commerce][^ch24-senate-interstate-commerce]
- Nearly 12 million immigrants arrived between 1870 and 1900, most through New York; Ellis Island opened on January 1, 1892; and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 suspended Chinese labor immigration for ten years, extended in 1892 and made permanent in 1902.[^ch24-loc-immigration][^ch24-nps-ellis][^ch24-archives-chinese-exclusion][^ch24-loc-chinese-exclusion]
- The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 began at Martinsburg on July 16 and was suppressed by militia and federal troops; Haymarket (1886) produced a bombing, a mass trial, and the hangings of four anarchists in 1887.[^ch24-loc-1877][^ch24-chicagohistory-haymarket]
- The Knights of Labor reached roughly 700,000 members by 1886 and declined after Haymarket, while the American Federation of Labor, founded the same year under Samuel Gompers, organized skilled craft workers.[^ch24-loc-organized-labor]
- At Homestead in 1892, at least three Pinkertons and seven workers were killed and 8,500 National Guard troops occupied the town; the union was broken.[^ch24-loc-homestead]
- The Pullman lease tied housing to company control, the strike of 1894 joined a company town to a nationwide boycott, and *In re Debs* (1895) upheld the federal injunction that broke the strike.[^ch24-ely-pullman][^ch24-constitutioncenter-pullman]
- Henry George called the association of poverty with progress the “great enigma of our times” and proposed a single tax on land, while Andrew Carnegie defended the concentration of business as “essential for the future progress of the race.”[^ch24-yawp-george][^ch24-yawp-carnegie]

> *A closing note.* The second industrial revolution made the United States the world’s leading industrial power and made the corporation the organizing institution of American life. It standardized time, built the trust, filled the cities, and broke the great strikes; and it left a republic with more productive capacity than it had institutions to govern it. The questions it raised — about the boundary between private and public power, the meaning of a wage, and who belonged to the industrial nation — were not settled in 1900.[^ch24-loc-two-noons][^ch24-archives-sherman][^ch24-loc-organized-labor]
