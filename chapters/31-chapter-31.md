# Chapter 31. The First World War and Its Aftermath (1917–1920)

> “The world must be made safe for democracy. Its peace must be planted upon the tested foundations of political liberty. We have no selfish ends to serve.”[^ch31-yawp-wilson-war]

> — President Woodrow Wilson, address to a joint session of Congress, April 2, 1917.[^ch31-yawp-wilson-war]

> “They have always taught you that it is your patriotic duty to go to war and slaughter yourselves at their command. You have never had a voice in the war. The working class who make the sacrifices, who shed the blood, have never yet had a voice in declaring war.”[^ch31-nara-debs]
>
> — Eugene V. Debs, speech at Canton, Ohio, June 16, 1918.[^ch31-nara-debs]

> “In 1920, states ratified the Nineteenth Amendment and thereby guaranteed voting rights to women across the country. Or so it is said.”[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]
>
> — Lisa Tetrault, “Winning the Vote: A Divided Movement Brought About the Nineteenth Amendment,” *Humanities*, 2019.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]

A president promised that the war would make the world safe for democracy. A socialist answered that the working class had no voice in the war that spent its blood. A historian warns that the amendment which ended one exclusion is a story told too simply. The three disagree about what total mobilization did to a democracy. This chapter asks whether a republic can conscript, censor, and classify its own people and remain the republic it claims to be.[^ch31-yawp-wilson-war][^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-check-epigraphs]

At 9:30 a.m. on July 20, 1917, Secretary of War Newton D. Baker drew the first capsule from a glass bowl. The capsule held a slip of paper with the number 258. The drawing continued into the early hours of the next morning, and 10,500 numbers were drawn. Each number stood for a man who had registered under the Selective Service Act, which President Woodrow Wilson had signed on May 18. The act required men between 21 and 30 to register, and a later amendment extended the ages to 18 through 45. Local boards registered them, assigned serial numbers, classified them, and called them forward. The serial numbers were printed on slips, sealed in gelatin capsules, mixed with a ladle, and drawn. The local boards and the lottery together were meant to establish a feeling of fairness. The bowl, the capsules, and the ladle were part of the presentation.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]

The lottery was necessary because volunteering had failed. The United States entered the war in April 1917. The government expected about a million volunteers and received about 73,000. Within several months of the act’s passage, more than 10 million men had registered; by the war’s end, about 24 million had.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft][^ch31-selective-service]

The record establishes the date, the officials, the bowl, the capsules, the first number, and the count. It does not record what Baker said, what the spectators felt, or what any registrant thought when the numbers were posted. No account consulted here describes the room or the weather. The scene must be built from what the record holds and must stop where the record stops.[^ch31-check-scene]

What did total mobilization do to a democracy?

This chapter covers the years from 1917 to 1920. It follows the machinery of conscription and economic mobilization; the raising, training, and shipping of the American Expeditionary Forces; the propaganda, censorship, and sedition that narrowed wartime dissent; the Great Migration, the racial violence of 1919, the influenza pandemic, the labor strikes, and the completion of the Nineteenth Amendment; and the peace, the Senate’s rejection of the Treaty of Versailles, and the Red Scare.[^ch31-check-scope]

It draws on statutes and presidential messages, the records of the Selective Service System and the American Expeditionary Forces, the case files of the Espionage and Sedition Acts, the casualty and health statistics of the War Department, the reports of the Bureau of Investigation, and the secondary literature on the war. It treats “mobilization,” “total war,” “loyalty,” “sedition,” “slacker,” “the Red Scare,” and “self-determination” as contested period terms that require definition rather than neutral descriptions.[^ch31-check-scope][^ch31-check-names]

The official record over-represents the state and its enforcers and under-represents the conscripts, evaders, dissidents, migrants, strikers, women, and Black Americans whose lives the war reordered. The statutes, boards, casualty lists, case files, and raid reports show what the state decided and recorded, not what those populations believed. The imbalance is named here rather than filled with invented voices.[^ch31-check-silences]

The chapter does not re-narrate the neutrality crisis and the decision for war, which belong to Chapter 30, or Progressive reform before 1917, which belongs to Chapter 29, or the 1920s, which belong to Chapter 32.[^ch31-check-ch29][^ch31-check-ch30][^ch31-check-ch32]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines conscription and economic mobilization. §2 follows the raising and shipping of the American Expeditionary Forces. §3 turns to propaganda, censorship, and sedition. §4 examines migration, racial violence, the influenza pandemic, labor conflict, and the Nineteenth Amendment. §5 traces the peace conference, the Senate’s rejection of the treaty, and the Red Scare. §6 asks how historians have explained the war’s effect on the American state and on democratic citizenship. The chapter begins on July 20, 1917, when Newton D. Baker drew the first capsule from a glass bowl and called a nation’s young men to war by lottery.[^ch31-check-scope]

## 1. Mobilization and the draft

After April 1917 the United States turned the power it had exercised abroad on its own people. The draft was the entering wedge. It required the state to register, classify, compel, and punish. Alongside it, federal agencies directed production, food, fuel, and railroads, and an information apparatus managed opinion. The chapter treats the three as one system.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-check-scope]

### 1.1 The claim: conscription as the hinge

The federal government’s decision to raise the army by conscription rather than by volunteers transformed the war from a foreign-policy decision into a domestic regime. A volunteer army asks; a draft compels. The difference is not merely administrative. Conscription required the state to reach into every locality, register its citizens, classify them, compel them, and punish those who refused.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]

The phrase “war state” in this chapter means the emergency apparatus of conscription, economic direction, and censorship, not the permanent government. That apparatus rested on three instruments. The draft conscripted bodies. The economic agencies directed production, food, fuel, and railroads. The information and enforcement apparatus managed opinion and punished dissent. Each depended on the others: the draft needed the economy to equip the army, the economy needed the army’s contracts, and both needed a public that would accept the sacrifice.[^ch31-check-scope]

The record can show the statutes, the registrations, the agencies, and the prosecutions. It cannot show that the public consented to them or that they were administered fairly. Consent is contested rather than assumed.[^ch31-check-scene][^ch31-check-silences]

### 1.2 The Selective Service Act and the machinery of the draft

Wilson signed the Selective Service Act on May 18, 1917. The act compelled all men between 21 and 30 to register, and a later amendment extended the age range to 18 through 45. It ended the bounty system and the practice of paying one’s way out of service, and it authorized random drawings to decide the order of induction. Its designers presented it as fair because it forbade the substitutions and purchased exemptions that had discredited the Civil War draft.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]

The machinery was local. Local boards handled registration, assigned order and serial numbers, classified men, and called and entrained the draftees. More than 10 million men registered within several months of the law’s passage, and by the war’s end about 24 million had registered. Of the roughly 4.7 million Americans who served, more than half were conscripted. The counts vary by whether they cover the Army alone or include the Navy and Marine Corps, and the chapter states the definition with each total.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-check-numbers]

The lottery’s appeal to chance was meant to make compulsion look like fairness. A man could not buy a substitute, but he could still be classified, deferred, or exempted, and the boards that made those decisions were local and human. The record can show the statute and the registrations. It cannot show that the lottery was genuinely random or that the boards treated rich and poor alike.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft][^ch31-check-scene]

### 1.3 Economic mobilization and the war state

Alongside the draft, the war produced a set of federal agencies that directed the economy. The War Industries Board, established in the Council of National Defense on July 28, 1917, analyzed industrial requirements, issued clearances on government orders, set priorities in commodity production and delivery, arranged price-fixing agreements for raw materials, and supervised Allied purchasing in the United States. It became an independent agency in May 1918 and was abolished on December 31, 1918, with liquidation completed the following July.[^ch31-nara-wib]

The Food Administration, created by executive order on August 10, 1917, under the Lever Act, controlled the production, distribution, and conservation of food. Herbert Hoover ran it. It licensed dealers, published fair-price lists, and relied heavily on publicity and voluntary cooperation; by the armistice it had more than 3,200 local food administrators. The Fuel Administration, created on August 23, 1917, regulated coal, coke, natural gas, and petroleum products. The Railroad Administration, established by proclamation on December 26, 1917, operated the seized railroads, coastwise steamship lines, inland waterways, and telephone and telegraph companies. The National War Labor Board, created on March 29, 1918, settled labor disputes by arbitration, mediation, and conciliation.[^ch31-nara-food-admin][^ch31-nara-fuel-admin][^ch31-nara-railroad-admin][^ch31-nara-nwlb]

These agencies were improvisations, but they were not trivial. They set prices, allocated materials, ran carriers, and mediated disputes. Historians disagree about whether they were a temporary expedient that dissolved after the armistice or the seedbed of an enduring national administrative capacity. Ronald Schaffer’s “war welfare state” interpretation stresses the responsibilities the war created for soldiers, veterans, and the economy. David M. Kennedy’s synthesis stresses improvisation, uneven administration, and dismantling. The chapter states the disagreement and does not settle it by fiat.[^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-kennedy-over-here][^ch31-check-debate-works]

The record can show the executive orders, the boards, and the production figures where they exist. It cannot show that the agencies succeeded on their own terms. Official intentions and popular action must be kept distinct.[^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The limits of consent: exemptions, slackers, and enforcement

Conscription was contested from the first drawing. Following the first drawing, some 50,000 men applied for exemptions, and many of those called simply did not report. In one 1918 instance, New York City arrested 16,000 men who had failed to report after being selected. These are evidence of resistance, not proof that the draft failed.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-check-numbers]

The period’s word for a man who evaded service was “slacker.” The term carried a moral charge: it turned a legal classification into a judgment about character and loyalty. The chapter uses it only in quotation and explains why it is reproduced. The same vocabulary of loyalty would soon be turned on dissenters, immigrants, and strikers who had never been subject to the draft at all.[^ch31-check-names]

The record can show the exemption applications and the arrests. It cannot measure how many men evaded selection without being caught. The count is a floor, not a total.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-check-numbers]

## 2. The American Expeditionary Forces

The army that the draft raised had to be trained, equipped, shipped, and commanded.

### 2.1 Raising and shipping an army

The American Expeditionary Forces were built from almost nothing into an army of millions in little more than a year. The combined Army, Navy, and Marine forces numbered about 4.7 million, of whom the Army accounted for about 4.1 million and the Navy about 600,000. More than 2 million American troops reached Europe. The first American combat deaths came in November 1917, and the force grew slowly through the winter before fighting in large formations in 1918. The AEF spent only about 200 days in combat, from April 25, 1918, to the armistice.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]

The speed of the build shaped the war. Units arrived without full training, equipment, or transport, and they depended on the Allies for much of what they needed. The record can show the totals, the shipping, and the combat days. It cannot show the experience of the ordinary soldier from a table; memoirs and unit records supply that, and they are thinner for the men who served in labor battalions than for the officers who wrote books.[^ch31-keene-soldier][^ch31-check-silences]

### 2.2 Training, command, and the French alliance

The AEF fought as an independent American army under General John J. Pershing, but it depended on French and British shipping, artillery, and training. That dependence made the alliance a source of both strength and friction. Pershing insisted on an open-warfare doctrine and on keeping the AEF as a separate force, even as the Allies pressed to use American troops as replacements in their own depleted units. The dispute over amalgamation was a command decision with consequences on the battlefield.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-check-dates]

The AEF fought in distinct sectors: Cantigny, Belleau Wood, Château-Thierry, Saint-Mihiel, and the Meuse-Argonne. Map 24 locates them rather than leaving the campaign as a list of names. The map must distinguish American sectors from Allied ones, give the date of each engagement, and state its sources; it is an argument about operational geography, not a single shaded claim on the Western Front.[^ch31-check-map][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]

### 2.3 The 369th and the segregated army

Black soldiers served in a segregated army that assigned most of them to labor. The most celebrated Black combat regiment, the 369th Infantry, fought under French command. The 369th was formerly the 15th New York National Guard Regiment, and it was known as the “Harlem Hellfighters.” It landed at Brest, France, in December 1917 and was assigned to the French army in 1918. It spent 191 days in the front-line trenches, more than any other American unit of its size, and suffered more than 1,400 total casualties, more than any other American regiment. More than 170 of its members received the *Croix de Guerre*.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-abmc-hellfighters]

More than 350,000 African Americans served in the war, the majority in labor and stevedore battalions. Only about one in 10 served in a combat role. Roughly 200,000 served in Europe, about 42,000 of them in combat. The arrangement exposed the army’s refusal to use Black soldiers as combat troops on the same terms as White soldiers. The contradiction reaches its sharpest form here: the army that fought to make the world safe for democracy segregated its own soldiers and denied most of them the chance to fight.[^ch31-abmc-hellfighters][^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-check-race]

The record can show the unit’s service, its casualties, and its decorations. It cannot show that the recognition redeemed the army’s discrimination. Those must be kept separate. The historians Adriane Lentz-Smith and Chad Williams have argued that Black soldiers and communities used the war’s language of democracy to press claims at home; the evidence for that assertion lies in Black newspapers, letters, and organizing, not in the army’s own records.[^ch31-lentz-smith][^ch31-williams-torchbearers][^ch31-check-race]

The Meuse-Argonne Offensive ran from September 26 to November 11, 1918. It was the largest operation of the AEF, with over a million American soldiers participating, and the deadliest campaign in American history to that date, with over 26,000 killed in action and over 120,000 total casualties. The American cemetery at Romagne contains more graves than the American cemetery at Omaha Beach in Normandy.[^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]

Total American deaths from all causes came to 116,516, of which 53,402 were battle deaths and 63,114 were non-combat deaths, largely from the influenza pandemic. Two-thirds of all American deaths occurred in the last three months of the war.[^ch31-byerly-losses]

## 3. Propaganda, dissent, and sedition

The third instrument of the war state was the management of opinion. The government did not merely censor dissent; it produced a coordinated campaign to create consent, and then it punished those who withheld it.

### 3.1 The Committee on Public Information

Wilson created the Committee on Public Information on April 13, 1917, by executive order, and placed the journalist George Creel at its head. The committee released government news, sustained morale, and administered a voluntary press censorship. It worked through news, films, posters, pamphlets, and speakers. Its best-known program enrolled volunteer speakers, the “Four Minute Men,” to give short patriotic addresses in theaters and public gatherings. The committee was abolished in August 1919.[^ch31-nara-cpi-records][^ch31-ww1c-cpi]

The campaign was an effort to manufacture consent. The record can show the committee’s creation, its chief, and its methods. It cannot measure how much of the public was persuaded. Attendance at a rally or a signed pledge card is evidence of participation, not of belief.[^ch31-check-cpi][^ch31-check-silences]

### 3.2 The Espionage Act of 1917 and its enforcement

The Espionage Act of June 15, 1917, converted criticism of the war into a federal crime. It punished causing insubordination, disloyalty, mutiny, or refusal of duty in the military, and obstructing recruiting or enlistment, among other offenses. The Sedition Act of 1918 amended its Section 3 and added the censorship of the mails. Its enforcement made the war the first great test of the First Amendment’s limits.[^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

The Department of Justice used the act broadly against supposed enemies of the war. The prosecutions were a system of enforcement, not a list of isolated cases: they targeted socialists, anarchists, pacifists, German-language editors, and immigrant radicals, and they relied on local informers as well as federal agents. The record can show the statutes, the indictments, and the convictions. It cannot show that the prosecutions deterred dissent effectively, and the law’s reach must be distinguished from its measured effect.[^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties][^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 3.3 The Sedition Act of 1918 and the In Focus

**In Focus.** Section 3 of the Sedition Act of 1918 is a statute, not an indictment, and it should be read as a legal instrument. Congress passed the act in May 1918, and Wilson signed it on May 16. It amended Section 3 of the Espionage Act to punish anyone who, “when the United States is at war, shall willfully utter, print, write or publish any disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language about the form of government of the United States or the Constitution of the United States, or the military or naval forces of the United States, or the flag of the United States, or the uniform of the Army or Navy of the United States into contempt, scorn, contumely, or disrepute,” or who shall “willfully … urge, incite, or advocate any curtailment of production” of goods needed for the war, with intent to cripple the war effort. The penalty was a fine of up to $10,000 or imprisonment for up to 20 years, or both. Read closely, the section criminalized not only acts that interfered with the war but language that expressed contempt for the government, its Constitution, its flag, or its uniform. It reached speech that obstructed no enlistment. The text can be compared with the Espionage Act’s recruitment provisions and with the state sedition laws of 1918. It cannot be compared with a reconstructed courtroom scene, because the statute is the evidence. How much speech can a democracy punish before it has punished democracy?[^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-govinfo-sedition][^ch31-check-focus]

### 3.4 The courts: Schenck, Debs, and Abrams

The Supreme Court upheld the Espionage Act and supplied the test that governed free speech until the mid-twentieth century. The wartime narrowing of liberty came with judicial sanction, not only executive action.[^ch31-justia-schenck][^ch31-cornell-debs][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

In *Schenck v. United States* (1919), the Court sustained a conviction for circulating leaflets that urged resistance to the draft. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote that the question was whether the words created a “clear and present danger” of bringing about the substantive evils Congress had a right to prevent.[^ch31-justia-schenck]

Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist Party leader, was indicted on June 29, 1918, for a speech at Canton, Ohio, on June 16. The indictment charged him with making false statements “to promote the success of the enemy of the United States.” He was convicted on September 12, 1918, and sentenced to 10 years. The Supreme Court affirmed the conviction on March 10, 1919. President Warren G. Harding commuted the sentence in December 1921.[^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-cornell-debs][^ch31-check-free-speech]

In *Abrams v. United States* (1919), the Court affirmed convictions of five Russian-born radicals who had distributed leaflets against the intervention in Russia. Holmes dissented, and his dissent began to argue for a marketplace-of-ideas standard: “the best test of truth is the power of the thought to get itself accepted in the competition of the market.” The same justice who had written *Schenck* and *Debs* for the Court now wrote that the defendants had as much right to publish their leaflets as the government had to publish the Constitution. The turn was not a conversion to absolute liberty. It was a narrower claim: speech could be punished only when it threatened an immediate evil.[^ch31-cornell-abrams][^ch31-check-free-speech]

### 3.5 Vigilantism and the boundaries of loyalty

Coercion was not only official. Private citizens policed loyalty through boycotts, denunciations, and violence. The American Protective League enrolled citizens to surveil their neighbors. Sauerkraut became “liberty cabbage,” German-language instruction was banned in many schools and churches, and pacifists and immigrants were attacked. The wartime culture of conformity was made as much from below as from above.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-race]

Historians have argued that this popular enforcement made loyalty a matter of civic obligation that long outlasted the war. The argument is strong where the evidence shows citizens organizing, and weaker where it infers belief from compliance. The record can show the organizations and documented attacks. It cannot show how many people complied out of conviction and how many out of fear.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-silences]

## 4. Migration, Red Summer, influenza, labor, and suffrage

The war’s demands did not stop at the battlefield. They pulled people across the country, exposed them to disease, set them against one another in the workplace, and opened a question about who belonged to the political community.

### 4.1 The Great Migration and the wartime labor market

The war’s demand for labor, combined with the collapse of European immigration, drew hundreds of thousands of Black southerners north. By the summer of 1919, approximately 500,000 African Americans had resettled in northern cities, and the departure of White workers into the army had opened industrial jobs to them.[^ch31-nara-red-summer][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer]

The arrival remade the cities they entered and provoked the violence of 1919. Contemporaneous reporting, including Carl Sandburg’s 1919 articles in the *Chicago Daily News*, described the migration as it happened; Sandburg was the only White reporter to examine the social causes. The record can show the census totals, the destinations, and the hostility the migrants met. It cannot show the migrants’ own reasons through statistics alone; letters, oral histories, and the Black press supply that, and the chapter turns to them where they survive.[^ch31-nps-chicago-1919][^ch31-check-silences]

### 4.2 Red Summer and racial violence

In 1919 the racial violence that the war had sharpened erupted across the country. The “Red Summer” affected at least 26 cities. Race riots and lynchings killed hundreds and injured thousands. Lynchings rose from 64 in 1918 to 83 in 1919. Officials often hesitated or looked away, and ordinary White civilians and veterans, not only organized terror groups, formed most of the mobs.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nara-red-summer]

A four-day riot in Washington, D.C., began on July 19, 1919, after a rumor that Black men had assaulted a White woman. Black residents armed themselves and fought back, and Wilson ordered troops into the streets. In Chicago, rioting broke out on July 27 after the Black teenager Eugene Williams drowned when struck by a stone while on a raft near a de facto White beach. The rioting lasted until August 3, killed 38 people, 23 Black and 15 White, injured 537, and left up to 1,000 Black families homeless.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nps-chicago-1919]

The likely deadliest single incident came at Elaine, Arkansas, on September 30 and October 1, 1919, after a shootout outside a Black sharecroppers’ meeting. The governor ordered troops to suppress what was labeled an “insurrection.” Estimates of the Black dead run from more than 100 to 200 or higher. The chapter presents that range as an estimate, not a settled count.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nara-red-summer][^ch31-check-numbers]

W. E. B. Du Bois, in *The Crisis*, called on Black veterans to “return fighting.” The violence of 1919 led to a broader commitment to fight back in the streets, the courts, and the voting booth. That is an outcome of the year, not only a record of victimization.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-check-race]

### 4.3 The influenza pandemic

The influenza pandemic killed more Americans than the war itself and demonstrated how total mobilization spread the virus across the globe. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that the 1918 pandemic infected about one-third of the world’s population and killed at least 50 million people worldwide, with about 675,000 deaths in the United States. The Census Bureau reports that October 1918 was the deadliest month, with an estimated 195,000 American deaths, and that 1918 saw 477,467 deaths from influenza and pneumonia. The global total is an estimate; the Census Bureau suggests it may have reached 100 million, and the chapter presents the figure as a range.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-census-influenza][^ch31-check-influenza-figures]

Influenza sickened about 26 percent of the army, more than one million men, and the pandemic compromised the AEF’s performance in the Meuse-Argonne, which opened as the second wave struck. At Camp Devens, more than 14,000 soldiers were sick in September 1918 and 757 died. The pandemic fell hardest on the poor and on Native communities. The Census Bureau reports extreme losses among Alaska Native and American Indian populations, including 72 of the 80 residents of Brevig Mission and estimated mortality among the Navajo of about 12 percent.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-census-influenza][^ch31-check-race]

The record can show the mortality statistics and the spread through military camps. It cannot give an exact global or national total. The figures are ranges and estimates, and each is named to its source.[^ch31-check-numbers]

### 4.4 Labor conflict: Seattle and after

The armistice did not bring peace to the workplace. 1919 was one of the most turbulent years in American labor history, and the Seattle general strike showed both the movement’s power and the limits of solidarity. On the morning of February 6, 1919, Seattle, a city of about 315,000 people, stopped working. 35,000 shipyard workers had already struck, and 25,000 other union members joined them. 101 AFL unions had voted to walk out in solidarity, and most of the remaining workforce stayed home as stores closed and streetcars halted. An elected Strike Committee coordinated essential services, the teamsters kept hospitals and food deliveries running, and an unarmed labor guard patrolled the streets. The mayor threatened martial law, and two battalions of federal troops took up positions, but the city remained calm. The Central Labor Council declared the strike over on February 11. Police and vigilantes then rounded up radicals, and federal agents closed the labor-owned *Seattle Union Record*. Mayor Ole Hanson declared that “Americanism” had triumphed over “Bolshevism.”[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike]

The Seattle strike was part of a wider wave. The Boston police strike of September 1919, the steel strike, and the coal strike followed. The record can show the strike’s dates, participants, and the peaceful conduct the organizers claimed. It cannot show that the strike advanced the workers’ cause. Its outcome is contested.[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike][^ch31-check-dates][^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The Nineteenth Amendment completed

The Nineteenth Amendment completed the enfranchisement of women, but ratification in 1920 crowned a campaign built partly on the exclusion of Black women and left many women unable to vote. The House passed the amendment on May 21, 1919, by 304 to 89, and the Senate followed on June 4. Suffrage lobbyists tied the defense of democracy abroad to the extension of democracy at home. Representative James R. Mann argued that the time was ripe, the people were ready, and the beneficiaries of the amendment were eager and able to perform the duties of citizenship.[^ch31-house-19th-amendment]

Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify on August 18, 1920, completing the three-fourths requirement. The decisive vote in the Tennessee House was cast by the 24-year-old representative Harry Burn, who acted after receiving a letter from his mother. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified the ratification on August 26, 1920.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-nara-prologue-19th]

Ratification did not enfranchise all women. State voting laws and discrimination continued to keep many, and especially Black women, from the polls. The historian Lisa Tetrault argues that the amendment’s history has been simplified and that its constitutional meaning remained unsettled. The record can show the votes, the states, and the certification. It cannot show that ratification produced an equal electorate.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-check-suffrage][^ch31-check-race]

## 5. Versailles, the Senate, and the Red Scare

The war ended in November 1918, but the peace did not. The treaty that Wilson brought home became a test of the Senate’s constitutional power, and the fear that the war had fed turned against radicals at home.

### 5.1 The Fourteen Points and the peace

Wilson went to Paris with a program that joined a general association of nations to a vision of self-determination. In his address of January 8, 1918, he set out fourteen points, including open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, reduced armaments, an impartial adjustment of colonial claims, and, in the fourteenth, “a general association of nations … for the purpose of affording mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike.” The peace that emerged disappointed much of that program, which is why the treaty became a domestic political fight.[^ch31-avalon-fourteen-points][^ch31-check-dates]

The distance between the program and the treaty was a source of both foreign and domestic opposition. The terms against Germany were punitive, the colonial settlements divided the spoils of the defeated empires, and the interests of many peoples were excluded. The program must not stand in for the treaty.[^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The Senate fight and the reservations

The Treaty of Versailles failed in the Senate because Wilson would not accept the reservations that a majority would have required and because the opposition, led by Henry Cabot Lodge, would not ratify the League as Wilson insisted. Wilson and Lodge had disliked each other for years, and each considered himself the country’s preeminent scholar in politics. The 1918 midterm elections gave the Republicans control of the Senate, making Lodge both majority leader and Foreign Relations Committee chairman. Wilson offended the Senate by refusing to include senators among the negotiators and by making the conference results public before discussing them with the committee. He denounced Lodge and his allies as “contemptible, narrow, selfish, poor little minds that never get anywhere but run around in a circle and think they are going somewhere.”[^ch31-state-versailles]

Lodge’s committee added reservations, and in November 1919 Lodge sent the treaty to the floor with fourteen reservations but no amendments. During a cross-country speaking tour in October 1919, Wilson suffered a physical collapse that removed him from the fight. The treaty’s defeat was therefore both a personal and an institutional event: two stubborn men, a president who would not compromise, and a Senate that would not consent.[^ch31-state-versailles][^ch31-check-dates]

### 5.3 Table 2: the Senate votes

The Senate’s roll calls show that the treaty failed not once but three times, and that the decisive obstacle was the two-thirds threshold, not a simple majority. On November 19, 1919, the Senate defeated the treaty with Lodge’s fourteen reservations, 39 to 55. It also rejected the original treaty without amendments, 38 to 53. On March 19, 1920, after a bipartisan effort to pass the treaty with reservations, the Senate again rejected it, 49 to 35, which was a majority but short of the two-thirds the Constitution requires.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace][^ch31-check-dates-appD]

### 5.4 The Red Scare and the Palmer Raids

The fear of radicalism that the war had fed continued after the armistice. On June 2, 1919, a militant anarchist blew up the front of Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer’s home in Washington, and that day brought coordinated attacks in eight cities. Earlier that spring, mailed bombs had reached officials and business leaders, and a postal worker intercepted some. Palmer created a small intelligence division and placed the young J. Edgar Hoover in charge of it.[^ch31-fbi-palmer]

The Department of Justice began arresting suspected radicals under laws including the Sedition Act, detaining well-known figures such as Emma Goldman and Alexander Berkman. On November 7, 1919, the first series of raids brought about 200 arrests. On January 2, 1920, a nationwide roundup arrested about 3,000 people. Palmer warned of a revolutionary plot for May 1, 1920, and when it failed to materialize he was discredited. The Senate Judiciary Committee investigated the raids’ legality from January to March 1921.[^ch31-loc-palmer][^ch31-fbi-palmer]

The record can show the bombings, the raids, the arrests, and the deportations. It cannot establish that those arrested were guilty of the revolutionary conspiracy Palmer described. The raids were the state’s response to fear, not to demonstrated danger, and the chapter presents them that way.[^ch31-fbi-palmer][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

### 5.5 Demobilization and the election of 1920

The war’s institutions were dismantled faster than they had been built. The army shrank rapidly after the armistice, and the wartime agencies were wound down, even as veterans’ claims on the federal government grew through rehabilitation and benefits programs. The informal capacities, habits of surveillance, and expectations of federal action outlasted the armistice. That is the chapter’s bridge to the 1920s.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-check-ch32]

The election of 1920 was the political expression of the postwar mood. The chapter notes the Harding–Cox contest and leaves its full treatment to Chapter 32, which opens at its broadcast. The Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22 also belongs to Chapter 32’s international context, not to this chapter.[^ch31-check-ch32][^ch31-pabook-kdka][^ch31-state-naval-conference]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

Conscription was the hinge. The draft reached into every locality and compelled service, marking the war as a domestic regime and not only a foreign undertaking.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]

The war narrowed dissent substantially. The Espionage and Sedition Acts, their enforcement, and the courts’ approval produced the most severe federal restrictions on speech since the Sedition Act of 1798, and the Debs case shows a leading dissenter imprisoned for a speech.[^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-justia-schenck]

The pandemic dwarfed the battlefield. Influenza killed far more Americans than combat, and the war’s mobilization spread it.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-byerly-losses]

The racial contradiction was central. A segregated army fought a war for democracy while denying Black soldiers equal standing, and 1919 brought racial violence in at least 26 cities.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer]

The treaty failed on the two-thirds threshold. The Senate rejected ratification three times, and the decisive barrier was the constitutional supermajority.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace]

The Nineteenth Amendment was partial. Ratification in 1920 enfranchised many women but did not produce an equal electorate.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]

### 6.2 What is open

Did the war build an enduring state or a temporary one? One position holds that the wartime agencies and the war-welfare regime created capacities that lasted; another holds that the mobilization was improvised and largely dismantled, leaving habits rather than institutions. The chapter states each at its strongest.[^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-kennedy-over-here]

Was the coercion official, popular, or both? One account stresses federal law and prosecution; another stresses the grassroots enforcement of loyalty by citizens. The evidence for both belongs side by side.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

What did the war do to the color line? One line of argument stresses the possibilities the war opened for Black assertion and organization; another stresses how firmly the war entrenched the color line and how limited the gains were. The chapter marks the question as open rather than choosing a winner in advance.[^ch31-lentz-smith][^ch31-williams-torchbearers][^ch31-check-debate-works]

Did the Nineteenth Amendment democratize or preserve exclusion? One account treats 1920 as the culmination of a long struggle; another stresses the movement’s exclusions and the amendment’s limited effect. The chapter presents both and does not reduce the amendment to either.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-tetrault-myth]

Why did the treaty fail? One account stresses Wilson’s rigidity and his refusal to compromise; another stresses Lodge’s partisanship and the Republicans’ control of the Senate. The chapter presents both mechanisms.[^ch31-state-versailles][^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which total mobilization simultaneously widened and narrowed American citizenship. It drew new people into the national state and its political life, and it punished dissent, segregated its soldiers, and left the peace unfinished. The positions overlap rather than form teams.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-check-debate-works]

The synthesis should distinguish the wartime agencies, which were largely dissolved, from the capacities and expectations they left behind, which shaped the next two decades. That distinction is the chapter’s contribution to the book’s argument about how settlements change the terms of conflict without settling them.[^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-kennedy-over-here]

It should also keep the state’s intentions — to mobilize, to persuade, to punish — distinct from the popular enforcement of loyalty and the popular resistance to it. Conflating the two would let the government speak for a public that acted on its own.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-silences]

The war’s deepest democratic question was not whether the nation could win but whether it could compel, censor, and conscript its own people and still be the republic it claimed to be. The answer the record supports is: partly, and at a cost that the next decade would reckon.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-check-table3]

## Legacies

The wartime state outlasted its own institutions. The draft ended and the wartime agencies were dismantled, but the administrative habit of federal direction and the expectation that Washington would act on wages, prices, and welfare did not disappear. The Selective Service System would be rebuilt in 1940, and the administrative precedents of 1917–18 shaped the New Era and the New Deal alike.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state]

The wartime narrowing of liberty outlasted the armistice. The sedition prosecutions ended, but the federal government’s surveillance of radicals and its use of deportation did not. The prosecutions and the Red Scare left a pattern that recurred across the century, and the civil-liberties arguments they provoked became the foundation of a lasting movement.[^ch31-fbi-palmer][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

The war’s racial contradiction outlasted the peace. Black veterans returned to a country that lynched more people in 1919 than in 1918, and the Double V campaign of the next war would inherit the unfinished fight that the first war had left.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-lentz-smith]

The Nineteenth Amendment also left a double legacy: the enfranchisement of millions of women, and the persistence of exclusions that would take the Voting Rights Act of 1965 to address in law. Both are consequences the record supports.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The draft, the AEF, and the influenza pandemic were all transatlantic; the peace conference redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, and the influenza virus traveled with the armies. These connections belong in notes rather than in a claim that Europe caused American decisions.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-census-influenza]
- **Archives and silences.** The official record — statutes, boards, casualty lists, case files, and raid reports — over-represents the state and its enforcers and under-represents the conscripts, evaders, dissidents, migrants, strikers, women, and Black Americans whose lives the war reordered. The chapter names that asymmetry rather than speaking for the silenced.[^ch31-check-silences][^ch31-nara-debs]

## Summary

- The Selective Service Act of May 18, 1917, built a national conscription system through local boards and forbade the substitutions and purchased exemptions that had discredited the Civil War draft.[^ch31-selective-service]
- On July 20, 1917, Secretary of War Newton D. Baker drew the first capsule, number 258, and 10,500 numbers were drawn.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- About 24 million men registered for the draft, more than half of the roughly 4.7 million Americans who served were conscripted, and conscription met resistance from exemption claims and failures to report.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-byerly-losses]
- The American Expeditionary Forces fought about 200 days in combat from April 25, 1918, and the Meuse-Argonne Offensive of September 26–November 11, 1918, was the largest and deadliest AEF operation, with over a million participants, over 26,000 killed, and over 120,000 total casualties.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]
- Total American deaths came to 116,516, of which 63,114 were non-combat deaths, largely from influenza.[^ch31-byerly-losses]
- The all-Black 369th Infantry spent 191 days in the front-line trenches and suffered over 1,400 casualties, while more than 350,000 African Americans served and most were assigned to labor.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-abmc-hellfighters]
- The Committee on Public Information ran a coordinated propaganda campaign, and the Espionage and Sedition Acts made criticism of the war a federal crime.[^ch31-ww1c-cpi][^ch31-sedition-act]
- The Supreme Court sustained the Espionage Act in *Schenck v. United States* and upheld the conviction of Eugene V. Debs for his Canton speech.[^ch31-justia-schenck][^ch31-nara-debs]
- The war drew some 500,000 Black southerners into northern cities by the summer of 1919, and the Red Summer of 1919 brought racial violence to at least 26 cities, killing 38 in Chicago’s riot and an estimated 200 or more at Elaine, Arkansas.[^ch31-nara-red-summer][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nps-chicago-1919]
- The influenza pandemic killed about 675,000 Americans, with an estimated 195,000 deaths in October 1918 alone, and it compromised the AEF in the Meuse-Argonne.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-census-influenza]
- The Seattle general strike of February 6–11, 1919, involved more than 60,000 workers and remained peaceful despite federal troops and martial-law threats.[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike]
- The Nineteenth Amendment passed the House on May 21, 1919, and the Senate on June 4, 1919, and was ratified on August 18, 1920, when Tennessee became the 36th state.[^ch31-house-19th-amendment][^ch31-nara-19th-amendment]
- The Senate rejected ratification of the Treaty of Versailles three times — 39–55 and 38–53 on November 19, 1919, and 49–35 on March 19, 1920 — each time short of two-thirds.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace]
- The Palmer Raids of November 1919 and January 1920 arrested about 200 and then about 3,000 suspected radicals, and Palmer’s predicted May Day revolution failed to materialize.[^ch31-loc-palmer][^ch31-fbi-palmer]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* Between 1917 and 1920 the United States conscripted its own citizens, directed its economy, censored its press, imprisoned its dissidents, segregated its soldiers, defeated its strikes, buried some 675,000 of its people in a pandemic that traveled with its armies, enfranchised its women in part, and then refused the peace its president had brought home. The chapter’s question — what did total mobilization do to a democracy? — is answered by a record in which the same state that made the world safe for democracy also made dissent a crime and left the republic more powerful, more policed, and more unfinished than it had been.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-capitol-senate-votes]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on the evening of November 2, 1920, when Leo Rosenberg, speaking for the Westinghouse station KDKA in East Pittsburgh, broadcast the Harding–Cox election returns. The mobilized society the war had assembled would now be addressed as a mass audience, by a new medium, in a decade of prosperity, prohibition, and conflict. Chapter 32 takes up the 1920s, and asks who belonged to the new mass society.[^ch31-check-ch32][^ch31-check-bridge][^ch31-check-radio][^ch31-fcc-radio][^ch31-pabook-kdka]
