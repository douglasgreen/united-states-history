A statute assumed that the state could protect a shared moral community by guarding the mail. A social theorist argued that goods were a language of rank. A historian argued that the culture itself had split into high and low and left the popular arts devalued. The three disagree about whether the era’s shared forms unified the nation, sorted it, or were policed from above. This chapter asks whether a national audience dissolved social boundaries or reorganized them.[^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-veblen][^ch28-levine][^ch28-check-epigraphs]

In February 1900 the Eastman Kodak Company put a small box on the market and called it the Brownie. It measured five inches long, three inches high, and three inches wide. It was built of jute board reinforced with wood and covered with imitation leather. It had a rotary shutter and an f/14 meniscus lens, and it made six negatives, each two and a quarter inches square, on a roll of film. It sold for one dollar. The company could make it for about fifty cents.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-gac]

Eastman named the camera for the Brownie characters of the Canadian illustrator Palmer Cox, hoping to appeal to children. Adults bought it for themselves. The company advertised it in the popular press, and more than 100,000 were sold within a year. The camera was the consumer end of a system Eastman had built since the 1880s: dry plates, roll film, and processing and printing by mail. The slogan said what the system did: “You press the button, we do the rest.”[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-acs][^ch28-brownie-franklin]

A dollar was not the whole price of a photograph. Film and processing cost more; a spool of film cost fifteen cents. The Brownie made a technical practice available to people who had never handled a camera, but it did so as a purchase, and the purchase continued with every roll. The record establishes the date, the price, the materials, the six exposures, and the first year’s sales. It does not establish who bought the camera, what they photographed, or what a dollar meant in any particular household. In February 1900 no one knew that the Brownie would still be sold, in new models, eight decades later, or that its snapshots would become historical evidence.[^ch28-check-scene]

Did a national audience dissolve social boundaries, or did it reorganize them?

This chapter covers the last three decades of the nineteenth century, from about 1870 to 1900. Chapter 27 followed the agrarian revolt, the depression, the election of 1896, and the turn to empire; this chapter turns to the culture, thought, and everyday life of the same years. It asks whether the national audience that the press, the goods, the entertainments, the universities, the churches, and the sciences created dissolved social boundaries or reorganized them.[^ch28-check-scope][^ch28-check-ch27]

It draws on newspapers and magazines, catalogs and advertisements, the records of companies and colleges, the writings of novelists, philosophers, and reformers, the publications of scientists and physicians, and the reports of associations. It treats “culture,” “highbrow,” “lowbrow,” “mass culture,” the “New Woman,” and “eugenics” as contested period terms. “Culture” here means both the shared forms of a society and the period’s own claim that some works deserved the name with a capital letter. “Highbrow” and “lowbrow” were period words for ranked taste. “Mass culture” means commercial forms addressed to a large audience. The “New Woman” was the period’s name for the educated woman who claimed a public life. “Eugenics,” defined in §4.3, was the program for improving inherited stock.[^ch28-check-names]

The archive over-represents editors, publishers, professors, physicians, and manufacturers and under-represents working-class, Black, immigrant, and rural readers, audiences, and consumers. The catalogs and the magazines show what was offered, not what was taken. The imbalance is named here rather than filled with invented voices.[^ch28-check-silences]

The chapter proceeds in six movements. §1 examines the press, the telephone, and national audiences. §2 follows catalogs, department stores, sport, vaudeville, and photography. §3 asks what the realists, the pragmatists, and the churches made of the new age. §4 examines the research university, medical reform, and the sciences of race. §5 turns to women, education, suffrage, and the household. §6 asks how historians have explained the emergence of a mass culture and a cultural hierarchy. The chapter begins with a box camera that cost a dollar and a slogan that promised to do the rest.[^ch28-check-scope]

## 1. Press, telephone, and national audiences

The national audience was not a metaphor. It was built out of paper, wire, and mail, and it was built unevenly. The press reached the city and the countryside; the telephone reached the office; rural delivery reached the farm. Each carried the same forms to more people, and each set terms of admission that were social as well as technical.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs][^ch28-telephones-pbs][^ch28-rfd-postal]

### 1.1 The daily press and the making of a mass readership

The daily paper became a mass commodity in the 1880s and 1890s. Aggregate circulation rose through the period and would keep rising into the 1940s, even as the number of titles began to fall. By 1900 most cities of moderate size had competing morning and evening papers, and the front page had become a product designed for a broad readership.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs]

The competition was personal and expensive. Joseph Pulitzer’s New York *World* and William Randolph Hearst’s New York *Journal* fought for the same readers. The term “yellow journalism” came from their contest over Richard F. Outcault’s “Hogan’s Alley” cartoon and its “Yellow Kid,” and it then named the sensational style the two publishers used in their coverage of Cuba in 1896–98.[^ch28-yellow-journalism]

The style had consequences, but the legend that Hearst manufactured the war is not supported. The State Department’s account holds that yellow journalism “helped to create a climate conducive” to war but “did not by itself cause the war,” and it notes that other factors played a greater role. The press set an agenda and amplified a case; it did not decide the war alone.[^ch28-yellow-journalism]

What the record can show is circulation, ownership, format, and content. It cannot show what every reader believed, which story persuaded a given voter, or what a publisher intended beyond what the record says. The history of the press is a history of power exercised through attention, and attention is hard to measure after the fact.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs][^ch28-check-silences]

### 1.2 The magazine revolution, the telephone, and rural delivery

Two technologies and one price war reorganized the national audience in the 1890s. The cheap illustrated magazine, the telephone, and rural free delivery each stretched the reach of common information beyond the city and the neighborhood, though unevenly.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-telephones-pbs][^ch28-rfd-postal]

The magazine revolution began with a price cut. Frank Munsey cut *Munsey’s* to ten cents in 1893, and its circulation rose from 40,000 to 500,000 in 1895 and 700,000 in 1897. *McClure’s*, founded in 1893, cut its price to fifteen cents against the usual twenty-five or thirty-five cents and reached about 250,000 by 1896. The *Ladies’ Home Journal* passed half a million by 1900. These magazines carried national advertising and a mix of fiction, news, and departments that presupposed a general reader.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-mcclure-britannica]

The American Antiquarian Society’s catalog record for Frank Luther Mott’s 1954 study summarizes his argument that the new ten-cent magazines were “a revolution in magazine publishing and reading.” New engraving processes, new advertising, and hard times made the cheap magazine possible, and it appealed first to middle-class self-culture before it became a vehicle for the muckraking of the next decade.[^ch28-magazines-aas]

The audience the magazines imagined was not everyone. They addressed a professional and managerial readership, and they ignored Black life under Jim Crow and trivialized women in the very pages that courted them as readers. The national audience was national in reach and limited in address.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-check-silences]

The telephone reached fewer people. By 1900 the country had about 1.35 million telephone lines, roughly 18 lines for every thousand people, and almost 8 million calls a day, mostly for business. The telephone was not yet ordinary household equipment; that came by 1940. Access was concentrated in cities and offices, and the instrument connected the people who already had business to conduct.[^ch28-telephones-pbs]

Rural free delivery reached a different periphery. Experimental rural delivery began in 1896, and the service became an official part of the Post Office Department in 1902. It let farm families receive mail at home rather than travel to a country store, and it joined the catalog and the magazine in bringing the same goods and texts to isolated households.[^ch28-rfd-postal]

Its source targets are the Census volumes and Ayer directories, with App. B, Table 7 for the communications series. The series are incomplete, and the figure marks the missing years rather than filling them.[^ch28-check-figure][^ch28-check-numbers]

Not everyone was reached. The reach became national, and the terms of admission were set by price, language, race, and place. The culture of the 1890s was shared because it circulated, and it was unequal because circulation had a price.[^ch28-check-ch24]

## 2. Catalogs, department stores, sport, vaudeville, and photography

Goods and amusements did what print did: they carried a common stock of forms to more people and sorted the people as they went. The catalog reached the farm, the store reached the city, and the park and the camera reached the crowd. Each offered a common experience, and each charged for it in money, time, or propriety.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-shopping-smithsonian][^ch28-coney-parks]

### 2.1 The mail-order catalog and the reach of goods

The mail-order catalog carried the department store’s selection to households that had no store, and it made price and choice the same in a Kansas farmhouse as in a city block.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-sears-archive]

The 1897 Sears, Roebuck catalog ran to eight hundred pages. Its first item was an eighteen-pound chest of tea for fifty-two cents, and its last was a sixty-cent cotton surcingle for a large horse. In between it sold organs, pianos, firearms, sewing machines, and new “safety” bicycles at twenty-five to fifty-six dollars. Its size and ubiquity made it, in one reader’s phrase, a “cornucopia” of what Americans wanted and could imagine owning.[^ch28-sears-engine]

The catalog can show prices, goods, and the claims of its advertisers. It cannot show what any household actually bought, on what credit, or what a reader made of the abundance on the page.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-check-silences]

The catalog depended on the same postal system that rural free delivery extended. It turned rural households into a national market, and it made the mail an instrument of consumption as well as of correspondence. The delivery described in §1 and the goods described here belong together.[^ch28-rfd-postal][^ch28-sears-engine]

### 2.2 The department store and the woman consumer

The department store made shopping a pleasure and a public activity. Marshall Field in Chicago, John Wanamaker in Philadelphia, and Macy’s in New York built palatial stores that offered wide selections at set prices and encouraged shoppers to spend the day. Field added a bargain basement for less affluent buyers, and the stores used pneumatic tubes and centralized cash offices to supervise money and to hire less skilled clerks.[^ch28-shopping-smithsonian]

The store recruited the same single women it addressed as customers. Department stores employed growing numbers of single women, who also became consumers, and owners sought middle- and upper-class customers with money to spend on impulse. Working-class people generally lacked the time to shop as a leisure activity, and the shopper of the store’s imagination was not everyone.[^ch28-shopping-smithsonian][^ch28-check-gender]

The store was also a school in rank. Its floors, departments, and prices taught a customer where she belonged, and its clerks learned the same lesson from the other side of the counter. Thorstein Veblen gave the lesson a name in 1899 when he wrote that “conspicuous consumption of valuable goods is a means of reputability to the gentleman of leisure.” The sentence described a man, but the store addressed a woman, and the era’s consumption was organized around both.[^ch28-veblen][^ch28-shopping-smithsonian][^ch28-check-gender]

### 2.3 Sport, vaudeville, and commercial amusements

Sport and entertainment became organized, commercial, and national in these decades, and their promoters deliberately recast rowdy amusements as respectable middle-class leisure.[^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-vaudeville-arizona][^ch28-coney-parks]

The National League of Professional Base Ball Clubs was formed on February 2, 1876, by owners who separated management from play, restricted membership to one club per city, and raised the admission price to fifty cents to price out the rough crowd and attract a higher-income audience. The league was a business built on monopoly as much as on sport.[^ch28-baseball-sabr]

Benjamin Franklin Keith worked to gentrify variety entertainment into “wholesome” family fare, opening a Boston dime museum in 1883 and a theater that drew on the “continuous” variety show. Keith’s career shows the industry’s bargain: cultural respectability sold tickets to the middle class even as it disciplined the performers and the audience.[^ch28-vaudeville-arizona]

At Coney Island the amusement park became a fenced, ticketed product. Paul Boyton fenced and charged admission at Sea Lion Park in 1895, and George C. Tilyou opened Steeplechase Park in 1897 with twenty-five attractions for twenty-five cents. Tilyou had introduced a Ferris wheel on the site in 1894, inspired by the wheel at Chicago’s 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition, and the park drew crowds estimated in the tens of thousands on hot days.[^ch28-coney-parks]

William F. “Buffalo Bill” Cody opened Buffalo Bill’s Wild West on May 19, 1883, at Omaha, an outdoor variety show of frontier vignettes that toured nationally and abroad. Lakota and other Native performers took part, and their participation was itself contested. The history of conquest and dispossession belongs to Chapter 25; here the show is evidence of how the frontier became commercial entertainment.[^ch28-buffalo-bill][^ch28-check-ch25]

### 2.4 Photography and the snapshot

Photography passed in a single generation from a professional craft to an amateur habit, and the Brownie completed a system Eastman Kodak had assembled before it.[^ch28-brownie-acs][^ch28-brownie-smok]

Eastman introduced roll film from paper in 1885 and the transparent celluloid negative soon after, the handheld Kodak camera in 1888, and the Brownie in 1900. Each step lowered the skill and the cost required to take a picture and increased the demand for Kodak film and processing.[^ch28-brownie-acs]

By 1900 photography could be done by “anyone” in the company’s advertising, and millions of snapshots entered family life as a new kind of personal record. The snapshot was both a widening of expression and a commercial form shaped by what the camera and the marketplace made easy to photograph.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-neh]

### 2.5 In Focus: the bicycle pages of the 1897 Sears catalog

**In Focus.** The bicycle pages of the 1897 Sears, Roebuck catalog do two things at once. They price a new consumer technology precisely — safety bicycles at twenty-five to fifty-six dollars — and they gender the purchase, showing men’s and women’s frames, clothing, and accessories. The page is reproduced as Image 4. It is a sales document, not a record of what was bought; its pictures show how the market imagined its buyers, not how every reader lived. The bicycle was the decade’s great consumer good and its great symbol of mobility. The safety bicycle gave women a means of moving outside the home, the craze strengthened the argument for “rational clothing,” and the machines remained expensive enough that most who rode were White and affluent. Read together, the catalog page and the bicycle craze show the pattern of the decade: a shared commodity that widened access while carrying the era’s prices, gender rules, and racial limits inside it.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-sears-archive][^ch28-bicycle-smithsonian][^ch28-check-focus][^ch28-check-image]

## 3. Realism, pragmatism, and religion

American thought responded to the same changes that the press and the store carried. Realists described the present, pragmatists tested ideas by their consequences, and the churches divided over what science and the Bible required. The three responses were not separate compartments. They were arguments about what could be known and who had the authority to say it.[^ch28-howells-wsu][^ch28-james-will][^ch28-darwin-pew]

### 3.1 Realism and the truth of the present

American writers turned from romance toward the close observation of ordinary life, and their realism was a claim about how the modern world should be described.[^ch28-howells-wsu]

William Dean Howells, the most influential editor and critic of his generation, defined realism as “the truthful treatment of material.” The novels of the 1880s and 1890s — by Howells, Mark Twain, Henry James, and, more harshly, Stephen Crane and Frank Norris — took contemporary society as their subject. Realism was a contested program, not the natural way to write.[^ch28-howells-wsu][^ch28-check-literature]

By the 1890s a younger generation pushed realism toward naturalism, in which character was shaped by environment and heredity. The turn mattered because it changed the moral question: a realist asked what people did, and a naturalist asked what had made them do it.[^ch28-howells-wsu]

### 3.2 Pragmatism and the American philosophical temper

American philosophy in these decades abandoned the search for fixed foundations for a method that tested ideas by their consequences, and this pragmatism was both a technical philosophy and a popular attitude.[^ch28-peirce-sep][^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]

Charles Sanders Peirce stated the pragmatic maxim in 1878: the meaning of an idea lies in its practical consequences. William James carried it to a wide readership, and in “The Will to Believe” (1896) he argued that in some choices “our passional nature” must decide, since the evidence cannot. “I have said, and now repeat it,” James wrote, “that not only as a matter of fact do we find our passional nature influencing us in our opinions, but that there are some options between opinions in which this influence must be regarded both as an inevitable and as a lawful determinant of our choice.” John Dewey built instrumentalism out of the same tradition in Chicago in the 1890s.[^ch28-peirce-sep][^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]

The quotation defends the right to believe; it does not claim that all beliefs are true. James was arguing that some questions cannot be settled by evidence alone and that choosing to act is not the same as proving. The stakes were existential. Pragmatism was a way of living without certainties, and it belonged to the same moment as the realists’ refusal of romance and the churches’ quarrel with Darwin.[^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]

### 3.3 The churches, Darwin, and the social gospel

American Protestantism both absorbed and resisted the new science, and the same decades produced a socially engaged gospel and an organized opposition to Darwinism.[^ch28-rauschenbusch-yawp][^ch28-darwin-pew][^ch28-moody-moody]

Dwight L. Moody, a Chicago evangelist, perfected the mass revival and used the era’s business methods to reach vast audiences. By the end of the century he and other popular preachers were attacking Darwinism as a threat to biblical truth and public morality.[^ch28-moody-moody][^ch28-darwin-pew]

Walter Rauschenbusch’s ministry in New York’s Hell’s Kitchen from 1886 to 1897 and his later *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) argued that Christianity must transform social relations, not merely individual souls. The book appeared after 1900, so it is the maturing statement of a movement that took shape in the 1890s, not contemporary evidence of 1890.[^ch28-rauschenbusch-yawp][^ch28-check-dates]

From the 1890s to the 1930s the major Protestant denominations divided into modernist and evangelical camps, a schism driven by new science, new questions about the Bible, and new social theories. The split began in these years and culminated later; the later fundamentalist-modernist controversy must not be read back into 1890.[^ch28-darwin-pew]

The Comstock Act of 1873 gave federal force to a private moral campaign. Anthony Comstock’s New York Society for the Suppression of Vice pressed Congress to bar “obscene” matter and contraceptives from the mail. The statute shows the era’s confidence that a shared moral community could be policed, and its vagueness shows how much that community disagreed with itself.[^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-comstock-pbs]

## 4. Research universities, medicine, and racial science

The years that widened the audience also concentrated the authority to define knowledge. The research university, the reformed medical school, and the sciences of race were parts of one reorganization of expertise. Each raised a standard and each drew a boundary around who could speak.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hopkins-medicine][^ch28-hoffman-jstor]

### 4.1 The research university and the doctorate

The American university was remade in these decades around research and graduate training, and the new institutions concentrated the authority to define knowledge in a credentialed few.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hopkins-medicine]

Johns Hopkins University opened in 1876 under Daniel Coit Gilman with 54 graduate students and combined the German research model with American features. It awarded its first PhD in 1878 and by 1889 had produced 151 doctorates, more than Harvard and Yale combined, and its graduates carried the model to other universities. Clark University opened in 1889 as the first all-graduate institution, and the University of Chicago opened in 1890 under William Rainey Harper.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond]

Land-grant colleges and new state universities extended higher education, and Black institutions such as Tuskegee Institute built a separate system of practical training. Tuskegee opened on July 4, 1881, under Booker T. Washington and emphasized trades and self-help. Washington’s Atlanta Exposition Address of 1895 made the case for industrial training and social separation in the same breath. He told White employers to “cast down your bucket,” and he put the bargain plainly: “In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress.” Chapter 26 takes up the bargain the address offered; here it belongs to the era’s expansion of schooling.[^ch28-tuskegee][^ch28-washington-atlanta][^ch28-check-ch26]

### 4.2 Medicine and the professions

Medicine reorganized itself around laboratory science and university training, and in doing so it raised standards and narrowed who could practice.[^ch28-hopkins-medicine][^ch28-flexner]

The Johns Hopkins Hospital opened in 1889 and the School of Medicine in 1893, recruiting William H. Welch, William Osler, William S. Halsted, and Howard Kelly, and requiring a college education and examinations in basic science for admission. It made bedside teaching and laboratory research central and created the clinical residency. Women were admitted on the same terms as men.[^ch28-hopkins-medicine]

The Carnegie Foundation’s Flexner Report of 1910 surveyed the medical schools and found most inadequate, holding up Johns Hopkins as the model. The report belongs to the next decade. It is the consequence of reforms begun in the 1890s, not a fact of them.[^ch28-flexner][^ch28-check-dates]

### 4.3 Racial science, eugenics, and classification

The decades that professionalized the sciences also produced a “scientific” ranking of peoples, and this racial science was not marginal to the thought of the 1890s but central to it.[^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-eugenics-genome]

Francis Galton coined the word “eugenics” in 1883 and defined it as the science of improving stock. The idea spread to the United States, where it was later institutionalized in Charles Davenport’s Eugenics Record Office in 1910. Social Darwinism supplied a language for ranking races and classes, and eugenics supplied a program. Most of the American institutional apparatus came after 1900, but its roots lay in the 1890s.[^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-eugenics-pbs][^ch28-darwin-pew]

Frederick L. Hoffman’s *Race Traits and Tendencies of the American Negro* (1896) used census and insurance statistics to argue that Black Americans were degenerating and would die out. W. E. B. Du Bois reviewed it in 1897 and showed that Hoffman had misused the statistical method, comparing incomparable groups and reading general conclusions from city death rates. Kelly Miller published a fuller critique the same year. The exchange is the clearest evidence in the decade that racial science was contested from the start.[^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman]

This is a history of a science that ranked human beings, and it is written against that science. Du Bois and Miller are the era’s own critics, and the later discrediting of eugenics does not excuse the era’s scientists.[^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman][^ch28-check-race]

## 5. Women, education, suffrage, and household change

The transformation of women’s lives was the largest change in everyday life, and it was fought over in the same institutions described in the preceding sections: the college, the organization, the store, and the state.[^ch28-womenscolleges][^ch28-nawsa]

### 5.1 The women’s colleges and the “New Woman”

Women entered higher education in growing numbers in these decades, and their entry was treated as a dangerous experiment that might unfit them for marriage and motherhood.[^ch28-womenscolleges][^ch28-gfwc]

Mount Holyoke had begun in 1837 as a female seminary; Vassar (1865), Wellesley (1870), Smith (1871), and Bryn Mawr (1885) built the collegiate system, and state universities became coeducational. Founders and critics alike feared that college might unhinge women, and the early campuses were designed to supervise them closely.[^ch28-womenscolleges]

The educated “New Woman” became a cultural type, and women’s clubs gave her a public life. The General Federation of Women’s Clubs was formed on April 24, 1890, when 63 clubs ratified a constitution. The clubs turned private reading and reform into organized public work.[^ch28-gfwc][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-check-gender]

### 5.2 Suffrage and the temperance alliance

Suffrage was reorganized on a national footing in 1890, but the movement’s strategy and its coalition were contested, and its gains by 1900 were western and partial.[^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-willard-nwh]

The National Woman Suffrage Association and the American Woman Suffrage Association merged in 1890 to form the National American Woman Suffrage Association, with Elizabeth Cady Stanton as president and Susan B. Anthony leading the work. Anthony served as president from 1892 to 1900 and trained Carrie Chapman Catt and Anna Howard Shaw as successors.[^ch28-nawsa]

Frances Willard led the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union from 1879 until her death in 1898; under her leadership it became the largest women’s organization in the country. She argued that women needed the vote to make and keep their towns dry, and she drew many women into public life who might not have joined a suffrage society. The WCTU linked temperance, suffrage, and a broad program of social reform.[^ch28-willard-nwh][^ch28-willard-dpla]

By 1900 women had full suffrage in four states — Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, and Idaho — and in no other state. The national amendment and much of the South remained out of reach. The Nineteenth Amendment is completed in Chapter 31, and 1920 must not be read back into 1900.[^ch28-suffrage-flag][^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-check-ch31]

The movement’s unity was also a unity of the included. NAWSA did not exclude Black women at the national level, but its state and local affiliates did, and its southern conventions, including Atlanta in 1895, were segregated. The suffrage movement widened women’s public role while reproducing the racial order around it.[^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-check-ch26]

### 5.3 The household, contraception, and Black women’s clubs

The household itself changed as women’s education and paid work expanded, and the era’s law restricted the knowledge women could get about controlling their own fertility.[^ch28-gilman][^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-nacw]

Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s *Women and Economics* (1898) argued that women’s economic dependence on men stunted both women and society and called for women’s entry into productive work. The book is the era’s sharpest statement that the household was an economic institution, not a private refuge.[^ch28-gilman]

The Comstock Act barred contraceptives and information about them from the mail, and the law did not clearly define what “obscene” meant. Doctors and reformers who gave women information about pregnancy and contraception faced fines and prison, so the era’s widening of education stopped short of reproductive knowledge.[^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-comstock-pbs]

Black women built their own federation. The National Association of Colored Women was founded in July 1896, with Mary Church Terrell as its first president, and it pursued a broad reform program alongside suffrage. It was part of the era’s women’s movement, and the southern racial order it worked within belongs to Chapter 26.[^ch28-nacw][^ch28-check-ch26]

The “New Woman,” the store clerk, the temperance organizer, and the clubwoman were overlapping figures in a single transformation of women’s public lives. The transformation was real, and it was bounded by race, class, and region.[^ch28-check-gender][^ch28-check-race]

## 6. The Debate

The argument over the culture of the 1890s began while it was being made. The evidence supports some conclusions firmly and leaves others open. The debate is not a contest between a faith in progress and a nostalgia for a lost republic; it is a disagreement about which mechanisms mattered most and how they fit together.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-peiss][^ch28-check-table]

### 6.1 What is settled

The audience widened. The press, the magazines, the telephone, the mails, the catalog, the store, the sport, and the amusement park reached more people in 1900 than in 1870, and the Brownie made a camera an ordinary household object.[^ch28-newspapers-pbs][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-brownie-smok]

The widening carried hierarchies. The same forms that widened access carried new ranks and exclusions: fifty-cent admissions, store departments for the wealthy and the basement for the less affluent, a camera that cost more than many could spare, and magazines that ignored Black life under Jim Crow and trivialized women.[^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-shopping-smithsonian][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia]

Culture stratified as well as spread. The decades saw both a mass culture and the emergence of high and low categories, and Levine’s account of “sacralization” describes the second without denying the first.[^ch28-levine]

Science both advanced and ranked. The research university and the reformed medical school raised standards, and the racial science of the 1890s ranked peoples and helped justify exclusion.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-eugenics-genome]

The moral community was contested. The Comstock Act shows a confident attempt to police a shared morality, and the ambiguity of “obscene” shows how little agreement lay beneath it.[^ch28-comstock-act]

Women’s public lives changed. Women entered colleges, clubs, and paid work in growing numbers and reorganized the suffrage movement, though the vote remained partial in 1900.[^ch28-womenscolleges][^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-willard-nwh]

### 6.2 What is open

Did commercial culture democratize or stratify? One position holds that it gave ordinary people more choices and more shared experience; another holds that it stratified taste, audiences, and authority into highbrow and lowbrow. Levine, Peiss, and Kasson disagree about how much agency popular audiences had, and each position should be stated at its strongest.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-peiss][^ch28-kasson]

Who made the new culture? Some accounts stress the producers — publishers, promoters, universities, and advertisers; others stress the audiences and consumers, especially working-class women and immigrant and Black communities, who used commercial leisure for their own purposes. The record over-represents the producers.[^ch28-peiss][^ch28-ohmann][^ch28-check-silences]

Was the culture “incorporated”? Alan Trachtenberg’s account folds culture into the corporate reorganization of American life; T. J. Jackson Lears’s account finds in antimodernism a therapeutic accommodation rather than a resistance. The two agree that culture and capitalism were entangled and disagree about what that meant for the people who lived it.[^ch28-trachtenberg][^ch28-lears]

How should the racial science be weighed? One reading treats eugenics and scientific racism as a marginal pseudoscience that the era’s real scientists rejected; another treats them as mainstream products of the same institutions that professionalized science. Du Bois’s and Miller’s early critiques support the second reading and complicate the first.[^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman][^ch28-gould]

When did mass culture begin? Some accounts locate it in the 1890s magazine and catalog revolution; others find its fuller form in the movies, radio, and advertising of the twentieth century. The 1900 endpoint is a choice of period, and anything after it must be labeled as consequence.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-check-dates]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

The evidence supports a history in which the decade’s shared forms were at once democratizing and stratifying, and in which access is not the same as authority. The positions overlap rather than form teams.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-peiss][^ch28-trachtenberg][^ch28-check-sources]

A person might read the same magazine, ride the same bicycle, and sit in the same park as a wealthier neighbor and still not share the neighbor’s authority over what counted as culture, who was admitted, or who was classified as fit.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-hoffman-jstor]

The republic assembled a national audience in these decades and organized that audience at the same time, with a new cultural hierarchy above it and scientific classifications cutting through it. The synthesis holds the widening and the ranking together without claiming that one was the other’s hidden purpose.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-trachtenberg][^ch28-check-table]

## Legacies

The forms built in these decades outlasted the people who made them. The mass circulation magazine, the national advertisement, the mail-order catalog, the department store, the amusement park, and the snapshot became ordinary parts of American life in the twentieth century, and the Brownie remained in production in new models for decades; the last appeared in 1980. The national audience that the 1890s assembled would be reassembled by the movies, the radio, and television.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-brownie-smok]

The cultural hierarchy that Levine describes did not vanish. The categories of highbrow and lowbrow persisted, and the argument over them returned in later debates over the canon, the curriculum, and public funding for the arts. That contested memory is part of the history, and the evidence on each side belongs in the account.[^ch28-levine]

The sciences of race left the heavier legacy. The eugenic program that grew from 1890s roots produced state sterilization laws and immigration restriction in the twentieth century, and its victims were counted in the tens of thousands before the movement was discredited. The university and the reformed medical school, meanwhile, became the model for American higher education and professional training, and the authority they concentrated in credentialed experts is part of the government and the culture that the following chapters describe.[^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-eugenics-pbs][^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-flexner]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The cheap magazine, the mass press, the bicycle, the department store, and the eugenic idea were not uniquely American; they circulated through the same Atlantic economy that carried goods and people. The comparison belongs in a note rather than in a claim that the forms were identical or that one country caused the other.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-check-sources]
- **Archives and silences.** Editors, publishers, professors, and manufacturers left abundant records, while the reading, listening, and buying of ordinary people, and especially of Black and immigrant audiences, survive unevenly. The catalogs and the magazines show what was offered, not what was taken. The asymmetry is named here rather than filled by speaking for the silenced.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-check-silences]

## Summary

- In February 1900 Eastman Kodak launched the Brownie, a one-dollar box camera that made six negatives on roll film and sold more than 100,000 in its first year, widening photography into a mass amateur practice.[^ch28-brownie-smok][^ch28-brownie-franklin]
- The daily press grew into a mass commodity, and the competition of Pulitzer’s *World* and Hearst’s *Journal* produced “yellow journalism,” which shaped but did not by itself cause the war with Spain.[^ch28-yellow-journalism][^ch28-newspapers-pbs]
- The cheap magazine revolution of the 1890s, led by *Munsey’s*, *McClure’s*, and the *Ladies’ Home Journal*, created a national reading public and national advertising; by 1900 there were about 1.35 million telephone lines, and rural free delivery, begun in 1896, carried mail to farm households.[^ch28-magazines-encyclopedia][^ch28-mcclure-britannica][^ch28-telephones-pbs][^ch28-rfd-postal]
- The 1897 Sears catalog sold from an eighteen-pound chest of tea to safety bicycles at twenty-five to fifty-six dollars, and the department store made shopping a pleasure and employed the women it addressed as customers.[^ch28-sears-engine][^ch28-shopping-smithsonian]
- The National League was formed in 1876 around a fifty-cent admission and business monopoly; B. F. Keith gentrified vaudeville for a family audience; and Coney Island’s Sea Lion Park (1895) and Steeplechase Park (1897) began the modern amusement park.[^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-vaudeville-arizona][^ch28-coney-parks]
- Howells defined realism as “the truthful treatment of material,” and a younger generation turned toward naturalism; Peirce, James, and Dewey built pragmatism and instrumentalism out of the test of consequences.[^ch28-howells-wsu][^ch28-peirce-sep][^ch28-james-will][^ch28-dewey-sep]
- Dwight L. Moody perfected mass revivalism and attacked Darwinism, while Walter Rauschenbusch’s social gospel argued that Christianity must transform social relations; from the 1890s the Protestant denominations began to divide into modernist and evangelical camps.[^ch28-moody-moody][^ch28-rauschenbusch-yawp][^ch28-darwin-pew]
- Johns Hopkins opened in 1876 as the first American research university and, with Clark and the University of Chicago, established the PhD and the research faculty; its hospital (1889) and medical school (1893) remade medical education, a reform the Flexner Report would codify in 1910.[^ch28-hopkins-richmond][^ch28-hopkins-medicine][^ch28-flexner]
- Francis Galton coined “eugenics” in 1883, and the American movement that grew from these roots was institutionalized after 1900; Frederick Hoffman’s *Race Traits* (1896) used statistics to argue that Black Americans were degenerating, and Du Bois’s and Miller’s 1897 reviews showed the misuse of the method.[^ch28-eugenics-genome][^ch28-hoffman-jstor][^ch28-dubois-hoffman][^ch28-miller-hoffman]
- The women’s colleges and coeducation expanded in these decades, the National American Woman Suffrage Association formed in 1890, and Frances Willard’s WCTU made temperance a school for women’s public work.[^ch28-womenscolleges][^ch28-nawsa][^ch28-willard-nwh]
- Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s *Women and Economics* (1898) treated the household as an economic institution, while the Comstock Act restricted contraception and information about it; the National Association of Colored Women was founded in 1896 under Mary Church Terrell.[^ch28-gilman][^ch28-comstock-act][^ch28-nacw]

## Closing note and bridge

*A closing note.* In the last three decades of the nineteenth century the United States built a national audience and then organized it. The press, the magazine, the telephone, the mail, the catalog, the store, the park, and the camera reached more people than ever before and gave them shared forms. But the same decades sorted those forms into high and low, priced some people out of the new pleasures, ignored Black life and trivialized women in the very pages that claimed to speak to everyone, and produced a science that ranked human beings as fit or unfit. The chapter’s question — did a national audience dissolve social boundaries or reorganize them? — is answered by a record in which the widening and the ranking happened together.[^ch28-levine][^ch28-baseball-sabr][^ch28-hoffman-jstor]

*And a bridge.* The next chapter begins at the temporary White House in Washington on October 3, 1902, when Theodore Roosevelt summoned the anthracite operators and the miners’ union to settle a strike that threatened the winter’s coal supply. The audience that the 1890s had assembled for goods and images would now be asked to follow a president into the reform of industrial society. Chapter 29 takes up the Progressive movement — its reformers, its experts, its regulations, and its exclusions — and asks who benefited when public authority was enlarged.[^ch28-check-ch29]
