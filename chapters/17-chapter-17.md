## Epigraphs

— Andrew Jackson, Second Annual Message to Congress, December 6, 1830, announcing the progress of removal.[^ch17-jackson-removal-message]

> “We are denationalized; we are disfranchised. We are deprived of membership in the human family! We have neither land nor home, nor resting place that can be called our own.”[^ch17-nps-ross-protest]

— Principal Chief John Ross, letter “To the Senate and House of Representatives,” Red Clay Council Ground, Cherokee Nation, September 28, 1836, protesting the Treaty of New Echota.[^ch17-nps-ross-protest]

> “The Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community, occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force.”[^ch17-worcester]

— Chief Justice John Marshall, *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832).[^ch17-worcester]

> “Indian Removal … was an unusual construction that left unstated who was removing whom.”[^ch17-saunt-review]

— Claudio Saunt, *Unworthy Republic: The Dispossession of Native Americans and the Road to Indian Territory* (2020), as quoted in a scholarly review.[^ch17-saunt-review]

The four disagree about whether removal was humane or coercive, lawful or lawless, inevitable or chosen.[^ch17-check-epigraphs]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place.** New Echota, at the headwaters of the Oostanaula River in present-day Gordon County, Georgia, had been the capital of the Cherokee Nation since the national legislature established it in 1825. In its short life it held the first Native American newspaper office, the print shop of the *Cherokee Phoenix*; the missionary Samuel Worcester’s home; and the council and court houses of a national government modeled on that of the United States.[^ch17-gastateparks-newechota][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]
- **The summons.** In the preamble of the treaty, the United States commissioners William Carroll and John F. Schermerhorn stated that they had “appoint[ed] and notif[ied] a general council of the nation to convene at New Echota on the 21st day of December 1835,” and that those who did not come “they should conclude gave their assent and sanction to whatever should be transacted at this council.”[^ch17-new-echota-treaty]
- **The instrument.** On December 29, 1835, the commissioners and a group of Cherokees “in general council assembled” concluded a treaty. Its first article ceded “all the lands owned claimed or possessed by them east of the Mississippi river” for five million dollars and lands west; a later article required the Cherokee to remove “within two years from the ratification of this treaty.”[^ch17-new-echota-treaty]
- **The signers.** The signatories included Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, Stand Watie, Andrew Ross, James Starr, George Chambers, and others. The National Park Service records that only 300 to 500 Cherokees were present, that “none were elected officials of the Cherokee Nation,” and that twenty signed. These signers later became known as the Treaty Party; the majority who opposed the treaty became the National Party under Principal Chief John Ross.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-smithsonian-resisting-removal][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]
- **The absence of authority.** The elected Cherokee government did not authorize the signers. A petition from the Aquohee and Taquoee districts, signed by thousands and now in the Records of the U.S. Senate, argued that “a few individuals making a treaty vitally affecting the liberties, the property and the personal rights of a whole people” was “so utterly repugnant to reason and justice and every dictate of humanity” that the Senate should reject it. Ross’s 1836 letter insisted that “the instrument in question is not the act of our Nation; we are not parties to its covenants; it has not received the sanction of our people.”[^ch17-docsteach-petition][^ch17-nps-ross-protest]
- **The ratification.** On May 23, 1836, the Senate ratified the Treaty of New Echota by a single vote, over the protests of Henry Clay and Daniel Webster and against the petitions of most Cherokee.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-history-state-removal]

### The scene’s argument

- **A capital making a document it did not authorize.** The scene should open on New Echota as a seat of government and print, then narrow to the December council and the treaty it produced. The point is not that the signers were without standing in Cherokee life — Ridge and Boudinot were prominent men — but that the document claimed an authority the elected government had refused to grant.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]
- **The claim and the contradiction.** The treaty’s preamble says the signers acted “being duly authorized by the people in general council assembled,” while the Cherokee government and thousands of petitioners said the opposite. The chapter should let the contradiction stand rather than resolve it by accepting the victor’s language, because the contest over who could speak for a nation is the chapter’s first subject.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-docsteach-petition]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did sovereignty survive when federal and state power rejected it?
- **The turn.** The answer is not that sovereignty failed. The Cherokee built a written constitution, a national press, and a legal strategy that won in the Supreme Court; the nation survived in the West and, for some, in the East. The treaty was an act of force that the record does not show to have been authorized — and it did not extinguish the nation it claimed to bind.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **The widening.** The scene stands at New Echota in December 1835. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader back to the 1820s and outward to the Old Northwest, the Southern Plains, Texas, and Oregon.[^ch17-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, room, or dialogue.** The place, the summons, the instrument, the signers, and the protests are documented. New Echota’s weather that December, the room where the document was signed, who stood where, and any spoken exchange are not attested in the cited sources; do not stage them.[^ch17-check-scene]
- **No exact count stated as fact.** The sources give the number present as a range of 300 to 500 and the number of signers as twenty; do not convert the range into a precise figure.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]

## 1. Cherokee government and the courts

### 1.1 Nation-building: Sequoyah’s syllabary, the constitution, and the *Cherokee Phoenix*

- **The claim.** By the 1820s the Cherokee had built the institutions of a modern nation-state to defend a sovereignty the surrounding states denied, and those institutions were the ground of the legal fight that followed.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]
- **The syllabary.** Sequoyah, a Cherokee silversmith, developed a syllabary for the Cherokee language in the early 1820s. It gave the nation a written language and, with it, literacy and printed government. The National Park Service dates the syllabary’s adoption to about 1821 and records that by the 1820s it “brought literacy and a formal governing system with a written constitution.”[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-gastateparks-newechota]
- **The constitution.** In 1827 the Cherokee Nation adopted a written constitution modeled on that of the United States, with a principal chief, a bicameral council, and courts. The Cherokee Nation’s own history records that the Nation was led by a principal chief “regularly elected by chiefs from Cherokee towns,” and that the 1827 constitution was the nation’s answer to Georgia’s claim of jurisdiction.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The press.** In 1828 the General Council established the *Cherokee Phoenix* at New Echota, with Elias Boudinot as editor; it was the first Native American newspaper and printed in parallel English and Cherokee columns. The paper announced the nation’s laws and documents and carried the removal crisis to subscribers across the United States and Europe. It was an instrument of the national leadership, not a free-speech forum, and Boudinot was forced to resign in 1832 after he came to support voluntary removal.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix]
- **The consequence.** The Cherokee were not a people waiting to disappear. They were a nation with a constitution, a capital, a newspaper, and a legal strategy — and that is precisely why the states moved to abolish their government by law.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

### 1.2 Georgia’s extension laws and the Cherokee response

- **The claim.** Georgia answered Cherokee statehood with statutes that purported to extend state jurisdiction over Cherokee land and to abolish the Cherokee government, courts, and laws.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The extension.** In 1827 the Cherokee adopted their constitution; the next year Georgia declared the Cherokee government abolished and its citizens subject to Georgia law, annexed Cherokee lands, and set up a process for distributing that land to White citizens. Georgia recognized Cherokee landholding as subject to state regulation and prohibited Cherokee testimony against Whites, making abuse of Cherokee people difficult to remedy.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The missionaries’ law.** Georgia enacted a law prohibiting “white persons” from residing within the Cherokee Nation without a state license, aimed at the missionaries who advised the Cherokee leadership. It gave them until March 1, 1831, to obtain a license or leave.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The crisis beneath the statutes.** The discovery of gold on Cherokee land in 1830 intensified the pressure; the National Park Service records that Georgia held lotteries to distribute Cherokee land and gold rights to Whites and barred Cherokee people from conducting tribal business, contracting, testifying against Whites, or mining gold. The statutes and the gold rush together made the Cherokee a nation under legal siege.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-check-race]
- **The consequence.** Georgia’s laws forced the Cherokee to choose between submission and a legal challenge to the states’ power over them — the challenge that produced the Cherokee cases.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]

### 1.3 The Cherokee cases and In Focus: *Worcester v. Georgia*

- **The claim.** The Cherokee won a decisive statement of tribal sovereignty in the Supreme Court and could not enforce it, a gap that defines the chapter’s central question.[^ch17-worcester]
- **The first case.** In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831), the Court held that it lacked jurisdiction to hear the Cherokee suit because the Cherokee were not a foreign state. Chief Justice Marshall’s opinion called them a “domestic, dependent nation” under the guardianship of the United States, a phrase that would shape Indian law for two centuries.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-history-state-removal]
- **The second case.** The Cherokee supported the case of Samuel Worcester, a missionary and federal postmaster, who was arrested, tried, and sentenced to four years at hard labor under Georgia’s residency law. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) the Court struck down Georgia’s extension laws.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **In Focus: *Worcester v. Georgia*.** **In Focus.** The box reads a single text: the reported opinion in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832). Marshall wrote that the treaties and laws of the United States “contemplate the Indian territory as completely separated from that of the States” and that “the Indian nations had always been considered as distinct, independent political communities retaining their original natural rights as undisputed possessors of the soil.” He concluded that “the Cherokee nation … is a distinct community, occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force,” and that “the act of the State of Georgia under which the plaintiff in error was prosecuted is consequently void, and the judgment a nullity.”[^ch17-worcester]
- **How to read it.** The opinion is an instrument for reading the sovereignty claim at its strongest and its most exposed. Its reasoning is expansive — Indian nations are “distinct, independent political communities” — yet its remedy depended on federal enforcement that did not come. The chapter should read the text as law and as a claim the surrounding statutes and eviction orders defied.[^ch17-worcester][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The text and its limits.** The opinion’s “distinct community” language is quoted character by character from the reported decision and must be collated against the *United States Reports*. The box should note that the Court rested on the treaty relationship, not on a general theory of tribal independence, and that Marshall still described the tribes within the republic’s protection.[^ch17-check-focus][^ch17-worcester]

### 1.4 The limits of law: non-enforcement and the treaty

- **The claim.** The victory in *Worcester* exposed the difference between a legal right and a power to enforce it, and removal proceeded in the gap.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The non-enforcement.** Georgia ignored the ruling and refused to release the missionaries; President Jackson did not enforce it and instead urged the Cherokee to relocate. The New Georgia Encyclopedia records that Jackson’s actual words to General John Coffee were that the Court’s decision “has fell still born,” and it flags that the popular line attributed to him — “John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it” — is not what the record shows he wrote.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The pardon.** The missionaries accepted a pardon; Georgia repealed the law used to convict them, and Worcester and the others were released in January 1833. The case that defined tribal sovereignty ended for the plaintiffs in a state pardon.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester]
- **The turn to removal.** With enforcement unavailable, the federal government pressed a removal treaty on a faction willing to sign. The choice at New Echota, and the protest against it, is the subject of §2.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-check-ch16]
- **The consequence.** The Cherokee cases established a principle the nation would use for generations and could not use in time to prevent removal. The chapter should hold both facts: *Worcester* was a real victory and a real defeat.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-worcester]

## 2. Removal and its routes

### 2.1 The Indian Removal Act and the close votes

- **The claim.** The Indian Removal Act of 1830 made removal a national project and passed by narrow margins after a sectional fight.[^ch17-loc-removal-guide]
- **The statute.** Congress passed the act on May 28, 1830, authorizing the president to set aside districts west of the Mississippi for tribes that agreed to exchange their homelands, to guarantee the new country “forever,” and to pay removal and subsistence costs. Section 8 appropriated five hundred thousand dollars.[^ch17-removal-act]
- **The votes.** The Library of Congress records that the Senate voted 28 to 19 and the House 102 to 97 for the bill. The House history records that northern Members were almost universally opposed and southern Members demanded passage, that opponents tried quorum calls and substitute amendments to stall it, and that on three occasions Speaker Andrew Stevenson had to vote to keep the bill alive.[^ch17-loc-removal-guide][^ch17-house-removal]
- **The president’s argument.** In his Second Annual Message of December 6, 1830, Jackson called removal a “benevolent policy” approaching “a happy consummation,” said it would “incalculably strengthen the southwestern frontier,” and argued that separating the tribes from the states would “retard the progress of decay, which is lessening their numbers.”[^ch17-jackson-removal-message]
- **The consequence.** The act converted a long-running pressure into a funded federal program and set the terms — exchange, treaty, removal — by which the southeastern nations would be dispossessed.[^ch17-removal-act][^ch17-loc-removal-guide]

### 2.2 The southeastern nations and the routes west

- **The claim.** Removal fell unevenly across the southeastern and midwestern nations, and the treaties, not the act alone, did the dispossessing.[^ch17-history-state-removal]
- **The scope.** The National Park Service records that between 1816 and 1840 the tribes between the original states and the Mississippi signed more than 40 treaties ceding land, and that between 1830 and 1850 about 100,000 American Indians moved west. Other accounts give different totals — the Office of the Historian counts nearly 50,000 during Jackson’s presidency, and Saunt writes of “nearly eighty thousand” deportations in the 1830s — and the chapter must give these as conflicting estimates with their periods and definitions rather than combine them.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-history-state-removal][^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-check-numbers]
- **The nations.** The Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, Cherokee, and Seminole of the Southeast and nations of the Old Northwest were all affected. Each negotiated or resisted under different circumstances; the chapter should not let the Cherokee stand for the whole removal.[^ch17-nps-liri-trail-of-tears][^ch17-smithsonian-removal-overview]
- **The “permanent Indian frontier.”** Elliott West describes how the policy set aside an Indian Territory and promised a “permanent Indian frontier” of military posts to keep White intruders out — a guarantee the same expansionist forces would soon breach.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]
- **The consequence.** Removal was a federal land program with a legal form and a military backstop. Its routes and destinations — not only its causes — are what Map 13 must show.[^ch17-check-map][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-agency]

### 2.3 The Treaty of New Echota and the Trail of Tears

- **The claim.** The Treaty of New Echota, signed by a minority and ratified by one vote, produced the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838–39.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **The treaty.** The treaty ceded all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for five million dollars and lands west and required removal within two years of ratification. It was concluded December 29, 1835, and ratified May 23, 1836, by a single vote. Most Cherokee, under Ross, refused to treat it as binding.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **The roundup.** In May 1838 federal troops and state militias began gathering Cherokee people into stockades; families were separated and people were allowed only moments to collect possessions before troops led them away and looters followed.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **The routes and the death toll.** After a summer of water and rail routes, twelve groups of about 1,000 each went overland, roughly 800 miles, in the autumn and winter; two-thirds were trapped between ice-bound rivers in January. Sources differ on the dead: the National Park Service and Cherokee Nation give about 4,000 of 16,000 (roughly a quarter), the Office of the Historian “between three and four thousand,” and the Gilder Lehrman essay fewer; the chapter must present the range and its sources, not a single figure.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-history-state-removal][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west][^ch17-check-numbers]
- **The consequence.** The Trail of Tears is the chapter’s central documented event of coerced movement. The survivors rebuilt the nation in Indian Territory, adopted a new constitution in 1839, and made Tahlequah their capital; about 1,000 Cherokee in North Carolina escaped the roundup and became the Eastern Band.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]

### 2.4 Map 13: removal routes and Indian Territory, 1830–1846

- **The claim.** Map 13 visualizes the coerced movement as routes, destinations, and nations, showing that removal was a spatial and political reorganization, not a single march.[^ch17-check-map]
- **What the map shows.** Origin nations and their homelands; the principal overland and water routes; the Indian Territory west of Arkansas and Missouri; and the dates of the major removals, 1830–1846. It should distinguish documented routes from general directions and identify that the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Old Northwest nations arrived on different timelines and by different paths.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death][^ch17-check-map]
- **Definitions and caveats.** The map must state its date and the difference between routes attested in records and reconstructed general directions. It should not imply that Indian Territory was empty before removal; it was already the homeland of other nations. Underlying route and census data belong in App. B, and the map’s source line must cite the records actually used.[^ch17-check-map][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **The consequence.** A map of routes makes the argument spatial: dispossession moved people, and it moved them onto lands that were themselves contested.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-map]

## 3. The Old Northwest, Plains, and Comanchería

### 3.1 Black Hawk’s war and the Sauk and Meskwaki

- **The claim.** In the Old Northwest, removal took the form of war, defeat, and treaty, and the Sauk and Meskwaki case shows how a contested treaty could become a forced cession.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk]
- **The disputed treaty.** The Wisconsin Historical Society explains that at St. Louis in 1804 William Henry Harrison negotiated a treaty with two Sauk representatives who, the Sauk chiefs held, had no authority to speak for the nation, and that the government believed it had bought all Sauk land east of the Mississippi for a small sum. Black Hawk did not accept the treaty as valid.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk]
- **The war.** In 1832 Black Hawk led a band of Sauk, Meskwaki, and Kickapoo back across the Mississippi into Illinois; the campaign ended in the Battle of Bad Axe that August, where soldiers and volunteers killed men, women, and children as the band tried to surrender and cross the river.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk]
- **The consequence.** The war produced a treaty that effectively removed the Sauk and Meskwaki west, and it demonstrated that resistance in the Old Northwest would be met with overwhelming force. It belongs in this chapter because it shows removal operating by a different mechanism and timeline than the southeastern treaties.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-check-race]

### 3.2 The Potawatomi and removals across the Old Northwest

- **The claim.** Removal reached the Great Lakes nations through roundups and forced marches, not only through negotiated exchange.[^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]
- **The march.** The Citizen Potawatomi Nation records that on September 4, 1838, General John Tipton assembled 859 Potawatomi at Menominee’s village near Twin Lakes, Indiana, with their leaders shackled in a wagon, burned their houses and fields behind them, and forced a 660-mile march to a reserve in present-day Kansas. More than forty people died on what the Potawatomi call the Trail of Death.[^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]
- **The pattern.** The Potawatomi march, the Black Hawk War, and the southeastern treaties together show that “removal” covered a range of methods — treaty, war, and direct militia coercion — and that the Old Northwest nations experienced it as forcibly as the Southeast did.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should not let the Cherokee dominate the story. Removal was national in scope and varied in form, and the Potawatomi case belongs on Map 13 alongside the southern routes.[^ch17-check-map][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]

### 3.3 Comanchería and Indigenous power on the Southern Plains

- **The claim.** On the Southern Plains, the Comanche built a power that shaped the borderlands and that neither Mexico nor the United States could ignore, complicating any story of removal as a completed conquest.[^ch17-tsha-comanche]
- **The power.** The Texas State Historical Association records that the Comanche, mounted and mobile, dominated a vast “Comanchería” across much of Texas and the Southern Plains from the eighteenth century, controlled the commerce of the region by trade as well as raid, and by 1836, when Texas won independence, were “still in absolute control of the Texas plains.”[^ch17-tsha-comanche]
- **The raids and the borderlands.** Comanche and allied raids on Texas and northern Mexico continued through the 1830s and 1840s. The TSHA entry documents the May 1836 Fort Parker raid, including the taking of nine-year-old Cynthia Ann Parker, and the violence of the Lamar years, including the Council House Fight of 1840 and the retaliatory Linnville raid.[^ch17-tsha-comanche]
- **The historiographical point.** Hämäläinen and DeLay argue that Native power in the Mexican North shaped the fate of the region and even of the U.S.-Mexican War, making the borderlands a story of Indigenous sovereignty rather than only of American conquest. The TSHA account, written earlier, still acknowledges Comanche dominance in the period.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the Southern Plains as a place where removal was not the only force and where Indigenous nations remained powerful actors into the 1840s.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-saunt-review]

### 3.4 The “permanent Indian frontier” and its fragility

- **The claim.** The Indian Country promised by the 1830 act was contested from the start and was already being undermined by the same expansion it was meant to contain.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]
- **The promise.** Elliott West explains that the act created an Indian Territory and planned a “permanent Indian frontier” of military posts along its eastern boundary to keep White settlers out, and that removed nations helped carry the institutions of the society that expelled them into the West.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]
- **The pressure.** The same decades brought trader pressure, resource depletion, and intertribal conflict; West notes the fur trade shifted from beaver to bison robes by the 1840s, and that removed nations fought with nations already in the West. The “permanent” frontier was a promise measured in years, not generations.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]
- **The consequence.** Indian Country was real and consequential — nations rebuilt governments and communities there — but it was never a secure reservation from the expansion the chapter elsewhere describes.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

## 4. Texas and the Mexican North

### 4.1 Empresarios, Anglo colonization, and Mexico’s abolition of slavery

- **The claim.** Anglo colonization of the Mexican province of Texas after 1821 created a settler society whose labor system collided with Mexico’s antislavery law.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The colonization.** Moses Austin obtained a Spanish commission to bring 300 families; after his death in 1821 his son Stephen F. Austin fulfilled the grant and became the first empresario. The Mexican Constitution of 1824 joined Coahuila and Texas, and the state’s Colonization Act of 1825 offered generous terms that set off a “Texas land rush,” with most immigrants arriving from the southern United States “many with their slaves in tow.”[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The abolition.** Mexico moved against slavery in the 1820s and abolished it in 1829 by decree of President Vicente Guerrero. Enslaved people from Texas and Louisiana fled toward Mexico, where the border offered freedom, though the journey was long and dangerous and no reliable figures exist for how many made it.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- **The 1830 law.** The Law of April 6, 1830, forbade Anglo-American immigration and further introduction of slaves. It angered the colonists but “only slowed immigration and the Peculiar Institution, but did not curb them.”[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The consequence.** By the early 1830s Texas held a growing Anglo population and an enslaved population whose status depended on resisting Mexican law — a contradiction central to the coming revolution.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]

### 4.2 The revolution of 1836 and the protection of slavery

- **The claim.** The Texas revolution began as a federalist revolt against Santa Anna’s centralism and ended as an independence movement whose new republic protected slavery.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The arc.** After Santa Anna abandoned federalism and imposed the centralist Siete Leyes, revolts spread across Mexican states, including Texas. The first clash came at Gonzales in October 1835; the Alamo fell in March 1836; the convention at Washington-on-the-Brazos declared independence on March 2, 1836; and Houston’s army won at San Jacinto on April 21, 1836, capturing Santa Anna.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The constitution.** The February 1836 convention adopted a constitution that “guaranteed protection of slavery,” and the Republic’s congress was barred from legislating against the institution. The revolution’s leaders took care to preserve enslavement even as they invoked liberty.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- **The causes, contested.** The Handbook of Texas sets out the competing explanations — economic interest in cheap land, a clash of cultures, Texan violations of Mexican terms, Santa Anna’s centralism, “slaveowners’ concern with protecting their peculiar institution,” and a supposed U.S. conspiracy (for which it finds “scant evidence”) — and concludes that the revolution “resulted from a complex set of preconditions and ‘a spark that ignited them.’”[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the revolution as overdetermined: land, federalism, and slavery each mattered, and the outcome protected slavery whether or not it was the sole cause.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-check-race]

### 4.3 The Republic of Texas and the annexation controversy

- **The claim.** Independent Texas kept the slavery and annexation questions open, and its republic was itself contested ground among Mexicans, Tejanos, Anglos, and Native nations.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The republic.** The new republic faced Mexicans who never accepted its independence, Tejanos whose part in the revolution did not secure them equality, and Native nations — Comanche, Cherokee, and others — who controlled much of the territory. The TSHA entry notes that Native groups numbered about 20,000 in Texas and “far outnumbered the Anglo settlers and Tejanos.”[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The annexation question.** Annexation to the United States became a live national issue, entangled with slavery’s extension and with the risk of war with Mexico. The chapter owns this controversy “before Polk”; the 1845 decision and its consequences belong to Chapter 18.[^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **The consequence.** Texas was a republic built on land taken from Mexico and Native nations and on the labor of enslaved people — a combination that made annexation a national crisis rather than a simple territorial addition.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-check-ch18]

### 4.4 The Mexican North and flight to freedom

- **The claim.** The international border between Texas and Mexico made the Mexican North a destination for enslaved people seeking freedom, giving the borderlands a meaning that American expansion threatened.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- **The route.** The NEH essay on the southern Underground Railroad records that escape routes to Mexico “were known among slaves in Texas,” that Mexico had abolished slavery in 1829, and that a nineteenth-century Texas Ranger estimated four thousand escapes though “quantifying this is never going to happen.”[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- **The borderland.** The essay notes complicity by Tejanos and some German settlers, and a widely told story, which the historian Sean Kelley treats as significant whether or not it is literally true, of a man who floated to freedom on a bale of cotton.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- **The consequence.** Texas’s annexation would extend slavery’s border and turn a border of freedom into one of danger for enslaved people — an argument the chapter should make along with the expansionist and the slaveholding-strategy readings of annexation.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom][^ch17-check-race]

## 5. Oregon and the Mormon migration

### 5.1 The Oregon Trail, missions, and the Whitmans

- **The claim.** Overland settlement to Oregon joined religious mission, national rivalry, and settler sovereignty into a single movement.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]
- **The context.** After the Anglo-American Convention of 1818 opened the Oregon Country to both powers’ citizens, the United States needed settlers to make good its claim against a British population anchored by the Hudson’s Bay Company. The common Eastern view treated the route as a “howling wilderness,” and “experts said that respectable women and families would never make it to, or live in, Oregon.”[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]
- **The Whitmans.** Narcissa Prentiss Whitman and her husband Marcus joined a caravan to Oregon in 1836. Her letters and journal entries, circulated and published in eastern newspapers, reassured readers that rivers were fordable, wagon travel was practical, and the people along the route were “generally friendly,” and they helped open the Oregon Trail to families.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]
- **The scale and the ending.** By the mid-1850s some 400,000 had made the journey, with perhaps 30,000 dying en route, mostly of disease. In 1847 the Whitmans were killed at their Walla Walla mission by Cayuse people who resented the mission’s proselytizing, its trading practices, and the measles epidemic that devastated them.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]
- **The consequence.** The Oregon migration was at once a religious mission and a claim of sovereignty, and it rested on the same displacement of Native nations the chapter describes elsewhere.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-check-race]

### 5.2 The Mormon exodus from Nauvoo

- **The claim.** The Mormon migration was a religiously driven settlement project that sought a place apart, under its own governance, beyond the reach of its persecutors.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- **The departure.** After the killing of Joseph Smith and rising hostility in Illinois, Brigham Young led the first wave of Latter-day Saints out of Nauvoo, Illinois, beginning February 4, 1846. The National Park Service records that the 1846 trek ran from Nauvoo to the Missouri River near present-day Omaha, and the 1847 journey continued to the Great Salt Lake Valley.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- **The hardship.** The first 260 miles tested the vanguard most severely: freezing nights, steady rain, axle-deep mud, and flooding streams; the first death came March 18, 1846. Young reorganized the camp into companies of 100 families and established way stations at Garden Grove and Mount Pisgah to feed later emigrants.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- **The consequence.** The Mormons sought a sovereign community of their own in the Great Basin; their migration intensified the question, central to the chapter, of who could claim land and self-government in the expanding republic.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]

### 5.3 Migration, religion, and settler sovereignty

- **The claim.** The Oregon and Mormon migrations show that expansion was not only a federal policy but a movement of settler communities claiming land and self-rule.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- **The common ground.** Both migrations crossed Native homelands, both framed their journeys as escapes or callings, and both sought to establish communities with their own laws. Neither could be separated from the displacement of Native nations on the routes and at the destinations.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should connect the Oregon and Mormon migrations back to its central question: expansion created new sovereignties for some and destroyed or threatened those of others.[^ch17-check-race][^ch17-check-scope]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Removal was a federal program.** Congress passed the Indian Removal Act in 1830 by close votes and funded it; the president negotiated some seventy removal treaties during Jackson’s presidency; and the army and state militias carried removal out.[^ch17-loc-removal-guide][^ch17-removal-act][^ch17-history-state-removal]
- **The Cherokee resisted through law and lost to force.** Sequoyah’s syllabary, the 1827 constitution, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, and *Worcester v. Georgia* are documented, and so is the non-enforcement of the ruling and the coerced removal of 1838–39.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-worcester][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **Removal was national in scope.** It reached the Old Northwest as well as the Southeast, and it took the form of war and militia coercion as well as treaty.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]
- **The nations survived.** The Cherokee rebuilt a government in Indian Territory and some remained in the East; the Eastern Band and the Cherokee Nation continue to exercise sovereignty today.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears]
- **Texas and the borderlands were contested.** The Texas revolution, the republic, and annexation entangled land, slavery, and Native power; Mexico’s antislavery law made the border a route to freedom.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was removal inevitable?** Saunt argues emphatically that it was not, that individual and institutional choices produced it; older accounts framed it as the unavoidable result of “civilization” meeting “savagery.” The chapter should weigh contingency against structural pressure.[^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-jackson-removal-message]
- **How many were removed and how many died?** Estimates range from about 50,000 to about 100,000 for the removed and from hundreds to several thousand for Cherokee deaths. The differences reflect dates, definitions, and sources, and the chapter must give ranges rather than one number.[^ch17-history-state-removal][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west][^ch17-check-numbers]
- **What was the weight of slavery in the Texas revolution?** Historians range from arguing that it was a leading cause to treating it as one among several. The Handbook of Texas sets out the positions and warns against monocausal answers.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-check-race]
- **How should borderlands sovereignty be told?** Hämäläinen and DeLay center Indigenous power in the Mexican North; other scholars caution against overstating a Comanche “empire.” Both the power and its limits need evidence.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-sources]
- **Whose voices survive?** Government records, treaties, and court opinions document the policy and the signers; Cherokee petitions, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, and nation histories document resistance. Many removed people left no direct testimony, and the chapter must name that silence rather than speak for them.[^ch17-docsteach-petition][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix][^ch17-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which removal was a deliberate federal project, contested by Indigenous nations who built modern institutions to resist it, and fatal in its consequences without ever extinguishing the nations it displaced — and in which the borderlands to the south and west were shaped by Indigenous power as much as by American expansion.
- **The benevolent-necessity tradition.** The policy’s own defenders, from Jackson’s message to the commissioners of Indian affairs, framed removal as humane and inevitable, a way to save a “vanishing” people. The chapter should treat this as a claim to be tested against the record, not as description.[^ch17-jackson-removal-message][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]
- **The deportation and dispossession synthesis.** Saunt’s *Unworthy Republic* reframes removal as state-sponsored deportation and expulsion, tied to capitalism extending land and slavery and framed as a model others would follow, and insists that it was contingent.[^ch17-saunt-review]
- **The Indigenous-centered and sovereignty accounts.** Perdue and Green, the Cherokee Nation’s institutional history, and legal historians of *Worcester* center Cherokee nation-building, legal resistance, and the survival of sovereignty, reading removal from inside the nation as well as from Washington.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-check-sources]
- **The borderlands turn.** Hämäläinen and DeLay place Comanche and other Indigenous power at the center of the Mexican North and argue it shaped the region’s history and the U.S.-Mexican conflict.[^ch17-tsha-comanche][^ch17-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions show that removal and survival, conquest and sovereignty, were parts of one history. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not teams, and test each against the 1830 votes, the treaty texts, the routes, and the persistence of the nations.[^ch17-check-table][^ch17-check-sources]

## Legacies

Removal did not erase the nations it displaced. The Cherokee rebuilt a government at Tahlequah under a new constitution in 1839, and about a thousand Cherokee who escaped the roundup in North Carolina became the Eastern Band; both nations continue to exercise treaty rights and sovereignty today. The same is true of the Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, and the Great Lakes nations whose removals this chapter records.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]

The chapter’s central question has a double answer. Sovereignty survived because nations carried it with them: they wrote constitutions, printed newspapers, filed suits, and rebuilt governments. But survival came at a cost the policy intended to be total — thousands dead, homelands lost, and a century of further dispossession ahead.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]

The borderlands left a different inheritance. Texas entered the Union as a slave state in 1845, and the Mexican North’s border, once a route to freedom, became a line that American slavery pressed against and eventually crossed. The unresolved questions of Texas annexation, slavery’s extension, and the status of Indian Country carried forward into the war and crisis the next chapters describe.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom][^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The United States’ removal policy and its borderlands were entangled with Mexico, Britain, and an Atlantic economy in land and cotton. Saunt argues the American handling of the “Indian question” became a model for other nations expelling populations, and the Texas question drew in British and Mexican diplomacy.[^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
- **Archives and silences.** Treaties, statutes, court opinions, and removal rolls record the policy and its agents most fully; Cherokee petitions, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, and nation histories preserve an Indigenous counter-archive, while most removed people left no direct testimony. The chapter should name that unevenness and seek the displaced in petitions, rolls, and missionary and traveler records without speaking for them.[^ch17-docsteach-petition][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix][^ch17-check-race]
- **Distant connections.** The market revolution that made land and cotton valuable belongs to Chapter 15; the political history of the 1830 removal vote belongs to Chapter 16; the Mexican-American War and the Guadalupe Hidalgo settlement belong to Chapter 18; and the later Plains conquest belongs to Chapter 25.[^ch17-check-ch16][^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-check-scope]

## Summary

- By the 1820s the Cherokee had a written language, a constitution, a national newspaper, and a government that the state of Georgia moved to abolish.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-phoenix]
- In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) the Marshall Court called the Cherokee a “domestic, dependent nation”; in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) it held that Georgia’s laws “can have no force” in Cherokee territory — a ruling Georgia and the president declined to enforce.[^ch17-georgiaencyclopedia-worcester][^ch17-worcester]
- The Indian Removal Act of 1830 passed the Senate 28–19 and the House 102–97 and funded a federal removal program that Jackson called a “benevolent policy.”[^ch17-loc-removal-guide][^ch17-jackson-removal-message]
- The Treaty of New Echota (December 1835), signed by a minority of Cherokees and ratified by one Senate vote, led to the forced removal of 1838–39; sources estimate the Cherokee dead at several hundred to about 4,000 of roughly 16,000.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-check-numbers]
- Removal reached the Old Northwest through war and militia coercion, including Black Hawk’s defeat in 1832 and the Potawatomi Trail of Death of 1838.[^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk][^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death]
- On the Southern Plains, Comanche power defined Comanchería and shaped the Mexican North into the 1840s.[^ch17-tsha-comanche]
- Anglo colonization of Mexican Texas collided with Mexico’s 1829 abolition of slavery; the 1836 revolution produced a republic whose constitution protected slavery, and annexation became a national controversy.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- The Oregon and Mormon migrations carried settler communities across Native homelands and made new claims to land and self-government.[^ch17-tah-whitman-letters][^ch17-nps-mormon-1846]
- The debate turns on whether removal was humane necessity, federal deportation, a contest Indigenous nations survived, or a borderlands history of Indigenous power, and the honest answer holds several of these together.[^ch17-saunt-review][^ch17-check-table]

### Primary documents and contemporary accounts

- **The Treaty of New Echota (December 29, 1835), and the supplementary and western-Cherokee articles.** The chapter’s opening document; read the preamble’s claim of authority against the signers and the required two-year removal.[^ch17-new-echota-treaty]
- **John Ross, letter “To the Senate and House of Representatives” (September 28, 1836), and the 1836 Cherokee petition in protest of the treaty.** The Cherokee constitutional protest; the petition, signed by thousands, is in the Records of the U.S. Senate.[^ch17-nps-ross-protest][^ch17-docsteach-petition]
- **Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), and the Indian Removal Act of 1830.** The sovereignty ruling and the statute; quote the opinion and the act exactly and cite the *United States Reports* and the *Statutes at Large*.[^ch17-worcester][^ch17-removal-act]
- **Andrew Jackson’s Second Annual Message (December 6, 1830), and Narcissa Whitman’s Oregon Trail letters (1836).** The policy’s own justification and a settler’s view of the West; both should be read for their work of persuasion.[^ch17-jackson-removal-message][^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]

### Removal, nations, and the making of Indian Country

- **The Cherokee Nation, “The History of the Cherokee Nation.”** The Nation’s account of the constitution, the cases, the treaty, removal, and the rebuilding at Tahlequah; an institutional Indigenous source.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history]
- **The National Park Service, “What Happened on the Trail of Tears?” and the Florida Department of State, “The Seminole Wars.”** Accessible government syntheses of removal and the Seminole wars; verify the figures against the records.[^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-florida-seminole-wars]
- **The Citizen Potawatomi Nation Cultural Heritage Center, “Trail of Death,” and the Wisconsin Historical Society, “The Black Hawk War.”** The Old Northwest removals by militia march and by war; read them together with Map 13.[^ch17-potawatomi-trail-of-death][^ch17-wisconsinhistory-blackhawk]

### Texas, the borderlands, and migration

- **The Handbook of Texas Online, “Texas Revolution” and “Comanche Indians.”** Detailed, sourced accounts of the revolution’s causes and of Comanche power; the Handbook sets out the competing explanations of the revolution.[^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution][^ch17-tsha-comanche]
- **The National Endowment for the Humanities, “South to Freedom” (interview with Sean M. Kelley).** The southern Underground Railroad and the Texas–Mexico border as a route to freedom.[^ch17-neh-south-to-freedom]
- **The National Park Service, “The 1846 Trek,” and Teaching American History’s “Letters and Journals from the Oregon Trail.”** The Mormon exodus and the Whitman migration; use them for the settlers’ own words and for the routes.[^ch17-nps-mormon-1846][^ch17-tah-whitman-letters]

### The debate and the historiography

- **Claudio Saunt, *Unworthy Republic: The Dispossession of Native Americans and the Road to Indian Territory* (2020).** The strongest statement of removal as federal deportation tied to slavery and capitalism; read it before judging it.[^ch17-saunt-review]
- **Elliott West, “The Indian Removal Act,” *History Now* (2009).** A synthesis of the policy, the “permanent Indian frontier,” and the West the removed nations entered.[^ch17-gilderlehrman-west]
- **Theda Perdue and Michael D. Green, *The Cherokee Nation and the Trail of Tears* (2007).** The leading Indigenous-centered account of the Cherokee removal; discovered but not yet consulted in full.[^ch17-check-sources]
- **Pekka Hämäläinen, *The Comanche Empire* (2008), and Brian DeLay, *War of a Thousand Deserts* (2008).** The borderlands turn in Native history; discovered but not yet consulted in full.[^ch17-check-sources]

> *A closing note.* The United States removed tens of thousands of Native people to make room for a republic that called itself free, and it did so through statutes, treaties, courts, and soldiers. The Cherokee answered with a constitution, a newspaper, and a lawsuit, and the Supreme Court agreed with them; the state and the president did not. What the chapter records is not the disappearance of nations but the attempt to make them disappear, and the survival of peoples who carried their sovereignty into Indian Country and beyond. The chapter’s question is how that survival was possible, and what it cost.[^ch17-cherokee-nation-history][^ch17-worcester][^ch17-nps-trail-of-tears][^ch17-saunt-review]

Chapter 18 takes up the annexation decision’s international consequences and the Mexican-American War.[^ch17-check-ch18][^ch17-tsha-texas-revolution]
