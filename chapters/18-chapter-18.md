## Epigraphs

> “It is … our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions.”[^ch18-osullivan-manifest]

— John L. O’Sullivan, “Annexation,” *The United States Magazine and Democratic Review*, July–August 1845, urging the annexation of Texas.[^ch18-osullivan-manifest]

> “But now, after reiterated menaces, Mexico has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.”[^ch18-polk-war-message]

— President James K. Polk, Special Message to Congress, May 11, 1846, asking for a declaration of war.[^ch18-polk-war-message]

> “Whether the particular spot of soil on which the blood of our citizens was so shed, was, or was not, our own soil.”[^ch18-lincoln-spot]

— Representative Abraham Lincoln, “Spot Resolutions,” introduced in the House of Representatives, December 22, 1847.[^ch18-lincoln-spot]

> “For myself, I was bitterly opposed to the measure, and to this day regard the war, which resulted, as one of the most unjust ever waged by a stronger against a weaker nation.”[^ch18-grant-memoirs]

— Ulysses S. Grant, *Personal Memoirs of U. S. Grant* (1885), recalling his service as a young lieutenant in Mexico.[^ch18-grant-memoirs]

- **Why these four.** An editor told the nation that expansion was providence. A president told Congress that Mexico had attacked the United States on its own soil. A freshman congressman asked which soil. A general who fought the war called it unjust. The four disagree about whether the war was destiny, defense, theft, or tragedy — and about who had the authority to decide where the nation’s boundary ran.[^ch18-check-epigraphs]
- **The attribution rule.** O’Sullivan’s sentence reaches this outline through the American Yawp Reader and must be collated against the *Democratic Review* (vol. 17, July–August 1845). Polk’s sentence must be collated against the printed message and the *Congressional Globe*; the message also contains the variant “shed the blood of our fellow-citizens on our own soil,” which must be distinguished. Lincoln’s sentence comes from the House’s presentation of the Spot Resolutions and must be collated against the *Congressional Globe*. Grant’s sentence comes from the 1885–86 *Personal Memoirs* via a teaching edition and the U.S. Army’s appreciation and must be collated against the printed memoir.[^ch18-check-epigraphs][^ch18-osullivan-manifest][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-lincoln-spot][^ch18-grant-memoirs]

Grant is the chapter’s most nearly historical voice — a participant writing a retrospective judgment — but he is not a professional historian. ## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The place.** Rancho de Carricitos lay on the north bank of the Río Grande, about 28 miles upriver from the American post at Fort Texas, in a large farm field fenced by high, nearly impenetrable chaparral thickets. The National Park Service notes that the ranch’s exact location has since been forgotten because the river has shifted.[^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The patrol.** On the morning of April 25, 1846, Captain Seth Thornton led a party of 63 U.S. Dragoons to investigate reports that a large Mexican force had crossed the river the previous day.[^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The trap.** Mexican General Anastasio Torrejón had brought almost 1,600 cavalry and infantry across the Río Grande and, learning of Thornton’s approach, surrounded the fenced field and blocked its only gate with infantry in line. Surprised and unable to cut their way out, the dragoons surrendered.[^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The toll.** Eleven U.S. soldiers lay dead on the field, and 46 — including Captain Thornton — were taken prisoner; Mexican forces suffered only minor casualties. The American Battlefield Trust gives the engaged strengths as about 60 Americans against about 1,600 Mexicans and the American casualties as 17.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos]
- **The frontier of a disputed claim.** The land between the Nueces River and the Río Grande — the “Nueces Strip” — was claimed by the United States as Texas and by Mexico as its own. Mexico held that the boundary was the Nueces; the United States held that it was the Río Grande. Thornton’s patrol was therefore operating on ground whose national ownership both governments denied.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-grant-memoirs]
- **The message to Washington.** News of the skirmish reached Fort Texas the same day and Washington, D.C., on May 10, 1846. Polk told Congress that Mexico “has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil,” and Congress declared war on May 13.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-house-work29]

### The scene’s argument

- **A skirmish on contested ground.** The scene should open on the field at Carricitos and narrow to the moment the dragoons found the gate blocked. The point is not that Thornton blundered but that the clash occurred on ground whose ownership was disputed — the very question the war would be fought to answer by force.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-ahc-thornton]
- **The claim and the contradiction.** Polk told Congress that Mexico had invaded “our territory” and shed “American blood upon the American soil.” Mexico answered that Thornton’s men were the invaders on Mexican soil. The chapter should let the contradiction stand, because the contest over where the boundary ran, and who could decide, is the chapter’s first subject.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: how did a republic founded on consent govern a conquest that enlarged and destabilized it?
- **The turn.** The war did not settle the boundary by consent; it enlarged the republic by force and then made the new territory the object of a domestic conflict over slavery, citizenship, and property that military victory could not resolve.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The widening.** The scene stands at Carricitos in April 1846. The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader back to the 1844 election and outward to Oregon, California, and Washington.[^ch18-check-scope]

### What must not be invented

- **No weather, dialogue, or interior thought.** The place, the patrol’s size, the ambush, the toll, and the disputed boundary are documented. The day’s weather, what Thornton said, and what either commander intended are not attested in the cited sources; do not stage them.[^ch18-check-scene][^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **No precise “spot.”** Contemporary polemic and later commemoration both tried to locate the exact ground where the war “began.” The National Park Service states that the shifting Río Grande has made the exact site uncertain, and the battlefield trust agrees. The chapter should report the uncertainty rather than resolve it.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos]
- **No exact casualty figure stated as fact.** The sources give 11 killed and 46 captured, or 17 American casualties, depending on definitions; present the figures with their sources rather than harmonizing them silently.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-abt-carricitos][^ch18-check-numbers]
- **No modern phrasing in their mouths.** Quotations come from the consulted texts character by character; do not modernize or paraphrase inside quotation marks.[^ch18-check-epigraphs]

### Drafting target

Three hundred to four hundred words. Open at Carricitos, establish the patrol and the ambush, and let Polk’s claim that American blood had been shed on American soil collide with Mexico’s claim that the soil was its own. End with the guiding question, not a verdict. The framing paragraph follows and supplies the years, scope, and limits.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-check-scene]

## 1. Polk, Texas, and Oregon

### 1.1 The election of 1844 and the annexation of Texas

- **The claim.** Texas annexation became the central issue of the 1844 election, and its resolution by joint resolution, on the eve of a war the new president would provoke, set the terms of everything that followed.[^ch18-state-texas-annex]
- **The decision.** President John Tyler, lacking a party and seeking reelection, made annexation his cause; a treaty he concluded in April 1844 failed in the Senate, and annexation was finally accomplished by a joint resolution that Tyler signed on March 1, 1845. Texas was admitted to the Union on December 29, 1845.[^ch18-state-texas-annex]
- **The election.** In 1844 the Democrat James K. Polk defeated Henry Clay in a close contest in which annexation and the “reoccupation” of Oregon were central. Polk understood his victory as a mandate for expansion, though the margin was narrow.[^ch18-state-texas-annex][^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The inherited dispute.** With Texas came the boundary question. Mexico had never recognized Texan independence and had warned that annexation meant war; it insisted the southern boundary was the Nueces, while the United States claimed the Río Grande.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-state-texas-annex]
- **The consequence.** Annexation converted a dormant border dispute into an international crisis. The chapter should make clear that the decision was contested at home — the election was close — and that it was taken with full knowledge that it risked war.[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-state-texas-annex]

### 1.2 “54°40′ or fight” and the Oregon compromise

- **The claim.** Polk coupled the Texas claim with an Oregon claim, then settled Oregon by compromise, revealing that expansion was a set of negotiable calculations, not a single providential program.[^ch18-state-oregon]
- **The claim and the slogan.** Expansionists demanded the whole of Oregon to “54°40′,” the latitude of the southern boundary of Russian Alaska; the slogan “Fifty-four Forty or Fight” framed the 1844 campaign. Polk proposed a settlement at the 49th parallel.[^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]
- **The compromise.** Great Britain and the United States signed the Oregon Treaty on June 15, 1846, ending 28 years of joint occupation and setting the 49th parallel as the boundary west of the Rockies, with Vancouver Island remaining British. The Senate ratified it by a vote of 41 to 14 on June 18, 1846.[^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]
- **The significance.** Polk got less than the slogan demanded and accepted the compromise rather than fight Britain, even as the Mexican crisis reached its climax. His willingness to bargain in the north and to force matters in the south is central to the chapter’s argument about choice and calculation.[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]
- **The consequence.** The Oregon settlement shows that “manifest destiny” did not dictate a single outcome. It depended on assessments of power, risk, and cost — assessments that differed sharply between Oregon and the Río Grande.[^ch18-state-oregon]

### 1.3 The Slidell mission and the failure of diplomacy

- **The claim.** Before the fighting began, Polk tried to buy what he wanted; Mexico’s refusal to negotiate on his terms, and his decision to station troops on the disputed border, produced the war.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The mission.** In November 1845 Polk sent John Slidell to Mexico with authority to settle the Texas boundary and to purchase California and New Mexico. Mexico, whose government was already unstable, refused to receive him.[^ch18-polk-war-message]
- **The provocation.** Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor to move the “Army of Observation” from Corpus Christi to the Río Grande — into the disputed territory that Mexico claimed — and simultaneously prepared a Pacific naval squadron. Diplomacy and coercion proceeded together.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-ahc-thornton]
- **The Mexican position.** Mexico had broken diplomatic relations over annexation and did not regard the territory between the Nueces and the Río Grande as American. The chapter should present Mexico’s claim, and Mexico’s internal instability, from the Mexican side, not merely as a refusal.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-sources]
- **The consequence.** By the spring of 1846 an American army stood on contested ground, a diplomatic mission had failed, and a clash was probable. Thornton’s patrol produced it.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-check-consent]

## 2. The disputed border and the war decision

### 2.1 The Nueces Strip and the boundary dispute

- **The claim.** The war began over a boundary that both governments claimed and neither had settled — a conflict of sovereignty, not a sudden surprise attack.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-grant-memoirs]
- **The rival claims.** After San Jacinto, the captured Santa Anna had agreed in the Treaties of Velasco to a Río Grande boundary, but Mexico repudiated the agreement as the act of a prisoner. Mexico insisted the historic Texas boundary was the Nueces; the United States asserted the Río Grande, which carried the boundary far to the south and west.[^ch18-ahc-thornton]
- **The strategic stake.** The Río Grande claim mattered because the disputed strip led toward the territories Polk wanted — New Mexico and Alta California. Grant later wrote that the annexationists “wanted more territory than they could possibly lay any claim to.”[^ch18-grant-memoirs]
- **The consequence.** The chapter should present the boundary dispute as a genuine legal and diplomatic disagreement that Polk resolved by moving an army into the disputed ground — not as an unambiguous invasion of the United States.[^ch18-ahc-thornton][^ch18-check-consent]

### 2.2 Thornton’s patrol and Polk’s war message

- **The claim.** The skirmish at Carricitos gave Polk the incident he used to declare that Mexico had attacked the United States, a claim Lincoln and others immediately contested.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message]
- **The incident.** On April 25, 1846, Thornton’s 63 dragoons were surrounded and captured at Rancho de Carricitos by a much larger Mexican force; 11 Americans were killed and 46 taken prisoner. It was the first armed clash of the war.[^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The message.** On May 11, 1846, Polk told Congress that Mexico “has passed the boundary of the United States, has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.” He asked for a declaration of war and for authority to call out militia.[^ch18-polk-war-message]
- **The contested premise.** The message assumed precisely what was in dispute: that the Río Grande was the boundary. Polk’s own text elsewhere described the ground more loosely, and the House’s later account of the episode preserved the phrase “American blood upon America’s soil” as the contested claim it was.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-polk-war-message-house]
- **The consequence.** The war’s justification rested on a boundary claim, not a settled fact. Every later argument about the “spot” grew from this message.[^ch18-lincoln-spot]

### 2.3 The war vote, the dissent, and the “spot”

- **The claim.** Congress declared war by lopsided margins, but a substantial minority — including many Whigs — regarded the war as an unconstitutional and unnecessary aggression.[^ch18-house-work29][^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The votes.** The House voted 174 to 14 on May 11–13, 1846, and the Senate voted 40 to 2. Polk signed the declaration on May 13.[^ch18-house-work29][^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The dissent.** Former president John Quincy Adams called it “this most outrageous war,” waged for territorial aggrandizement and the extension of slavery. On January 3, 1848, the House passed a resolution declaring that the war had been “unnecessarily and unconstitutionally commenced by the president.”[^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The “spot.”** On December 22, 1847, Representative Abraham Lincoln introduced resolutions demanding to know “whether the particular spot of soil on which the blood of our citizens was so shed, was, or was not, our own soil.” In a House speech on January 12, 1848, he argued that Polk’s evidence fell “far short of proving his justification.”[^ch18-lincoln-spot][^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The consequence.** The war’s legitimacy was contested from the first week and remained contested for a century. The chapter should give the dissent its full weight and its strongest arguments, not treat it as eccentric.[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-peacehistory]

### 2.4 The Wilmot Proviso and the turn to slavery

- **The claim.** Before the war was won, the question of slavery in any territory taken from Mexico split Congress along sectional lines, revealing that the conquest would poison domestic politics.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot]
- **The proviso.** On August 8, 1846, Representative David Wilmot of Pennsylvania introduced an amendment to a war-appropriations bill prohibiting slavery in any territory acquired from Mexico. It passed the House but failed in the Senate, and was reintroduced repeatedly without becoming law.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-battlefields-impact]
- **The sectional shift.** The vote aligned northern Democrats and northern Whigs against southerners of both parties, shifting politics from party division toward sectional division. The proposal’s defenders often framed free territory as reserved for White settlement, not as a commitment to Black freedom.[^ch18-battlefields-impact][^ch18-check-race]
- **The consequence.** The war had barely begun before its likely fruits became the object of the sectional conflict that would produce the Civil War. The chapter should connect the proviso to the peace and the 1850 settlement in §5.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

- **The claim.** Map 14 makes the war’s geography visible: the disputed boundary, the theaters of campaign, and the territory ceded in 1848 — distinct from the later Gadsden Purchase.[^ch18-check-map]
- **What the map shows.** The United States and Mexican claims in Texas (the Nueces versus the Río Grande); the principal American campaigns — Taylor’s in northeastern Mexico, Kearny’s to New Mexico and California, Scott’s from Veracruz to Mexico City; the 1848 boundary established by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo; and the territory ceded, with the later Gadsden Purchase explicitly omitted or shown only as clearly labeled post-1848 context.[^ch18-check-map][^ch18-archives-guadalupe]

## 3. Northern campaigns

### 3.1 Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma

- **The claim.** Taylor’s first battles on the Río Grande blunted the Mexican advance and opened the invasion of northeastern Mexico, demonstrating American tactical superiority that the war’s outcome made decisive.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-archives-guadalupe]
- **The battles.** On May 8–9, 1846, Taylor defeated General Mariano Arista at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma near the mouth of the Río Grande. The engagements followed Torrejón’s move east after Carricitos.[^ch18-abt-carricitos][^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The observers.** Among the young officers present were Ulysses S. Grant, Robert E. Lee, and other men who would command in the Civil War. Grant, stationed at Corpus Christi before the march south, called the fighting his first taste of combat.[^ch18-army-grant][^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The consequence.** Military success in the north made the war appear quick and cheap to the public while the political questions went unanswered.[^ch18-battlefields-impact]

### 3.2 Monterrey, Buena Vista, and the occupation of northern Mexico

- **The claim.** Taylor’s campaign captured Monterrey and defeated Santa Anna at Buena Vista, but the north alone did not bring Mexico to terms, pushing Polk toward the invasion of the heartland.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The operations.** In September 1846 Taylor’s army assaulted Monterrey in fierce street fighting; in February 1847 Taylor’s smaller force defeated Santa Anna’s larger army at Buena Vista. Grant, who was with Taylor’s army, later wrote that he served through every major battle except Buena Vista.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-grant-memoirs]
- **Taylor the politician.** Taylor’s victories made him a national hero and, in 1848, the Whig candidate for president — a general who had opposed the war becoming the president who inherited its consequences.[^ch18-nps-1848]
- **The consequence.** The war enlarged Polk’s problems: the general he distrusted became a rival for the presidency, and the north’s success did not produce a peace.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]

### 3.3 New Mexico, California, and the Bear Flag Revolt

- **The claim.** While Taylor fought in the east, Kearny’s Army of the West and the Pacific Squadron brought New Mexico and California under American control, where conquest collided with the existing Californio society.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **New Mexico.** General Stephen Kearny marched west from Fort Leavenworth and occupied Santa Fe in August 1846 without a major battle, then continued toward California.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **California.** In June 1846 American settlers rose against Mexican authority in the Bear Flag Revolt at Sonoma, seizing the Californio commander Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo, who had favored American immigration and was taken prisoner despite not resisting. Commodore John D. Sloat and, later, Commodore Robert F. Stockton and Lieutenant Colonel John C. Frémont secured the territory; Californios rose briefly against the American garrison in Los Angeles before the Treaty of Cahuenga ended the fighting in January 1847.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **Vallejo’s story.** Vallejo was an extraordinary Californio — soldier, colonizer, and founder of Sonoma — who welcomed American settlers before the revolt and was imprisoned by them afterward. His life dramatizes the chapter’s argument that the conquest’s promise of citizenship and property was already broken in practice.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-check-citizenship]
- **The consequence.** By the time the treaty was signed, the United States occupied the whole territory it would claim, and the Californios faced an American order that would soon strip many of the rights the treaty promised.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-homestead-article10]

### 3.4 The volunteers, the occupation, and the cost

- **The claim.** The war was fought substantially by volunteers whose conduct and losses shaped how Americans and Mexicans experienced the conflict.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-ecwc]
- **The volunteers.** The Regular Army was small; Congress authorized large numbers of volunteers, many from the South and West, whose discipline was uneven and whose violence against Mexican civilians was documented. Zachary Taylor found some volunteer units “more trouble than they were worth.”[^ch18-ecwc]
- **The occupation.** U.S. forces occupied Mexican towns and, later, the capital, imposing martial law and suppressing guerrilla resistance; Mexican civilians suffered from violence, requisition, and bombardment. The war became the first sustained American counterinsurgency abroad.[^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The cost.** Of roughly 90,000 U.S. soldiers who served, nearly 14,000 died — the highest death rate of any American foreign war — and the great majority died of disease, not battle wounds. Mexican military and civilian deaths reached into the tens of thousands.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-numbers]
- **The consequence.** The human cost was far larger than the brief, victorious battles suggested. The chapter should measure the war by its dead as well as its territory.[^ch18-peacehistory]

## 4. Veracruz to Mexico City

### 4.1 The Veracruz landing and siege

- **The claim.** Scott’s campaign opened with the largest American amphibious landing yet attempted, giving the United States a base to march on the Mexican capital.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The landing.** On March 9, 1847, Scott landed about 12,000 troops near Veracruz, supported by Commodore Matthew C. Perry’s squadron. The landing was the first major American amphibious operation.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The siege.** Mexican General Juan Morales held Veracruz with about 3,400 men; a bombardment opened a gap in the walls, and the city surrendered on March 25–29 with only about 80 American casualties. Future Civil War commanders, including Lee, Grant, Meade, and Longstreet, took part.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The consequence.** The base at Veracruz made the advance inland possible and put Mexico City within reach.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]

### 4.2 Cerro Gordo and Puebla

- **The claim.** Scott’s victory at Cerro Gordo and his bloodless occupation of Puebla opened the road to the capital.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The battle.** Santa Anna entrenched at Cerro Gordo to block the mountain road; Scott, aided by Robert E. Lee’s reconnaissance, flanked the position over difficult terrain and defeated the Mexicans on April 18, 1847. Santa Anna suffered more than 1,000 casualties and some 3,000 captured, against about 400 American casualties.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **Puebla.** Scott’s army entered Puebla on May 1, 1847; the city, Mexico’s second largest, capitulated without resistance and became the American base and supply depot.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The consequence.** The road to Mexico City lay open, but the occupation raised the problem of maintaining order and supply in a hostile country.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-peacehistory]

### 4.3 The Valley of Mexico and the fall of the capital

- **The claim.** Scott’s capture of Mexico City in September 1847 ended the conventional war but not the conflict, because Mexico refused to concede and the American occupation had no government to treat with.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The campaign.** Scott fought a series of battles around the capital — Contreras and Churubusco in August, and Molino del Rey and Chapultepec in September — defeating Santa Anna’s army and entering Mexico City on September 14, 1847.[^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The fall.** The American flag rose over the capital, and the Mexican government fled. Hostilities continued in the countryside, and Mexico’s leaders debated whether to continue the fight or negotiate.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-state-texas-annex]
- **The consequence.** Military victory did not produce peace on its own. The treaty would depend on diplomacy and on Mexicans willing to sign — and on an American diplomat who defied his own president.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-state-texas-annex]

### 4.4 Guerrilla war, the San Patricios, and the meaning of the occupation

- **The claim.** The occupation exposed the war’s coercive character and the conflicted loyalties it produced, especially among the Irish Catholic soldiers who deserted to fight for Mexico.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-ecwc]
- **The resistance.** Mexican irregulars and civilians resisted the occupation throughout, and American forces responded with punitive measures that fell on civilians as well as combatants.[^ch18-peacehistory]
- **The San Patricios.** The Batallón de San Patricio, largely composed of Irish Catholic immigrants who deserted the U.S. Army, fought for Mexico and were captured; some were executed after the battles around Mexico City. Their story raises the question of who belonged to “the people” whose consent the republic claimed to govern by.[^ch18-check-citizenship][^ch18-ecwc]
- **The consequence.** The occupation revealed that the war was not only between governments but also among divided loyalties within both nations, and it foreshadowed the citizenship conflicts the conquest would create.[^ch18-check-race][^ch18-ecwc]

## 5. Treaty, land claims, California, and the 1850 settlement

### 5.1 Trist and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo

- **The claim.** The peace was made by a diplomat whom Polk had recalled, and the treaty he signed ceded half of Mexico while promising to protect the people and property already there.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe]
- **The negotiation.** Nicholas P. Trist, Polk’s chief clerk, negotiated with a Mexican commission at Guadalupe Hidalgo in early 1848. Polk had recalled him in October 1847, but Trist, believing Washington misread Mexico’s situation, ignored the recall and signed the treaty on February 2, 1848. He wrote that the “decision is altogether my own.”[^ch18-archives-guadalupe]
- **The terms.** Mexico ceded about 525,000 square miles — nearly 55 percent of its territory, including California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and Colorado, and parts of New Mexico, Oklahoma, Kansas, and Wyoming — acknowledged the Río Grande boundary and relinquished Texas; the United States paid $15 million and assumed the claims of American citizens against Mexico.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-avalon-guadalupe]
- **The ratification.** The Senate ratified the treaty on March 10, 1848. Sources disagree on the vote — the National Archives and several institutional accounts give 34 to 14, while the Library of Congress, the Capitol’s own history, and the State Department give 38 to 14 — and the chapter must state the discrepancy rather than choose silently.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-check-numbers]
- **The consequence.** The treaty’s terms were those of a conqueror, but its promises to the conquered were enforceable in principle and largely sacrificed in practice.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-check-citizenship]

### 5.2 In Focus: Article VIII and the promise to the conquered

- **The claim.** Article VIII of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo promised the Mexicans within the ceded territory the freedom to stay or leave and the protection of their property, a promise whose text must be distinguished from its later implementation.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe]
- **In Focus: Article VIII.** **In Focus.** The box reads a single text: Article VIII of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (signed February 2, 1848). It provided that Mexicans “now established in territories previously belonging to Mexico, and which remain for the future within the limits of the United States … shall be free to continue where they now reside, or to remove at any time to the Mexican Republic, retaining the property which they possess in the said territories.” Those who stayed could “either retain the title and rights of Mexican citizens or acquire those of citizens of the United States,” under an obligation to choose “within one year from the date of the exchange of ratifications.” And property “now belonging to Mexicans not established there, shall be inviolably respected.”[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe]
- **How to read it.** The article is an instrument for reading conquest at its most protective and its most fragile. It promised choice, property, and equal guarantees to the conquered — yet it made citizenship a deadline and left enforcement to the government that had just taken the land.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-check-citizenship]
- **The related articles.** Article IX promised that those who chose American citizenship would be “admitted at the proper time (to be judged of by the Congress of the United States) to the enjoyment of all the rights of citizens”; Article X, which would have guaranteed Mexican land grants, was struck out by the Senate; Article XII set the $15 million payment. The box should note the contrast between Article VIII’s text and the treaty as ratified.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10]
- **The text and its limits.** The quotations are transcribed character by character from the consulted treaty text and must be collated against the enrolled treaty and the *Statutes at Large*. The box should note that the treaty’s promises were addressed to Mexican citizens choosing to remain, not to Indigenous peoples, whose rights the treaty did not recognize.[^ch18-check-focus][^ch18-check-citizenship]

### 5.3 Land claims, the Californios, and the broken promise

- **The claim.** The treaty’s property guarantees were undermined by the Senate’s removal of Article X, by a burdensome claims process, and by the land hunger of American settlers, so that many Californios and other Mexican residents lost the property the treaty had promised to protect.[^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-sonoma-vallejo]
- **The strike.** The Senate removed Article X, which would have guaranteed the protection of Mexican land grants, before ratifying the treaty. The promise of property protection survived only in general language, and the property-rights provisions became the subject of decades of litigation.[^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-archives-guadalupe]
- **The burden.** Californio and New Mexican landholders had to prove their titles before American commissions under unfamiliar law, at great cost and delay, while squatters and tax burdens eroded their holdings. Vallejo, who had been imprisoned by the American conquerors, lost much of his estate in the decades that followed.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-check-citizenship]
- **The precedent.** The chapter should treat the broken promise as a central consequence of the war, not a footnote: the republic that claimed to govern by consent subjected the conquered to a property regime that tested and often denied their rights.[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-check-race]
- **The consequence.** The treaty’s text and its implementation diverged, and that divergence shaped the place of Mexican Americans in the United States for generations.[^ch18-check-citizenship][^ch18-avalon-guadalupe]

### 5.4 The Gold Rush and the making of California

- **The claim.** Gold, discovered nine days before the treaty’s signing, transformed the conquered territory into a magnet for migration and sped California toward statehood on American terms.[^ch18-nps-goldrush]
- **The discovery.** On January 24, 1848, James Marshall found gold at a sawmill on the South Fork of the American River for John Sutter. News spread despite efforts to keep it quiet, and President Polk confirmed the finds to Congress in December 1848.[^ch18-nps-goldrush]
- **The rush.** By the spring of 1849 the largest migration in American history to that point was under way, with about 25,000 arriving that year and an estimated 140,000 over the California Trail between 1849 and 1854.[^ch18-nps-goldrush]
- **The costs.** The rush devastated Native Californians through disease, starvation, and violence, and it overwhelmed the Californio society the treaty had promised to protect. California’s non-Native population soared within two years.[^ch18-nps-goldrush][^ch18-check-race]
- **The consequence.** Gold made California too populous and too important to be governed as a conquered province, forcing the statehood question that drove the settlement of 1850.[^ch18-nps-goldrush][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]

### 5.5 The Compromise of 1850 and the settlement of the conquest

- **The claim.** The Compromise of 1850 briefly settled the sectional crisis the conquest had created, admitting California free and strengthening the fugitive-slave law — deferring, not resolving, the conflict over slavery in the territories.[^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The crisis.** By 1849 California sought admission as a free state, threatening the sectional balance. Henry Clay proposed a package of resolutions; after seven months of debate, Stephen A. Douglas steered substitute bills through Congress in September 1850.[^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The terms.** The five statutes admitted California as a free state, organized Utah and New Mexico without a slavery restriction, adjusted the Texas boundary and assumed part of Texas’s debt, abolished the slave trade (not slavery) in the District of Columbia, and enacted a stricter Fugitive Slave Act requiring federal and local enforcement and penalizing those who aided escape.[^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The consequence.** The compromise postponed the sectional reckoning at the price of making the fugitive-slave question national and explosive. Its enforcement is the subject of Chapter 19.[^ch18-check-ch19][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The exclusion.** The chapter stops here and does not carry the story into the Gadsden Purchase of 1853–54, which belongs to Chapter 19 and App. B, Table 3.[^ch18-check-gadsden]

### 5.6 The peace as a problem in self-government

- **The claim.** The conquest enlarged the republic and multiplied the questions of membership it could not answer: whether the conquered would be citizens, whether slavery would spread, and by what right a republic of consent ruled people who had not consented.[^ch18-check-consent][^ch18-check-citizenship]
- **The problem stated.** The treaty promised citizenship and property; the Senate struck the strongest property guarantee; the Gold Rush made California a free state; the Compromise of 1850 bought time with a harsher fugitive-slave law.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The consequence.** This is the chapter’s core argument: military victory did not resolve the disputes over slavery, sovereignty, citizenship, and property that expansion magnified. It relocated and intensified them.[^ch18-battlefields-impact][^ch18-check-consent]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The war was provoked on a disputed boundary.** Historians across schools agree that American forces occupied territory Mexico claimed, that Thornton’s patrol clashed there in April 1846, and that Polk used the clash to obtain a declaration of war.[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-lincoln-spot]
- **The war was a conquest that seized half of Mexico.** The United States won every major battle, captured Mexico City, and by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo took about 525,000 square miles for $15 million.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-battlefields-scott]
- **The conquest reopened and intensified the slavery question.** The Wilmot Proviso, the sectional votes, and the Compromise of 1850 are documented, and the war’s leading participant-historian connected it directly to the coming Civil War.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-archives-compromise1850][^ch18-grant-memoirs]
- **The treaty promised rights that were largely denied.** Article VIII promised choice and property, Article IX promised citizenship, and Article X was struck; the implementation fell short of the text.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10]
- **The cost was heavy.** Roughly 13,000–14,000 American soldiers died, the great majority of disease, and Mexican military and civilian deaths were far higher.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-numbers]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was the war Polk’s personal project or a popular movement?** Howe argues the war was largely the implementation of one man’s vision, forced on a divided nation; others stress broad land hunger and racial ideology. The chapter should weigh contingency against structure.[^ch18-check-sources][^ch18-ecwc]
- **What was the weight of slavery?** Some historians treat the war as a slaveholders’ conspiracy; others stress Polk’s fixation on California, which was never likely to be a slave state. The chapter should test both against the decisions and the outcome.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-zinn-douglass][^ch18-check-race]
- **Was Mexico bound to lose, and was American victory inevitable?** The United States won every battle, but Mexico’s internal divisions, not only American power, shaped the outcome. Mexican historians emphasize Mexico’s own fractured politics and the diplomatic and human costs on the Mexican side.[^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-sources]
- **How should the treaty’s broken promises be weighed?** The gap between Article VIII’s text and the postwar land regime is documented, but the degree of deliberate dispossession and the persistence of Mexican American rights remain debated.[^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-check-citizenship]
- **Whose voices survive?** Presidential messages, treaties, statutes, and court records document the policy and its makers; the dissenting press, memoirs, and Mexican accounts preserve an opposition and a Mexican perspective, while the conquered poor and the enslaved left few direct records. The chapter must name that silence.[^ch18-zinn-douglass][^ch18-peacehistory][^ch18-check-race]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the Mexican-American War was a war of conquest provoked on contested ground, legitimated by a racialized doctrine of destiny and mission, opposed by a real minority, and concluded by a treaty whose promises to the conquered were largely unkept — and in which the conquest, far from settling the republic’s questions, intensified the conflict over slavery and membership that soon produced the Civil War.
- **The mission and destiny tradition.** Frederick Merk distinguished a broad popular “mission” of extending self-government from a narrower, disappointed “manifest destiny,” and Albert K. Weinberg traced expansionist ideology as a set of nationalist arguments. Read critically, this tradition takes the era’s own civic language seriously but can confuse a claim of consent with consent itself.[^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-check-sources]
- **The slaveholding-strategy reading.** Grant, Douglass, Adams, and later “Slave Power” scholarship argue that annexation and war served the extension of slavery. It connects the war to slavery’s political economy but can overstate a unified conspiratorial plan.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-zinn-douglass]
- **The conquest-and-race synthesis.** Reginald Horsman’s study of racial Anglo-Saxonism, Amy Greenberg’s account of a “wicked war,” Paul Foos on the volunteers, and Timothy Henderson’s Mexican-side account recover the ideology, the violence, and the cost. They strengthen the case for conquest but can flatten the variety of motives and the contingency of the outcome.[^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-check-sources]
- **The contingency account.** Howe and John Schroeder stress the shallowness of popular support and the importance of Polk’s decisions, while Anders Stephanson treats Manifest Destiny as a durable language of national chosenness. This literature restores agency and contingency but can understate structural pressure.[^ch18-ecwc][^ch18-check-sources]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Read together, these positions show that destination, slavery, and conquest were parts of one history. The chapter should present the debate as overlapping positions, not teams, and test each against the boundary dispute, the votes, the treaty text, and the persistence of Mexican American communities.[^ch18-check-table][^ch18-check-sources]

## Legacies

The peace did not settle the questions the war had raised. The territory won in 1848 reopened the conflict over slavery in the territories, and Ralph Waldo Emerson’s forecast — that the United States would “conquer Mexico, but it will be as the man who swallows the arsenic which will bring him down in turn” — captured the sense that the conquest had poisoned the republic’s politics. Grant put it more bluntly: “The Southern rebellion was largely the outgrowth of the Mexican war.”[^ch18-digitalhistory-significance][^ch18-grant-memoirs]

The conquest also created enduring communities and enduring wrongs. Mexican Americans in the Southwest trace their citizenship and their dispossession to the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, whose Article VIII promised property and choice and whose implementation often delivered neither. Californios such as Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo, who had welcomed American settlers and was imprisoned by them, lost much of what the treaty had promised to protect.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-check-citizenship]

The chapter’s central question has a double answer. Military victory enlarged the republic and made it a continental power. But it did so on terms that intensified the conflict over slavery, membership, and property, so that the war’s most durable result was the crisis that led, a dozen years later, to the Civil War.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-archives-compromise1850][^ch18-battlefields-impact]

## Summary

- In 1844 annexation became the central issue of the presidential election; Congress annexed Texas by joint resolution on March 1, 1845, and Texas entered the Union on December 29, 1845, over Mexico’s warning of war.[^ch18-state-texas-annex]
- Polk paired the Texas claim with Oregon, then compromised at the 49th parallel in the Oregon Treaty of June 15, 1846, showing that “manifest destiny” was a set of negotiable calculations.[^ch18-state-oregon][^ch18-oregonency]
- After the Slidell mission failed, Polk moved Taylor’s army onto the disputed Nueces Strip; on April 25, 1846, Thornton’s patrol was captured at Rancho de Carricitos, and Polk told Congress that Mexico had shed “American blood upon the American soil.”[^ch18-nps-carricitos][^ch18-polk-war-message]
- Congress declared war by 174 to 14 in the House and 40 to 2 in the Senate, over the dissent of Adams, Clay, and later Lincoln, whose “Spot Resolutions” asked which soil had been shed upon.[^ch18-house-work29][^ch18-lincoln-spot][^ch18-peacehistory]
- The Wilmot Proviso of August 1846 sought to bar slavery from any territory taken from Mexico; it passed the House, failed in the Senate, and shifted politics toward sectional alignment.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-battlefields-impact]
- Taylor won at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma, Monterrey, and Buena Vista; Kearny occupied New Mexico; and Americans seized California in the Bear Flag Revolt while imprisoning the Californio commander Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-sonoma-vallejo]
- Scott landed at Veracruz, won at Cerro Gordo, and captured Mexico City on September 14, 1847; roughly 13,000–14,000 American soldiers died, most of disease, and Mexican deaths were far higher.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-peacehistory]
- Nicholas Trist, recalled by Polk, negotiated the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo on February 2, 1848; Mexico ceded about 525,000 square miles for $15 million, and the Senate struck Article X’s protection of Mexican land grants. Article VIII’s promise of choice and property was largely unkept.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10]
- Gold, found nine days before the treaty’s signing, transformed California and drove the Compromise of 1850, which admitted California free and strengthened the Fugitive Slave Act — deferring the sectional crisis rather than resolving it.[^ch18-nps-goldrush][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- The debate turns on whether expansion was democratic opportunity, slaveholding strategy, conquest, or Polk’s contingent war, and the honest answer holds several of these together.[^ch18-check-table][^ch18-check-sources]

## Further Reading

This is a selective guide, not a citation ledger. The groups move from the primary documents through the war and its aftermath to the historiographical debate. Unless the annotation says otherwise, the works below were consulted for this outline at the level noted, and are cited in the combined notes. ### Primary documents and contemporary accounts

- **John L. O’Sullivan, “Annexation,” *The United States Magazine and Democratic Review* (July–August 1845).** The essay that coined “manifest destiny”; read it for how expansion was argued, not merely asserted.[^ch18-osullivan-manifest]
- **James K. Polk, Special Message to Congress (May 11, 1846), and Abraham Lincoln’s “Spot Resolutions” (December 22, 1847).** The war’s official justification and its most pointed legal challenge; quote both exactly and cite the *Congressional Globe*.[^ch18-polk-war-message][^ch18-lincoln-spot]
- **The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (February 2, 1848), Articles VIII, IX, X, and XII.** Read the property and citizenship promises against the treaty as ratified; the National Archives transcript and the Avalon Project text are complementary.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-archives-guadalupe]
- **Wilmot Proviso (August 8, 1846) and the Compromise of 1850.** The slavery question enters the war’s appropriations and returns in the settlement; use the National Archives transcriptions.[^ch18-docsteach-wilmot][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **Ulysses S. Grant, *Personal Memoirs* (1885), and Frederick Douglass, “The War with Mexico,” *North Star* (January 21, 1848).** A conqueror’s retrospective verdict and an abolitionist’s denunciation; both should be read for their work of persuasion and memory.[^ch18-grant-memoirs][^ch18-zinn-douglass]

### The war, the conquest, and its aftermath

- **The National Park Service, “Rancho de Carricitos” and the Palo Alto Battlefield materials.** The opening scene’s most reliable public account of the first clash and the disputed ground.[^ch18-nps-carricitos]
- **The National Archives, “Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)” and “Compromise of 1850 (1850).”** Accessible government syntheses of the treaty and the settlement, with transcriptions; verify the Senate vote discrepancy against the Senate record.[^ch18-archives-guadalupe][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
- **The Office of the Historian, U.S. Department of State, “The Annexation of Texas” and “The Oregon Territory, 1846.”** Concise diplomatic accounts of annexation and the Oregon compromise, with treaty dates.[^ch18-state-texas-annex][^ch18-state-oregon]
- **The American Battlefield Trust, “Winfield Scott’s Mexican Invasion,” “Rancho Carricitos,” and “The Impact of the Mexican American War.”** Campaign narrative and the war’s political aftermath; use for the sequence of battles and the Wilmot Proviso’s significance.[^ch18-battlefields-scott][^ch18-abt-carricitos][^ch18-battlefields-impact]
- **The City of Sonoma, “General Vallejo,” and the National Park Service, “The California Gold Rush.”** The Californio experience of conquest and the Gold Rush that remade California.[^ch18-sonoma-vallejo][^ch18-nps-goldrush]

### The debate and the historiography

- **Frederick Merk, *Manifest Destiny and Mission in American History* (1963), and Albert K. Weinberg, *Manifest Destiny* (1935).** The classic accounts of expansionist ideology, distinguishing popular mission from programmatic destiny; discovered but not yet consulted in full.[^ch18-check-sources]
- **Reginald Horsman, *Race and Manifest Destiny* (1981), and Amy S. Greenberg, *A Wicked War* (2012).** The racial ideology of expansion and the war’s domestic and moral cost; discovered but not yet consulted in full.[^ch18-check-sources]
- **Daniel Walker Howe, *What Hath God Wrought* (2007), and John H. Schroeder, *Mr. Polk’s War* (1973).** The contingency and opposition literature — the case that the war was Polk’s and deeply contested; discovered but not yet consulted in full.[^ch18-check-sources]
- **Timothy J. Henderson, *A Glorious Defeat: Mexico and Its War with the United States* (2007).** The war from the Mexican side; discovered but not yet consulted in full.[^ch18-check-sources]

> *A closing note.* The United States fought Mexico to settle a boundary it had drawn across land Mexico claimed and then took half of Mexico’s territory in a peace negotiated by a diplomat its own president had recalled. The treaty promised the conquered freedom of choice, citizenship, and the protection of their property; the Senate struck the strongest guarantee, and the promise was broken in practice. The chapter’s question is what a republic founded on consent does when it wins a war of conquest, and the answer its own history gives is that military victory did not settle its disputes — it magnified them, and the poison Emerson named worked through the politics of the next decade.[^ch18-avalon-guadalupe][^ch18-homestead-article10][^ch18-digitalhistory-significance][^ch18-grant-memoirs]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins in Syracuse, New York, on October 1, 1851, where a crowd rescued the fugitive Jerry from federal custody under the Fugitive Slave Act. That act was the price of the Compromise of 1850, which this chapter has shown was the settlement of a conquest. Chapter 19 takes up the fugitive-slave crisis, the collapse of compromise, and the coming of the Civil War.[^ch18-check-ch19][^ch18-archives-compromise1850]
