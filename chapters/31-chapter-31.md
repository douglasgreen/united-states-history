## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The setting and the date.** On July 20, 1917, the United States held the first drawing of its new conscription lottery. The drawing was held under the Selective Service Act, which President Woodrow Wilson had signed on May 18, 1917.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The machinery.** Registration used local draft boards, which registered men, assigned serial numbers, classified them, and called them forward. Serial numbers were printed on slips of paper, sealed in gelatin capsules, placed in a large glass bowl, and mixed with a ladle before drawing.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The act of the scene.** Secretary of War Newton D. Baker, who administered the act, drew the first capsule at 9:30 a.m. It held the number 258. The drawing continued into the early hours of the following morning, and 10,500 numbers were drawn.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The larger context of the day.** The United States had entered the war in April and had received only about 73,000 volunteers when it expected roughly a million, which is why conscription became necessary. The first registration had produced more than 10 million names within several months.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft][^ch31-selective-service]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the officials, the method, the first number, and the number of capsules. It cannot show what the room looked like, what Baker or the spectators felt, or what any registrant thought when the numbers were posted, because no such account is cited here.[^ch31-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In July 1917 no one knew how many men would be called, that the war would end in November 1918, or that the draft would mark a lasting change in the federal government’s reach. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch31-check-scene][^ch31-check-dates]

### The scene’s argument

- **Conscription as the hinge.** The scene’s claim is that the draft was the clearest instrument of the chapter’s subject: a democratic state claiming the power to compel its own citizens into military service and, in doing so, reaching into every locality through a national network of boards. The lottery’s appeal to chance was meant to make compulsion look like fairness.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The stakes.** The scene sets up the chapter’s question. The same machinery that registered and selected soldiers produced the censorship, the sedition prosecutions, the racial violence, and the labor conflict of the following three years. The draft was the war state’s entering wedge.[^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-check-scope]
- **The question.** The scene ends with the chapter’s guiding question: what did total mobilization do to a democracy?
- **The widening.** The framing paragraph follows and carries the reader from the lottery bowl to the training camps, the trenches of the Meuse-Argonne, the Chicago South Side, the Seattle shipyards, the Senate chamber, and the deportation trains of 1919–20.

### What must not be invented

- **No invented weather, room, or dialogue.** The date, the officials, the first number, and the number of capsules are documented. The scene may not stage the crowd, the room, the sounds, or any exchange the record does not contain.[^ch31-check-scene]
- **No interior thought.** The private reflections of Baker, Wilson, or the registrants must not be staged beyond what documents state.[^ch31-check-scene]
- **No borrowed hindsight.** The scene must not give its subjects knowledge of the armistice, the Sedition Act, the Red Scare, or the phrase “war state.”[^ch31-check-scene][^ch31-check-dates]

## 1. Mobilization and the draft

### 1.1 The claim: conscription as the hinge of the war state

- **The claim.** The federal government’s decision to raise the army by conscription rather than by volunteers transformed the war from a foreign-policy decision into a domestic regime, because it required the state to reach into every locality, register its citizens, classify them, compel them, and punish those who refused.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The pattern.** The war state rested on three instruments: the draft, which conscripted bodies; the economic agencies, which directed production, food, fuel, and railroads; and the information and enforcement apparatus, which managed opinion and punished dissent. The chapter should present the three as one system rather than three separate stories.[^ch31-check-cpi][^ch31-check-scope]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the registrations, the agencies, and the prosecutions. It cannot show that the public consented to them or that they were administered fairly, and the chapter should treat consent as contested rather than assumed.[^ch31-check-scene][^ch31-check-silences]

### 1.2 The Selective Service Act and the machinery of the draft

- **The claim.** The Selective Service Act of May 18, 1917, built a national conscription system through local civilian boards, and its designers sold it as fair because it forbade the substitutions and purchased exemptions that had discredited the Civil War draft.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The act.** Wilson signed the Selective Service Act into law on May 18, 1917. It compelled all men between the ages of 21 and 30 to register and later was amended to include men between 18 and 45. It ended the old bounty system and the practice of paying one’s way out of service, and it authorized random drawings to decide the order of induction.[^ch31-selective-service]
- **The machinery.** Local boards handled registration, assigned order and serial numbers, classified men, and called and entrained the draftees. More than 10 million men were registered within several months of the law’s passage, and by the war’s end about 24 million had registered.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The numbers who served.** Of the roughly 4.7 million Americans who served, more than half were conscripted. The chapter should give the totals with their definitions, because the counts vary by whether they cover the Army alone or include the Navy and Marine Corps.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-byerly-losses]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statute, the registrations, and the inductions. It cannot show that the lottery was genuinely random or that the boards treated rich and poor alike, and the chapter should mark those as claims the record illuminates only partly.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft][^ch31-check-scene]

### 1.3 Economic mobilization and the war state

- **The claim.** Alongside the draft, the war produced a set of federal agencies that directed the economy, and their powers, not their temporary names, are the chapter’s subject.[^ch31-check-cpi][^ch31-check-numbers]
- **The agencies.** The chapter should name the principal wartime bodies and what each controlled: the War Industries Board over production and priorities, the Food Administration over food, the Fuel Administration over coal and oil, the Railroad Administration over the rails, and the National War Labor Board over disputes, among others. Each name should carry an exact source locator before it is asserted.[^ch31-check-numbers][^ch31-check-cpi]
- **The interpretation.** Historians disagree about whether these agencies were an improvisation that dissolved after the armistice or the seedbed of an enduring national administrative capacity; the chapter should state the disagreement and not settle it by fiat.[^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-kennedy-over-here][^ch31-check-debate-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the executive orders, the boards, and the production figures where they exist. It cannot show that the agencies succeeded on their own terms, and the chapter should distinguish official intentions from popular action.[^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The limits of consent: exemptions, slackers, and enforcement

- **The claim.** Conscription was contested from the first drawing, which shows that the draft’s fairness was a claim to be tested, not a fact.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The evidence.** Following the first drawing, some 50,000 men applied for exemptions, and many of those called simply did not report; in one 1918 instance New York City arrested 16,000 men who had failed to report after being selected. The chapter should present these as evidence of resistance, not as proof that the draft failed.[^ch31-selective-service]
- **The language.** The period’s term “slacker” carried a moral charge, and the chapter should use it only in quotation and explain in the same paragraph why it is reproduced.[^ch31-check-names]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the exemption applications and the arrests. It cannot measure how many evaded selection without being caught, and the chapter should let the count stand as a floor rather than a total.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-check-numbers]

## 2. The AEF

### 2.1 Raising and shipping an army

- **The claim.** The American Expeditionary Forces were built from almost nothing into an army of millions in little more than a year, and the speed of that build shaped everything about how they fought.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]
- **The scale.** The combined Army, Navy, and Marine forces numbered about 4.7 million, of whom the Army accounted for about 4.1 million and the Navy about 600,000. The AEF spent only about 200 days in combat, from April 25, 1918, to the armistice.[^ch31-byerly-losses]
- **The overseas force.** More than 2 million American troops reached Europe. The first American combat deaths came in November 1917, and the force grew slowly through the winter before fighting in large formations in 1918.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-byerly-losses]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the totals, the shipping, and the combat days. It cannot show the experience of the ordinary soldier from a table, and the chapter should turn to memoirs and unit records for that.[^ch31-check-silences][^ch31-keene-soldier]

### 2.2 Training, command, and the French alliance

- **The claim.** The AEF fought as an independent American army under General John J. Pershing, but it depended on French and British shipping, artillery, and training, which made the alliance a source of both strength and friction.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-check-dates]
- **The doctrine.** Pershing insisted on an open-warfare doctrine and on keeping the AEF as a separate force, even as the Allies pressed to use American troops as replacements in their own depleted units. The chapter should present the dispute over amalgamation as a command decision with consequences on the battlefield.[^ch31-check-debate-works]
- **The geography.** The AEF fought in distinct sectors — Cantigny, Belleau Wood, Château-Thierry, Saint-Mihiel, and the Meuse-Argonne — and Map 24 should locate them rather than leave the campaign as a list of names.[^ch31-check-map][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]

### 2.3 The 369th and the segregated army

- **The claim.** Black soldiers served in a segregated army that assigned most of them to labor, and the most celebrated Black combat regiment, the 369th Infantry, fought under French command because the American army would not use it as the French would.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-abmc-hellfighters]
- **The unit.** The 369th, formerly the 15th New York National Guard Regiment and known as the “Harlem Hellfighters,” landed at Brest, France, in December 1917 and was assigned to the French army in 1918. It spent 191 days in the front-line trenches, more than any other American unit of its size, and suffered more than 1,400 total casualties, more than any other American regiment.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-abmc-hellfighters]
- **The wider service.** More than 350,000 African Americans served in the war, the majority in labor and stevedore battalions; only about one in 10 served in a combat role. Roughly 200,000 served in Europe, about 42,000 of them in combat.[^ch31-abmc-hellfighters][^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters]
- **The contradiction.** The chapter’s central question reaches its sharpest form here: the army that fought to make the world safe for democracy segregated its own soldiers and denied most of them the chance to fight. The chapter should name the contradiction rather than smooth it.[^ch31-check-race][^ch31-lentz-smith][^ch31-williams-torchbearers]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the unit’s service, its casualties, and the decorations it received, including the Croix de Guerre awarded to more than 170 of its members. It cannot show that the recognition redeemed the army’s discrimination, and the chapter should keep those separate.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-check-race]

### 2.4 Map 24 and Table 1: the AEF in France and its cost

- **The claim.** Set side by side, the operational geography and the casualty accounting show that the AEF’s brief campaign was also the costliest in American history to that point, and that most of its deaths came in its final weeks.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]
- **The Meuse-Argonne.** The Meuse-Argonne Offensive ran from September 26 to November 11, 1918. It was the largest operation of the AEF, with over a million American soldiers participating, and the deadliest campaign in American history to that date, with over 26,000 killed in action and over 120,000 total casualties. The number of graves at the American cemetery at Romagne exceeds those at Omaha Beach in Normandy.[^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]
- **The deaths.** Total American deaths from all causes came to 116,516, of which 53,402 were battle deaths and 63,114 were non-combat deaths, largely from the influenza pandemic; two-thirds of all American deaths occurred in the last three months of the war.[^ch31-byerly-losses]
- **The table.** The registered table `ch31-table-01` (Ch. 31, Table 1) belongs here. It gives the personnel and casualty series, the observation period, the service definitions, and the source for each figure.[^ch31-check-table1]
- **The map.** The registered visual `ch31-map-01` (Map 24, “The AEF in France, 1918”) belongs in the same section. It must locate the AEF’s major engagements and their dates, distinguish American sectors from Allied ones, and state its date and sources, rather than shade the whole Western Front a single color.[^ch31-check-map]
- **The comparison.** The chapter should draw the comparison the table and map enable: a nation that entered late and fought briefly still paid, in its final weeks, a price near the worst of any campaign, which is why 1918 is the hinge of the American century’s military experience.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]

## 3. Propaganda, dissent, and sedition

### 3.1 The Committee on Public Information

- **The claim.** The government did not merely censor dissent; it produced a coordinated propaganda campaign to create consent, which makes the wartime management of opinion the third instrument of the war state after the draft and the economic agencies.[^ch31-ww1c-cpi][^ch31-check-cpi]
- **The agency.** President Wilson created the Committee on Public Information in 1917 and placed the journalist George Creel at its head. The committee worked through news, films, posters, pamphlets, and speakers to promote the war and demonize the German enemy.[^ch31-ww1c-cpi][^ch31-check-cpi]
- **The speakers.** Its best-known program enrolled volunteer speakers, the “Four Minute Men,” to give short patriotic addresses in theaters and public gatherings. The exact number of speakers and the committee’s other divisions should be sourced to the National Archives records and the Library of Congress exhibition before they are stated.[^ch31-check-cpi][^ch31-loc-cpi][^ch31-nara-cpi-records]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the committee’s creation, its chief, and its methods. It cannot measure how much of the public was persuaded, and the chapter should present the campaign as an effort to manufacture consent, not proof that it succeeded.[^ch31-check-cpi][^ch31-check-silences]

### 3.2 The Espionage Act of 1917 and its enforcement

- **The claim.** The Espionage Act of June 15, 1917, converted criticism of the war into a federal crime, and its enforcement made the war the first great test of the First Amendment’s limits.[^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]
- **The statute.** The act punished causing insubordination, disloyalty, mutiny, or refusal of duty in the military, and obstructing recruiting or enlistment, among other offenses. The Sedition Act of 1918 amended its Section 3 and added the censorship of the mails.[^ch31-sedition-act]
- **The enforcement.** The Department of Justice used the act broadly against supposed enemies of the war, and the chapter should present the prosecutions as a system of enforcement rather than a list of isolated cases.[^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

### 3.3 The Sedition Act of 1918 and the In Focus

**In Focus.** The In Focus box reads Section 3 of the Sedition Act of 1918 as a statute, not as an unidentified indictment. Passed by Congress in May 1918 and signed by President Wilson, the act amended Section 3 of the Espionage Act to punish anyone who, “when the United States is at war, shall willfully utter, print, write or publish any disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language about the form of government of the United States … or shall willfully … urge, incite, or advocate any curtailment of production,” with a fine of up to $10,000 or imprisonment for up to 20 years. Read closely, the section shows how far the state went: it criminalized not only acts that interfered with the war but language that expressed contempt for the government, its Constitution, its flag, or its uniform. The box should quote the section at its exact locator in the *Statutes at Large* and collate it against the printed volume. It should treat the text as a legal instrument whose reach can be compared with the Espionage Act’s recruitment provisions and with the state sedition laws of 1918, not as a stage for reconstructed action. The box should end on the question the chapter asks of every wartime power: how much speech can a democracy punish before it has punished democracy?[^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-govinfo-sedition][^ch31-check-focus]

### 3.4 The courts: Schenck, Debs, and Abrams

- **The claim.** The Supreme Court upheld the Espionage Act and supplied the test that governed free speech until the mid-twentieth century, which shows that the wartime narrowing of liberty came with judicial sanction, not only executive action.[^ch31-justia-schenck][^ch31-justia-debs]
- **Schenck.** In *Schenck v. United States* (1919), the Court sustained a conviction for circulating leaflets that urged resistance to the draft and announced that the question was whether the words created a “clear and present danger” of bringing about the substantive evils Congress had a right to prevent.[^ch31-justia-schenck]
- **Debs.** Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist Party leader, was indicted on June 29, 1918, for a speech at Canton, Ohio, on June 16, 1918, that the indictment charged with making false statements “to promote the success of the enemy of the United States.” He was convicted on September 12, 1918, and sentenced to 10 years; the Supreme Court affirmed on April 12, 1919, and President Warren G. Harding commuted the sentence in December 1921.[^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-justia-debs]
- **Abrams.** The chapter should name *Abrams v. United States* (1919) and Oliver Wendell Holmes’s dissent, which began to argue for a marketplace-of-ideas standard, as the counterpoint within the Court, and supply the case citation before quoting.[^ch31-check-free-speech]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the statutes, the convictions, and the opinions. It cannot show that the prosecutions deterred dissent effectively, and the chapter should distinguish the law’s reach from its measured effect.[^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Vigilantism and the boundaries of loyalty

- **The claim.** Coercion was not only official; private citizens policed loyalty through boycotts, denunciations, and violence, which means the wartime culture of conformity was made as much from below as from above.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-cpi]
- **The pattern.** The chapter should present the American Protective League’s citizen surveillance, the renaming of sauerkraut and the banning of German-language instruction, the vigilantism against suspected radicals, and the attacks on pacifists and immigrants as instances of a wider phenomenon, not curiosities.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-race]
- **The interpretation.** Historians have argued that this popular enforcement made loyalty a matter of civic obligation that long outlasted the war; the chapter should state the argument and test it rather than assume it.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-debate-works]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the organizations and documented attacks. It cannot show how many people complied out of conviction and how many out of fear, and the chapter should mark that boundary.[^ch31-check-silences]

## 4. Migration, Red Summer, influenza, labor, and suffrage

### 4.1 The Great Migration and the wartime labor market

- **The claim.** The war’s demand for labor, combined with the collapse of European immigration, drew hundreds of thousands of Black southerners north, and their arrival remade the cities they entered and provoked the violence of 1919.[^ch31-nara-red-summer][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer]
- **The movement.** The Great Migration, roughly from the 1910s, eventually moved about six million Black people from the South to the North, Midwest, and West; by the summer of 1919 alone, approximately 500,000 African Americans had resettled in northern cities. During World War I the departure of white workers into the army and the decline of European immigration opened industrial jobs to Black migrants.[^ch31-nara-red-summer][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer]
- **The journalistic record.** The chapter should use contemporaneous reporting, including Carl Sandburg’s 1919 articles in the *Chicago Daily News*, to show how the migration was described as it happened, while noting that Sandburg was the only White reporter to examine the social causes.[^ch31-nps-chicago-1919]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the census totals, the movement’s destinations, and the hostility the migrants met. It cannot show the migrants’ own reasons through statistics alone, and the chapter should turn to letters, oral histories, and the Black press for that.[^ch31-check-silences]

### 4.2 Red Summer and racial violence

- **The claim.** In 1919 the racial violence that the war had sharpened erupted across the country, and the worst violence combined white mobs, police failures, and the determined self-defense of Black communities.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nara-red-summer]
- **The pattern.** The “Red Summer” of 1919 affected at least 26 cities. Race riots and lynchings killed hundreds and injured thousands; lynchings rose from 64 in 1918 to 83 in 1919; and officials often hesitated or looked away, while ordinary white civilians and veterans, not only organized terror groups, formed most of the mobs.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer]
- **Washington and Chicago.** A four-day riot in Washington, D.C., began on July 19, 1919, after a rumor that Black men had assaulted a white woman; Black residents armed themselves and fought back, and Wilson ordered troops into the streets. In Chicago, rioting broke out on July 27 after the Black teenager Eugene Williams drowned when struck by a stone while on a raft near a de facto white beach; the rioting lasted until August 3, killed 38 people (23 Black and 15 White), injured 537, and left up to 1,000 Black families homeless.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nps-chicago-1919]
- **Elaine, Arkansas.** The likely deadliest single incident came at Elaine, Arkansas, on September 30 and October 1, 1919, after a shootout outside a Black sharecroppers’ meeting; the governor ordered troops to suppress what was labeled an “insurrection,” and estimates of the Black dead run to 200 or higher, a range the chapter must present as an estimate rather than a settled count.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nara-red-summer]
- **The Black response.** W. E. B. Du Bois, in *The Crisis*, called on Black veterans to “return fighting,” and the violence of 1919 led to a broader commitment to fight back in the streets, the courts, and the voting booth, which the chapter should present as an outcome of the year rather than only as victimization.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-check-race]

### 4.3 The influenza pandemic

- **The claim.** The influenza pandemic killed more Americans than the war itself and demonstrated how total mobilization spread the virus across the globe, which makes the pandemic part of the war’s history rather than an unrelated natural disaster.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-census-influenza]
- **The scale.** The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that the 1918 pandemic infected about one-third of the world’s population and killed at least 50 million people worldwide, with about 675,000 deaths in the United States. The U.S. Census Bureau reports that October 1918 was the deadliest month, with an estimated 195,000 American deaths, and that 1918 saw 477,467 deaths from influenza and pneumonia.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-census-influenza]
- **The military incidence.** Influenza sickened about 26 percent of the army — more than one million men — and the pandemic compromised the AEF’s performance in the Meuse-Argonne, which opened as the second wave struck. At Camp Devens, more than 14,000 soldiers were sick in September 1918 and 757 died.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-census-influenza]
- **The unequal toll.** The pandemic fell hardest on the poor and on Native communities; the Census Bureau reports that Alaska Native and American Indian populations suffered extreme losses, including 72 of 80 residents of Brevig Mission and estimated mortality among the Navajo of about 12 percent.[^ch31-census-influenza][^ch31-check-race]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the mortality statistics and the spread through military camps. It cannot give an exact global or national total, and the chapter should present the figures as ranges and estimates, naming each source.[^ch31-check-numbers]

### 4.4 Labor conflict: Seattle and after

- **The claim.** The armistice did not bring peace to the workplace; 1919 was one of the most turbulent years in American labor history, and the Seattle general strike showed both the movement’s power and the limits of solidarity.[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike]
- **Seattle.** On the morning of February 6, 1919, Seattle, a city of about 315,000 people, stopped working. 35,000 shipyard workers had already struck, and 25,000 other union members joined them; 101 AFL unions had voted to walk out in solidarity, and most of the remaining workforce stayed home as stores closed and streetcars halted. An elected Strike Committee coordinated essential services, the teamsters kept hospitals and food deliveries running, and an unarmed labor guard patrolled the streets. The mayor threatened martial law and two battalions of federal troops took up positions, but the city remained calm. The Central Labor Council declared the strike over on February 11, and afterward police and vigilantes rounded up radicals and federal agents closed the labor-owned *Seattle Union Record*. Mayor Ole Hanson declared that “Americanism” had triumphed over “Bolshevism.”[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike]
- **The wider wave.** The chapter should name the other 1919 conflicts, including the Boston police strike of September 1919, and place them in the pattern the strike database describes, rather than treating Seattle as isolated.[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike][^ch31-check-dates]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the strike’s dates, participants, and the peaceful conduct the organizers claimed. It cannot show that the strike advanced the workers’ cause, and the chapter should present its outcome as contested.[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike][^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 4.5 The Nineteenth Amendment completed

- **The claim.** The Nineteenth Amendment completed the enfranchisement of women, but ratification in 1920 crowned a campaign built partly on the exclusion of Black women and left many women unable to vote, so the amendment’s meaning is contested.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]
- **The action.** The House passed the amendment on May 21, 1919, by 304 to 89, and the Senate followed on June 4, 1919. Suffrage lobbyists tied the defense of democracy abroad to the extension of democracy at home, and Representative James R. Mann argued that “the time is ripe, the people are ready and the beneficiaries of this amendment are eager, willing and able to perform the duties of citizenship.”[^ch31-house-19th-amendment]
- **The ratification.** Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify on August 18, 1920, completing the three-fourths requirement; the decisive vote in the Tennessee House was cast by the 24-year-old representative Harry Burn, who acted after receiving a letter from his mother. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified the ratification on August 26, 1920.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-nara-prologue-19th]
- **The limits.** Ratification did not enfranchise all women, because state voting laws and discrimination continued to keep many, and especially Black women, from the polls. The historian Lisa Tetrault argues that the amendment’s history has been simplified and that its constitutional meaning remained unsettled.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-check-race]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the votes, the states, and the certification. It cannot show that ratification produced an equal electorate, and the chapter should keep the amendment’s achievement and its limits in the same paragraph.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-check-suffrage]

## 5. Versailles, the Senate, and the Red Scare

### 5.1 The Fourteen Points and the peace

- **The claim.** Wilson went to Paris with a program that joined a general association of nations to a vision of self-determination, and the peace that emerged disappointed much of that program, which is why the treaty became a domestic political fight.[^ch31-avalon-fourteen-points][^ch31-check-dates]
- **The program.** In his address of January 8, 1918, Wilson set out fourteen points, including open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, reduced armaments, an impartial adjustment of colonial claims, and, in the fourteenth, “a general association of nations … for the purpose of affording mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike.”[^ch31-avalon-fourteen-points]
- **The gap.** The chapter should present the distance between the program and the treaty — the punitive terms against Germany, the colonial settlements, and the exclusion of the interests of many peoples — as a source of both foreign and domestic opposition, and should not let the program stand in for the treaty.[^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 5.2 The Senate fight and the reservations

- **The claim.** The Treaty of Versailles failed in the Senate because Wilson would not accept the reservations that a majority would have required and because the opposition, led by Henry Cabot Lodge, would not ratify the League as Wilson insisted.[^ch31-state-versailles][^ch31-capitol-senate-votes]
- **The antagonists.** Wilson and Lodge had disliked each other for years and each considered himself the country’s preeminent scholar in politics. The 1918 midterm elections gave the Republicans control of the Senate, making Lodge both majority leader and Foreign Relations Committee chairman. Wilson offended the Senate by refusing to include senators among the negotiators and by making the conference results public before discussing them with the committee, and he denounced Lodge and his allies as “contemptible, narrow, selfish, poor little minds that never get anywhere but run around in a circle and think they are going somewhere.”[^ch31-state-versailles]
- **The collapse.** Lodge’s committee added reservations, and in November 1919 Lodge sent the treaty to the floor with fourteen reservations but no amendments. During a cross-country speaking tour in October 1919, Wilson suffered a physical collapse that removed him from the fight.[^ch31-state-versailles]

### 5.3 Table 2: the Senate votes

- **The claim.** The Senate’s roll calls show that the treaty failed not once but three times, and that the decisive obstacle was the two-thirds threshold, not a simple majority.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace]
- **The votes.** On November 19, 1919, the Senate defeated the treaty with Lodge’s fourteen reservations, 39 to 55. It also rejected the original treaty without amendments, 38 to 53. On March 19, 1920, after a bipartisan effort to pass the treaty with reservations, the Senate again rejected it, 49 to 35, which was a majority but short of the two-thirds the Constitution requires.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace]

### 5.4 The Red Scare and the Palmer Raids

- **The claim.** The fear of radicalism that the war had fed continued after the armistice, and the federal government’s raids of 1919–20 made the suppression of dissent a permanent feature of the postwar order.[^ch31-fbi-palmer][^ch31-loc-palmer]
- **The bombings.** On June 2, 1919, a militant anarchist blew up the front of Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer’s home in Washington, and that day brought coordinated attacks in eight cities; the previous month, mailed bombs had reached officials and business leaders, and a postal worker intercepted more. Palmer created a small intelligence division and placed the young J. Edgar Hoover in charge of it.[^ch31-fbi-palmer]
- **The raids.** The Department of Justice began arresting suspected radicals under laws including the Sedition Act, detaining well-known figures such as Emma Goldman and Alexander Berkman. On November 7, 1919, the first series of raids brought about 200 arrests; on January 2, 1920, a nationwide roundup arrested about 3,000 people. Palmer warned of a revolutionary plot for May 1, 1920, and when it failed to materialize he was discredited; the Senate Judiciary Committee investigated the raids’ legality from January to March 1921.[^ch31-loc-palmer][^ch31-fbi-palmer]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the bombings, the raids, the arrests, and the deportations. It cannot establish that those arrested were guilty of the revolutionary conspiracy Palmer described, and the chapter should present the raids as the state’s response to fear rather than to demonstrated danger.[^ch31-fbi-palmer][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

### 5.5 Demobilization and the election of 1920

- **The claim.** The war’s institutions were dismantled faster than they had been built, but the informal capacities, habits of surveillance, and expectations of federal action outlasted the armistice, which is the chapter’s bridge to the 1920s.[^ch31-check-ch32][^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state]
- **The demobilization.** The army shrank rapidly after the armistice and the wartime agencies were wound down, even as veterans’ claims on the federal government grew through the rehabilitation and benefits programs.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-check-ch32]
- **The election of 1920.** The chapter should note the 1920 election as the political expression of the postwar mood, and should leave the election’s full treatment to Chapter 32, which opens at its broadcast.[^ch31-check-ch32][^ch31-pabook-kdka]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **Conscription was the hinge.** The draft reached into every locality and compelled service, marking the war as a domestic regime and not only a foreign undertaking.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- **The war narrowed dissent substantially.** The Espionage and Sedition Acts, their enforcement, and the courts’ approval produced the most severe federal restrictions on speech since the founding, and the Debs case shows a leading dissenter imprisoned for a speech.[^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-justia-schenck]
- **The pandemic dwarfed the battlefield.** Influenza killed far more Americans than combat, and the war’s mobilization spread it.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-byerly-losses]
- **The racial contradiction was central.** A segregated army fought a war for democracy while denying Black soldiers equal standing, and 1919 brought racial violence in at least 26 cities.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer]
- **The treaty failed on the two-thirds threshold.** The Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles three times, and the decisive barrier was the constitutional supermajority.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace]
- **The Nineteenth Amendment was partial.** Ratification in 1920 enfranchised many women but did not produce an equal electorate.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Did the war build an enduring state or a temporary one?** One position holds that the wartime agencies and the war-welfare regime created capacities that lasted; another holds that the mobilization was improvised and largely dismantled, leaving habits rather than institutions. The chapter should state each at its strongest.[^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-kennedy-over-here]
- **Was the coercion official, popular, or both?** One account stresses federal law and prosecution; another stresses the grassroots enforcement of loyalty by citizens. The chapter should set the evidence for both side by side.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]
- **What did the war do to the color line?** One position holds that the war’s demands and Black service created new possibilities of assertion and organization; another holds that the war entrenched the color line and that its gains were largely rhetorical. The chapter should mark the question as open.[^ch31-lentz-smith][^ch31-williams-torchbearers]
- **Did the Nineteenth Amendment democratize or preserve exclusion?** One account treats 1920 as the culmination of a long struggle; another stresses the movement’s exclusions and the amendment’s limited effect. The chapter should present both and not reduce the amendment to either.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-tetrault-myth]
- **Why did the treaty fail?** One account stresses Wilson’s rigidity and his refusal to compromise; another stresses Lodge’s partisanship and the Republicans’ control of the Senate. The chapter should present both mechanisms.[^ch31-state-versailles][^ch31-check-debate-works]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which total mobilization simultaneously widened and narrowed American citizenship: it drew new people into the national state and its political life, and it punished dissent, segregated its soldiers, and left the peace unfinished. The chapter should present the positions as overlapping rather than as teams.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-check-debate-works]
- **Temporary institutions and enduring capacities.** The chapter’s synthesis should distinguish the wartime agencies, which were largely dissolved, from the capacities and expectations they left behind, which shaped the next two decades. That distinction is the chapter’s contribution to the book’s argument about how settlements change the terms of conflict without settling them.[^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state][^ch31-kennedy-over-here]
- **Official intentions and popular action.** The chapter’s synthesis should keep the state’s intentions — to mobilize, to persuade, to punish — distinct from the popular enforcement of loyalty and the popular resistance to it, because conflating the two would let the government speak for a public that acted on its own.[^ch31-capozzola-unc-sam][^ch31-check-silences]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** The chapter should say that the war’s deepest democratic question was not whether the nation could win but whether it could compel, censor, and conscript its own people and still be the republic it claimed to be, and that the answer the record supports is: partly, and at a cost that the next decade would reckon.[^ch31-tetrault-suffrage][^ch31-nara-debs][^ch31-check-table3]

## Legacies

The wartime state outlasted its own institutions. The draft ended and the wartime agencies were dismantled, but the Selective Service System, the administrative habit of federal direction, and the expectation that Washington would act on wages, prices, and welfare did not disappear, and they shaped the New Era and the New Deal alike.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-schaffer-welfare-state]

The wartime narrowing of liberty outlasted the armistice. The Espionage and Sedition Acts were repealed in 1921, but the prosecutions and the Red Scare left a pattern of surveillance and deportation that recurred across the century, and the civil-liberties arguments they provoked became the foundation of a lasting movement.[^ch31-fbi-palmer][^ch31-murphy-civil-liberties]

The war’s racial contradiction outlasted the peace. Black veterans returned to a country that lynched more people in 1919 than in 1918, and the Double V campaign of the next war would inherit the unfinished fight that the first war had left.[^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-lentz-smith]

The Nineteenth Amendment also left a double legacy: the enfranchisement of millions of women, and the persistence of exclusions that would take the Voting Rights Act of 1965 to address in law. The chapter should present both as consequences the record supports.[^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-tetrault-suffrage]

## Optional threads

- **The transnational frame.** The draft, the AEF, and the influenza pandemic were all transatlantic; the peace conference redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East and the influenza virus traveled with the armies. - **Archives and silences.** The official record — statutes, boards, casualty lists, case files, and raid reports — over-represents the state and its enforcers and under-represents the conscripts, evaders, dissidents, migrants, strikers, women, and Black Americans whose lives the war reordered. ## Summary

- The Selective Service Act of May 18, 1917, built a national conscription system through local boards and forbade the substitutions and purchased exemptions that had discredited the Civil War draft.[^ch31-selective-service]
- On July 20, 1917, Secretary of War Newton D. Baker drew the first capsule, number 258, and 10,500 numbers were drawn.[^ch31-smithsonian-draft]
- About 24 million men registered for the draft, more than half of the roughly 4.7 million Americans who served were conscripted, and conscription met resistance from exemption claims and failures to report.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-byerly-losses]
- The American Expeditionary Forces fought about 200 days in combat from April 25, 1918, and the Meuse-Argonne Offensive of September 26–November 11, 1918, was the largest and deadliest AEF operation, with over a million participants, over 26,000 killed, and over 120,000 total casualties.[^ch31-byerly-losses][^ch31-nara-meuse-argonne]
- Total American deaths came to 116,516, of which 63,114 were non-combat deaths, largely from influenza.[^ch31-byerly-losses]
- The all-Black 369th Infantry spent 191 days in the front-line trenches and suffered over 1,400 casualties, while more than 350,000 African Americans served and most were assigned to labor.[^ch31-nmaahc-hellfighters][^ch31-abmc-hellfighters]
- The Committee on Public Information ran a coordinated propaganda campaign, and the Espionage and Sedition Acts made criticism of the war a federal crime.[^ch31-ww1c-cpi][^ch31-sedition-act]
- The Supreme Court sustained the Espionage Act in *Schenck v. United States* and upheld the conviction of Eugene V. Debs for his Canton speech.[^ch31-justia-schenck][^ch31-nara-debs]
- The war drew some 500,000 Black southerners into northern cities by the summer of 1919, and the Red Summer of 1919 brought racial violence to at least 26 cities, killing 38 in Chicago’s riot and an estimated 200 or more at Elaine, Arkansas.[^ch31-nara-red-summer][^ch31-ww1c-red-summer][^ch31-nps-chicago-1919]
- The influenza pandemic killed about 675,000 Americans, with an estimated 195,000 deaths in October 1918 alone, and it compromised the AEF in the Meuse-Argonne.[^ch31-cdc-influenza][^ch31-census-influenza]
- The Seattle general strike of February 6–11, 1919, involved more than 60,000 workers and remained peaceful despite federal troops and martial-law threats.[^ch31-uw-seattle-strike]
- The Nineteenth Amendment passed the House on May 21, 1919, and the Senate on June 4, 1919, and was ratified on August 18, 1920, when Tennessee became the 36th state.[^ch31-house-19th-amendment][^ch31-nara-19th-amendment]
- The Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles three times — 39–55 and 38–53 on November 19, 1919, and 49–35 on March 19, 1920 — each time short of two-thirds.[^ch31-capitol-senate-votes][^ch31-state-gov-peace]
- The Palmer Raids of November 1919 and January 1920 arrested about 200 and then about 3,000 suspected radicals, and Palmer’s predicted May Day revolution failed to materialize.[^ch31-loc-palmer][^ch31-fbi-palmer]

> *A closing note.* Between 1917 and 1920 the United States conscripted its own citizens, directed its economy, censored its press, imprisoned its dissidents, segregated its soldiers, wheeled on its strikers, buried some 675,000 of its people in a pandemic that traveled with its armies, enfranchised its women in part, and then refused the peace its president had brought home. The chapter’s question — what did total mobilization do to a democracy? — is answered by a record in which the same state that made the world safe for democracy also made dissent a crime and left the republic more powerful, more policed, and more unfinished than it had been.[^ch31-selective-service][^ch31-sedition-act][^ch31-nara-19th-amendment][^ch31-capitol-senate-votes]

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter begins on the evening of November 2, 1920, when Leo Rosenberg, speaking for the Westinghouse station KDKA, broadcast the Harding–Cox election returns and became, in the usual telling, radio’s first announcer. The mobilized society the war had assembled would now be addressed as a mass audience, by a new medium, in a decade of prosperity, prohibition, and conflict. Chapter 32 takes up the 1920s, and asks who belonged to the new mass society.[^ch31-check-ch32][^ch31-check-bridge][^ch31-check-radio]
