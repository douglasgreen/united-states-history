## Epigraphs

> “Never before in our history have Americans been called upon to face three grave threats to our very existence, any one of which could destroy us. We face a disintegrating economy, a weakened defense and an energy policy based on the sharing of scarcity.”[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]

— Ronald Reagan, acceptance of the Republican presidential nomination, Detroit, July 17, 1980.[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]

> “We live in a new world. The Cold War has ended, the arms race has stopped, as has the insane militarization which mutilated our economy, public psyche and morals.”[^ch44-gorbachev-resignation]

— Mikhail Gorbachev, resignation address as president of the Soviet Union, December 25, 1991.[^ch44-gorbachev-resignation]

> “What one can now say is that Ronald Reagan saw Soviet weaknesses sooner than most of his contemporaries did.”[^ch44-hoover-gaddis]

— John Lewis Gaddis, on Reagan and the end of the Cold War, as quoted in a 2025 Hoover Institution interview.[^ch44-hoover-gaddis]

- **Why these three.** Reagan named the crisis as American weakness — an economy, a defense, and an energy policy that had failed — and promised to reverse it. Gorbachev named the outcome as the end of a costly confrontation, and did not concede that the United States had won it. Gaddis attributes the timing to Reagan’s perception of Soviet weakness and his combination of “reassurance, persuasion, and pressure.” The three disagree about the nature of the crisis, about whether the Cold War’s end was a victory or a mutual release, and about which actors deserve the credit.[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980][^ch44-gorbachev-resignation][^ch44-hoover-gaddis]
- **The attribution rule.** Reagan’s sentences are quoted from the Reagan Library transcript of the July 17, 1980, address and must be collated against the *Public Papers of the Presidents of the United States: Ronald Reagan, 1980*, Book II. Gorbachev’s sentences are quoted from the World History Commons text of the December 25, 1991, address, reproduced at the Marxists Internet Archive, and must be collated against the *New York Times* transcript of December 26, 1991, and the Soviet broadcast record. The Gaddis sentence is quoted from a video transcript and should be collated against his published work, *Strategies of Containment*, before publication; the transcript renders the following sentence with a garbled word and the epigraph rests on the clean first sentence alone.[^ch44-check-epigraphs][^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980][^ch44-gorbachev-resignation][^ch44-hoover-gaddis]
- **What the epigraphs do not do.** They do not prove that the 1980 election was a mandate for any single policy, that the Cold War ended because of one leader, or that the era’s costs were shared evenly. They stage the chapter’s question.[^ch44-check-epigraphs]

## Opening scene blueprint

### What the record establishes

- **The date and the occasion.** Reagan accepted the Republican nomination at the party’s national convention in Detroit on July 17, 1980, and opened by accepting the nomination “with a deep awareness of the responsibility conferred by your trust.”[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]
- **The speech’s own diagnosis.** Reagan said the nation faced “three grave threats,” and he named them as “a disintegrating economy, a weakened defense and an energy policy based on the sharing of scarcity.” He asserted that the Democratic leadership believed the nation had “passed its zenith” and that he “utterly reject[ed] that view.”[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]
- **The coalition’s language.** The speech repeated a formula for the coalition he sought: “a community of values embodied in these words: family, work, neighborhood, peace and freedom.” It promised to “unify our country” and to carry that message to “every American, regardless of party affiliation.”[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]
- **The economic program sketched in advance.** Reagan said he had “long advocated a 30 percent reduction in income tax rates over a period of three years,” beginning “with a 10 percent down payment tax cut in 1981,” and described the economy in the figure of an “indigestible economic stew, one part inflation, one part high unemployment, one part recession, one part runaway taxes, one part deficit spending and seasoned by an energy crisis.”[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]
- **The hostages named from the podium.** Reagan pointed to the Americans held in Iran: “more than 50 of our fellow Americans have been held captive for over eight months by a dictatorial foreign power.” Chapter 43 owns the seizure and the first year of the crisis; this chapter owns the release of January 1981 and the completed episode.[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980][^ch44-check-ch43]
- **The rhetoric of restoration.** Reagan closed on a phrase he would use again: he asked voters to join “a great national crusade to make America great again,” and quoted Franklin D. Roosevelt’s 1932 acceptance speech against government waste.[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980]
- **What the record can show.** It can show the date, the place, the text, the program Reagan sketched, and the coalition language. It cannot show what individual delegates or listeners felt, what the November result would be, or that the speech caused the outcome. The chapter should attribute each claim to its evidence.[^ch44-reagan-acceptance-1980][^ch44-check-scene]
- **What the scene cannot assume.** In July 1980 no one at the podium knew that the hostages would be released on January 20, 1981, that the economy would fall into a second recession, that the Soviet Union would dissolve, or that the era’s gains and costs would fall so unevenly. The scene must preserve that uncertainty.[^ch44-check-scene][^ch44-check-dates-appD]

## 1. Coalition and tax changes (1980–1986)

### 1.1 The claim: a new coalition and a tax program that outran its own forecasts

- **The claim.** The 1980 election assembled a coalition and produced a tax program, but the program’s results — lower rates, larger deficits, and a changed debate about government — did not match either the promises of its advocates or the fears of its critics, and the mismatch is the section’s subject.[^ch44-app-1980][^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-reagan-inaugural-1981]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1980 election and the January 1981 hostage release through the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, the Tax Reform Act of 1986, the PATCO strike, and the deficits that followed.[^ch44-app-1980][^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-taxreform-1986][^ch44-miller-patco]

### 1.2 The 1980 election, the transition, and the hostages’ release

- **The result.** Reagan won 489 electoral votes and about 50.7 percent of the popular vote to Jimmy Carter’s 49 electoral votes and about 41.0 percent; the independent John B. Anderson took about 6.6 percent. Reagan’s margin in the popular vote was about 8.4 million, and the Electoral College margin was far larger than the popular-vote margin. The chapter should give the shares with their rounding and note that a plurality, not a majority, is not the same as a mandate for every policy.[^ch44-app-1980][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The hostage release.** Chapter 43 leaves the crisis open; this chapter owns its end. The 52 American hostages held since November 4, 1979, were released on January 20, 1981, under the Algeria Accords, minutes after Reagan took the oath of office, completing a captivity of 444 days. The chapter should present the release as the end of an episode the captors’ terms and the inauguration’s timing both shaped, and should verify the count and the documentary terms against the State Department record.[^ch44-check-ch43][^ch44-state-iran-release-check]
- **The inaugural frame.** Reagan took office on January 20, 1981, and said in his inaugural address that “in this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem.” He named “[t]he economic ills we suffer,” the “longest and one of the worst sustained inflations in our national history,” and pledged that “these will be our first priorities, and on these principles there will be no compromise.” The inaugural belongs to the chapter’s narrative of §1, not to the July 1980 scene.[^ch44-reagan-inaugural-1981]
- **The caution.** The election returns measure votes cast, not voters’ reasons; the chapter should not read a mandate for any single policy off an aggregate result, and should note that the 1980 result also reflected the third-party vote and a depressed Democratic turnout.[^ch44-app-1980][^ch44-check-debate-works]

### 1.3 The tax program: ERTA and the 1986 act

- **The 1981 act.** The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, H.R.4242, was introduced on July 23, 1981, and became Public Law 97-34 on August 13, 1981. Its conference summary reduced “the highest marginal tax rate for all types of income from 70 to 50 percent, effective in 1982,” established a maximum long-term capital-gains rate of 20 percent for sales after June 9, 1981, and required annual cost-of-living adjustments to rates and brackets beginning in 1985.[^ch44-erta-1981]
- **The across-the-board cut.** Popular and scholarly accounts describe the act as cutting personal income-tax rates by 25 percent over roughly three years. The chapter should state the cut by year and bracket from the statute or the Treasury, and should distinguish the headline reduction from the bracket-indexation provision that was, in the long run, more consequential.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-taxreform-1986][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The 1986 act.** The Tax Reform Act of 1986, H.R.3838, reduced the top individual rate from 50 percent to 28 percent and collapsed more than a dozen brackets into two, at 15 and 28 percent; it also raised the standard deduction and broadened the base by removing many deductions and preferences. Politically it was bipartisan, and it is often treated as the completion of the 1981 project; the chapter should note the redistribution within the rate structure that its base-broadening accomplished.[^ch44-taxreform-1986]
- **The supply-side claim.** The program rested on the argument, associated with the “Laffer curve,” that rate cuts would so increase growth that revenues would rise. The chapter should present the claim at its strongest — that high marginal rates discouraged work, saving, and investment — before testing it against the deficits of the decade.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-check-debate-works]

### 1.4 The PATCO strike and the labor turn

- **The strike.** On August 3, 1981, Reagan said of the striking air traffic controllers: “They are in violation of the law, and if they do not report for work within 48 hours, they have forfeited their jobs and will be terminated.” About 13,000 members of the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization, one of the few unions that had endorsed Reagan in 1980, had walked off the job. On August 5, the administration fired 11,345 of them, and Reagan wrote in his diary that day: “How do they explain approving of law breaking — to say nothing of violation of an oath taken by each a.c. [air controller] that he or she would not strike.”[^ch44-miller-patco]
- **The workaround.** The Federal Aviation Administration reported that supervisors, non-striking controllers, and military controllers handled about 80 percent of the prior workload in short order, while the agency began hiring replacements and took years to restore pre-strike staffing.[^ch44-miller-patco]
- **The meaning.** The firing became a symbol of the administration’s stance toward organized labor drawn from the public sector, and administration veterans recalled it as a signal “way beyond domestic politics.” The chapter should present it as one documented episode in a longer labor history, not as the single cause of the decline of private-sector unionization, which had other drivers.[^ch44-miller-patco][^ch44-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The exact number of controllers who struck, the number fired, and the date of the decertification of PATCO must be reconciled across the Miller Center account, the FAA’s own history, and the Federal Labor Relations Authority record before assertion.[^ch44-miller-patco][^ch44-check-numbers]

### 1.5 The deficits and the Treasury’s arithmetic

- **The deficit turn.** The tax cuts were not matched by equivalent spending reductions; the defense buildup of §3 increased outlays, and the federal deficit grew substantially over the decade. The chapter should give the deficit and debt figures from the Office of Management and Budget or the Treasury, in current and inflation-adjusted terms, and should distinguish annual deficits from the accumulated debt.[^ch44-cbo-defense][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The internal critic.** David Stockman, Reagan’s first director of the Office of Management and Budget, later wrote a book, *The Triumph of Politics* (1986), describing his disillusionment with the supply-side forecasts and the difficulty of cutting the budget. The chapter should use the book as a participant’s account of the internal debate, not as neutral evidence of the program’s failure.[^ch44-stockman-triumph][^ch44-check-sources]
- **The redistribution question.** The cuts changed the distribution of federal taxes as well as their level; the chapter should connect the rate structure to the distributional evidence in §2 rather than assert effects the section does not establish.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-cbo-inequality]

## 2. Recession, deregulation, inequality, and finance (1981–1989)

### 2.1 The claim: a disinflation bought at a price, and a financial turn that outran its rules

- **The claim.** The Federal Reserve broke the inflation of the 1970s at the cost of the worst recession since the 1930s, and the same years saw a deregulation of finance, a widening distribution of income and wealth, and a boom-and-bust cycle whose 1987 crash and savings-and-loan failures exposed the limits of the new rules.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-frb-savings-loan][^ch44-frb-crash-1987]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1981–82 recession and the Volcker disinflation through the savings-and-loan crisis, the distributional data, and the financial boom and the crash of October 1987.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-frb-savings-loan][^ch44-cbo-inequality][^ch44-frb-crash-1987]

### 2.2 The 1981–82 recession and the Volcker disinflation

- **The recession.** The recession ran from July 1981 to November 1982 and was, before 2007–09, the worst downturn since the Great Depression. Unemployment rose from 7.4 percent at the start to nearly 10 percent a year later; the Bureau of Labor Statistics records 10.8 percent at the end of 1982, a post–World War II record at the time. Goods-producing industries, only about 30 percent of employment, accounted for about 90 percent of the job losses in 1982, and residential construction and auto manufacturing ended the year with 22 percent and 24 percent unemployment.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-bls-unemployment-1982]
- **The cause.** Both the 1980 and 1981–82 recessions followed tight monetary policy aimed at inflation. Paul A. Volcker, appointed Fed chairman in August 1979, shifted policy toward targeting the money supply rather than interest rates; the federal funds rate approached 20 percent in 1980–81, and Volcker insisted the Fed would not “back down” when unemployment rose.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982]
- **The result.** By October 1982 inflation had fallen to about 5 percent, long-term interest rates began to decline, and unemployment fell from its peak to about 8 percent a year later. The chapter should present the disinflation as an achievement with a measured cost, and must not attribute to its makers knowledge of the eventual recoveries of the 1980s and 1990s.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-check-dates-appD]
- **The caution.** Give every figure with its series, base dates, and definitions; the unemployment rate used by the Federal Reserve and the rate in the Bureau of Labor Statistics series must be reconciled, and a monthly peak is not an annual average.[^ch44-check-numbers]

### 2.3 Deregulation and the savings-and-loan crisis

- **The deregulation.** Rising interest rates in the late 1970s and early 1980s made savings-and-loan institutions, which held long-term fixed-rate mortgages funded by short-term deposits, especially vulnerable; the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 and the Garn–St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 loosened the rules, and regulators, lacking resources to close insolvent institutions, practiced forbearance and reduced capital standards.[^ch44-frb-savings-loan]
- **The insurance expansion.** The limit on deposit-insurance coverage rose from $40,000 to $100,000, making it easier for troubled institutions to attract deposits to lend with; from 1982 to 1985, thrift-industry assets grew 56 percent, more than twice the 24 percent rate at banks.[^ch44-frb-savings-loan]
- **The reckoning.** Texas was the epicenter; in 1988, more than 40 percent of thrift failures nationwide occurred there. Congress responded with the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery and Enforcement Act of 1989, which abolished the Federal Home Loan Bank Board and the FSLIC, created the Office of Thrift Supervision, and established the Resolution Trust Corporation. The RTC closed 747 institutions with assets of over $407 billion and was closed on December 31, 1995; the ultimate cost to taxpayers was estimated at as high as $124 billion. The chapter should present the episode as the product of interest-rate shocks, deregulation, regulatory forbearance, and risk-taking together, not as evidence that regulation or deregulation alone was to blame.[^ch44-frb-savings-loan][^ch44-check-debate-works]
- **The caution.** The several estimates of the taxpayer cost (about $124 billion in the Federal Reserve account, higher figures elsewhere) and the exact number of failed institutions must be reconciled across the FDIC, the Federal Reserve, and the National Commission report before assertion.[^ch44-frb-savings-loan][^ch44-check-numbers]

### 2.4 Inequality and the distribution of the gains

- **The distribution.** The Congressional Budget Office found that between 1979 and 2007, income grew by 275 percent for the top 1 percent of households, far faster than for the rest of the distribution, and that the share of income after transfers and taxes going to the top 1 percent rose from about 7 percent in 1979 to about 14 percent by 2022. Tax-data studies by Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, extended by Saez and Gabriel Zucman, found the top 1 percent share gaining roughly 13.5 points over 1978–2012. The chapter should give the 1979 baseline and the endpoints explicitly, and should not present a 2022 figure as though it were a 1980s measurement.[^ch44-cbo-inequality][^ch44-saez-zucman-2014][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The mechanism.** The chapter should distinguish the pre-tax distributional effects of the tax changes, the effects of the disinflation and of asset-price gains, and the effects of changes in labor-market institutions, rather than assigning the whole shift to one policy.[^ch44-cbo-inequality][^ch44-erta-1981]
- **The counterevidence.** Within the decade, high earners also paid a rising share of federal income tax even as their rates fell, and the top marginal rate moved twice; the chapter should state both the rate history and the share history and explain why they are not contradictory.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-taxreform-1986][^ch44-cbo-inequality]

### 2.5 Finance: leveraged buyouts, junk bonds, and Black Monday

- **The boom.** The mid-1980s saw a wave of leveraged buyouts and hostile takeovers financed by high-yield “junk” bonds, and a cultural argument about whether corporate restructuring was renewal or raid. The chapter should describe the mechanisms — debt-financed acquisitions, asset sales, and the market for corporate control — and cite the actual deal and securities records where it names an example.[^ch44-check-debate-works][^ch44-frb-crash-1987]
- **Black Monday.** On Monday, October 19, 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6 percent — about 508 points — the largest single-day percentage decline in its history at the time. The chapter should give the percentage, the point change, and the market context, and should note that the crash did not produce the general depression that some feared.[^ch44-frb-crash-1987][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The caution.** The chapter should not reduce the era’s finance to a morality tale about any single figure, and should connect the savings-and-loan failures and the 1987 crash to the regulatory questions rather than treating them as freestanding scandals.[^ch44-frb-savings-loan][^ch44-frb-crash-1987]

## 3. Rearmament, intervention, Iran-Contra, and Gorbachev (1981–1988)

### 3.1 The claim: confrontation and negotiation were the same policy

- **The claim.** The administration that built up the armed forces and supported anti-communist insurgencies also negotiated the first treaty that eliminated a class of nuclear weapons, and the two faces were not a contradiction but a strategy: strength, in Reagan’s terms, as the precondition of bargaining.[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign][^ch44-state-sdi][^ch44-state-inf]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the defense buildup and the Strategic Defense Initiative through the interventions in Lebanon, Grenada, and Libya, the Reagan Doctrine and Iran-Contra, and the summits with Mikhail Gorbachev that produced the INF Treaty, with Map 35 for the Eastern European and Soviet endgame.[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign][^ch44-pbs-iran-contra][^ch44-state-inf][^ch44-check-map35]

### 3.2 The defense buildup and the Strategic Defense Initiative

- **The buildup.** In March 1981 the administration set a defense budget of about $220 billion, described as the largest peacetime military budget in history, and proposed roughly 7 percent annual real increases through 1985, totaling nearly $1 trillion. The objectives were to strengthen the forces, reassure allies, and persuade the Soviets to bargain; the buildup also enlarged the deficits.[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign][^ch44-cbo-defense]
- **The “evil empire.”** In a March 8, 1983, speech Reagan called the Soviet Union an “evil empire” and “the focus of evil in the modern world.” The chapter should quote the speech and place it in the sequence that led to the negotiations, rather than treating it as either mere rhetoric or the cause of the Soviet collapse.[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign]
- **SDI.** On March 23, 1983, in a televised address, Reagan announced research into a national defense against ballistic missiles that could render nuclear weapons obsolete; the program became the Strategic Defense Initiative, nicknamed “Star Wars.” Critics noted that the technology was unproven, that it strained the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty, and that it might destabilize deterrence; Reagan refused to trade it away, and it became the sticking point in the arms talks until the two sides delinked defense and intermediate-range forces.[^ch44-state-sdi]
- **The near-miss.** On September 26, 1983, a defective Soviet satellite system reported a supposed American missile attack; the officer on duty did not report it up the chain, and the incident has become the standard illustration of how close the era came to accidental war. The chapter should present it as a documented episode with a source rather than as a legend.[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign][^ch44-check-sources]

### 3.3 Interventions and the Reagan Doctrine

- **The doctrine.** Under the “Reagan Doctrine,” the CIA trained and assisted anti-communist insurgencies worldwide, including the Contras opposing Nicaragua’s Sandinista government, whom Reagan called “the moral equivalent of our Founding Fathers.”[^ch44-pbs-iran-contra]
- **Lebanon.** On October 23, 1983, a suicide truck bomb destroyed the United States Marine barracks in Beirut, killing 241 American service members, including 220 Marines, along with 58 French personnel in a near-simultaneous attack; it was the deadliest single-day attack on the Marines since Iwo Jima. Reagan addressed the nation on October 27, 1983, defending the peacekeeping mission.[^ch44-arlington-beirut][^ch44-reagan-lebanon-grenada]
- **Grenada.** On October 25, 1983, United States forces invaded Grenada, a Caribbean island whose government the administration said had come under Cuban and Soviet influence; Reagan linked the action to the Beirut attack and the defense of American interests in the same October 27 address. The chapter should present the intervention with its stated justifications and the criticisms of the operation’s scale, evidence, and consequences.[^ch44-reagan-lebanon-grenada][^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign]
- **Libya.** In April 1986, after a bombing in West Berlin that the administration attributed to Libya, United States aircraft struck targets in Libya. The chapter should verify the dates, the casualty figures, and the evidence linking the Berlin attack before asserting responsibility.[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign][^ch44-check-sources]
- **Central America.** Congress restricted the administration’s support for the Contras through the Boland Amendment; a strengthened version in 1984 made assistance nearly impossible, and Reagan told his national security adviser to “do whatever you have to do to help these people keep body and soul together.” The chapter should present the constitutional conflict over covert action as a dispute about law and authority, not only about policy.[^ch44-pbs-iran-contra][^ch44-check-debate-works]

### 3.4 Iran-Contra

- **The disclosure.** In November 1986 it was disclosed that the United States had, from August 1985 onward, sold military equipment to Iran in secret, with an apparent linkage to efforts to free American hostages held in Lebanon, and that proceeds may have been diverted to the Contras.[^ch44-tower-commission]
- **The violations.** The sales ran counter to declared policies of neutrality in the Iran-Iraq war and an arms embargo on Iran, and to the stated refusal to pay ransom to hostage-takers; Congress was not informed; the National Security Council staff rather than the CIA appeared to run the operation, and the president appeared unaware of key elements.[^ch44-tower-commission]
- **The review.** Reagan established a special review board on December 1, 1986, chaired by former Senator John Tower, and said he wanted “all the facts to come out.” The Tower Commission, published on February 27, 1987, interviewed over 80 witnesses; Vice Admiral John Poindexter and Lieutenant Colonel Oliver North refused to appear, and the president declined to order them to do so. The board criticized the administration’s management of the NSC system and found that Reagan’s disengagement had created conditions that made the diversion possible.[^ch44-tower-commission][^ch44-pbs-iran-contra]
- **The allegations and the outcome.** The independent counsel Lawrence Walsh investigated for years; fourteen people were charged, North’s conviction was later overturned on a technicality, and President Bush issued six pardons in 1992. The chapter should distinguish proven findings, contested allegations, and political judgment, and should not treat either the commission’s conclusions or the later pardons as a final verdict.[^ch44-pbs-iran-contra][^ch44-check-debate-works]

### 3.5 Gorbachev and the turn to negotiation

- **The turn.** The Soviet leadership passed to Mikhail Gorbachev in March 1985, and the two leaders met at Geneva in November 1985 and at Reykjavik in October 1986. At Reykjavik Gorbachev proposed eliminating intermediate-range forces and, then, shorter-range forces as well, reviving the “zero option” as a “double zero,” but linked the agreement to American abandonment of SDI, and the talks stalled again.[^ch44-state-inf][^ch44-hoover-gaddis]
- **The treaty.** On December 8, 1987, Reagan and Gorbachev signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty in Washington, eliminating an entire class of ground-based intermediate- and shorter-range missiles and providing for intrusive verification; it was the first arms-control agreement completed since SALT II failed ratification, and the first to require the destruction of existing weapons rather than merely limiting future deployments.[^ch44-state-inf]
- **The wall.** On June 12, 1987, at the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin, Reagan challenged Gorbachev: “Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!” The chapter should date the speech and the wall’s fall, which came more than two years later, and should not imply that the speech caused the fall.[^ch44-nara-brandenburg][^ch44-state-berlin-wall]
- **The caution.** The Cold War’s ending belongs jointly to Chapters 44 and 45; this section carries the diplomacy to 1988, and the revolutions of 1989 and the Soviet dissolution belong to §5. The chapter should give Gorbachev’s internal reforms and constraints their own weight rather than presenting him as a passive respondent to American pressure.[^ch44-agent-hoover][^ch44-check-debate-works]

## 4. AIDS, drugs, punishment, technology, and social movements (1981–1991)

### 4.1 The claim: a decade that remade daily life and left some of its people behind

- **The claim.** The same years that produced the personal computer and widened some forms of expression produced a lethal epidemic met with official silence, a punitive turn in crime and drug policy, and culture wars over who belonged to the nation; the section holds the gains and the costs together.[^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline][^ch44-brennan-drug-war][^ch44-ibm-pc]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the first reports of AIDS in 1981 through the punitive turn, the rise of personal computing, and the social movements that contested the decade’s direction, with the In Focus on a single documented quilt panel.[^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline][^ch44-brennan-drug-war][^ch44-computerhistory-1984][^ch44-aidsmemorial-history]

### 4.2 The AIDS epidemic and the response to it

- **The onset.** On June 5, 1981, the Centers for Disease Control published a report in its *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* describing cases of a rare pneumonia in five previously healthy young gay men in Los Angeles; this edition is the first official reporting of what became the AIDS epidemic. By the end of 1981 there were 337 reported cases and 130 deaths.[^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline]
- **The community response.** Gay Men’s Health Crisis, the first community-based AIDS service provider in the United States, was founded in New York in January 1982; in 1987 Larry Kramer, previously its co-founder, helped found ACT UP, a more militant organization demanding faster research and an end to discrimination.[^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline][^ch44-history-aids-reagan]
- **The official silence.** Federal leaders were largely silent for four years; Reagan first mentioned AIDS publicly on September 17, 1985, in response to a reporter’s question, calling it a “top priority.” In 1986 the Institute of Medicine and Surgeon General C. Everett Koop issued reports calling for a coordinated response, and in 1987 the government mailed the Surgeon General’s findings to every household. The chapter should give the dates and the funding sequence, and should name the silence plainly.[^ch44-history-aids-reagan][^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline]
- **The caution.** The record of the epidemic’s early years over-represents officials and reporters and under-represents the dead, the bereaved, and the communities most affected; the chapter should name that asymmetry and should not attribute the entire response or its delay to any single official. The date sequence and the funding history must come from the primary public-health records, not only from the institutional timelines.[^ch44-check-silences][^ch44-history-aids-reagan][^ch44-check-aids]
- **In Focus.** The close reading reads one documented panel of the NAMES Project AIDS Memorial Quilt — the first panel, made by Cleve Jones in memory of his friend Marvin Feldman — placed within the inaugural display of October 11, 1987, on the National Mall during the National March on Washington for Lesbian and Gay Rights. The display included 1,920 panels and was visited by half a million people over the weekend; six teams of eight volunteers unfolded the sections at sunrise and volunteers read the 1,920 names aloud. The box should use the panel as the smallest unit of a memorial of standardized three-by-six-foot panels, read its documented maker-and-subject relationship, and avoid assigning to it any detail the sources do not establish.[^ch44-aidsmemorial-history][^ch44-aidsmemorial-panel][^ch44-nlm-quilt]
- **The image record.** Image 7 (`ch44-image-01`) is the registered reproduction of the panel. It requires an exact panel identifier, consent from the maker or the National AIDS Memorial, a credit line, and rights evidence before reproduction; until then the box is a close reading and the asset is `planned`.[^ch44-check-focus][^ch44-check-rights]

### 4.3 Crime, drugs, and the punitive turn

- **The statutes.** The Comprehensive Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1984 eliminated parole in the federal system; the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 established mandatory minimum sentences, including the ratio that treated five grams of crack cocaine as equivalent to 500 grams of powder cocaine — a 100-to-1 disparity; its 1988 expansion added a broad conspiracy definition. These laws flooded the federal system with people convicted of low-level and nonviolent drug offenses.[^ch44-brennan-drug-war]
- **The consequences.** Because crack was more common in Black communities while household surveys showed larger numbers of White users, the facially neutral penalties fell disproportionately on Black defendants. The chapter should give the statutory text, the sentencing schedule, and incarceration counts from the Bureau of Justice Statistics, and should distinguish the laws’ language from their measurable effects.[^ch44-brennan-drug-war][^ch44-hinton-war-on-poverty][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The campaign.** The decade’s public anti-drug messaging, including the “Just Say No” campaign associated with Nancy Reagan, belongs here. The chapter should present the war on drugs as a policy with documented racial consequences, and should represent the argument for it — that the crack epidemic imposed real harm on affected communities — before testing that argument against the record.[^ch44-brennan-drug-war][^ch44-check-debate-works]
- **The historiography.** Elizabeth Hinton argues that mass incarceration’s origins lie partly in the social programs of the 1960s rather than in the 1980s alone; the chapter should note the longer-run argument and place the 1980s statutes within it. Hinton was identified through bibliographic records and did not read for this outline; supply page locators or mark the row unverified.[^ch44-hinton-war-on-poverty][^ch44-check-sources]

### 4.4 Technology and the information economy

- **The personal computer.** IBM introduced its Personal Computer, the Model 5150, in 1981, and within a year more than 750 software packages were available for it. In 1984 Apple introduced the Macintosh, the first successful mouse-driven computer with a graphical user interface, priced at $2,495. The chapter should describe the machines as documented products, not as symbols whose effects were known at the time.[^ch44-ibm-pc][^ch44-computerhistory-1984]
- **The stake.** The personal-computer industry connected the decade’s defense-funded research, its entrepreneurial culture, and the beginnings of a networked economy; the chapter should distinguish what happened in the 1980s from the commercial internet’s arrival in the 1990s, and should not read later outcomes back into the decade.[^ch44-computerhistory-1984][^ch44-check-dates-appD]
- **The caution.** Sales, diffusion, and productivity figures are contested and unit-sensitive; give each with its source series and base year, and label estimates as estimates.[^ch44-check-numbers]

### 4.5 Social movements and the culture wars

- **The movements.** The decade saw the growth of gay and lesbian political organization, including AIDS activism; contested battles over abortion and the Equal Rights Amendment that Chapter 43 carries into this period; immigration reform in the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986; and ongoing Indigenous, Latino, and Black organizing. The chapter should note these without re-narrating the ERA fight or the 1979–82 organizing that Chapter 43 owns.[^ch44-check-ch43][^ch44-history-aids-reagan]
- **The culture wars.** Conflicts over school curricula, the family, sexuality, and the boundary of the nation expressed a contest over belonging that the chapter should present as the work of identifiable organizations and campaigns, not as a spontaneous national mood.[^ch44-check-debate-works][^ch44-check-silences]
- **The caution.** The record over-represents organized, media-visible movements and under-represents the poor, the rural, and the immigrant. Name the asymmetry.[^ch44-check-silences]

## 5. George H. W. Bush, the Gulf War, and the Soviet collapse (1988–1991)

### 5.1 The claim: a presidency of continuity that completed the Cold War

- **The claim.** George H. W. Bush’s presidency inherited Reagan’s program, broke with part of it, and presided over the end of the Cold War and a short, decisive war in the Persian Gulf; the section asks how much Bush chose and how much the structural collapse of the Soviet bloc and the decisions of others made inevitable.[^ch44-app-1988][^ch44-state-gulf-war][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse]
- **The structure.** The section runs from the 1988 election through the Gulf War, the revolutions of 1989, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union, with Map 35.[^ch44-app-1988][^ch44-state-gulf-war][^ch44-state-berlin-wall][^ch44-check-map35]

### 5.2 The 1988 election and the Bush presidency

- **The result.** Bush won 426 electoral votes and about 53.4 percent of the popular vote to Michael Dukakis’s 111 electoral votes and about 45.6 percent; one Democratic elector in West Virginia voted for Lloyd Bentsen. Bush had pledged at the 1988 Republican convention, “Read my lips: no new taxes,” and the phrase became the measure of his presidency when he accepted a 1990 budget agreement that included tax increases.[^ch44-app-1988][^ch44-bush-tax-reversal]
- **The frame.** The chapter should present the 1988 campaign and the tax reversal as the hinge between the Reagan and Bush presidencies, and should not treat the reversal as the single explanation of the 1992 election, which belongs to Chapter 45.[^ch44-app-1988][^ch44-check-ch45]

### 5.3 The Gulf War

- **The invasion.** On August 2, 1990, about 100,000 Iraqi troops invaded Kuwait and overran the country; on August 28 Iraq declared Kuwait its nineteenth province. Iraq, emerging from the Iran-Iraq War with debts of about $37 billion to Gulf creditors, had demanded debt forgiveness and made claims on Kuwaiti territory and oil.[^ch44-state-gulf-war]
- **The response.** The United Nations Security Council passed resolutions demanding withdrawal, imposing sanctions, and declaring the annexation null and void. A United States-led coalition deployed to Saudi Arabia; after an October 30 decision to use force if necessary, the Security Council passed Resolution 678, authorizing force and giving Iraq a 45-day grace period.[^ch44-state-gulf-war]
- **The war.** The coalition air campaign began on January 16, 1991, and a ground campaign of just 100 hours began on February 24; by February 28 Iraqi forces had been driven from Kuwait and the United States declared a cease-fire. Resolution 686 set cease-fire conditions, and Resolution 687 established the United Nations inspection commission. The chapter should give the war’s phases, the coalition’s composition (thirty-four countries), and the casualty and cost figures from the Department of Defense record, and should present the outcome as a liberation of Kuwait that left the Iraqi government in place.[^ch44-state-gulf-war][^ch44-check-numbers]
- **The domestic aftermath.** The war’s high approval ratings did not guarantee Bush’s reelection; the chapter should name the 1992 election only as the hand-off to Chapter 45.[^ch44-check-ch45]

### 5.4 1989: the revolutions in Eastern Europe (Map 35)

- **The wall.** During the night of November 9, 1989, crowds of Germans began dismantling the Berlin Wall, the barrier that had symbolized the Cold War division of Europe for almost thirty years. By October 1990 Germany was reunified, and the other East European regimes collapsed in turn.[^ch44-state-berlin-wall]
- **The sequence.** The revolutions were not one event but many, with different paths and dates: negotiated transitions in Poland and Hungary, the Velvet Revolution in Czechoslovakia in November 1989, a violent overthrow in Romania in December 1989, and the push for independence in the Baltic states and the Caucasus feeding into 1990–91. Map 35 is the registered visual for this section and must render those differences rather than a single date.[^ch44-state-berlin-wall][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse][^ch44-check-map35]
- **The American role.** The Bush administration supported the changes but was largely reactive, careful not to worsen Gorbachev’s position; Gorbachev’s decision not to use force in Eastern Europe was decisive. The chapter should present the outcome as the product of Eastern European and Soviet decisions as much as of American policy.[^ch44-state-soviet-collapse][^ch44-check-debate-works]

### 5.5 The dissolution of the Soviet Union

- **The unravelling.** After the May 1990 elections Gorbachev faced pressure from Boris Yeltsin and the pluralist movement for faster reform and from hard-line Communists against him; in January 1991 Soviet troops intervened in Lithuania and Latvia. In July 1991 Bush signed the START treaty at the Moscow Summit.[^ch44-state-soviet-collapse]
- **The coup and the end.** A hard-line coup against Gorbachev in August 1991 failed, diminished Gorbachev’s power, and propelled Yeltsin forward; Ukraine and Belarus declared independence, and in early December the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus formed the Commonwealth of Independent States at Brest. On December 25, 1991, the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time, and Gorbachev resigned as president of the Soviet Union, leaving Yeltsin as president of Russia.[^ch44-state-soviet-collapse][^ch44-gorbachev-resignation]
- **The question for the chapter.** The dissolution must be presented as an outcome whose forms, timing, and relative peacefulness were produced by many actors — Gorbachev’s choices, the national movements, the failed coup, and the conduct of the Bush administration — rather than as the automatic result of any one policy.[^ch44-state-soviet-collapse][^ch44-check-debate-works]

## 6. The Debate

### 6.1 What is settled

- **The 1980 election was won by Reagan.** He took 489 electoral votes and about 50.7 percent of the popular vote; Carter took 49 electoral votes and about 41.0 percent, and Anderson about 6.6 percent.[^ch44-app-1980]
- **The tax rates changed.** The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 reduced the top marginal rate from 70 to 50 percent, and the Tax Reform Act of 1986 reduced it from 50 to 28 percent.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-taxreform-1986]
- **The recession of 1981–82 followed a deliberate disinflation.** The Federal Reserve under Volcker targeted the money supply; unemployment reached 10.8 percent in late 1982 before inflation and unemployment fell.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-bls-unemployment-1982]
- **Defense spending rose sharply, and the Strategic Defense Initiative was announced on March 23, 1983.**[^ch44-miller-reagan-foreign][^ch44-state-sdi]
- **Iran-Contra involved secret arms sales to Iran from 1985 and a suspected diversion of proceeds to the Contras, disclosed in November 1986.** The Tower Commission reported on February 27, 1987.[^ch44-tower-commission]
- **The INF Treaty was signed on December 8, 1987.**[^ch44-state-inf]
- **AIDS was first reported on June 5, 1981, and the Reagan administration’s public acknowledgment came only in 1985.**[^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline][^ch44-history-aids-reagan]
- **The Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989, and the Soviet Union dissolved on December 25, 1991.**[^ch44-state-berlin-wall][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse]
- **The Gulf War ran from the August 2, 1990, invasion of Kuwait to the February 28, 1991, cease-fire, with a 100-hour ground campaign beginning February 24.**[^ch44-state-gulf-war]

### 6.2 What is open

- **Was 1980 a realignment or an interruption?** The realignment reading treats 1980 as the beginning of a durable conservative era in national politics; the continuity reading treats the Reagan years as a change in rhetoric and some policy that left the New Deal institutional order substantially intact. The chapter should state each at its strongest and test the claims against specific policy outcomes rather than rhetoric.[^ch44-wilentz-age-reagan][^ch44-schwab-illusion][^ch44-check-debate-works][^ch44-check-continuity]
- **How much did tax policy cause the widening distribution?** The tax changes, the disinflation, asset-price gains, and labor-market institutions all moved together; the chapter should separate the mechanisms rather than assign the whole shift to one cause.[^ch44-cbo-inequality][^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-check-debate-works]
- **Who ended the Cold War?** One account credits Reagan’s pressure and perception; another locates the collapse in the Soviet Union’s internal failures; another emphasizes Gorbachev’s decisions; the professional consensus favors multiple causation. The chapter should present the rival arguments fairly and weigh them against the archival record.[^ch44-schweizer-victory][^ch44-gaddis-coldwar][^ch44-brown-gorbachev-factor][^ch44-kotkin-armageddon][^ch44-check-coldwar]
- **Did the 1980s make the AIDS response or delay it?** The epidemic’s early course, the funding sequence, and the timing of official acknowledgment remain subjects of debate among historians of public health, and the chapter should treat the question as one about evidence and responsibility, not as a settled verdict.[^ch44-history-aids-reagan][^ch44-brier-infectious-ideas][^ch44-shilts-band]
- **Was the punitive turn a response to crime or a cause of mass incarceration?** The statutes of 1984, 1986, and 1988 and the longer history of social policy both bear on the question; Hinton’s argument about origins in the 1960s should be weighed against accounts centered on the 1980s crack epidemic.[^ch44-brennan-drug-war][^ch44-hinton-war-on-poverty]

### 6.3 The historiographical lesson

- **The claim.** The evidence supports a history in which the conservative turn was real but partial, in which the Cold War’s end was multi-causal and not a single leader’s victory, and in which the era’s costs fell unevenly and were often borne by people with the least voice in the record.[^ch44-wilentz-age-reagan][^ch44-gaddis-coldwar][^ch44-cbo-inequality]
- **Contingency.** The Volcker disinflation, the 1981 tax cut, the decision not to trade SDI away, the Iran-Contra decisions, the AIDS response, and the Gulf War were choices that could have gone otherwise; the chapter should identify the alternatives actually considered and why they were rejected.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-state-sdi][^ch44-tower-commission]
- **Structure and choice together.** The chapter should hold the structural conditions — inflation, the oil shocks, the Soviet economy, the crack epidemic, the financial deregulation — together with the choices made within them, rather than choosing one register of explanation over the other.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse][^ch44-check-scope]
- **The synthesis for this chapter.** Between 1980 and 1991 the United States cut its taxes, broke its inflation, watched its inequality widen, lost tens of thousands to an epidemic it met late, built a military it used in a short war, and saw its principal adversary dissolve from within; the chapter’s question — what changed and what did not — is answered by holding those developments together.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse]

## Legacies

The years from 1980 to 1991 left a federal government that taxed less and borrowed more, and a political debate organized around that trade. The rate reductions of 1981 and 1986 fixed a lower structure and, through indexation, changed how bracket creep worked; the deficits and the debt that grew alongside them set the terms of budget politics for a generation. The chapter should connect these to the present as documented changes in fiscal policy, not as a political verdict.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-taxreform-1986][^ch44-cbo-defense]

The era also left a wider distribution and a longer punitive reach. The disinflation that ended the inflation of the 1970s began decades in which the gains at the top outran those in the middle and the bottom; the drug statutes of 1984, 1986, and 1988 began the mass incarceration whose scale and racial incidence the following chapters treat. And the epidemic left a memorial practice — the Quilt — that outlasted the officials who met it late.[^ch44-cbo-inequality][^ch44-brennan-drug-war][^ch44-aidsmemorial-history]

And it left a transformed world position. The INF Treaty, the revolutions of 1989, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union ended the Cold War that had organized American politics since the 1940s, and left the United States without its principal adversary. Chapter 45 takes up the order that followed; this chapter should present the ending as the achievement of many actors and the beginning of questions no one had yet answered.[^ch44-state-inf][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse][^ch44-check-ch45]

## Summary

- Reagan won the 1980 election with 489 electoral votes and about 50.7 percent of the popular vote; the Iran hostages were released on January 20, 1981, completing a 444-day captivity.[^ch44-app-1980][^ch44-state-iran-release-check]
- The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 reduced the top marginal rate from 70 to 50 percent, and the Tax Reform Act of 1986 reduced it from 50 to 28 percent.[^ch44-erta-1981][^ch44-taxreform-1986]
- On August 5, 1981, the administration fired 11,345 striking air traffic controllers after about 13,000 walked off the job.[^ch44-miller-patco]
- The recession of July 1981 to November 1982 followed the Federal Reserve’s disinflation; unemployment reached 10.8 percent in late 1982 before inflation and unemployment fell.[^ch44-fed-recession-1982][^ch44-bls-unemployment-1982]
- The savings-and-loan crisis produced the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery and Enforcement Act of 1989 and the Resolution Trust Corporation, at an estimated taxpayer cost as high as $124 billion; on October 19, 1987, the Dow fell 22.6 percent.[^ch44-frb-savings-loan][^ch44-frb-crash-1987]
- Income growth at the top far outpaced the rest of the distribution after 1979, and the top 1 percent share rose over the following decades.[^ch44-cbo-inequality][^ch44-saez-zucman-2014]
- Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative on March 23, 1983; the Lebanon barracks bombing killed 241 Americans on October 23, 1983; and the administration invaded Grenada on October 25, 1983.[^ch44-state-sdi][^ch44-arlington-beirut][^ch44-reagan-lebanon-grenada]
- Iran-Contra, disclosed in November 1986, involved secret arms sales to Iran from 1985 and a suspected diversion to the Contras; the Tower Commission reported on February 27, 1987.[^ch44-tower-commission]
- Reagan and Gorbachev signed the INF Treaty on December 8, 1987, eliminating an entire class of ground-based missiles.[^ch44-state-inf]
- AIDS was first reported on June 5, 1981; Reagan first mentioned it publicly on September 17, 1985; the NAMES Project AIDS Memorial Quilt was first displayed on October 11, 1987.[^ch44-hiv-gov-timeline][^ch44-history-aids-reagan][^ch44-aidsmemorial-history]
- The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 established the 100-to-1 crack-to-powder sentencing disparity.[^ch44-brennan-drug-war]
- Bush won the 1988 election; the Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989; the Gulf War ended with a cease-fire on February 28, 1991; and the Soviet Union dissolved on December 25, 1991.[^ch44-app-1988][^ch44-state-berlin-wall][^ch44-state-gulf-war][^ch44-state-soviet-collapse]
